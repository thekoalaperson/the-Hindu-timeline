# Śatākṣī / Śākambharī and the slaying of Durgamāsura

> Where this sits: a self-contained Śākta myth-cycle within **[Deities & Cosmic Hierarchy](README.md)** — the story that makes the Great Goddess the source of *rain and food itself*, and supplies the Devī-Bhāgavata's own etymology for the name **Durgā**. It is the full narrative behind the one-line foretelling in the **[Devī Māhātmya](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md#6-uttama-carita--the-śumbha-niśumbha-cycle-ch-513)** (Nārāyaṇī Stuti, ch. 11) and the roster entry in **[Devī Forms & Navadurgā](devi-forms-and-navadurga.md)**. The myth is **cyclic / cross-yuga** (foretold as a future descent, fixed to no manvantara); its living cult at Sambhar, Saharanpur and Badami is **[Kali Yuga — documented history](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)**.

---

## Orientation — one Goddess, three names in sequence

- **A single episode, three earned epithets** — the Devī-Bhāgavata tells one continuous story in which the Goddess acquires, in order, three of her most famous names: **Śatākṣī** ("Hundred-Eyed," from the tears she weeps), **Śākambharī** ("Herb/Vegetable-Bearer," from the food she grows from her body), and **Durgā** ("she who slew Durgama"). The narrative is the charter for all three. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)
- **Canonical home** — the fullest telling is the **Saptama Skandha (Book VII), chapter 28** of the **[Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/devi-bhagavata-upapurana.md)**, narrated by Vyāsa to King Janamejaya. A **[Skanda Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md)** parallel and local sthala-purāṇas carry the same core. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28; Skanda Purāṇa*)
- **The seed-verse** — the whole story is compressed into a few ślokas of the **Nārāyaṇī Stuti** in the [Devī Māhātmya ch. 11](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) (= [Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/markandeya-purana.md)), where the Goddess *foretells* she will take these forms in a future age of drought — so the Devī-Bhāgavata reads as the expansion of a promise already made in the older text. (*Devī Māhātmya 11.44–51*)

## The theft of the Vedas and the hundred-year drought

- **The demon Durgama** — an asura named **Durgama** (**Durgamāsura**), given in the Devī-Bhāgavata as the son of **Ruru**, born in the demon-line of **Hiraṇyākṣa**. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)
  - *Variant (patronymic):* recensions and retellings differ on the parentage; the Ruru/Hiraṇyākṣa descent is the Devī-Bhāgavata's, and other Śākta sthala-texts leave it unspecified. (*[disputed]; recension variance*)
- **The reasoning behind the theft** — Durgama concludes that the strength of the gods rests entirely on the **Vedas**: it is the Vedic yajña that feeds the devas and, through the offering to Agni that rises to the Sun, *causes the rains*. Cut off the Vedas, and heaven starves. He therefore performs fierce **tapas to Brahmā** and asks for **the four Vedas** as his boon. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)
- **Brahmā grants the Vedas** — Brahmā gives the Vedas into Durgama's keeping; they vanish from the mouths of the Brāhmaṇas, who forget the mantras and the rites. All sacrifice, sandhyā, and study cease. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)
- **Cosmic consequence — the rains fail** — with yajña extinguished, no offerings reach the gods; the rain-cycle collapses and a drought said to last **a hundred years** grips the earth. Rivers and tanks dry, the ground cracks, crops and herbs wither; men, cattle and the very gods weaken from hunger and thirst, corpses lie unburnt. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)
- **The Brāhmaṇas take refuge** — surviving sages and Brāhmaṇas gather in the Himālaya and turn in desperation to the **Mother of the Vedas**, singing hymns to the supreme Devī to save a world dying of famine. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)

## Śatākṣī — the Hundred-Eyed who wept the rains

- **The Goddess appears bearing a hundred eyes** — moved by the sages' hymns, the Devī manifests in a benign, dark-blue radiant form covered with **innumerable eyes** — canonically **a hundred** — with which she gazes upon the starving world; from this she is famed as **Śatākṣī**, "She of the Hundred Eyes." (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28; Devī Māhātmya 11 — "śatākṣī"*)
  - *Variant (eye-count):* the epithet is fixed as **Śata** (hundred); some retellings gloss it as "innumerable / thousand-eyed," but *Śatākṣī* is the name the texts award. (*[folk] gloss*)
- **She weeps the rain** — seeing the misery, tears stream ceaselessly from her hundred eyes; from her weeping fall torrents of **water/rain** that flood the parched land, refill the rivers, and end the drought. The Goddess's grief *is* the returning monsoon. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)
  - *Note (nine nights):* a liturgical tradition maps the rain-weeping to **nine nights**, aligning the episode with Navarātri; the Purāṇa itself does not enumerate the nights. (*[folk]/liturgical*)

## Śākambharī — nourisher from her own body

- **Food grown from her own body** — to feed a world whose grain and herbs had all perished, the Goddess brings forth from **her own body** an abundance of **śāka** (leafy greens, vegetables), together with fruits, roots, and edible plants, distributing them to the starving. From this she is named **Śākambharī** — "She who bears/nourishes with vegetables" (*śāka* + *bharī*, "bearing"). (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28; Devī Māhātmya 11 — "śākambharī"*)
- **The nourishing iconography** — as Śākambharī she is depicted holding and radiating **vegetables, greens, fruits and flowers** (often with a lotus, and a bow and arrows for the coming battle); she is the Goddess as **Annapūrṇā-like giver of food**, her fierce Śakti turned to feeding rather than slaying. (*Śākambharī iconographic tradition*)
- **The benevolent counterpart to the war-goddess** — this episode is deliberately the *saumya* (gentle, provident) face of the same Devī who elsewhere is the martial Caṇḍī/Durgā; the [Devī Māhātmya](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) files Śākambharī among the Goddess's "vibhūti" descents alongside Raktadantikā, Bhīmā and Bhrāmarī. (*Devī Māhātmya 11*)

## The slaying of Durgama and the name 'Durgā'

- **Durgama attacks** — enraged that the Goddess has undone his famine, **Durgama** marches with a vast asura host to seize her and the Vedas back. The Devī raises a **fortification / a wall of light** around the refuge of the gods and sages and prepares for war. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)
- **The army of Śaktis** — from her own body she emits a host of protective **Śaktis** (Kālikā and other emanations) who, with the Devī, rout Durgama's forces in a great battle. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)
- **The death of Durgama** — the Goddess looses a shower of **arrows** and pierces Durgama through, felling him; as he dies his **tejas (life-radiance) merges into her** — the standard Śākta motif of the slain asura reabsorbed into the Goddess (as with Mahiṣa in the [Devī Māhātmya](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md#5-madhyama-carita--mahiṣāsura-mardinī-ch-24)). (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)
  - *Variant (weapon/count):* details of the kill — a volley of arrows vs. a single shaft, the number loosed — vary by recension; the constant is death by the Goddess's bow. (*recension variance*)
- **She restores the Vedas** — with Durgama dead the Goddess **recovers the four Vedas** and returns them to Brahmā and the Brāhmaṇas; yajña, rain and dharma are re-established, and the world is made whole. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28*)

## Etymology of 'Durgā' — two derivations, catalogued not merged

The corpus keeps both traditional derivations of the name **Durgā** side by side; they are not reconciled.

- *Derivation A (this myth — Devī-Bhāgavata / Devī Māhātmya):* she is **Durgā** because she **slew the asura Durgama** — "Durgāṃ Durgama-nāśanāt," the name is the trophy of this victory. This is the etymology the Śatākṣī/Śākambharī episode exists to supply. (*Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa VII.28; Devī Māhātmya 11.49–50*)
- *Derivation B (the common gloss):* **durga** = "fort / impassable / hard-to-cross difficulty," so **Durgā** = "she who is impassable / who carries one across every *durga* (danger, hell, ocean of existence)." This is the derivation given in the **Durgā-dhyāna** and the popular tradition. (*Purāṇic/traditional etymology; cf. [Devī Forms & Navadurgā](devi-forms-and-navadurga.md)*)
- *Note (both, at once):* Śākta commentary happily holds both — the demon-slaying name and the "fortress / rescuer-from-difficulty" name reinforce each other; the [Devī Māhātmya file](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) records the Durgama derivation as the myth's own charter. (*[scholarly] harmonization*)

## The Devī Māhātmya foretelling & the Skanda parallel

- **Foretold in the Nārāyaṇī Stuti (older text)** — the [Devī Māhātmya ch. 11](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md#6-uttama-carita--the-śumbha-niśumbha-cycle-ch-513), after the Śumbha-Niśumbha war, has the Goddess *promise* these future forms: when for a hundred years rain fails, she will behold the sages with a hundred eyes (**Śatākṣī**), nourish the world with body-born vegetables until the rains return (**Śākambharī**), and slay the great asura **Durgama**, thereby becoming **Durgā**. The Devī-Bhāgavata VII.28 is the *fulfilment* of that prophecy. (*Devī Māhātmya 11.44–51 = [Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/markandeya-purana.md) ch. 91*)
- **Chronology of texts, not of events** — because the [Mārkaṇḍeya](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/markandeya-purana.md) core (Devī Māhātmya, c. 550 CE, [scholarly]) *predates* the [Devī-Bhāgavata](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/devi-bhagavata-upapurana.md) (c. 11th–13th c. CE, [scholarly]), the "foretelling → fulfilment" relationship is the later Purāṇa dramatizing a compact promise of the earlier one. (*[scholarly]*)
- **Skanda / sthala-purāṇa parallel** — the [Skanda Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md) and local temple-purāṇas carry cognate Śākambharī legends attached to specific shrines (below), with regional details of the drought and the vegetables produced. (*Skanda Purāṇa; sthala-purāṇas [regional]*)

## The living Śākambharī cult

The Śākambharī myth is not merely textual — it anchors a spread-out but continuous Goddess cult with its own festival calendar.

- **Śākambharī Śakti Pīṭha, Saharanpur (Uttar Pradesh)** — a major shrine in the **Śivālik** hills ~40 km from Saharanpur, counted among the Śākta pīṭhas; the presiding Śākambharī Devī is flanked by **Bhīmā** and **Bhrāmarī**, the very sister-forms listed beside Śākambharī in Devī Māhātmya 11. (*[regional]; sthala-tradition; cf. [Śakti Pīṭhas](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md)*)
- **Śākambharī / Sambhar (Rajasthan)** — the Goddess is the **kuladevī of the Cāhamāna (Chauhan) dynasty**, whose early capital **Śākambharī** (modern **Sambhar**, by Sambhar Salt Lake) is named for her; a local legend has the Goddess convert a plain into the great **salt lake** as a boon. The Chauhans styled themselves lords of Śākambharī. (*[regional]/historical; Cāhamāna tradition*)
  - *Two clocks:* the Śākambharī-Cāhamānas ruled from Sambhar from **c. 6th–12th c. CE** ([scholarly], documented history), long after the *scriptural* drought-myth that names the place. (*[scholarly]*)
- **Banaśaṅkarī, Badami (Karnataka)** — **Banaśaṅkarī** ("Vana-Śaṅkarī," the forest Śākambharī) at Cholachagudda near Badami is a prominent Śākambharī-form temple; her icon holds foliage and vegetables, and her **Banaśaṅkarī jātre** is a large regional fair. (*[regional]; Deccan tradition*)
- **Śākambharī Pūrṇimā / Navarātri** — the cult's chief festival is **Śākambharī Pūrṇimā** (full moon of **Pauṣa**, the "Śākambharī Jayantī"), preceded by the eight-day **Śākambharī Navarātri** (Pauṣa śukla-aṣṭamī to pūrṇimā). Devotees offer and distribute vegetables, greens and fruit — the food she once grew from her body. (*[regional]/living tradition*)

## Two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic time** — the episode fixes **no manvantara or yuga**: in the [Devī Māhātmya](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) it is a *future* descent the Goddess vows to make "when rain fails for a hundred years," an archetypal drought re-enacted in worship rather than dated. It sits **cross-yuga**, the provident (Śākambharī) and martial (Durgā) faces of the one Devī. (*Devī Māhātmya 11; Devī-Bhāgavata VII.28*)
- **Academic / historical time** — the **[Devī Māhātmya](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/markandeya-purana.md)** seed-text is **c. 550 CE**; the full **[Devī-Bhāgavata](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/devi-bhagavata-upapurana.md)** telling belongs to the mature Śākta Purāṇic stratum, **c. 11th–13th c. CE**; the Sambhar / Chauhan and Badami cult-centres are **documented history** of the mid-1st-millennium CE onward. (*[scholarly], [late text]*)
