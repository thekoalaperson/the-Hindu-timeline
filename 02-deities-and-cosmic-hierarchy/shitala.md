# Śītalā — the Cooling Goddess of Pox and Fever

> Deities-and-hierarchy node. **Śītalā** (Hindi *Śītalā Mātā / Sheetala Mata*, "the Cool One") is the pan-North-Indian goddess of **smallpox, pox-eruptions, measles and fevers** — the Hindi-belt counterpart of the Tamil **[Māriyammaṉ](mariamman.md)** — who *both sends and cools* the burning sicknesses of the body. ## 1. Two Clocks — Who Śītalā Is

The figure has a thin **scriptural** footprint and a thick, living **historical/folk** cult; keep them distinct.

- **Scriptural clock** — a goddess **Śītalā** appears only in late, hymnic/upapurāṇic material: the **Śītalāṣṭaka** ("Eight Verses to Śītalā") ascribed to the **Skanda Purāṇa** and to **Śiva** as speaker, plus stray naming in Tantric and *vrata* digests. She has **no developed myth** in the Vedas or core Purāṇas. (Skanda Purāṇa — Śītalāṣṭaka) [late text]
- **Historical clock** — the goddess actually worshipped is a **folk disease-mother** of the Gangetic plain (UP, Bihar, Rajasthan, MP, Bengal, Gujarat, Punjab, Nepal), whose festivals, shrines and **Śītalā-maṅgal** literature are attested over recent centuries — a **kali-historical** cult with no fixed yuga-date. [folk] [scholarly]
- **What she governs** — **smallpox (Hindi *mātā / chechak / baṛī māī*, "the big mother"), chickenpox, measles, pustular fevers, heat-rash and eye-disease**; the eruptions are read as the goddess's **presence in the body**, so the patient is "graced/possessed" by Śītalā, not merely diseased. [folk]
- **The cooling theology** — the governing folk physics is **heat vs. cool**: fever and pox are the goddess's *heat*; she is propitiated by **cooling** — cold/stale food, water-pots, neem, no fire, no spice — so that the burning subsides. Her very name, *śītalā*, means "she who cools / makes cool." [scholarly]

## 2. Names and Epithets (catalog)

- **Śītalā** — "the Cool One," the standard Sanskrit/Hindi name; an apotropaic, euphemistic name for a hot, dangerous power. [folk etymology]
- **Śītalā Mātā / Sheetala Mata / Śītalā Māī** — the everyday devotional names across the Hindi belt.
- **Baṛī Māī / Mātā** — "the Great Mother / the Mother," the deferential name for smallpox itself, the disease spoken of as the goddess in person. [regional]
- **Olāī Caṇḍī / Olā-Bibi overlap** — in Bengal the cholera/diarrhoea sister-goddess **Olāī Caṇḍī** and the Muslim **Olā-Bibi** stand beside Śītalā in the disease-mother set. [regional]
- **Vasanta** complex — pox/smallpox is itself called **vasanta** ("spring," when epidemics peaked); Śītalā heads the *vasanta* disease-goddesses. [folk]
- *Variant (Tantric assimilation):* in some Tantric and Śākta readings Śītalā is glossed as a form of **Pārvatī / Durgā / Kālī** descended to cure the world — a Sanskritizing equation rather than an old identity. [disputed]

## 3. Iconography — Donkey, Broom, Winnowing-Fan, Water-Pot

The standard *dhyāna* (drawn from the Śītalāṣṭaka itself) is one of the most fixed and recognizable in folk Hinduism.

- **The donkey (gardabha) vāhana** — Śītalā rides a **donkey/ass**, a low, humble, heat-bearing beast; she is almost always shown astride or accompanied by it — her single most diagnostic attribute. (Skanda Purāṇa — Śītalāṣṭaka)
- **The broom (mārjanī / jhāṛū)** — she carries a **broom**, read two ways: to **sweep away** the disease-germs/pustules, or to *spread* them — the same ambivalent hand that sends and removes. (Śītalāṣṭaka)
- **The winnowing-fan (śūrpa / sūp)** — a **winnowing basket**, to **fan and cool** the fevered body and to separate (winnow) the afflicted; a household tool turned divine emblem. (Śītalāṣṭaka)
- **The water-pot (kalaśa / ghaṭa)** — a **pot of cool water** (sometimes neem-water) for cooling and cleansing; in countless shrines the goddess is worshipped **as** a pot or a daubed stone rather than an image. (Śītalāṣṭaka) [folk]
- **Nakedness / a red garment, seated on the ass** — the *dhyāna* describes her as **digambarā (sky-clad) or red-clad**, holding the above, sometimes with a few neem-twigs; a stark, un-ornamented form. (Śītalāṣṭaka) [late text]
- **Aniconic shrines** — most village Śītalā shrines are a **plain platform with rough stones, a daubed mound, or a pot under a neem (nīm) tree**, neem being her tree as it is Māriyammaṉ's. (Source: village-shrine ethnography) [folk]
- *Variant (the seven sisters / Saptamātṛkā overlap):* Śītalā is frequently grouped with six other disease-mothers as **"seven sisters"** (with e.g. **Maśānī, Basantī, Mahāmāī, Polamde, Lamkaṛiyā, Agvāṇī, Choti Mata, Phūlamatī**, names varying by region) governing distinct poxes/fevers — never a fixed canonical seven. (Source: North Indian folk tradition) [regional]

## 4. Śītalā-aṣṭamī / Basoda — the Cold-Food Vrata

Her signature observance dramatizes the cooling theology by **extinguishing the hearth-fire**.

- **Śītalā-aṣṭamī (Basoda)** — celebrated on the **eighth (aṣṭamī) of the dark fortnight of Caitra** (March–April, just after Holi), in some regions on Caitra-śukla or other pox-season dates; in Bengal/East India observed in **Phālguna–Caitra** and around the hot months. [regional]
- **Basoda — eating only cold/stale food** — on Śītalā-aṣṭamī **no fire is lit and no fresh food cooked**; the family eats **food prepared the previous day (Basoda, "stale-day")** — cold *pūrī, halwā, rice, curd* — offered first to the goddess; the cold meal *is* the cooling worship. (vrata-kathā tradition) [folk]
- **The hearth cooled** — the kitchen fire is doused and the cold hearth itself honored — a once-a-year fire-fast that "cools" the household against the coming hot-season epidemics. [folk]
- **Worship of the goddess as pot/stone** — women visit the Śītalā shrine at dawn, bathe the stone/pot, offer the cold food, neem, water and *roli/mehndi*, and hear the **Śītalā vrata-kathā**. (vrata-kathā tradition) [folk]
- **The vrata-kathā plot** — the standard tale: a woman who **kept cold-food/ Basoda** is spared when fire/pox ravages the village, while a neighbour who **cooked fresh and offended Śītalā** is burnt or stricken — until she too submits and is restored; a classic *establish-my-worship* folk narrative, the same engine seen in **[Manasā](manasa.md)**'s cult. (vrata-kathā tradition) [folk]
- *Variant (regional dates):* in parts of Gujarat/Rajasthan the cold-food **Śītalā Sātam/Aṣṭamī** falls in **Śrāvaṇa** (monsoon) rather than Caitra — local calendars place the fire-fast at the locally hottest/epidemic-prone time. [regional]

## 5. The Texts — Śītalāṣṭaka and the Śītalā-Maṅgal

Two literary strata, on the two clocks.

- **Śītalāṣṭaka / Śītalā-stotra (Skanda Purāṇa)** — the **"Eight Verses to Śītalā,"** framed as spoken by **Śiva** (in some recensions to Indra/the gods), the core Sanskrit hymn that **fixes her iconography** (donkey, broom, winnowing-fan, water-pot) and promises that its recitation **wards off pox, fever, pustules, boils and eye-disease**; the single most-recited Śītalā text. (Skanda Purāṇa — Śītalāṣṭaka) [late text]
- **Śītalā-maṅgal (Bengali)** — Śītalā is one of the deities celebrated in the Bengali **maṅgal-kāvya** genre (the same village-sung devotional poem-cycles that produced the **[Manasā](manasa.md)**-maṅgal and Caṇḍī-maṅgal); the **Śītalā-maṅgal / Śītalā-maṅgal-kāvya** narrates how the pox-goddess **forces her worship** on an unwilling king/merchant by visiting smallpox on his realm until he submits. (Bengali Śītalā-maṅgal) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (named recensions):* Bengali tradition attaches the Śītalā-maṅgal to poets such as **Kṛṣṇarām Dās** (17th c., who also wrote a *Rāy-maṅgal*) and others — datable historical anchors for the cult, as with the Manasā poets. [scholarly]
- **Vrata-kathā digests** — vernacular **Śītalā vrata-kathā** booklets (Hindi, Bengali, Gujarati, etc.) carry the Basoda story and the dawn-rite liturgy; popular-devotional, not scriptural. (vrata-kathā tradition) [folk]
- **Tantric/upapurāṇic naming** — Śītalā is slotted into late Tantric and *nibandha* (digest) disease-goddess lists, usually after the fact, equating her with Durgā/Pārvatī. [late text] [disputed]

## 6. Major Shrines and the Disease-Mother Set

- **Śītalā Devī temple, Gurugram (Gurgaon), Haryana** — among the most famous Śītalā shrines, a major pilgrimage and *melā* centre on the Delhi–NCR fringe; the goddess here is also locally identified with a Brahmin matriarch of Guru Droṇa's line (a Sanskritizing legend). [regional]
- **Śītalā Mātā temples, Kolkata/Bengal and across the Gangetic plain** — innumerable neighbourhood and village Śītalā shrines (e.g. in Varanasi, the Braj region, and throughout Bengal/Bihar) — she is overwhelmingly a **domestic and village** goddess rather than a great-temple deity. [folk]
- **The vasanta / disease-mother set** — Śītalā heads a cluster of folk **disease-goddesses**: she takes the **poxes**, while sisters/associates take **cholera (Olāī Caṇḍī)**, other fevers, and (the masculine guardian) **Jvarāsura, the Fever-Demon** who in Bengali iconography stands as her **attendant/companion**, the three-legged, three-headed personification of fever. (Source: North Indian folk tradition) [folk/regional]
- **The Mariamman equation** — across the linguistic divide Śītalā **is the structural twin of [Māriyammaṉ](mariamman.md)**: pox-mother, neem-tree, water-pot, heat-vs-cool propitiation; the North names her *Cool*, the South names her *Rain/Death* — the same agrarian-epidemic goddess in two dialects. [scholarly]

## 7. Smallpox, Eradication, and the Living Cult

- **Smallpox eradication (1980) and the goddess** — global smallpox eradication (declared by the WHO in **1980**) removed the disease Śītalā most specifically governed; the cult did **not** die but **broadened** to cover chickenpox, measles, heat-fevers and general child-protection — a documented case of a disease-deity outliving her disease. (Source: anthropology of religion) [scholarly]
- **No yuga-date** — none of the above carries a scriptural yuga-anchor. The Śītalāṣṭaka's frame (Śiva speaking) is mythically timeless, but Śītalā as a *worshipped goddess* is a **kali-historical** folk cult — shrines, *maṅgal* poems, vrata-kathās and festivals attested in recent centuries, not in the Vedas or core Purāṇas. [scholarly]
