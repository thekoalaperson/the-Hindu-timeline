# Khaṇḍobā / Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava (Mallāri)

> Where this sits in the hierarchy: `02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/` — node in [Deities & Cosmic Hierarchy](README.md). A Deccan **folk-Śaiva warrior god**, regarded as a martial form of Śiva/Bhairava (see [shiva-avatars-and-forms.md](shiva-avatars-and-forms.md)) with strong absorption of [Kārttikeya/Skanda](ganesha-and-kartikeya.md); his cult belongs to the *living regional Śaivism* surveyed in [shaiva-schools.md](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/shaiva-schools.md). [folk] [regional]

Khaṇḍobā (Marathi *Khaṇḍobā*, also **Khaṇḍerāo**, **Mhāḷsākānt**, **Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava**, **Mallāri / Mallāńna**, Kannada **Mailāra / Mailāralinga**, Telugu **Mallanna**) is the most widely venerated *kuladevatā* (family deity) of Mahārāṣṭra and northern Karnataka, worshipped across caste lines — by Marāṭhās, Dhangars (shepherds), cultivators, and many others. He is theologically a descent of **Śiva as Bhairava** sent to destroy the demon-brothers **Maṇi** and **Malla**; his iconography (mounted warrior with sword, dog, and consort) and his **bhaṇḍāra** (turmeric-powder) worship are unmistakably of the folk-Deccan stratum, only secondarily Sanskritized through the *Mallāri Māhātmya*. This file catalogs his myth, consorts, attributes, and cross-regional identities, cataloging variants rather than merging them.

---

## Identity & names

- **Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava** — the Sanskritizing name: *Mārtāṇḍa* (a solar epithet, "sun / sprung from the cosmic egg") fused with **Bhairava** (Śiva's fierce form), encoding the god's double nature as a **solar warrior** and a **fierce Śaiva**. The solar element distinguishes him within the Bhairava family. (*Mallāri Māhātmya*) [scholarly]
- **Mallāri** — "enemy (*ari*) of Malla," the demon he slays; the title under which the *Māhātmya* itself is named. (*Mallāri Māhātmya*)
- **Khaṇḍobā / Khaṇḍerāo** — the everyday Marathi names: *khaṇḍā* = "sword/broadsword," + *obā/rāo* honorific — "the Lord of the Sword." Emphasizes the martial, royal aspect. [regional]
- **Mhāḷsākānt** — "husband of Mhāḷsā," naming him through his consort. [regional]
- **Cross-tradition identities** — recognized as a single god under many regional names: **Mailāra / Mailāralinga** (Karnataka), **Mallanna / Mallikārjuna-folk** (Telangana/Andhra), **Mārtāṇḍa** (Konkan). The cult forms a continuous **Deccan Mailāra–Mallāri–Khaṇḍobā complex** spanning Marathi, Kannada, and Telugu country. [scholarly] [regional]
  - *Variant (scholarly):* some folklorists read the figure as a **pastoralist/sheep-herder warrior god** later Brahmanized into a form of Śiva; the underlying layer is a Dhangar tutelary deity. [scholarly] [disputed]

## The slaying of Maṇi and Malla (the core myth)

- **The demons' tyranny** — the *daitya* brothers **Maṇi** and **Malla**, having won boons, ravage the sages and ascetics of the **Prempurī / Maṇicūla** forest region of the Deccan; the harassed ṛṣis appeal to Śiva for deliverance. (*Mallāri Māhātmya*)
- **Śiva descends as Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava** — Śiva manifests in a fierce mounted warrior form (often with the gods as his army), riding to battle on a white horse (or bull-into-horse), to confront the demons. The site is identified with **Jejuri** (and, in the Kannada telling, with the Mailāra shrines). (*Mallāri Māhātmya*) [regional]
- **The great battle and the slaying** — Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava defeats the demon host. He kills **Malla** (hence *Mallāri*); the demon **Maṇi**, dying, offers his white horse and asks that his name be joined to the god's worship and that goats/animals be offered — a boon granted. The god's epithet derives from these victories. (*Mallāri Māhātmya*)
  - *Variant (folk/Jejuri):* Maṇi's severed body becomes the hill of Jejuri itself, and his repentance is why the demon is honored at the shrine; **Malla**'s name survives in *Mallāri/Mallanna*. [folk] [regional]
  - *Variant (Kannada Mailāra):* the demon is **Maṇicūla / Mallāsura**, slain at the Mailāra shrines (Devaragudda, Bellary region); the deity is **Mailāralinga**, and the ecstatic **Goṛava / Vaggayya** devotees (dog-men) re-enact the conquest. [folk] [regional]
- **Why "turmeric"** — in the victory the god and his army are showered with / smeared in **bhaṇḍāra** (turmeric powder, *halad*), which becomes the defining offering of the cult: devotees hurl golden turmeric over the deity and each other (the cry *"Yelkoṭ! Yelkoṭ! Jai Mālhār!"*). [folk] [regional]

## Consorts: Mhāḷsā and Bāṇāī

- **Mhāḷsā (Mhalsa)** — the first/senior wife, a Brahmin/Liṅgāyat merchant's daughter, identified with **Pārvatī** (consistent with Khaṇḍobā = Śiva). Her marriage to the god is celebrated as the great wedding at Jejuri/Pāli; she represents the settled, Sanskritic, agrarian-Brahmin pole of the cult. (*Mallāri Māhātmya*; Marathi tradition) [regional]
- **Bāṇāī (Banai / Bāṇū)** — the second wife, a **Dhangar (shepherd) woman**, won by Khaṇḍobā in pastoral guise; she represents the pastoralist, non-Brahmin, "wild" pole. The god is said to have herded sheep / served as a cowherd to win her, and a rivalry-and-reconciliation between Mhāḷsā and Bāṇāī is a staple of the oral epics (*ovī* songs). [folk] [regional]
  - *Variant (folk):* Bāṇāī is a foundling raised by Dhangars, in some tellings a celestial woman or even a form of **Gaṅgā / a second Pārvatī-aspect**; her union with Khaṇḍobā encodes the **marriage of agrarian and pastoral communities** under one deity. [folk] [disputed]
- **The two-wife structure as social charter** — scholars read Mhāḷsā (Brahmin/settled) + Bāṇāī (Dhangar/pastoral) as the cult's mechanism for **uniting castes and economies** around one shrine; Khaṇḍobā is *kuladevatā* to both Marāṭhā cultivators and Dhangar herders. [scholarly]

## Attributes: dog, horse, sword, bhaṇḍāra

- **The horse** — Khaṇḍobā is pre-eminently an **equestrian god**, depicted mounted with drawn sword; the horse (from the demon Maṇi's gift, or the god's war-mount) is itself worshipped, and a stone/metal horse stands at his shrines. The mounted-warrior icon links him to the **Deccan martial / Marāṭhā ethos**. [regional]
- **The dog** — a **dog (*śvāna*)** accompanies him as a vāhana-companion, exceptional among Hindu deities; reflects the pastoral/hunting substratum (guardian of flocks). The Kannada **Goṛava** devotees impersonate dogs, barking and eating offerings, during festivals. [folk] [regional]
- **The sword (*khaṇḍā*)** — his defining weapon and the source of the name *Khaṇḍobā*; lifting a heavy sword is a feat of devotion at Jejuri.
- **Bhaṇḍāra (turmeric)** — turmeric powder is the god's substance: scattered as *prasāda* ("gold"), it colors the whole hill of Jejuri yellow during festivals; the *bhaṇḍāra* is held to be the god's protective, auspicious, martial "gold-dust." [folk] [regional]
- **Vāghyā and Muraḷī** — dedicated male (*Vāghyā*, "tiger") and female (*Muraḷī*) devotee-servants of the god, who sing his deeds and perform his cult; an institution of the living tradition. [folk] [regional]

## Sacred geography & festivals

- **Jejuri** — the principal shrine, the hill-temple in Puṇe district, Mahārāṣṭra — *Khaṇḍobāchā Jejuri*; the great pilgrimage centre where turmeric is showered. [regional]
- **Other shrines** — **Pāli (Pembar), Naḷadurg, Māḷegāon, Śegūḍ/Devaragudda (Mailāra, Karnataka), Āḍi-Mailāra, Komuravelli (Mallanna, Telangana)** — the network of the Mailāra–Mallāri–Khaṇḍobā complex. [regional]
- **Cāmpāṣaṣṭhī** — the six-day festival (Mārgaśīrṣa śukla 1–6) commemorating the slaying of the demons, the cult's chief calendar event; **Somavatī Amāvāsyā** is also major. [regional]

## Theological placement (the "two clocks")

- **Scriptural/cyclic clock** — within the Sanskritized frame Khaṇḍobā is **Śiva descending as Bhairava** in an unspecified demon-slaying *līlā* (Maṇi-Malla), placeable alongside the other fierce manifestations cataloged in [shiva-avatars-and-forms.md](shiva-avatars-and-forms.md); no yuga is fixed for it, so it sits as a **cross-yuga** Śaiva descent rather than a dated event. [scholarly]
- **Academic/historical clock** — the cult is attested archaeologically and textually as a **regional Deccan deity** whose worship coalesced over roughly the late-medieval period; the **Kannada Mailāra** tradition appears to be the older stratum, with the **Mallāri Māhātmya** Sanskritizing it and the Marathi **Khaṇḍobā** the most expansive later form. The *Māhātmya* is affiliated to the **Brahmāṇḍa** (sometimes Skanda) Purāṇa corpus as a *māhātmya* appendix, a typical mode of Brahmanizing a folk god. [scholarly] [late text]

## Cross-tradition links to Kārttikeya and Bhairava

- **To Bhairava/Śiva** — the *Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava* name and the demon-slaying mandate make him a folk-Deccan member of the **Bhairava family** (cf. Kāla-Bhairava in [shiva-avatars-and-forms.md](shiva-avatars-and-forms.md)); his ferocity, dog-companion, and martial cult all align with the Bhairava complex. [scholarly]
- **To Kārttikeya / Skanda** — like **Kārttikeya/Murugan** (see [ganesha-and-kartikeya.md](ganesha-and-kartikeya.md)), Khaṇḍobā is a **war-god son-of-Śiva type** with a hill-shrine cult, a martial weapon, a dual-consort structure (Devasenā/Vaḷḷi ↔ Mhāḷsā/Bāṇāī), and a hunter/pastoral following — leading some scholars to treat the Deccan Mailāra–Khaṇḍobā as a **regional cousin of, or absorbed with, Skanda-Murugan**. [scholarly] [disputed]
  - *Variant (folk):* in some Marathi tellings Khaṇḍobā is simply *Śiva himself*, not a son; the Skanda parallel is structural, not genealogical. [folk]

## Sources

- *Mallāri Māhātmya* — the Sanskrit *māhātmya* (affiliated to the **Brahmāṇḍa**, sometimes **Skanda**, Purāṇa corpus) narrating the slaying of Maṇi and Malla by Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava; the Brahmanizing textual basis of the cult.
- Marathi folk tradition — the *ovī* / oral epics, the Vāghyā-Muraḷī devotional songs, and the Jejuri *kuladevatā* practice. [folk] [regional]
- Kannada Mailāra / Goṛava tradition and Telugu Mallanna (Komuravelli) tradition — the wider Deccan Mailāra–Mallāri–Khaṇḍobā complex. [folk] [regional]
- Modern scholarship on the cult (Sontheimer's studies of Khaṇḍobā/Mailāra and Dhangar religion; folklorists on the pastoral-warrior god) — for the social-charter reading of the two consorts and the Skanda/Bhairava comparanda. [scholarly]
