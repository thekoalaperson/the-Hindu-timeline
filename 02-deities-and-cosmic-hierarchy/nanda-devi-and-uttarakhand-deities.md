# Nandā Devī, Golu Devtā and the Uttarakhand Pantheon

> Deities-and-hierarchy node. **Nandā Devī** — Pārvatī worshipped as the *dhiyāṇī* (married-out daughter) of the Uttarakhand hills — presides over a dense regional pantheon of Kumaon and Garhwal: **Golu Devtā** the bell-and-petition god of justice at Chitai, and the *jāgar*-possession *devtās* (Gaṅgānāth, Bholānāth, Kalbiśṭ, Airī and others). Like [Gugga](gugga-gogaji.md) in the northwest and [Muthappan](muthappan.md) in Kerala, these are **[folk] [regional]** cults whose authority is oral ballad, shrine and medium rather than Sanskrit canon — but Nandā herself is stitched to the high tradition through Pārvatī. ## 1. Two Clocks — What This Pantheon Is

- **Scriptural clock** — Nandā is read by her devotees as **Pārvatī herself, the daughter of Himavat/Himālaya**, whose birth, austerity and marriage to Śiva belong to the Purāṇic Pārvatī cycle (see [sati-daksha-and-parvati.md](sati-daksha-and-parvati.md)); in the local theology the yearly and twelve-yearly processions are the escorting of a married daughter back to her husband's home on **Kailāsa**. (Śiva Purāṇa Pārvatī-khaṇḍa frame; local *jāgar* ballads) [regional]
- **The name in the canon** — a goddess **"Nandā"** is named among the great future/protective forms of Devī in the **Mūrti-rahasya appendix to the Devī-Māhātmya** ("first, the form named Nandā…"), and *Nandā* recurs in Purāṇic lists of goddess names and *tīrthas*; hill tradition takes these as warrants for its Nandā, though the identification of the canonical name with the Uttarakhand goddess is a local/sectarian reading. (Devī-Māhātmya, Mūrti-rahasya appendix) [late text] [regional] [disputed]
- **Historical clock** — the organized Nandā cult is tied to the medieval hill kingdoms: the **Katyūrī dynasty (c. 8th–12th century CE)** and after them the **Chand rājās of Kumaon and the Parmār/Panwār rājās of Garhwal (c. 12th–18th century CE)**, for whom Nandā was **rāj-rājeśvarī, the royal tutelary goddess (kul-devī)** of both houses. [scholarly] [regional]
- **Golu, Bholānāth, Kalbiśṭ, Gaṅgānāth and the *jāgar* devtās** — deified historical or quasi-historical persons (princes, herdsmen, wronged men and women) of the Katyūrī–Chand era who possess mediums and dispense justice; a "historical clock" hero-cult stratum with essentially **no scriptural clock**, exactly as with [Gugga](gugga-gogaji.md). [folk] [oral tradition] [scholarly]

## 2. Nandā Devī — Pārvatī as Daughter of the Hills

- **The mountain as the goddess** — **Nandā Devī peak (7,816 m)**, the highest summit wholly in India, *is* the goddess's abode/body; the twin summits are read locally as **Nandā and her companion Sunandā**, and the ring-wall of peaks around the Nanda Devi Sanctuary as her guarded court. [folk] [regional]
- **Nandā as dhiyāṇī** — the emotional core of the cult: Nandā is the hill people's **own daughter, married out to distant Kailāsa**; her festivals are structured as a natal family receiving, feasting, lamenting over, and finally sending off a married daughter to her *sasurāl* (husband's house). The *jāgar* songs sung for her voice a bride's hardships in Śiva's ash-smeared, ascetic household. (Kumaoni/Garhwali Nandā ballads) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **Nandā = Pārvatī = Durgā** — in her temples (Almora, Nainital, Kot Bhrāmarī near Bageshwar, Kurur, Nauti) she is iconographically assimilated to **Durgā/Mahiṣamardinī**, and her great days fall in the **Bhādrapada Nandāṣṭamī** window; the high-canonical warrant for the buffalo-slaying goddess is the Devī-Māhātmya (see [devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)). [regional]
- **Royal goddess** — as **kul-devī of the Chand kings of Kumaon and the Garhwal rājās**, her cult was court-sponsored: Chand-era tradition credits the kings with installing Nandā at **Almora** (the Nandā Devī temple in the Malla Mahal precinct), and the deposed Katyūrī line with her seat at **Kot Bhrāmarī**. [regional] [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Kot Bhrāmarī sthala tradition):* at Kot near Bageshwar, Nandā is fused with **Bhrāmarī**, the bee-form of Devī who in the Devī-Māhātmya's Mūrti-rahasya and in Devī-Bhāgavata tradition destroys the demon Aruṇa with swarms of bees — the hill shrine claiming the pan-Indian bee-goddess as its own. [late text] [regional]

## 3. The Nandā Devī Rāj Jāt — the Royal Pilgrimage (once in ~12 years)

The **Nandā Devī Rāj Jāt** ("royal procession of Nandā") is the largest ritual event of the Central Himalaya: a barefoot escort of the goddess from her natal villages to the threshold of Kailāsa.

- **The route** — roughly a **280-kilometre, c. 19–22 stage barefoot yātrā** from **Nauti village (near Karnaprayag, Chamoli district)** through the Garhwal highlands via Wan and Bedni Bugyal, past **Roopkund**, over the c. 5,000-m **Jyunragali ridge** to **Homkund** ("the lake of the fire-offering") below Trishul — where the goddess-as-daughter is sent onward to Śiva. (Rāj Jāt ritual tradition) [regional]
- **The four-horned ram (chausiṅgyā khāḍū)** — the pilgrimage is convened when a **four-horned ram is born** in the region, read as Nandā's summons; the ram, adorned and laden with saddle-bags of offerings and a daughter's gifts, **leads the procession and at Homkund is released alone toward Kailāsa**, carrying the pilgrims' offerings to the goddess's husband's house while the humans turn back. [folk] [regional]
- **The royal convener** — the Jāt is led by the **Kunwars of Kansuwa** (kinsmen of the old Garhwal royal house) with the hereditary **Nauti Rājpurohits**; a **golden/royal umbrella (chhantoli)** and hundreds of village *chhantolis* (ringal-cane parasols) accompany local *ḍolīs* of allied devtās — the medieval royal cult still performing itself. [regional]
- **Periodicity** — nominally **once every twelve years** (a Himalayan analogue to the twelve-year cycles of the [Kumbh Melā](../06-sacred-geography/kumbh-mela-and-pilgrimage-cycles.md)), but in practice convened when the omens and the four-horned ram allow: modern Rāj Jāts were held in **1905, 1925, 1951, 1968, 1987, 2000 and 2014** (the 2013 date postponed a year after the Kedarnath flood disaster). [regional] [scholarly]
- **Roopkund, the skeleton lake** — on the route lies **Roopkund (c. 5,029 m)**, strewn with hundreds of human skeletons. The Jāt legend: a king — usually named **Jasdhaval of Kanauj**, travelling with his pregnant queen **Balampā**, dancers and drummers — **violated the goddess's rule of austerity on her mountain** (music, dancing, luxury on the sacred route), and Nandā's wrath — a hailstorm of iron-hard hailstones — destroyed the whole party at the lake. (Rāj Jāt / Roopkund legend) [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant [scholarly]:* radiocarbon and osteological studies date many Roopkund skeletons to **c. 9th century CE**, consistent with a single catastrophic event (skull injuries compatible with huge hailstones); a **2019 ancient-DNA study** complicated the picture, finding at least three genetic groups deposited in more than one event — one of them, strikingly, of **eastern-Mediterranean ancestry dated c. 17th–19th century CE** — leaving the lake's full history unresolved. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The annual "small" jāts** — besides the Rāj Jāt, **yearly Nandā Jāts / Nandāṣṭamī processions** (in Bhādrapada, c. August–September) carry the goddess's *ḍolī* from her natal shrines toward the high pastures and back, and towns such as **Almora and Nainital hold the Nandā Devī Melā**, with the images ceremonially made and immersed — the daughter's annual visit home. [regional]

## 4. Golu Devtā — the God of Justice at Chitai

**Golu (Goljyū) Devtā** is Kumaon's supreme *nyāya-devtā* (god of justice): a deified prince who hears written petitions and repays granted prayers with brass bells.

- **Who he is** — folk theology makes Golu an **avatāra/aspect of Śiva as Bhairava** (his brothers/doubles **Garh Devī-linked Kalua and Bhōlanāth** filling out a Bhairava set), historicized as a **Katyūrī-era prince**. [folk] [regional]
- **The birth legend** — King **Jhāl Rāi (Jhālu Rāi)** of Champawat, hunting, meets the ascetic-born **Kālinkā (Kalindrā)**, who alone can part his fighting buffaloes; he marries her as his eighth queen. When she bears the long-awaited son, the **seven jealous co-queens substitute a stone (in tellings, a pumpkin/grindstone) for the newborn** and float the child down the river in a cage/box. (Kumaoni Golu ballad, sung in *jāgar*) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The fisherman and the wooden horse** — a fisherman raises the boy; grown, the child rides to the river on a **wooden horse** and, taunted by the queens for watering a wooden toy, answers: **"If a wooden horse cannot drink water, how can a queen give birth to a stone?"** — the riddle exposes the fraud, the queens are punished, mother and son are restored, and the boy in time rules justly. (Golu ballad) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **Deification as judge** — after death the just prince becomes **Goljyū, the divine magistrate of Kumaon**: wrongs that human courts cannot reach are brought to him, and he possesses his mediums in *jāgar* to deliver verdicts. [folk]
- **Chitai — the court of petitions** — at the **Chitai Golu temple (near Almora)** devotees file their cases as **written petitions — traditionally on stamped judicial paper, complete with legal phrasing** — tied in their thousands to the temple railings; when the prayer is granted the petitioner returns and **hangs a brass bell**, so the shrine is sheathed in uncounted bells. Offerings of white cloth, white turban and a white horse figure suit his iconography: **Golu rides a white horse**. (Chitai sthala tradition) [regional]
- **Other seats** — major Golu temples at **Ghorakhal (near Bhowali)**, **Champawat** (held to be his original seat/capital) and **Chamarkhan**; nearly every Kumaoni village keeps a Golu shrine or stone. [regional]

## 5. The Jāgar — Possession as Liturgy

The **jāgar** ("waking" the god) is the ritual engine of the whole Uttarakhand folk pantheon.

- **The form** — by night, a bard-singer (**jagariyā**) drumming the **huṛkā** (hourglass drum) or **ḍhol-damau**, sings the deity's ballad; the god descends into a designated medium (**ḍaṅgariyā**), who dances, trembles, takes flame or grain on the body, and **speaks as the deity** — answering questions, diagnosing afflictions, settling disputes. [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The repertoire is the scripture** — the jagariyā's sung ballads (of Golu, Nandā, Gaṅgānāth, Bholānāth, the Pāṇḍavas) are the cult's only "texts": unfixed, regional, performative — the same oral-epic stratum as the Gugga-gāthā ([gugga-gogaji.md](gugga-gogaji.md)) and functionally parallel to Kerala's theyyam possession for [Muthappan](muthappan.md). [scholarly]
- **Pāṇḍav līlā / Pāṇḍav nṛtya** — in Garhwal the Mahābhārata itself is danced as possession-theatre: villagers embody the **Pāṇḍavas**, who are held to have passed through these mountains on their final journey (the *mahāprasthāna* toward Badarī and Svargārohiṇī — see [badrinath-badarikashrama-kshetra.md](../06-sacred-geography/badrinath-badarikashrama-kshetra.md)). [folk] [regional]

## 6. The Wider Devtā Roster (catalog)

One bullet per deity; all **[folk] [regional] [oral tradition]** unless noted.

- **Bholānāth** — a deified **Chand prince** of Almora tradition: an ascetic-turned prince treacherously slain (with his consort **Barhinī**), whose angry ghost shook the Chand capital until enshrined; now a benign Śiva-assimilated house-protector of Kumaon, honored with small shrines and lamps. (Kumaoni jāgar ballads)
- **Gaṅgānāth** — a **prince of Doti (western Nepal)** who renounced his throne as a *jogī* for love of **Bhānā**, a married Brahmin woman of Almora; the two (and in most tellings Bhānā's unborn child) were **murdered by her kinsmen**, and the wronged dead became powerful *jāgar* deities who possess mediums and champion the wronged — especially women. (Gaṅgānāth ballad)
- **Kalbiśṭ (Kalua Biśṭ)** — a heroic **herdsman-strongman of the Binsar region**, famed for protecting cattle and the poor, **killed by treachery** of a rival; deified as a guardian of herds and hill pastures, his cult twinned/opposed with that of his enemy turned devtā. (Kalbiśṭ ballad)
- **Airī (Aiḍī)** — the fierce **huntsman-god of the high ridges**, who sweeps the forests by night with his dogs and attendant **āncharis** (fairy-spirits); meeting his hunt unprotected brings harm, so herdsmen keep his open-air shrines. 
- **Chaumū** — cattle-protector devtā of eastern Kumaon (Champawat side); milk and bells are offered for the herds' safety.
- **Sāim (Saima)** — boundary/pasture guardian devtā of Kumaoni villages, keeper of thresholds.
- **Bhūmiyā** — the ubiquitous **field-and-land guardian (kṣetrapāla)** of both Kumaon and Garhwal villages, the local face of the pan-Indian village-guardian class cataloged in [gramadevatas-village-deities.md](gramadevatas-village-deities.md).
- **Nāgrājā (Garhwal)** — **Kṛṣṇa worshipped as the serpent-king** in Garhwal, with his chief seat at **Sem-Mukhem**; a distinctive Garhwali fusion of Kṛṣṇa and *nāga* cult. [regional]
- **Narasiṃha / Nṛsiṃha devtā (Garhwal)** — the Man-Lion as a *jāgar*-borne village devtā served by Jośī/Nāth ritualists, linked in local lore to Jośīmaṭh near [Badrināth](../06-sacred-geography/badrinath-badarikashrama-kshetra.md), where the softening arm of the Narasiṃha image is said to foretell the closing of the Badrī road. [regional] [folk]
- **Ghaṇḍiyāl / Ghaṇṭākarṇa (Garhwal)** — the "bell-eared" devtā (Ghaṇṭākarṇa), a fierce protector honored with bells and *jāgar*; hill tradition connects him to the Ghaṇṭākarṇa of Purāṇic/Jain lore as a guardian who would hear no name but his lord's. [folk] [disputed]
- **Masān / Bhūt-stratum** — below the devtās, the restless dead (**masān**, ghosts of cremation-grounds) afflict the living and are exorcised or *promoted* through jāgar — the mechanism by which new devtās (a wronged prince, a murdered lover) historically entered the pantheon. [folk] [scholarly]

## 7. Where This Sits in the Larger Landscape

- **Same mountains, two liturgies** — the Sanskrit-canonical layer of these same hills is the [Chota Char Dham](../06-sacred-geography/char-dham-and-chota-char-dham.md) circuit — [Kedārnāth](../06-sacred-geography/kedarnath-kedara-kshetra.md), [Badrināth](../06-sacred-geography/badrinath-badarikashrama-kshetra.md), Gangotri, Yamunotri — with the [Pañch Kedār and Pañch Badrī](../06-sacred-geography/pancha-kedar-pancha-badri.md) networks; the Nandā–Golu–jāgar layer is the **vernacular liturgy of the same landscape**, and the Rāj Jāt's send-off toward Kailāsa points at the same axis as the [Kailāsa–Mānasarovar yātrā](../06-sacred-geography/manasarovar-kailash-yatra.md). [scholarly]
- **Gateway** — the hills' pilgrim traffic enters through [Haridwar–Rishikesh](../06-sacred-geography/haridwar-rishikesh-ganga-dwara.md), and the Śākta reading of the region plugs into the pan-Indian goddess-geography of the [Śakti Pīṭhas](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md).
- **Typological kin** — deified-hero justice/possession cults elsewhere in this section: [Gugga/Gogājī](gugga-gogaji.md) (northwest), [Khaṇḍobā](khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md) (Deccan), [Muthappan](muthappan.md) (Kerala theyyam), [Manasā](manasa.md) (Bengal) — each a regional answer to the same need for a god who is *of* the people and *answers back*. [scholarly]
