# Yama

> The god of death and dharma, the first mortal to die and so the first king of the dead, judge of the karmic ledger, and guardian of the southern quarter. A roster node of [`02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/`](README.md). Yama is the son of [Sūrya/Vivasvat](surya-and-his-family.md), twin of Yamī/Yamunā, lord of the [Narakas](narakas-and-afterlife.md), one of the [Navagraha](navagraha.md) family (brother of Śani), and a Lokapāla of the [14-loka cosmos](devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md). Master index: [the Hindu Timeline](../README.md).

Yama is read on **two clocks**. In the oldest Vedic layer he is not a fearsome judge but a benevolent **pioneer of mortality** — the first human to die, who scouted the path to the ancestors and now welcomes the dead to a sunlit Svarga of feasting. In the Epic-Purāṇic layer he hardens into **Dharmarāja**, the impartial magistrate of karma who dispatches the **Yamadūtas**, reads **Chitragupta's** audit, and assigns souls to the [Narakas](narakas-and-afterlife.md). Both faces coexist in living tradition.

---

## 1. Names, epithets & functions

- **Yama** — the name means "twin" (he and Yamī are the primordial pair); later glossed as "the binder/restrainer" (one who curbs and holds, *yam-*). The earliest proper personal name of the death-god (Ṛg Veda 10.14).
- **Yamarāja / Yama Dharmarāja** — "King Yama," "Yama the king of dharma": the judicial title under which he weighs deeds and dispenses cosmic law (Purāṇas; Garuḍa Purāṇa).
- **Dharmarāja / Dharma** — "Lord of righteousness"; frequently identified with **Dharmadeva** (Dharma personified). As Dharma he fathers **Yudhiṣṭhira** on Kuntī (Mahābhārata). [scholarly] Note: Yama and Dharmadeva have *distinct origins* and are conflated only later — sources split on whether they are one being.
- **Kāla** — "Time," death as the inexorable devourer; overlaps with Mahākāla/Śiva as time-the-destroyer.
- **Antaka** — "the Ender," "he who makes an end."
- **Mṛtyu** — "Death" itself; sometimes a separate personification (a goddess/figure of Death) who serves Yama rather than being him. [disputed — Mṛtyu vs. Yama as the same or distinct]
- **Vaivasvata** — patronymic, "son of Vivasvat (the Sun)"; the same epithet names the **current 7th Manu**, who is Yama's brother — they share the father (Ṛg Veda; Purāṇas).
- **Pitṛpati / Pretarāja** — "Lord of the ancestors (Pitṛs)" / "King of the departed (pretas)"; in the Vedic register Yama rules the blessed dead, not a torture-realm.
- **Daṇḍadhara / Daṇḍī** — "wielder of the rod (daṇḍa)," the staff of punishment and royal authority.
- **Pāśī / Pāśahasta** — "noose-holder," for the *pāśa* with which he lassoes the soul out of the body.
- **Dakṣiṇāśādhipati** — "lord of the southern direction" (see § Lokapāla below).
- **Śrāddhadeva** — "god of the śrāddha rites" (the offerings to the dead) — an epithet shared with Vaivasvata Manu, again marking the brother-link. [folk/regional]

## 2. Birth & the Vedic origin — the first to die

- **Parentage** — son of **Vivasvat/Sūrya** and **Saraṇyū** (Vedic) / **Sañjñā** (Puranic), twin of **Yamī**; brother of **Vaivasvata Manu**, half-brother of **Śani** and **the Aśvins** by Chhāyā. Full household at [Sūrya & His Family](surya-and-his-family.md) (Ṛg Veda 10.17; Purāṇas).
- **The first mortal to die (Ṛg Veda 10.14)** — Yama is *"the first of mortals who died, the first that travelled to the other world."* Having found and trodden the path of death before anyone, he becomes its lord and guide; the dead "go to Yama" along the road he opened. He is a **benevolent host**, not a tormentor — RV 10.14 has the mourners send the dead to "rejoice with Yama" in a realm of light, reunited with the Pitṛs (Ṛg Veda 10.14; Atharva Veda 18).
- **The two four-eyed dogs (Ṛg Veda 10.14.10-12)** — Yama keeps two brindled, four-eyed, broad-nosed dogs, **Śyāma ("dark") and Śabala/Śarvara ("brindled/spotted")**, sons of **Saramā** (Indra's bitch); they guard the path and "wander among men" seeking out those whose time has come. The soul is told to "run past" them swiftly to reach Yama's peace (Ṛg Veda 10.14).
- **Yama in the Vedic Svarga** — the early afterlife under Yama is a **paradise**: feasting under a leafy tree, sweet music, reunion with forefathers — punishment-hells (Naraka) are a *later* overlay (Ṛg Veda; Atharva Veda). The shift from "kindly king of the blessed dead" to "judge who consigns sinners to hell" is the central diachronic fact about Yama. [scholarly]
- **The voluntary death variant** — some readings (and later glosses) cast Yama's death as a *willing renunciation* of his immortal portion so that mortality and the ancestral path could exist for humans, deepening his role as humanity's forerunner. [disputed]

## 3. Yama & Yamī — the twins and the rejected union (Ṛg Veda 10.10)

- **The dialogue-hymn (Ṛg Veda 10.10)** — one of the most discussed Vedic hymns: **Yamī proposes sexual union to her twin Yama** so that the human race may be propagated from the first pair; **Yama refuses** on grounds of dharma — the gods' spies see all, the kin-bond forbids it, and "what is sin tomorrow we should not do today." Yamī presses with frustration across the verses; Yama holds. (Ṛg Veda 10.10)
  - *Significance:* the hymn stages dharma (moral restraint) winning over desire and necessity at the very origin of humankind — fitting that the refuser becomes **Dharmarāja**. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Avestan parallel):* the Iranian **Yima** and his twin/sister **Yimak** are cognate; in some Zoroastrian/Iranian tellings the primal twins *do* unite to start humanity — the Vedic version pointedly reverses this. [scholarly]
- **Yamī becomes the Yamunā** — in Puranic tradition the sister is identified with the sacred **river Yamunā**; her grief at Yama's death is said to have made her the river, and bathing in the Yamunā on **Yama Dvitīyā / Bhāi Dūj** invokes the sibling bond (Purāṇas; folk). See [Sūrya & His Family § children](surya-and-his-family.md).

## 4. Lord of the South — the Lokapāla / Dikpāla

- **Guardian of the southern quarter** — Yama is the **Dikpāla/Lokapāla of the South (Dakṣiṇa)**, the direction of death; the dead travel south, the south door of cremation grounds, the feet-to-south rule for the dying all flow from this (Purāṇas; Vāstu/Dharmaśāstra).
  - The standard eight-Dikpāla scheme: **Indra** (E), **Agni** (SE), **Yama** (S), **Nirṛti** (SW), **Varuṇa** (W), **Vāyu** (NW), **Kubera** (N), **Īśāna/Śiva** (NE). [late text — Purāṇic/Tantric systematization]
- **Vāhana — the buffalo (Mahiṣa)** — Yama rides a black **water-buffalo**; the buffalo's association with death makes it his characteristic mount (Purāṇas).
  - *Variant:* some iconography and regional cults give him a **horse** or show him on foot; the buffalo is the dominant Puranic form.

## 5. Iconography & weapons

- **Form** — dark/storm-cloud or **green** complexion, fierce eyes and protruding fangs (in his judging aspect), often **two- or four-armed**, robed in red/blood-red, crowned, sometimes flame-haloed; majestic and kingly in benign depictions, terrifying in punitive ones (Purāṇas; Āgamic iconography).
- **Daṇḍa** — the rod/staff/mace of punishment and kingship, his defining attribute (hence **Daṇḍadhara**).
- **Pāśa** — the **noose** with which he draws the *jīva* (soul) out of the body at death.
- **Buffalo mount** — see above.
- **Attendants** — flanked by **Chitragupta** (scribe) and the **Yamadūtas** (messengers); the dogs Śyāma and Śabala; in some texts **Kāla** and **Mṛtyu** as personified agents.

## 6. The administration of death — Yamaloka, the dūtas, and the ledger

- **Yamaloka / Yamapurī / Saṃyamanī** — Yama's capital-city and realm of judgment, located in the **south**, below the seven Pātālas, above the Garbhodaka ocean at the cosmos' base; the soul is brought here to face the audit before assignment to Svarga, rebirth, or [Naraka](narakas-and-afterlife.md) (Bhāgavata 5.26; Garuḍa Purāṇa).
  - **The four gates / the Vaitaraṇī** — the wicked approach Yamapurī by a harsh road across the river **Vaitaraṇī** (of blood, pus, filth); the righteous enter by a pleasant gate. (Garuḍa Purāṇa)
- **Yamadūtas** — Yama's messengers/agents who come to seize the soul at the appointed moment and drag the sinful before him; in the famous **Ajāmila** episode (Bhāgavata 6.1-3) the Yamadūtas are *driven off by Viṣṇu's Viṣṇudūtas* when the dying sinner cries "Nārāyaṇa," establishing that devotion can override Yama's jurisdiction. (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6)
- **Chitragupta & the Agrasandhānī ledger** — **Chitragupta** ("hidden picture"), Yama's celestial accountant, maintains the **Agrasandhānī**, the master-ledger recording *every* deed, great and small, of every being; at judgment he reads the audit and the verdict follows the law of karma — "beyond bribe, beyond favoritism." (Garuḍa Purāṇa; Padma Purāṇa)
  - *Origin variant (Kāyastha tradition):* Chitragupta is said to have sprung from the **body (kāya) of Brahmā** to keep records when Yama could not track all deeds — hence the **Kāyastha** community claim him as progenitor and worship him at **Chitragupta Pūjā / Yama Dvitīyā**. [folk/community tradition]
- **Karma, not eternal hell** — Naraka under Yama is **temporary/purgatorial**: the soul exhausts its sin and is reborn; the same Yama who punishes also releases. (Bhāgavata 5.26)

## 7. The great Yama encounters

### Nachiketā — the dialogue with Death (Kaṭha Upaniṣad)

- **The setup** — at his father **Vājaśravas's** flawed sacrifice (giving away worthless old cows), the boy **Nachiketā** asks "to whom will you give *me*?"; the angered father snaps, **"I give you to Death (Yama)!"** Bound by the word, Nachiketā goes to Yama's house and waits **three nights** without food. (Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1)
- **The three boons** — finding the boy unattended for three days, Yama grants three boons in recompense:
  1. that his father's anger be gone and he greet Nachiketā kindly on return;
  2. knowledge of the **fire-sacrifice (the Nāchiketa Agni)** that leads to heaven — Yama teaches it and names the rite after the boy;
  3. **the secret of what lies beyond death** — "when a man dies, some say he is, some say he is not; teach me the truth."
- **Yama's evasion and teaching** — Yama tries to deflect the third boon, offering wealth, long life, sons, and pleasures instead; Nachiketā refuses them all as transient. Pleased, Yama then expounds the supreme doctrine: the distinction of **śreyas (the good)** from **preyas (the pleasant)**; the imperishable **Ātman**, "unborn, eternal, not slain when the body is slain"; the famous **chariot simile** (body=chariot, senses=horses, mind=reins, Ātman=rider) of *Kaṭha* 1.3. This makes Yama a **revealer of the highest knowledge (Brahmavidyā)**, not merely an executioner. (Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1-2)

### Sāvitrī & Satyavān — the wife who out-argued Death (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva)

- **Frame** — told by **Mārkaṇḍeya** to Yudhiṣṭhira during the exile, to console him about Draupadī's plight (Mahābhārata, Vana/Āraṇyaka Parva, "Pativratā-māhātmya / Sāvitryupākhyāna").
- **The story** — Princess **Sāvitrī** (daughter of King Aśvapati) chooses **Satyavān**, an exiled prince, though **Nārada** warns he will die in exactly one year. On the fated day she follows him to the forest; he collapses and **Yama himself** comes (not the dūtas — a mark of Satyavān's worth) and draws out his soul with the *pāśa*.
- **The pursuit and the boons** — Sāvitrī follows Yama relentlessly; charmed by her wisdom and devotion he grants boon after boon **except Satyavān's life**: (1) her blind father-in-law's sight and kingdom restored; (2) her own father to have a hundred sons; (3/4) prosperity; then she asks for **a hundred sons of her own by Satyavān** — and Yama, having already granted it and being **bound by his own word (dharma)**, must return Satyavān to life. (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva)
  - *Theme:* Dharmarāja is defeated not by force but by **dharma itself** (his own truthfulness) wielded by a devoted, clever wife — the basis of the **Vaṭa Sāvitrī / Vaṭa Pūrṇimā** vow.

### Mārkaṇḍeya — and Śiva who kills Death (Kālāntaka)

- **The story** — sage Mṛkaṇḍu's son **Mārkaṇḍeya** is fated to die at **sixteen**; a supreme Śiva-devotee, at the fatal hour he clings to the **Śivaliṅga**. Yama casts his *pāśa*; the noose falls on the liṅga itself. Śiva bursts forth as **Kālāntaka** ("Ender of Death/Time"), kicks/strikes Yama with his trident and **slays the god of death**, then grants Mārkaṇḍeya **eternal youth at sixteen** (a Chiranjīvi). (Skanda, Liṅga, Padma Purāṇas; Śaiva tradition)
  - *Revival of Yama:* the gods plead, and Śiva **restores Yama to life** so the cosmic order of death can continue — hence Śiva's epithets **Kālakāla / Mṛtyuñjaya** ("conqueror of death"). Brahmā had earlier given Mārkaṇḍeya the **Mahā-Mṛtyuñjaya mantra**. See [Śiva avatars & forms](shiva-avatars-and-forms.md).
  - *Variant (who acts):* some tellings have Śiva merely **forbid/restrain** Yama rather than kill him; the "kicked and slain" version is the dramatic Śaiva Purāṇic form. [folk/sectarian]
- **Sectarian point** — the episode asserts **Śiva's supremacy over death**: even Yama is mortal before Mahādeva. Vaiṣṇava counterparts make the same claim via Viṣṇu (the Ajāmila and Mārkaṇḍeya-pralaya episodes in the Bhāgavata).

## 8. Yama as father & relative

- **Yudhiṣṭhira** — as **Dharma**, Yama fathers **Yudhiṣṭhira** (the eldest Pāṇḍava) on **Kuntī** via the boon-mantra; Yudhiṣṭhira is "Dharmaputra/Dharmarāja Jr." and embodies truthfulness. (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva)
- **The Yakṣa-praśna (Yakṣa's questions)** — Yama, disguised as a **crane/Yakṣa** at a lake, kills the four younger Pāṇḍavas (who drink without answering) and tests Yudhiṣṭhira with riddles on dharma; satisfied, he revives the brothers and reveals himself as his father. (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva — *Yakṣa-praśna / Araṇeya Parva*)
- **Vidura** — at times said to be an **incarnation of Dharma/Yama**, cursed by sage Māṇḍavya to be born as the wise śūdra-born minister of Hastināpura. (Mahābhārata)
- **Śani (Saturn)** — half-brother (Chhāyā's son); the two stern karmic enforcers are often paired in worship. See [Navagraha](navagraha.md).

## 9. Festivals & living cult

- **Yama Dvitīyā / Bhāi Dūj / Bhāu Bīj** — second day after Diwali; based on Yamunā honoring her brother Yama, sisters honor brothers; bathing in the Yamunā on this day is said to free one from Yama's hells. (Purāṇic/folk)
- **Vaṭa Sāvitrī / Vaṭa Pūrṇimā** — married women's vow under the banyan, re-enacting Sāvitrī's victory over Yama for the long life of their husbands. (Vrata tradition)
- **Naraka Chaturdaśī / Yama-dīpa** — lamps lit for Yama on the eve of Diwali to avert untimely death (Yama-dīpadāna). (Folk/regional)
- **Modern road-safety "Yamarāj"** — contemporary Indian public-safety campaigns personify Yama (buffalo, noose, mustache) to warn against helmetless riding and reckless driving — a living, popular afterlife of the death-god. [folk/contemporary]

## 10. Cross-cultural cognates

- **Iranian Yima** — Avestan/Zoroastrian cognate, the first king and lord of a golden age / ruler of the dead; shares the twin-pair motif (Yima–Yimak) with Yama–Yamī. [scholarly]
- **Buddhist Yama** — adopted as judge/king of the hells (Naraka) across Buddhist Asia.
- **Tibetan** — **Yama Dharmarāja** (Shinje) and the wrathful **Yamāntaka** ("slayer of Yama/Death," a form of Mañjuśrī).
- **East Asia** — **Yánluó (Yama) Wáng** in China, **Enma / Enma-ō** in Japan, **Yeomra** in Korea — king of the underworld courts.
