# Tejaji / Veer Teja — the Jat Serpent-Hero of Kharnal, and the Rajasthani Panch Pīrs

> Deities-and-hierarchy node. **Tejājī** (also **Vīr Tejā, Tejājī Mahārāj**) is the great **Jat folk deity of Rajasthan** — a deified peasant-warrior hero (*vīr*) of **Kharnal (Nagaur district)** whose truth-keeping vow to a serpent made him, like [Gugga/Gogājī](gugga-gogaji.md), a **snakebite-curing god** invoked with the tying of a *tāṅtī* thread. ## 1. Two Clocks — Who Tejaji Is

Tejaji belongs entirely to the "historical clock": a folk hero-cult with a traditional medieval kernel and no Sanskrit-scriptural footprint at all.

- **Historical clock** — folk-hagiographic tradition makes Tejaji a **Jat chieftain's son of the Dhauliyā (Dhaulyā) clan of Kharnal**, a village near Nagaur in Marwar, conventionally **born Māgha śukla caturdaśī, V.S. 1130 (= 1074 CE)** and **dying of the serpent's bite on Bhādrapada śukla daśamī, V.S. 1160 (= 1103 CE)**. These dates are hagiographic-traditional reckonings, not documentary. (Tejājī kathā / Kharnal shrine tradition) [folk] [disputed]
- **Scriptural clock** — Tejaji appears in **no Veda, epic or Purāṇa**; his authority is the oral **Tejājī kathā and Tejā-gīt** (songs sung in the fields during the monsoon), shrine tradition and possession-mediums, exactly like Gugga and Pābūjī. [scholarly]
- **What he governs** — cure of **snakebite** above all (and in popular practice dog-bite and other venoms), plus protection of **cattle, crops and the rains**; he is the pre-eminent deity of the Jat peasantry of Rajasthan, with a following extending into Malwa (Madhya Pradesh) and Haryana. [folk] [regional]
- **The theological engine** — where Gugga's snake-power comes through boon and heroic descent into the earth, Tejaji's comes through **satya (truth-keeping)**: he kept his promise to return and receive a serpent's bite even at the cost of his life, and the *nāga*, conquered by that truthfulness, granted that **no snake-venom would harm those who invoke Tejā's name**. [folk]
- **Divine identification** — many Jat devotees revere Tejaji as an **incarnation/emanation of Śiva**, and modern devotional tradition styles him a *devatā* outright; this identification is devotional, not textual. [folk] [disputed]

## 2. Names, Family and Attributes (catalog)

- **Tejājī / Vīr Tejā / Tejājī Mahārāj** — the standard devotional names; *Vīr* marks him as a deified hero of the *jhuṃjhār/vīr* class. [regional]
- **Parentage** — son of **Tāhaṛjī (Tāhaṛ Dev)**, Jat headman of Kharnal, and **Rāmkuṃvarī**; born, in the pious telling, by the **grace of the serpent-deity/Nāga** whom his mother propitiated for a son — the serpent-connection is thus present from birth. (Tejājī kathā) [folk]
- **The mare Līlaṇ** — Tejaji's inseparable mount, the mare **Līlaṇ (Leelan)**; his fixed icon is a **sword-bearing rider on Līlaṇ with a serpent striking at his tongue**, rendered on shrine-stones (*devalī*) across Rajasthan. [regional]
- **The bride Pemal** — **Pemal (Pemal De)**, daughter of a chief of **Paner** (in the Ajmer–Pushkar tract), married to Tejā in **childhood**; a **blood-feud between the two families at the time of the wedding** (kinsman slain by kinsman; recensions differ on who slew whom) left the marriage unconsummated and unmentioned in Tejā's home. (Tejājī kathā) [folk]
- **The sister Rājal** — Tejā's sister **Rājal (Rājal Bāī)** also receives worship in parts of the cult region as a deified kinswoman. [folk] [regional]

## 3. The Heroic Narrative (the core kathā, in order)

The oral tale is a truth-and-honor tragedy in four movements: the taunt, the vow to the serpent, the cattle-rescue, and the tongue-bite. Recensions vary in detail; the through-line is constant.

- **The sister-in-law's taunt** — Tejā, grown to manhood ignorant of his childhood marriage, is ploughing his field when his **bhābhī (elder brother's wife)** brings his meal late and, rebuked, **taunts him to go fetch his own wife from Paner** instead of scolding her. Stung, Tejā learns the story of the feud-broken marriage and sets out at once on Līlaṇ for Paner, disregarding his mother's warnings and the inauspicious omens on the road. (Tejājī kathā) [folk]
- **The serpent in the fire and the vow** — on the way Tejā finds a **serpent (nāga) trapped in a burning bush** and pulls it from the flames. Instead of gratitude the snake declares it must **bite him** — in the widespread telling because its **mate perished in the fire** while it alone was saved (in others because a mortal's touch has broken its state). Tejā does not refuse: he asks only **leave to complete his errand** — to bring his bride home — and **vows to return to the same spot to receive the bite**. The serpent releases him on his word. (Tejājī kathā) [folk]
  - *Variant (recension detail):* some tellings place the burning-serpent episode on the **outward** journey to Paner, others on the **return**; the vow-and-return structure is identical. [folk] [oral tradition]
- **Lāchhā Gūjarī's cattle carried off** — at Paner, where Pemal's kin (mindful of the old feud) resist the reunion, it is **Lāchhā (Lāchhā Gūjarī)**, Pemal's herdswoman companion, who aids the couple. That very night **raiders — in the standard telling the Mīṇās of Chāṅg — drive off Lāchhā's cattle**, and she cries to Tejā for help. (Tejājī kathā) [folk]
- **The cattle-rescue** — Tejā rides alone after the raiders, fights them, and **recovers the whole herd**, taking grievous wounds over his entire body in the battle. (Tejājī kathā) [folk]
  - *Variant (the missing calf):* in beloved recensions Tejā, having returned the herd, **rides back a second time to recover a single calf** still in the raiders' hands — completing the rescue to the last animal before turning to his darker vow. [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The tongue-bite** — bleeding from every limb, Tejā keeps his word and returns to the serpent. The snake finds **no unwounded spot on his body fit to strike**, so Tejā **offers his tongue**; the serpent bites him there. (Tejājī kathā) [folk]
- **The boon of the serpent** — conquered by such truthfulness, the *nāga* grants the dying hero the charter of his cult: **whoever, bitten by a snake, ties a thread (tāṅtī) in Tejā's name and remembers him shall take no harm from the venom**; snakes themselves will not harm his devotees. (Tejājī kathā) [folk]
- **Death at Sursurā and Pemal's satī** — Tejā dies of the bite on **Bhādrapada śukla daśamī** at **Sursurā (Ajmer district)**, where his death-shrine stands; in most tellings the news is carried home by the mare **Līlaṇ**, and **Pemal becomes satī** on his pyre, joining him in death. (Tejājī kathā / Sursurā sthala tradition) [folk] [regional]

## 4. The Tāṅtī-Thread Snakebite Cult

- **The tāṅtī** — the cult's signature rite: a **consecrated thread (tāṅtī)** is tied on a snakebite victim (commonly at the wrist or the affected limb) **in Tejājī's name**, binding the venom by the hero's boon; the victim (or kin) then owes a **vow-visit to a Tejājī shrine — ideally on Teja Daśamī — to untie the thread** and make offerings. [folk] [regional]
- **Scope of the cure** — in living practice the tāṅtī and Tejā's name are invoked not only for snakebite but also for **dog-bite and other venomous bites**, making Tejā a general poison-curing deity of the countryside. [folk]
- **Shrines (thāns / devrās)** — Tejājī is worshipped at village **thāns** and **devrās** across Rajasthan (densest in Nagaur, Ajmer and the surrounding Jat country), typically with a **hero-stone or image of the rider on Līlaṇ with the serpent at his tongue**; the principal seats are **Kharnal** (birthplace) and **Sursurā** (death-site), with major temples also at **Paner** and at **Parbatsar**. [regional]
- **Possession-mediums** — at Tejājī shrines and fairs, possession-mediums (the cult's *bhopā*-type functionaries, called **ghoṛlā/paujuṇiyā** in parts of the region) become vehicles of Tejā, dance under possession, and in dramatic displays **flagellate themselves with a whip** as proof of the god's presence while treating snakebite vows. [folk] [regional]
- **Songs of the monsoon** — the **Tejā-gīt / Tejā-ṭer**, sung by Jat cultivators in the fields during the rains (the ploughing and snake season), carry the kathā orally; like the Gugga-gāthā they are **anonymous, fluid and performative**, with no fixed literary text. [folk] [oral tradition] [scholarly]

## 5. Teja Daśamī and the Parbatsar Fair

- **Teja Daśamī** — Tejājī's festival falls on the **tenth (daśamī) of the bright fortnight of Bhādrapada (c. August–September)**, his traditional death-day — one day after Gugga's dark-fortnight Navamī in the same snakebite season that governs [Gugga/Gogājī](gugga-gogaji.md)'s and [Manasā](manasa.md)'s calendars. Devotees fulfil tāṅtī-vows, offer coconuts, *churma* and thread at the thāns, and processions carry Tejā's image. [regional]
- **The Parbatsar cattle fair** — the greatest gathering of the cult is the annual **Vīr Tejājī cattle fair at Parbatsar (Nagaur district)**, held around Teja Daśamī: one of Rajasthan's largest livestock fairs, where the herdsman-protector god presides over the trading of cattle, camels and horses — the cattle-rescuer honored with a cattle-market. [regional]
- **Kharnal and Sursurā observances** — parallel fairs and pilgrimages mark the day at **Kharnal** (the birthplace shrine) and **Sursurā** (the death-site), and among Jat migrant communities in Malwa and Haryana. [regional]

## 6. Significance — the Vīr-Cult Pattern

- **A peasant's god, not a priest's** — Tejājī is the outstanding example of the **Jat agrarian hero-cult**: his sanctity rests on kept truth, cattle-protection and death in service of a herdswoman — the values of the cultivating and herding castes — and his cult runs on **hero-stones, threads, possession and song**, outside Brahmanical temple-orthodoxy, exactly as with [Gugga](gugga-gogaji.md), [Khaṇḍobā](khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md) and the [village grāmadevatās](gramadevatas-village-deities.md). [scholarly]
- **Twinned with Gugga** — Rajasthan thus carries **two great snakebite-curing hero-gods** with adjacent Bhādrapada feast-days: Gugga the Chauhan Rajput of the Bāgar (dark-fortnight Navamī) and Tejā the Jat of Kharnal (bright-fortnight Daśamī); their cults overlap geographically but remain distinct in caste base, narrative and rite. [scholarly] [regional]

## 7. The Rajasthani Panch Pīrs — the Full Roster

- **The pāñch pīr couplet** — Rajasthani tradition gathers its five great deified folk-heroes (*pīrs*) in a famous couplet: ***"Pābū, Haṛbū, Rāmde, Māṅgaliyā Mehā — pāṁcūṁ pīr padhārjyo, Gogājī jehā"*** ("Pābū, Haṛbū, Rāmdev, Māṅgaliyā Mehā — come, all five pīrs, and Gogājī with them"). The canonical five are thus **Pābūjī, Haṛbhūjī (Harbhuji), Rāmdev Pīr, Mehājī Māṅgaliyā and Gogājī**; Tejājī, though of the same class and stature, stands **outside the classical five**. [folk] [regional]
  - **Pābūjī** — the Rāṭhoṛ camel-protector hero of the *phaḍ*-epic, treated in [../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md). [folk] [oral tradition]
  - **Haṛbhūjī (Harbhuji) Sāṅkhlā** — a **Sāṅkhlā Rajput warrior of Bhūṇḍel** (Marwar) who, by tradition, renounced arms to become a disciple of the Nāth yogī **Bālīnāth**; folk memory makes him a **kinsman and contemporary of Rāmdev Pīr**, and credits his blessing with **Rāo Jodhā's recovery of Mandore** (mid-15th century by the traditional synchronism) before the founding of Jodhpur. At his shrine at **Bhūṇḍel** his **cart (gāḍā)** is venerated — the cart in which, the legend says, the lamed saint carried fodder to helpless wild animals. [folk] [regional] [disputed]
  - **Rāmdev Pīr (Bābā Rāmdev / Rāmsā Pīr)** — the Tanwar Rajput saint-deity of **Runicha/Rāmdevrā** near Pokhran, venerated by Hindus and Muslims alike, with his great Bhādrapada fair at Rāmdevrā; traditionally placed in the 14th–15th century CE. [folk] [regional]
  - **Mehājī Māṅgaliyā** — deified warrior-hero of the **Māṅgaliyā Rajputs**, by tradition a boon-born hero who **fell in battle on Janmāṣṭamī** (on which day his fair is held), remembered with his famed horse **Kirad Kabrā**; principal shrine at **Bāpiṇī (Jodhpur district)**. His lore survives almost wholly in shrine tradition and local song. [folk] [regional]
  - **Gogājī** — the Chauhan serpent-warrior of Gogāmeḍī, treated in full in [gugga-gogaji.md](gugga-gogaji.md). [folk] [regional]
- **The pīr idiom** — as with Gogā-pīr and Rāmsā Pīr, the very title **pīr** (a Sufi saint-term) marks these cults as **shared Hindu–Muslim devotional ground** of the western desert, venerated across caste and creed lines. [scholarly] [regional]
