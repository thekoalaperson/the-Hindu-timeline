# Ayyaṉār / Śāstā — the Tamil Village Guardian

> Where this sits: a dedicated node within **[Deities & Cosmic Hierarchy](README.md)**, expanding the **grāmadevatā / kāval-deyvam (village-guardian)** line that runs alongside the goddess-roster of **[Forms of the Devī & the Navadurgā](devi-forms-and-navadurga.md)**. He is to be **disambiguated from** the celibate Keralan **[Ayyappa / Dharma Śāstā](ayyappa.md)** and read beside the Deccan warrior-guardian **[Khaṇḍobā / Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava](khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md)**.

**Ayyaṉār** (Tamil *aiyaṉār*, "the lord / the revered one"; also **Aiyaṉ, Śāstā, Cāttaṉ, Cāttā**) is the classic **boundary-and-night guardian** (*kāval deyvam*, "watchman-deity") of the Tamil village — the god who patrols the field-edge and the cremation-fringe on horseback through the dark, keeping demons, disease and cattle-thieves outside the settled land. He is one of the oldest and most pervasive **grāmadevatās** of Tamil country (and Kerala's *Cāttaṉ*/Śāstā), worshipped at open-air shrines on the village periphery, marked by **rows of giant terracotta horses (and elephants)** offered as his mounts. He is **martial-pastoral** in character and, unlike the Sabarimala celibate, is typically **married** — to the twin consorts **Pūrṇā** and **Puṣkalā**. This file catalogs the older Dravidian Ayyaṉār/Śāstā cult layer on its own terms, cataloging variants rather than merging them, and marks where it does and does not equate to [Ayyappa](ayyappa.md). ## Names & the Śāstā layer

- **Ayyaṉār / Aiyaṉ** — Tamil *aiyaṉ* = "lord, father, elder"; the everyday name of the village guardian. The *-ār* is an honorific plural. (Tamil grāma-devatā tradition) [regional]
- **Śāstā / Cāttaṉ** — the Sanskritic/Brahmanizing name *Śāstā* ("the ruler, the chastiser, the teacher") and its Tamil form **Cāttaṉ**; an old South Indian deity-name attested well before the crystallized Sabarimala cult, applied to the same guardian function. (Older South Indian Śāsta strata) [scholarly]
- **Hariharaputra overlay** — when Sanskritized, Ayyaṉār-Śāstā is given the **son-of-Śiva-and-Mohinī** parentage (see [Ayyappa](ayyappa.md)), folding the Dravidian guardian into the **[Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa](ayyappa.md)** Śāstā / *Bhūtanātha* genealogy. This is a **later theological graft** onto an older village god, not his original myth. (Purāṇic Śāstā tradition; harmonized) [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Buddhist/Jain-tinged Śāstā** — in some scholarly readings *Śāstā* ("teacher") carried an older **Buddhist or Jain** colouring in the Tamil–Kerala zone before its absorption into the Śaiva-Vaiṣṇava synthesis. [scholarly] [disputed]
  - *Variant (scholarly):* Ayyaṉār, Cāttaṉ/Śāstā, and the Keralan celibate Ayyappa are best treated as **distinct cult-figures later conflated** under shared names — the married, horse-riding village watchman is arguably a separate, older Dravidian deity from the *Naiṣṭhika Brahmacāri* of Sabarimala. [scholarly] [disputed]

## The boundary & night-riding guardian (his core function)

- **The kāval deyvam** — Ayyaṉār is the **watchman-god of the village**: his shrine stands not in the centre but at the **periphery** — by the tank-bund, the field-boundary, the grove, or the edge of the burning-ground — facing **outward** to ward what lies beyond. (Tamil grāma-devatā tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The night patrol** — by night he is believed to **ride out on horseback** around the village limits, sword or whip in hand, driving off **demons (pēy/picācu), epidemic, drought and thieves**; villagers report hearing his horses at night. This nocturnal boundary-circuit is the heart of his cult. (Tamil folk tradition) [folk]
- **Lord of the bhūtas / commander of the host** — like the Purāṇic **Bhūtanātha**, Ayyaṉār leads a **retinue of attendant spirits and minor guardians** (Karuppu/Karuppasāmi, Muṉīśvaraṉ, Maturai-Vīraṉ and other *kāval* deities) who serve as his lieutenants at the village edge. (Tamil folk tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **Protector of field and cattle** — a **pastoral-agrarian** god: invoked for the safety of herds, the success of the harvest and the holding of the rains; his pairing with the field-boundary makes him the guarantor of the cultivated against the wild. (Tamil grāma-devatā tradition) [folk]
- **Open-air, non-Brahman shrine** — classically a **roofless periphery shrine** served by **non-Brahman priests** (Vēḷḷāḷa, Kuyavar/potter, or other community priests), with **animal offering** historically central — marking him a folk deity only secondarily drawn into temple Hinduism. (Tamil folk tradition) [folk] [regional]

## The terracotta-horse cult

- **The giant horses (kutirai)** — Ayyaṉār's signature: avenues of **enormous fired-clay (terracotta) horses**, sometimes life-size or larger, lined up before and around his shrine — votive **mounts** given to the god so that he and his ghostly host may ride the night patrol. (Terracotta-horse cult lore) [folk] [regional]
- **The potter-priest (Kuyavar / Vēlar)** — the horses are made by the **village potter-caste (Kuyavar/Vēlar)**, whose making of the mount is itself a ritual act; the great horse-making and consecration is a community event. (Tamil folk tradition; terracotta-horse lore) [folk] [regional]
- **The vow-offering (nērttikkaḍaṉ)** — horses (and **terracotta elephants, bulls, and warrior-figures**) are pledged in fulfilment of vows for rain, healing, childbirth or protection; the shrine accretes new clay mounts over generations, the old ones left to weather back into the earth. (Tamil folk tradition) [folk]
- **The Aiyaṉār field-shrines of the south** — such horse-avenue shrines are densest in **southern Tamil Nadu** (Pudukkottai, Madurai, Sivaganga, Ramanathapuram country), where Ayyaṉār/Aiyaṉār worship and the terracotta-horse craft are especially strong. (Regional tradition) [regional]
  - *Variant (function of the elephants):* the elephants and bulls are read variously as **additional mounts**, as **vāhanas for his lieutenants**, or as offerings in their own right; rosters of votive animals vary by locality. [folk] [open-ended]

## Consorts: Pūrṇā and Puṣkalā

- **The twin consorts** — in his married (non-celibate) form Ayyaṉār-Śāstā is flanked by **Pūrṇā** ("the full / complete") and **Puṣkalā** ("the abundant / flourishing"), two goddesses standing at either side of the seated or mounted god. This **married iconography is exactly what distinguishes village Ayyaṉār from the celibate [Ayyappa](ayyappa.md)**. (Tamil grāma-devatā tradition; Śāstā temple iconography) [regional]
- **Iconographic reading** — Pūrṇā and Puṣkalā are often glossed as embodying **fullness/completion and prosperity/fertility** — fitting a god of field, herd and the well-being of the village. (Temple iconography) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Sanskritizing harmonization):* devotional sources reconcile the married Śāstā and the celibate Ayyappa by treating Pūrṇā–Puṣkalā as belonging to a **different aspect/form** of Śāstā, so that the same deity is celibate at Sabarimala and married in the Tamil village. This is a **post-hoc reconciliation** of two originally distinct cults. [folk] [disputed]
  - *Variant (Malikappurattamma overlay):* where the cults merge, the Tamil consort-goddesses are loosely associated with the Keralan **Malikappurattamma** of the [Ayyappa](ayyappa.md) cycle; the equation is loose and not consistent. [folk] [disputed]

## Disambiguation from Sabarimala Ayyappa

- **Two cult-figures, partly fused** — the **celibate** Keralan **[Ayyappa / Dharma Śāstā](ayyappa.md)** (the *Naiṣṭhika Brahmacāri* of Sabarimala, with the Manikaṇṭha/Pandalam legend and the 41-day vow) and the **married, horse-riding** Tamil **Ayyaṉār-Śāstā** (village watchman with Pūrṇā and Puṣkalā) share the names *Śāstā / Cāttaṉ* and a Śiva-Mohinī genealogy, **but are best treated as distinct deities later identified**. (Scholarly reading; see [Ayyappa](ayyappa.md)) [scholarly] [disputed]
- **What they share** — the name **Śāstā**, the **Hariharaputra/Bhūtanātha** genealogy, the **guardian-of-dharma** function, and a horse/martial-guardian register. (Common stratum)
- **What separates them** — **marriage vs celibacy** (Ayyaṉār married, Ayyappa *brahmacāri*); **boundary night-watchman vs hill-pilgrimage deity**; **open-air folk shrine with terracotta horses vs the Western-Ghats forest temple and 18 steps**; **Tamil village periphery vs Keralan Sabarimala**. (Comparative reading) [scholarly]
- **The "two clocks"** — the **Śiva–Mohinī birth and Purāṇic Śāstā layer** belong to *scriptural/Purāṇic* time (post-Manthana, indefinite); the **living Ayyaṉār village cult** — periphery shrines, terracotta-horse craft, non-Brahman priesthoods — is a *kali-historical* phenomenon with **no fixed scriptural date** and a **late/medieval-and-debated** crystallization. [scholarly]

## Open rosters & cautions

- **The Ayyaṉār–Śāstā–Ayyappa equation** — treat any flat statement that the three are "the same god" as a **devotional harmonization**, not a settled scholarly fact; this file keeps the **older Dravidian Ayyaṉār** distinct while noting the overlaps. [open-ended] [disputed]
- **Lieutenant-deities (Karuppu, Muṉīśvaraṉ, Maturai-Vīraṉ, etc.)** — the retinue of guardian deities attached to Ayyaṉār varies sharply by **locality**; rosters are open-ended, not canonical. [open-ended] [regional]
- **Votive-animal rosters** — horses, elephants, bulls, warrior-figures: which are mounts, which are lieutenants' vāhanas, which are stand-alone offerings differs village to village. [open-ended] [folk]
