# Tulja Bhavani of Tuljapur

> Deities-and-hierarchy node. **Tuḷjā Bhavānī** (Tulja Bhavani, **Āī Bhavānī**, **Turajā**, **Tvaritā**) is the great **tutelary goddess of the Marathi country** — an eight-armed **Mahiṣāsura-mardinī** form of the supreme **[Devī](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)** enthroned at **Tuljapur** (Dhārāshiv/Osmanabad district, Maharashtra), one of the **"three-and-a-half" (sāḍe-tīn) Śakti Pīṭhas** of Maharashtra and the **kuladevī of the Bhosale house of Shivaji** and countless Maratha clans. ## 1. Two Clocks — Who Tulja Bhavani Is

- **Scriptural clock** — Tuljā Bhavānī is worshipped as the **full Great Goddess**: her core temple-myth is a *sthala* application of the **[Devī-Māhātmya](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)** pattern (the Goddess descends at a devotee's cry, slays a buffalo-demon, and stays as a standing power-seat), transmitted through a **Tuljā-māhātmya attributed to the Skanda Purāṇa (Sahyādri-khaṇḍa)** and local *sthala-purāṇa* recitation. (Skanda Purāṇa, Sahyādri-khaṇḍa — attributed; sthala-tradition) [late text] [regional]
- **Historical clock** — the standing **Tuljapur temple** on the Bālā-ghāṭ hill is generally assigned to the **Yādava era (c. 12th–13th c. CE)** with heavy Maratha-period and modern rebuilding; the goddess's pan-Maharashtrian prominence is documented from the **Maratha period (17th c. CE)** onward, above all through the **Bhosale kuladevī bond**. [scholarly]
- **What she governs** — she is the **war-mother and clan-mother (kuladevī)** of the Marathi lands: protectress of lineages, giver of victory, the *āī* ("mother") whose name — **"Āī Rājā, Udo Udo!"** — is the battle-and-festival cry of her devotees. [regional] [folk]
- **The name** — **"Tuḷjā/Turajā"** is traditionally derived from Sanskrit **Tvaritā, "she who hastens"** — because she came *instantly* to her devotee's cry (§3); *Tvaritā → Turajā → Tuḷjā* in the sthala etymology. **Bhavānī** ("giver of being," feminine of Bhava-Śiva) marks her as Śiva's consort, the [Pārvatī / great-Goddess](sati-daksha-and-parvati.md) identity. (sthala-tradition) [regional]
  - *Variant ([scholarly] tantric stratum):* **Tvaritā** is independently attested as a **tantric goddess with her own vidyā** in Śaiva-Śākta tantra (e.g. the Tvaritā sections of tantric compendia); some scholars read the Tuljapur goddess as a Purāṇicized seat of this older Tvaritā cult. [scholarly] [disputed]

## 2. Names and Epithets (catalog)

- **Tuḷjā Bhavānī / Āī Bhavānī** — the cult-name: "Mother Bhavānī of Tuljapur." [regional]
- **Turajā / Tvaritā** — "she who hastens," from the origin-legend (§3). (sthala-tradition) [regional]
- **Tukāī** — an affectionate Marathi mother-form of the name, used in devotion and *povāḍā* ballads. [folk]
- **Ambā / Ambābāī, Jagadambā** — "Mother / Mother of the world," shared with [Kolhapur's Ambābāī](../06-sacred-geography/kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md); the famous Bhosale sword is named **"Jagdambā"** after her (§6). [regional]
- **Rāmavaradāyinī** — "she who gave the boon to Rāma": local tradition holds that **Rāma**, southbound in search of Sītā, was blessed here by the goddess — folding the seat into Rāmāyaṇa geography. (sthala-tradition) [folk] [regional]

## 3. The Origin Myth — Anubhūti, the Demon, and the Goddess Who Hastened

The core *sthala* narrative, told from the Tuljā-māhātmya (attributed to the Skanda Purāṇa, Sahyādri-khaṇḍa) and living temple recitation; recensions differ in the demon's name and details, the through-line is constant.

- **The widowed sage-wife Anubhūti** — **Anubhūti**, the devoted wife of the *ṛṣi* **Kardama**, is left widowed; rather than follow her husband in death (she has an infant son to raise, in the common telling), she undertakes **severe penance to the Devī** on the banks of the **Mandākinī** river. (Tuljā-māhātmya / Skanda Purāṇa, Sahyādri-khaṇḍa — attributed) [late text] [regional]
- **The demon's assault** — the *daitya* **Kukura (Kūkur)** sees the beautiful ascetic and tries to violate her penance; Anubhūti cries out to the Goddess for protection. (Tuljā-māhātmya) [regional]
- **The Goddess hastens — Tvaritā** — the **[Devī](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)** appears **instantly (tvarita)** at the cry — whence the name **Tvaritā/Turajā/Tuḷjā** — and battles Kukura. (Tuljā-māhātmya; sthala-tradition) [regional]
- **The buffalo-form and the slaying** — in the fight the demon takes the **form of a buffalo (mahiṣa)**; the Goddess **beheads him**, re-enacting the **Mahiṣāsura-mardinī** act of the [Devī-Māhātmya](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) on Deccan soil. (Tuljā-māhātmya; cf. Devī-Māhātmya / Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 81–93 for the pan-Indian archetype) [regional]
- **The Goddess stays — the seat on Yamunācalā** — at Anubhūti's prayer the Goddess consents to **remain permanently** on the **Yamunācalā hill of the Bālā-ghāṭ range** — the site of Tuljapur — as a standing refuge for devotees; the seat-fixing move that makes a *māhātmya* into a *pīṭha*. (Tuljā-māhātmya; sthala-tradition) [regional]
- **The Mahiṣāsura localization** — Tuljapur tradition also claims the **great Mahiṣāsura-slaying itself** for its region (the buffalo-demon slain on/near the Bālā-ghāṭ, the goddess resting here after battle) — a *sthala* appropriation of the Devī-Māhātmya's third-*carita* battle. [folk] [regional]
  - *Variant (Devī-Māhātmya, Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa):* in the canonical text the Mahiṣa-slaying is **not localized** to any Deccan site; it is narrated in the frame of the coming **Sāvarṇi manvantara** story told to king Suratha — the localization is the temple-tradition's own. (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 81–93) [scholarly]
- **The Mātaṅga slaying** — Tuljapur tradition further credits the goddess with destroying the demon **Mātaṅga**, who oppressed gods and sages; the gods led by Brahmā appeal to her, and she destroys him — a second demon-cycle attached to the seat. (sthala-tradition) [regional] [folk]

## 4. The Tuljapur Shrine and the Thrice-Yearly Nidrā

- **The kṣetra** — **Tuljapur** sits on a hill of the **Bālā-ghāṭ range** in **Dhārāshiv (Osmanabad) district**, Marathwada; the temple is entered by descending flights of steps through gateways to the sunken court of the goddess. The standing fabric is assigned to the **Yādava period (c. 12th–13th c. CE)** in the usual account, with extensive **Maratha-era and modern** additions. [regional] [scholarly]
- **The mūrti** — the image is a c. three-foot **granite/black-stone, eight-armed Mahiṣāsura-mardinī**: the goddess bearing weapons (trident, sword, bow, discus among them, in temple description), the **severed buffalo-demon** at her feet, held **svayambhū** (self-manifest) by tradition. (temple tradition) [regional]
- **A movable goddess — the rarity** — unusually among major *svayambhū* seats, Tuljā Bhavānī's image is **cala (movable)**: at fixed points of the year the mūrti is **taken off its throne (siṁhāsana)** — the ritual precondition for her famous sleep-cycles and processions. (temple tradition) [regional]
- **The thrice-yearly nidrā (sleep) cycles** — the goddess is held to **sleep three times a year**, for a traditional total of **about 21 days**, during which the image is removed from the siṁhāsana and **laid on a silver bed (cāndī palang)** in the temple; the cycles are keyed to her battles and to the two Navarātras: [regional] [folk]
  - **Ghor nidrā** — the "deep sleep" in **Bhādrapada**, ending as the goddess **wakes for the Śāradīya (Āśvina) Navarātri**, her great festival. [regional]
  - **Śram nidrā** — the "sleep of weariness" **after Vijayādaśamī/Daśaharā**, understood as her rest after the demon-battle. [regional] [folk]
  - **Bhog nidrā** — the "sleep of enjoyment" in **Pauṣa**, before the **Śākambharī Navarātri** (the Pauṣa Navarātri kept at Tuljapur). [regional]
  - *Note:* the **names, order and exact dates** of the three nidrās vary between temple calendars and popular accounts; the fixed points are *three sleeps, ~21 days, tied to the two Navarātras and the post-victory rest*. [disputed] [folk]
- **Festival cycle** — the **Śāradīya Navarātri** climaxing in **Vijayādaśamī (Daśaharā)** with the **Sīmollaṅghan** procession is the great annual observance; the **Kojāgirī/Āśvina full moon** fair follows; **Tuesdays, Fridays and pūrṇimās** are her days, and pilgrims — many walking with **palanquin (pālkhī) processions** — converge in vast numbers. [regional] [folk]
- **Gondhaḷ and the goddess's bards** — Tuljā Bhavānī (with [Reṇukā of Māhūr](renuka-yellamma.md)) is the chief goddess of the Marathi **gondhaḷ** rite: hereditary performer-priests (**Gondhaḷī**, **Bhope**) sing her deeds with torch and *sambal*-drum at weddings and lineage rites — the living kuladevī liturgy outside the Sanskrit frame. [folk] [regional]

## 5. Kuladevī of the Marathas

- **Kuladevī of the Bhosales** — Tuljā Bhavānī is the **clan-goddess (kuladevī) of the Bhosale dynasty** of **Chhatrapati Shivaji (r. 1674–1680 CE)** [scholarly]; Shivaji's devotion to Bhavānī is a constant of the Maratha chronicles (*bakhars*) and ballads (*povāḍās*), and "**Jay Bhavānī**" became — with "Jay Śivājī" — the Maratha war-cry. (Maratha bakhar and povāḍā tradition) [regional] [folk]
- **Kuladevī of the many** — beyond the royal house she is kuladevī to a vast range of **Maratha, Kunbi, Dhangar, Māḷī, Kolhāṭī, Gondhaḷī and other Marathi (and border Kannada–Telugu) communities**, invoked at marriages, first-hair rites and lineage vows; households keep her presence in the devghar and owe periodic Tuljapur *darśana*. [regional]
- **Afzal Khan and the goddess (1659 CE)** — Maratha tradition holds that the Bijapur general **Afzal Khan**, marching against Shivaji in **1659**, **desecrated the Tuljapur shrine** (the image he broke being a stand-in, the true mūrti hidden by its priests, in the devotional telling), and that **Bhavānī then assured Shivaji of victory** — fulfilled at **Pratapgad (10 Nov 1659)** where Afzal Khan was killed. The raid and the Pratapgad encounter are part of the documented Maratha–Bijapur war; the goddess's interventions are hagiography. (Maratha bakhar tradition) [regional] [folk] — battle dating [scholarly]
- **Pan-goddess identity** — in Marathi devotion Bhavānī of Tuljapur, [Ambābāī of Kolhapur](../06-sacred-geography/kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md), [Reṇukā of Māhūr](renuka-yellamma.md) and Saptaśṛṅgī of Vaṇī (§7) are all read as the **one Devī** in four seats — the sāḍe-tīn pīṭha theology (§7) — while each keeps her own myth, calendar and clientele. [regional]

## 6. The Bhavānī Sword of Shivaji [folk / historical]

- **The legend** — in the widely-told story, the goddess **Bhavānī herself appeared to Shivaji** and **presented him a divine sword** — the **"Bhavānī talvār"** — pledging him victory and the founding of *Hindavī svarājya*; in stronger tellings she **possessed/entered** him or his sword-arm at moments of crisis (the Afzal Khan meeting above all). (Maratha povāḍā and bakhar tradition; later hagiography) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The historians' account** — modern historians treat the divine gift as **legend**: the celebrated sword was by this account an excellent **European (commonly said Genoese/Portuguese-traded) blade acquired for Shivaji**, which he **named "Bhavānī"** in honor of his kuladevī — the naming, not the epiphany, being the historical kernel. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The "Jagdambā" sword in London** — a famous Bhosale sword named **"Jagdambā"**, kept by the Kolhapur Chhatrapatis and popularly identified with Shivaji's Bhavānī sword, was **presented to the Prince of Wales (later Edward VII) in 1875–76** and remains in the **British Royal Collection**; its return is a recurring Maharashtrian demand. Whether Jagdambā *is* the Bhavānī sword is itself contested. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Rāmdās and the Bhavānī current** — the Bhavānī devotion of Shivaji's circle runs together with the influence of **[Samarth Rāmdās](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/samarth-ramdas.md)**, whose Śakti-charged hymns and *Ānandavanabhuvana* vision fed the same goddess-and-svarājya piety; tradition links guru, goddess and king, though the details of Rāmdās's role are debated among historians. [regional] [disputed]

## 7. The Sāḍe-tīn Śakti Pīṭhas — and Saptaśṛṅgī of Vaṇī

- **The "three-and-a-half" pīṭhas of Maharashtra** — Marathi sacred geography counts **sāḍe-tīn (3½) Śakti Pīṭhas**: **Tuljā Bhavānī of Tuljapur**, **[Mahālakṣmī/Ambābāī of Kolhapur](../06-sacred-geography/kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md)**, **[Reṇukā of Māhūr](renuka-yellamma.md)** as the three full seats, and **Saptaśṛṅgī of Vaṇī** as the **ardha (half) pīṭha** — a living regional circuit **distinct from** the pan-Indian [51](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md) and 18-Mahā lists (see the [temple-networks](../06-sacred-geography/temple-networks-and-circuits.md) logic). [regional] [folk]
  - *Variant (assignments differ):* which seat is "half" — and which goddess is *ādi* (first) — **varies by telling**; most popular lists make Saptaśṛṅgī the half-seat, but some regional recensions rank her a full or even the original pīṭha. As always, the variants are cataloged, not resolved. [disputed] [folk]
- **Saptaśṛṅgī of Vaṇī (brief)** — the goddess of the **seven-peaked (sapta-śṛṅga) hill** at **Vaṇī, Nashik district**: a colossal (~8 ft) **eighteen-armed svayambhū image carved in the sheer cliff-face**, bearing the weapons the gods gave the Devī for the Mahiṣa war — read as the **Mahālakṣmī of the Devī-Māhātmya's middle episode**, and in local tradition the place where the goddess **rested after slaying Mahiṣāsura**. (sthala-tradition; cf. Devī-Māhātmya 2–4 for the eighteen-armed Mahiṣa-slayer archetype) [regional]
  - **Mārkaṇḍeya's hill** — tradition sets the *ṛṣi* **Mārkaṇḍeya** on the facing hill, **reciting the Devī-Māhātmya to the goddess** — tying the seat to the very text of the Great Goddess. (sthala-tradition) [folk] [regional]
  - **Satī-limb claim** — some devotional accounts fold Saptaśṛṅgī into the [Satī-dismemberment](sati-daksha-and-parvati.md) map (a limb of Satī falling on the hill); this is a **local claim not in the canonical 51-lists** (see [51 Śakti Pīṭhas](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md)). [folk] [disputed]
  - **Festivals** — the great fairs are the **Chaitra pūrṇimā yātrā** and **Āśvina Navarātri**, with the flag-raising on the peak as the climax. [regional]

> **Two clocks.** The *scriptural* clock of Tuljā Bhavānī is Purāṇic-timeless: the Anubhūti rescue, the Kukura/Mahiṣa and Mātaṅga slayings, and the Goddess's permanent enthronement are *māhātmya* events of the deep past kept active through the present **[Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)**. The *historical* clock is: Yādava-era temple fabric (c. 12th–13th c. CE), the Maratha kuladevī apogee (17th c.: Afzal Khan 1659, Shivaji's coronation 1674), the Jagdambā sword's 1875–76 passage to Britain — all [scholarly]. The Bhavānī-sword epiphany and the goddess's battlefield interventions live between the clocks as [folk] hagiography.
