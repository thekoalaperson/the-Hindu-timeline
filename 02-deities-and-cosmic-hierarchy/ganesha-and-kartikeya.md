# Ganesha & Kartikeya — The Sons of Shiva-Parvati

> Where this sits in the hierarchy: `02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/` — a deep expansion of the
> household of Śiva-Pārvatī sketched in [`trimurti-and-tridevi.md`](trimurti-and-tridevi.md) and
> [`shiva-myth-cycle.md`](shiva-myth-cycle.md). Those nodes fix the parents on the cosmic clock; **this
> file** catalogues the two sons — **Gaṇeśa** (the elder-or-younger remover of obstacles) and
> **Kārtikeya / Skanda / Murugan** (the war-god born to slay Tārakāsura) — each myth as a dense,
> source-attributed event with full Purāṇic and regional (Tamil) variants. These deeds recur across
> yugas but cluster in the [Devāsura wars](devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md) and the dictation of the
> [Mahābhārata](../04-deep-dives/mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md) in the [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md).
> The canonical home for Skanda's birth and the war with Tāraka is
> [`tarakasura-vadha.md`](../04-deep-dives/tarakasura-vadha.md).

## Part I — Gaṇeśa (Gaṇapati, Vighneśvara)

The lord of Śiva's *gaṇas* (hosts), invoked first before any rite — patron of beginnings, remover of
obstacles (*vighna-harta*) and, paradoxically, their placer (*vighna-karta*). The texts do **not** agree
on how he came to be or how he got his head; all major versions are catalogued below. The *Śiva
Purāṇa* itself says so: "due to the difference of Kalpas, the story of the birth of Gaṇeśa is told in
different ways" — in one, Śani's look cuts off his head; the one it tells is that of the Śveta-kalpa,
in which Śiva cuts it off. (Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.13.5–6, Shastri tr.)

### Birth & the elephant head

- **The body-scurf origin (most popular)** — Pārvatī's friends Jayā and Vijayā urge her to have a
  servant "of our own", since all the gaṇas obey Śiva. When Śiva walks in on her bath past Nandin, she
  forms a boy "out of the dirt from her body", dresses him, calls him "my son" and posts him at her door
  with a staff. He is wholly *her* creation, with no part of Śiva. (Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.13.10–30; Shastri's
  "dirt", glossed by him as "scurf") The **turmeric paste** (*haridrā*) of many retellings is not in
  the *Śiva Purāṇa* as translated. [folk]
- **The beheading by Śiva** — the boy, not knowing that the stranger is Pārvatī's husband, bars Śiva and
  strikes him with his staff (2.4.13.32–37). He routs Śiva's gaṇas and pulls out Brahmā's beard (2.4.15).
  Two Śaktis created by Pārvatī swallow and throw back the gods' weapons (2.4.15.45–49). Śiva decides he
  can be killed only by deception: **Viṣṇu** deludes him with *māyā* and splits his iron club with the
  discus (2.4.16.8–16, 2.4.16.29). While the boy fights Viṣṇu, Śiva cuts off his head with the
  **triśūla**. (Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.16.34; Nārada goads both sides, 2.4.15.67–72, 2.4.16.36)
- **The restoration with an elephant's head** — the grieving Pārvatī creates thousands of Śaktis to
  devour the gods and threatens an untimely deluge (2.4.17.6–16). She relents only if her son lives and
  is made chief among the gods (2.4.17.42–43). Śiva sends the gods **north** to cut off the head of the
  first creature they meet, and it is **a single-tusked elephant**. They fix the head on, sprinkle the
  body with mantra-water, and the boy wakes as the red-complexioned, elephant-faced **Gajānana**.
  (Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.17.47–57) Pārvatī gives him first worship (2.4.18.8–12). Śiva, Viṣṇu and Brahmā
  decree that he is worshipped before them, or no rite bears fruit (2.4.18.20–24), and Śiva makes him
  lord of the gaṇas (2.4.18.31). In this account the six-faced Kumāra already exists and fights him
  (2.4.15.38, 2.4.15.59), so Gaṇeśa is the younger son.
  - *Variant (Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, Gaṇapati-khaṇḍa):* Gaṇeśa is **Kṛṣṇa himself**, born as the fruit of
    Pārvatī's costly **Puṇyaka vow** (Sen tr. ch. 6). Viṣṇu/Hari comes to Śiva and Pārvatī's door as an
    old, hungry, thirsty brahmin. Śiva rises, and his seed drops on the ground instead of entering her
    womb. The brahmin vanishes, takes the form of an infant on Pārvatī's bed, and "mixed himself up with
    the semen of S'iva" (ch. 8). A voice then tells her that the infant is Hari himself (ch. 9). The
    prophecy in ch. 6 says instead that Kṛṣṇa "will be born through the womb of Parvati". In the
    assembly gathered after the birth, **Śani (Saturn)** keeps his eyes down, explaining that his wife
    cursed his glance to destroy whatever it falls on (ch. 11). Pārvatī presses him to look anyway.
    He glances from the corner of his left eye, and the child's head flies off to Goloka and merges in
    Kṛṣṇa. **Viṣṇu/Hari** rides Garuḍa **northwards to the Puṣpabhadrā river** and finds an elephant
    lying with its head to the north. He cuts off the head, revives the elephant with another head, and
    joins the first one to the child (ch. 12). Pārvatī curses Śani to be lame (ch. 12). Two backstories
    give the cause:
    - Kaśyapa cursed Śiva's son to be beheaded after Śiva speared the Sun (ch. 18).
    - The elephant was Indra's own; earlier in the chapter Indra dismounts from "his elephant, the
      Airāvata". Indra set the Pārijāta flower that Durvāsas gave him on his elephant's head instead of
      his own, and the elephant threw him and fled to the forest, where
      Hari later took its head (ch. 20).
    (Sen tr.; chapter numbers are Sen's)
  - *Variant (Varāha Purāṇa 23.8–31):* the gods ask Rudra for a being to obstruct wicked deeds. Rudra
    laughs, and from his laughter and the elements springs a radiant boy "like Rudra himself". When Umā
    stares at the boy, Rudra, angered "thinking of the fickleness of women's minds", curses him with "an
    elephant's face, protruding belly and *yajñopavīta* of serpents". Brahmā makes him chief of the
    Vināyakas who shake out of Rudra's body, and Rudra names him Vināyaka, Vighnakara, Gaṇeśa and
    Bhavaputra. Here he is **Śiva's, not Pārvatī's**. (Varāha Purāṇa 23.8–31, AITM tr.)
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 154.500–505):* longing for a son, Pārvatī plays with her friends at making
    toy sons. She rubs her body with scented oil and powders, and from the scrub (*udvartanaka*) she
    makes a figure that is already **elephant-faced** (*naraṃ cakre gajānanam*). She puts it in the
    water of her friend Gaṅgā (Jāhnavī). He grows huge, both goddesses call him "son", and the gods
    worship him as **Gāṅgeya** (*gāṅgeya iti devais tu pūjito 'bhūd gajānanaḥ*, 154.505), the epithet
    Skanda also bears. Brahmā gives him lordship of the Vināyakas. There is no beheading. The two
    mothers are also the usual gloss on his epithet **Dvaimātura** ("of two mothers"), which the Matsya
    does not use. [commentarial gloss]
  - *Variant (Liṅga Purāṇa 1.104–105):* the gods, led by Indra and Upendra, ask Śiva to create a lord of
    obstacles to hinder the daityas' rites (1.104). Śiva himself takes the form of **Gaṇeśvara**, and an
    elephant-faced boy holding trident and noose comes out of Śiva and Ambikā, dances, and is praised as
    **Ekadanta** (1.105.7–11). Śiva performs his birth-rites and charges him to obstruct
    unrighteous rites such as a sacrifice without fees, and he creates the host of obstacles
    (1.105.13–28). The chapter closes by calling the tale the birth of **Skanda's elder brother**
    (*skandāgrajasamudbhavam*, 1.105.30), so here Gaṇeśa is the elder son, against the *Śiva Purāṇa*
    order above. An earlier verse says only that Vināyaka was born in Śiva's garden at Kāśī
    (1.103.79).
  - *Note (the "always elephant-headed" strand):* in the Matsya, Liṅga and Varāha he is elephant-headed
    from his first appearance, so the beheading belongs to the Śiva and Brahmavaivarta Purāṇas. Reading
    the head as an emblem of *praṇava* (Oṃ) is Gāṇapatya theology, not a birth-narrative. [scholarly]

### The broken tusk (*Ekadanta*)

- **Single-tusked from the start** — in the *Śiva Purāṇa* the elephant whose head he receives is already
  "single-tusked" (2.4.17.49, Shastri tr.), and the Liṅga calls the newborn *Ekadanta* (1.105.10).
- **Fight with Paraśurāma** — the *Brahmavaivarta* foretells it at his birth: "the axe of Paraśurāma will
  remove one of his tusks, hence he will be called the god with one tooth" (Gaṇapati-khaṇḍa, Sen tr.
  ch. 6). Later Paraśurāma comes to Kailāsa while Śiva and Pārvatī are asleep and Gaṇeśa bars him.
  Gaṇeśa lifts him in his trunk and whirls him through the worlds, showing him Goloka. Paraśurāma then
  hurls the axe Śiva gave him, and Gaṇeśa, honouring his father's weapon, takes it on his **left
  tusk**, which falls. Kārtikeya tells the woken Pārvatī what happened. (Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa,
  Gaṇapati-khaṇḍa, Sen tr. ch. 41–43)
- **Scribe of the Mahābhārata** — after Brahmā tells Vyāsa to think of Gaṇeśa as writer, Gaṇeśa agrees
  on condition that his pen never stop. Vyāsa counters that Gaṇeśa must not write anything he has not
  understood, and inserts knotty verses to buy time. (Mahābhārata 1.1, vulgate; Ganguli §1) The
  passage is not in the constituted text of the critical edition, not even as a starred line.
  [interpolation] Neither
  the vulgate nor the critical edition says the pen broke or that he snapped off a tusk to write; that
  detail is a popular modern retelling. [folk; locus not verified] See [Vyāsa](../04-deep-dives/vyasa.md).
- *Variant (the moon-throwing):* the tusk he broke off to fling at the laughing moon (see below) is the
  lost one. [folk tradition]
- *Variant (Gajamukhāsura):* in Tamil tradition he breaks his tusk to slay the elephant-faced demon
  Gajamukhāsura, who turns into a mouse and becomes his mount. [regional; locus not verified: popular
  and temple sources only]

### The race around the parents (vs Kārtikeya)

- **The contest for marriage** — the two sons quarrel over who will marry first. Śiva and Pārvatī rule
  that whoever first goes **round the whole earth** and returns will be married first. Kumāra sets off at
  once. Gaṇeśa bathes, seats his parents, and circumambulates and bows to them **seven times**. He
  argues from Veda and śāstra that worshipping and circling one's parents equals circling the earth, and
  the parents accept it. (Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.19.9–55) He marries **Siddhi and Buddhi**, daughters of
  Prajāpati Viśvarūpa, and has two sons, **Kṣema** by Siddhi and **Lābha** by Buddhi. (Śiva Purāṇa
  2.4.20.1–8)
  - *Variant (the fruit of wisdom):* the prize is a single fruit of knowledge (*ñāṉappaḻam*, usually told
    as a mango) brought by **Nārada**. Murugan circles the world on his peacock, Gaṇeśa circles his
    parents and gets the fruit, and Murugan goes off to **Palani**. [regional; folk — Palani tradition]
  - *Consequence (Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.20.9–37):* Nārada tells the returning Kumāra that he was sent off by a
    trick. Angry, Kumāra leaves for the **Krauñca mountain** and from that day remains a bachelor
    (2.4.20.23–27). Pārvatī grieves, Śiva follows as the **Mallikārjuna** *jyotirliṅga*, and the parents
    visit him on new- and full-moon days (2.4.20.31–37). Shastri notes that the text puts Mallikārjuna on
    Krauñca, and that its identification with Śrīśaila is hard to explain. See
    [the 12 Jyotirliṅgas](../06-sacred-geography/12-jyotirlingas.md).
  - *Consequence (Tamil):* the slighted Murugan leaves Kailāsa for the south and settles in the Tamil
    country at Palani (see Part II), a myth that explains Murugan's southern home. [regional tradition]

### The moon's curse & Saṃkaṣṭī

- **The moon (Candra) mocks him** — Candra, sitting on Śiva's forehead, laughs again and again at
  Gaṇeśa's elephant-faced form, asking why he took such a grotesque (*vikaṭa*) shape. Gaṇeśa curses
  him: whoever looks at the moon will be a sinner beset by obstacles, and Candra grows dim
  (Mudgala Purāṇa 4.6, vv. c. 52–56). The world suffers without the moon, and the gods propitiate
  Gaṇeśa. He limits the curse to a single day: the moon must not be seen on **Bhādrapada śukla
  caturthī** (Gaṇeśa Caturthī) (4.6, vv. c. 88–92). The moon then takes its place on Gaṇeśa's forehead,
  whence **Bhālacandra** (4.6, c. v. 115). In the same passage Gaṇeśa ordains that the moon be
  worshipped with an *arghya* after him in the vow of the **kṛṣṇa-pakṣa caturthī**, and saluted on the
  bright second day (4.6, c. vv. 109–112). (Mudgala Purāṇa, Gajānana-carita, khaṇḍa 4, adhyāya 6, "the fault of
  seeing the moon on Bhādra śukla caturthī"; verse numbers approximate, from an OCR of the Sanskrit)
  - *Variant (the modaka fall):* gorged on *modakas*, the pot-bellied Gaṇeśa rides home on his mouse,
    which stumbles at a snake. His belly bursts, he ties the snake round it as a belt, and Candra
    laughs. Gaṇeśa breaks off his tusk and throws it, or simply curses him. The curse is softened so
    that the moon waxes and wanes, or so that it falls only on Bhādrapada śukla caturthī. [folk; popular
    *vrata* literature, locus not verified; often credited to the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, which was not checked]
  - *Continuation (the Syamantaka link):* later in the same chapter **Kṛṣṇa** saw the moon on Bhādrapada śukla
    caturthī and so was falsely accused over the **Syamantaka** jewel. **Nārada** tells him to worship
    Gaṇeśa with the *Saṃkaṣṭī* vow. Gaṇeśa then grants that whoever hears the Syamantaka story will be
    free of the fault of seeing the moon. (Mudgala Purāṇa 4.6, latter part) The older Syamantaka
    narratives (Bhāgavata 10.56–57; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13; Harivaṃśa 28–29) have no moon or Caturthī link.
    [late text] See [Syamantaka Jewel](../04-deep-dives/syamantaka-jewel.md).
- **The Caturthī vow in the Śiva Purāṇa** — Śiva tells Gaṇeśa that he was born on the fourth day of the
  dark half of Bhādra at moonrise ("dark half" is Shastri's rendering). He ordains a year-long caturthī
  *vrata* with *dūrvā* grass, a night fast, and worship of the **crescent moon** after Gaṇeśa's.
  (Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.18.35–60) The dark-half tithi fits the monthly *Saṃkaṣṭī* (kṛṣṇa) caturthī, as does
  the Mudgala's moon-worship in the kṛṣṇa-caturthī vow, and sits uneasily with the śukla date of the
  Bhādrapada festival. The tension is in the sources and is left
  unresolved here.

### Forms, sect & sacred sites

- **The eight avatāras of the Mudgala Purāṇa** — eight emanations of Gaṇeśa, each slaying a demon-vice:
  **Vakratuṇḍa** (vs Matsarāsura, envy), **Ekadanta** (Madāsura, pride), **Mahodara** (Mohāsura,
  delusion), **Gajānana** (Lobhāsura, greed), **Lambodara** (Krodhāsura, anger), **Vikaṭa** (Kāmāsura,
  lust), **Vighnarāja** (Mamāsura, possessiveness), **Dhūmravarṇa** (**Ahamāsura** or Ahaṃkārāsura,
  ego). (Mudgala Purāṇa; the Gajānana-carita is its fourth khaṇḍa) The Mudgala's own name for
  Dhūmravarṇa's demon is Ahamāsura (khaṇḍa 8, table of contents and colophons of 8.1–2).
  "Abhimānāsura" is the name used in some modern summaries.
  - *Variant (Gaṇeśa Purāṇa, Krīḍā-khaṇḍa):* a different set of four yuga-avatāras: **Mahotkaṭa
    Vināyaka**, **Mayūreśvara**, **Gajānana** and **Dhūmraketu**, riding a lion, a peacock, a mouse and
    a horse respectively. [locus not verified: secondary summaries]
  - These eight avatāras are *not to be confused with* the **Aṣṭavināyaka temple pilgrimage** of eight
    self-manifest (*svayambhū*) Gaṇeśa shrines around Pune, Maharashtra (Moreshwar/Morgaon,
    Siddhivinayak/Siddhatek, Ballaleshwar/Pali, Varadavinayak/Mahad, Chintamani/Theur,
    Girijatmaj/Lenyadri, Vighnahar/Ozar, Mahaganapati/Ranjangaon). [regional tradition] See
    [Aṣṭavināyaka](../06-sacred-geography/ashtavinayaka.md).
- **Epithets** — Vighneśvara/Vighnaharta (lord/remover of obstacles), Gaṇapati (lord of gaṇas), Lambodara
  (pot-bellied), Heramba, Vināyaka, Ekadanta, Gāṅgeya (Matsya 154.505), Dvaimātura, Siddhidātā.
  **Consorts:**
  - **Siddhi & Buddhi**, with sons Kṣema and Lābha. (Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.20.2–8)
  - **Riddhi & Siddhi**, the pair popular in northern and western lore. [regional tradition]
  - *Variant (Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, Gaṇapati-khaṇḍa, Sen tr. ch. 17):* Śiva marries him to **Puṣṭi**.
    In the Mudgala the gods give him their mind-born daughters (Sarasvatī from Brahmā, Puṣṭi from Viṣṇu,
    Yoginī from Śiva, Mohinī from Ambikā, Saṃjīvanī from Sūrya) as consorts. (Mudgala Purāṇa 4.6, vv.
    c. 36–37)
    The same Purāṇa has him refuse Tulasī's proposal; see
    [Tulasī's curse](../04-deep-dives/tulasi-ganesha-curse.md).
  - *Variant:* in much of the south Gaṇeśa is a celibate bachelor (*brahmacārī*). [regional tradition]
- **Mūṣika** the mouse/rat as his vāhana — symbol of desire/ego subdued, or of a transformed gandharva
  (Krauñca) cursed to mouse-form. (Gaṇeśa Purāṇa) [locus not verified: chapter not located]
- **The Gāṇapatya sect** — the stream that holds Gaṇeśa as the supreme Absolute (*para-brahman*). In the
  Smārta *pañcāyatana* worship Gaṇeśa is one of five deities (with Śiva, Viṣṇu, Devī and Sūrya), and
  the Gāṇapatya is counted among the six sects (*ṣaṇmata*). Its core texts are the **Gaṇeśa Purāṇa**,
  the **Mudgala Purāṇa** and the **Gaṇapati Atharvaśīrṣa**, a late "Upaniṣad" usually dated to the 16th
  or 17th century. [scholarly]

## Part II — Kārtikeya / Skanda / Murugan

The six-faced war-god, **Devasenāpati** (commander of the army of the gods), conceived by the devas for a
single purpose: to kill the otherwise-invincible **Tārakāsura**. Known as Skanda, Kumāra, Subrahmaṇya,
Guha, Mahāsena in the north; **Murugan / Cēyōṉ** — the Tamil *Tamiḻ-kaḍavuḷ* ("god of the Tamils") — in
the south. See the [Devāsura wars](devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md) for the cosmic-war framing and
[`tarakasura-vadha.md`](../04-deep-dives/tarakasura-vadha.md) for the full set of loci.

### The need for him & the conception

- **Tārakāsura's boon** — the asura Tāraka wins from Brahmā a boon that leaves one opening, and the texts
  word it differently:
  - he dies when a son of Śiva, as commander, attacks him (Śiva Purāṇa 2.3.15.39–41);
  - only a child of seven days can kill him (Matsya Purāṇa 148.20–23; Skanda Purāṇa Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa
    15.49–55);
  - no god, asura or rākṣasa can kill him, so the loophole is Agni's son (Mahābhārata 13.84.5–10).

  In the *Śiva Purāṇa* and Kālidāsa the plot turns on the ascetic, widowed (post-Satī) Śiva fathering
  no one. It is Brahmā, not Tāraka, who notes that Śiva is wifeless (Matsya 154.50; Śiva Purāṇa
  2.3.16.28–35).
  - *Variant (Tamil, Kanda Purāṇam):* the chief enemy is **Sūrapadman**, with his brothers Siṃhamukha
    and Tāraka. [regional; locus not verified: secondary summaries only]
- **Kāma's sacrifice & Pārvatī's penance** — to restart Śiva's householder life, the devas send **Kāma**
  to fire desire at the meditating Śiva; Śiva burns Kāma to ash with his third eye, but Pārvatī's long
  *tapas* eventually wins him. (Śiva Purāṇa 2.3.17–19, 2.3.22; Kumārasambhava 3.71–72, 5 — see
  [`shiva-myth-cycle.md`](shiva-myth-cycle.md))
- **The seed too potent to bear** — Śiva's seed (*tejas*) is so fiery that no one can hold it, and the
  texts relay it differently:
  - *Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.2.9–11, 25–67:* Agni, as a **dove**, swallows it (9–11). The gods, who share in what
    is offered to fire, grow pregnant with it and vomit it out at Śiva's command. The sages' wives,
    warming themselves at the fire, then conceive it. It passes through Himavat and **Gaṅgā** to a
    thicket of **śara reeds**.
  - *Mahābhārata 9.43.6–11:* Agni places it in **Gaṅgā**, who casts it onto a Himālaya peak, where the
    Kṛttikās find the child in a reed-clump (*śarastamba*).
  - *Rāmāyaṇa 1.36.14–22:* Gaṅgā releases the embryo on the earth, and the forest it falls on turns to
    gold, whence gold is called *jātarūpa*.
  - *Mahābhārata 13.84–86:* Gaṅgā drops it on Meru, and it is also said that the Kṛttikās carried it.
  - In other texts Agni is a pigeon (Skanda Purāṇa Kaumārikā 29) or a parrot (Matsya 158.28–36).

### The six-fold birth (*Ṣaṇmukha*)

- **The Kṛttikās nurse him** — the six **Kṛttikās** (the Pleiades) each claim the child. He grows **six
  faces** to drink from all six at once, whence **Ṣaṇmukha / Ṣaḍānana** and the name **Kārttikeya**
  ("son of the Kṛttikās"). (Mahābhārata 9.43.10–12; Rāmāyaṇa 1.36.23–28; Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.3.29–33) The
  Rāmāyaṇa derives **Skanda** from the "spilling" (*skanna*) of the embryo (1.36.27).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva 3.213–221):* Skanda is born of **Agni and Svāhā**. Svāhā takes
    the forms of six of the seven Ṛṣi-wives (she cannot copy Arundhatī) and drops the seed into a golden
    pot on the white mountain, and the child is born six-headed (3.214). The sages repudiate the six
    wives, who later go to heaven as the Kṛttikā constellation (3.215.5, 3.219.11). The text then
    reconciles this with Śiva: Rudra had entered Agni, so Skanda is also "Rudra's son" (3.218.27–30).
    The view that this is the oldest layer is modern. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (the Gaṅgā strand):* because Gaṅgā bore the seed he is **Gāṅgeya** (Mahābhārata 9.43.16).
    Because of the reeds he is **Śarajanman**, Tamil **Saravaṇabhava**.
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 158.36–159.5):* the seed Agni drank bursts out of the gods and forms a golden
    lake. Pārvatī drinks its water from the Kṛttikās' lotus-leaf, on condition that the son be theirs
    too (158.40–47). The six-faced boy then bursts from her right side and a second boy from her left,
    and Indra joins the two into one.
  - *Variant (Tamil Kanda Purāṇam):* Murugan springs from **six sparks of fire from Śiva's third eye**,
    carried to the Saravaṇa pool, where they become **six infants on six lotuses**, raised by the
    Kārttikai maidens. When Pārvatī embraces all six at once they fuse into one child with six faces and
    twelve arms. This Śaiva reading keeps Śiva as sole father. (Kanda Purāṇam, Kacciyappa Civācāriyar)
    [regional tradition; locus not verified]

### The slaying of the demon & generalship

- **Made general of the gods** — even as a boy Skanda is anointed **Devasenāpati**, commander of the
  celestial army:
  - In the Vana Parva the sages and Indra offer him **Indra's own sovereignty**. He refuses ("I am your
    servant, Śakra"), and Indra consecrates him general (Mahābhārata 3.218.6–16, 3.218.42).
  - In the Śalya Parva Brahmā grants the generalship and the gods consecrate him on the Sarasvatī
    (Mahābhārata 9.43.48–52, 9.44).
  - His weapon is the **śakti** (spear), Agni's gift (Mahābhārata 9.45.73 *agnidattayā*; Śiva Purāṇa
    2.4.5.45, where Śiva gives a spear as well, 2.4.5.46).
  - *Variant (Tamil):* the spear is the **Vel**, given by Pārvatī as the embodiment of her Śakti.
    [regional; locus not verified: temple tradition]
- **The killing of Tārakāsura** — Skanda leads the devas and slays Tāraka with his śakti, fulfilling his
  birth-purpose and ending the asura's terror of the three worlds. (Mahābhārata 9.45.60–64, 13.86.28;
  Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.10.28–30; Matsya Purāṇa 160.22–25) In the Śalya Parva he also kills Mahiṣa
  (9.45.65), and he splits the Krauñca mountain, where a fleeing foe had taken refuge, with Agni's
  śakti (9.45.72–81).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva 3.221.52–66):* his great foe there is the dānava **Mahiṣa**, whom
    he kills with his śakti.
  - *Variant (Tamil — Sūrapadman):* the great enemy is **Sūrapadman** of Vīramakēntiram. After the
    battle (traditionally at Tiruchendur) Sūrapadman hides in the sea as a **mango tree**, and Murugan
    splits it with the Vel. The halves become his **peacock (mayil)** vāhana and his **rooster
    (cēval)** banner-emblem, so the demon serves the god he fought. (Kanda Purāṇam) [regional
    tradition]
- **Vāhana & weapons** — the **peacock** as mount and the **cock** on his flag. In the Sanskrit epic
  **Garuḍa** gives him the peacock and **Aruṇa** the cock (Mahābhārata 9.45.46). In the Vana Parva Agni
  gives him the cock banner (3.218.32), and the newborn himself seizes a cock (3.214.23–24). In the
  *Matsya* Tvaṣṭṛ gives the cock as a plaything, and the gods' hymn hails him as having a
  shining peacock for his mount (*mayūrojjvalavāhana*) (Matsya 159.10, 159.16). His primary
  weapon is the śakti/Vel. Other names are **Subrahmaṇya, Kumāra, Guha** (the hidden one) and
  **Mahāsena**. The peacock's name **Paravāṇi** is [locus not verified].

### Marriages

- **Devasenā (Deivānai)** — the "army-bride":
  - In the *Mahābhārata* she is a daughter of **Prajāpati** whom Indra rescues from the demon Keśin
    (3.213.16). Indra then gives her to Skanda in a Vedic wedding with Bṛhaspati officiating
    (3.218.42–46), and she is identified with **Ṣaṣṭhī** (3.218.47).
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 159.8):* after Skanda's consecration, Indra (Śakra) gives him his own daughter,
    known as Devasenā, as his wife (*sutām asmai dadau śakro devaseneti viśrutām*).
  - In the *Brahmavaivarta* Brahmā gives Devasenā, "whom the savants called Ṣaṣṭhī", to Kārtikeya
    (Gaṇapati-khaṇḍa, Sen tr. ch. 17).
  - *Variant (Tamil):* Deivānai is **Indra's (adopted) daughter**, wedded at **Tiruparaṅkuṉṟam** after
    the demon-war. (Kanda Purāṇam) [regional tradition]
- **Vaḷḷi** — the "love-bride", foster-daughter of **Nampi** (Nambirājan), a chieftain of the Kuṟava
  hill-hunters. Murugan woos her in disguise, aided by Gaṇeśa, who appears as a wild elephant to
  frighten her into Murugan's arms. Devasenā and Vaḷḷi become co-wives. In the Tamil telling both are
  daughters of Viṣṇu reborn: **Amṛtavaḷḷi** as Devasenā and **Sundaravaḷḷi** as Vaḷḷi. (Kanda
  Purāṇam; Tamil tradition) [regional tradition] In the Sri Lankan Kataragama tradition the courtship
  happens among the **Vedda** people. [regional]

### The Tamil Murugan & the six abodes

- **Āṟupaṭai Vīṭu (the six war-camps/abodes)** — the six canonical Murugan temples of Tamil Nadu, each
  tied to an episode. The *Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai* names six resorts, and the later tradition identifies
  them with modern temples. The identification of Tiruvērakam with Swāmimalai and of
  "Kuṉṟutōṟāṭal" (every hill) with Tiruttaṇi is disputed. [disputed] The six:
  - **Tiruparaṅkuṉṟam** (Madurai) — marriage to Devasenā after the demon-war.
  - **Tiruchendur** (Tiruccīralaivāy, on the sea-shore) — the battle against the asuras.
  - **Palani / Paḻani** (Tiruvāviṉaṉkuṭi) — the renunciant boy, **Daṇḍāyudhapāṇi** ("holding the staff
    as weapon"), after the fruit-race sulk.
  - **Swāmimalai** — boy-Skanda teaches the meaning of **Praṇava / Oṃ** to his father Śiva and becomes
    *Swāminātha*, "teacher of the lord".
  - **Tiruttaṇi** — anger calmed after the war, and wedding Vaḷḷi.
  - **Paḻamutircōlai** — the forest hill, abundance and Vaḷḷi associations.

  (Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai, Nakkīrar; Kanda Purāṇam; temple tradition) [regional tradition] See
  [Arupadai Veedu](../06-sacred-geography/arupadai-veedu-murugan.md).
- **Praṇava-teaching (Swāmimalai)** — the child detains even Brahmā for failing to explain Oṃ, imprisons
  him, briefly takes over creation, and then expounds the syllable to Śiva himself — the *guru* even of
  his father. (Kanda Purāṇam; Swāmimalai temple tradition) [regional tradition; locus not verified,
  including any Sanskrit Skanda Purāṇa parallel]

### The estrangement & the celibate–married divide

- **The sulk that sent him away** — losing the race to Gaṇeśa (Part I), the proud Kārtikeya abandons
  Kailāsa:
  - *Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.20.23–27:* he goes to the Krauñca mountain.
  - *Tamil tradition:* he goes to Palani.

  The myth is read as a charter for why Skanda-worship faded in the north (where he remains chiefly
  **Subrahmaṇya**) but flourishes as **Murugan** in the south and among the Tamil diaspora. [scholarly
  interpretation]
- *Variant (celibate vs married):* the texts themselves disagree on whether he married.
  - *Celibate:* the *Śiva Purāṇa* makes him a lifelong bachelor after the race (2.4.20.27), and in some
    northern shrines women traditionally avoid his image. [regional]
  - *Married:* the *Mahābhārata* (3.218.42–47), the *Matsya* (159.8) and the *Brahmavaivarta* marry him
    to Devasenā, and the
    Tamil Murugan is the husband of Deivānai and Vaḷḷi.
