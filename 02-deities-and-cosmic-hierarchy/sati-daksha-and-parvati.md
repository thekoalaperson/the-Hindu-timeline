# Sati, the Daksha-Yajna & Parvati — The Goddess Reborn

> **Where this sits:** A myth-cycle of the Shaiva-Shakta corpus, set in [Brahma's current life](../01-manvantaras/README.md) in primordial / pre-historical cosmic time (no Kali-Yuga date — these events belong to the deva-age of cosmogony; the Vayu even spreads them across two manvantaras and two births of Daksha, see §2). The Goddess dies as **Sati**, daughter of Daksha, and returns as **Parvati**, daughter of Himavan, to re-marry Shiva. Parent: [Deities & Cosmic Hierarchy](README.md). See also [Trimurti & Tridevi](trimurti-and-tridevi.md), [Devi Mahatmya & the Great Goddess](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md), and [Sacred Geography → 51 Shakti Peethas](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md).

This file expands what the master index compresses into a single §4 marriage-cycle line and a §7 pitha footnote. It is the bridge-narrative explaining **why the Goddess has two births**, **how the 51/52/108 Shakti Pithas came to be**, and **how Shiva was drawn back from grief into householder life** — the prelude to the birth of [Skanda/Kartikeya and Ganesha](ganesha-and-kartikeya.md).

---

## 1. The First Birth: Sati, Daughter of Daksha

- **Sati's birth as Daksha's daughter** - At Brahma's urging Daksha (son of Brahma) propitiates the Great Goddess (Shivaa, the feminine of Shiva, also hymned as Durga), who agrees to be born of his wife as his daughter and to become Shiva's consort; she is born to **Virini (Asikni)**. (Shiva Purana 2.2.1.17, 2.2.12.21–31, 2.2.14 — Rudra-samhita, Sati-khanda)
  - *The warning (Shiva Purana 2.2.12.34):* granting the boon, the Goddess tells Daksha that if he ever grows disrespectful to her she will cast off her body, withdraw into herself or take another form.
  - *Variant (Devi-Bhagavata VII.30):* Daksha and the sages perform long tapas to the Bhuvaneshvari form of the supreme Devi; Daksha asks her to be born in his family, and the Shakti of Shiva is born in his house; the sages name her **Sati** because she is "of the nature of Para-Brahman and Truth". No warning is recorded here.
  - *Variant (Bhagavata Purana IV):* Sati's mother is **Prasuti**, Daksha's wife, and Sati is one of his daughters. (Bhagavata 4.5.9)
- **Sati's tapas for Shiva** - As a maiden, with her mother Virini's leave, Sati keeps the **Nanda-vrata** (worshipping Shiva on the Nanda tithis, beginning in Ashvina); Shiva appears, grants her the boon of himself as husband, and sends Brahma to ask Daksha for her. (Shiva Purana 2.2.15.12–28, 2.2.17.9–41)
- **The marriage** - In the Shiva Purana Daksha gives Sati to Shiva gladly, at an auspicious hour, with Brahma officiating; the rivalry comes only later. (Shiva Purana 2.2.18.10–13, 2.2.18.33–37)
  - *Variant (Bhagavata Purana IV):* Daksha says he never wanted the match and gave his daughter only at Brahma's request, as one might teach the Veda to a shudra. (Bhagavata 4.2.13, 4.2.16) The Shiva Purana gives Daksha the same complaint, but only in hindsight, at the yajna. (Shiva Purana 2.2.29.33)
- **The rupture at the assembly** - At an earlier great sacrifice of the world-creators, everyone rises when Daksha enters except **Brahma and Shiva**. Daksha reviles Shiva and curses him to have no share of sacrifice alongside the gods. **Nandi** counter-curses Daksha (who will soon have the face of a **goat**) and the Brahmins who tolerated the insult, and **Bhrigu** then curses Shiva's followers in turn. (Bhagavata 4.2.4–32; goat face 4.2.23)
  - *Variant (Shiva Purana 2.2.26):* the assembly is at **Prayaga**, and Shiva has come with Sati. Bhrigu and others only reproach Shiva; it is **Daksha** himself who curses Shiva's attendants to be outside Vedic rites. Nandi's curse falls on the Brahmins, on Brahma and on Daksha, who will become **goat-faced**, and Shiva then calms Nandi. (Shiva Purana 2.2.26.1–50; Daksha's curse on the Ganas 2.2.26.25–27; goat face 2.2.26.38–39)
  - *Variant (Vayu Purana 1.30; Devi-Bhagavata VII.30):* the cause of the enmity is different. In the Vayu, Daksha invites his eight daughters home but not **Sati, the eldest**, out of hatred for Shiva, who never bowed to him, and he says the sage-husbands of his younger daughters deserve more honour. In the Devi-Bhagavata, Durvasa gives Daksha a fragrant **garland** that was the Devi's own prasada. Daksha puts it on his marriage-bed and, roused by its scent, has intercourse; that "animal action" turns his mind against Shiva and Sati. (Vayu 1.30.40–50, Tagare; Devi-Bhagavata VII.30)

## 2. The Daksha-Yajna and the Insult

- **The great sacrifice** - Daksha holds a colossal **yajna** and invites the gods, sages and Prajapatis — **deliberately omitting Shiva** (as a *kapalin*, a skull-bearer) **and Sati**. The texts name the rite and place differently. The Bhagavata has a **Brihaspati-sava**, after a Vajapeya. The Shiva Purana sets it at **Kanakhala**, the Linga at Kanakhala near Gangadvara. The Mahabharata and the Vayu call it a **horse-sacrifice** (*hayamedha* / *ashvamedha*) at **Gangadvara**. (Bhagavata 4.3.3; Shiva Purana 2.2.27.12, 2.2.27.22–23; Linga 1.100.7; MBh 12.274.20–23, crit. ed.; Vayu 1.30.79, 1.30.94–96)
  - *Dadhichi's protest (Vayu 1.30.103–107; MBh 12.274 App. I no. 28; Shiva Purana 2.2.27.25–48):* the sage Dadhichi rebukes Daksha for leaving out Shiva. In the Vayu and the Mahabharata appendix Daksha replies that he knows no great god besides the eleven Rudras already present, and offers everything to Vishnu. (Vayu 1.30.105–107; MBh 12.274 App. I no. 28, lines 39–48) In the Shiva Purana he replies instead that Vishnu, Brahma and Indra are already present, and that Shiva, a lord of ghosts of no noble birth, was given Sati only at Brahma's urging; Dadhichi foretells the sacrifice's ruin and walks out. (Shiva Purana 2.2.27.37–48)
- **Sati resolves to attend** - In the Bhagavata, Sati sees the gods' wives streaming to her father's house in their aerial cars and begs Shiva to go. Shiva warns that an uninvited visit to one who finds fault with you brings dishonour. She leaves him anyway, in tears and anger, and his Ganas follow with Nandi. (Bhagavata 4.3.8–25, 4.4.1–5)
  - *Variant (Shiva Purana 2.2.28):* Sati learns of the yajna by seeing **Chandra going there with Rohini**. Shiva first refuses to go uninvited, then gives her his permission, and she rides off on Nandi. (Shiva Purana 2.2.28.3–8, 2.2.28.26–36)
  - *Variant (Mahabhagavata Purana; Brihaddharma Purana):* when Shiva forbids her, Sati takes ten terrifying forms, the **Dasha Mahavidyas**, which block him in every direction until he consents. [late text] (chapter locus not verified; see [Dasha Mahavidya](dasha-mahavidya.md))
- **The reception** - At the yajna no one greets Sati except her mother and sisters. Daksha ignores her, and she sees that **no share has been set aside for Shiva**. Sati then denounces her father before the assembly; in the Shiva Purana she also taunts Vishnu, Brahma, Indra and the sages. (Bhagavata 4.4.7–23; Shiva Purana 2.2.29.4–26)
- **The public humiliation** - In the Shiva Purana Daksha answers Sati directly and reviles Shiva to her face: "inauspicious", of no noble lineage, king of goblins, ghosts and spirits, cut off from Vedic rites. (Shiva Purana 2.2.29.30–34) The Bhagavata's catalogue of insults belongs to the earlier assembly (§1): Shiva haunts cremation grounds with ghosts, goes naked, laughs and weeps like a madman, smears himself with pyre-ash and wears a garland of skulls and bones. (Bhagavata 4.2.10–16)
- **Sati's self-immolation** - Unable to bear her husband's defamation, Sati renounces the body Daksha gave her. She sits facing north, sips water, closes her eyes, raises her vital airs by yoga, and burns herself up with **yogic fire** from within. (Bhagavata 4.4.24–27; Shiva Purana 2.2.30.2–8; Vayu 1.30.54–55 — *agneyi dharana*; Matsya 13.16; Devi-Bhagavata VII.30; Linga 1.99, "by the path of yoga"; Kumarasambhava 1.21, *yoga-visrishta-deha*)
  - *Variant (popular tradition):* Sati **leaps into the sacrificial fire-pit**. No primary text checked here says so. In the Shiva Purana, Sati only *says* she will "cast off my body and enter the fire" (2.2.29.39) before dying by yoga (2.2.30.8). The leap appears in the wisdomlib translator's footnote to 2.2.1, which is a translator's gloss.
  - *In the Shiva Purana,* about twenty thousand of Sati's attendants kill themselves with their own weapons. Bhrigu's Yajurveda oblations raise the **Ribhus**, who drive off the rest. (Shiva Purana 2.2.30.15–26; Bhagavata 4.4.31–34 has the Ribhus too)
  - *Etymology note:* this episode is the mythic root later (mis)appropriated by the custom of widow self-immolation called *sati/suttee* — though the goddess Sati was no widow and the act is a protest, not a funerary rite. [scholarly]
- **The two-manvantara frame (Vayu Purana 1.30; Matsya Purana 13)** - Here Sati's death and the destruction of the yajna are separate events.
  - In the Vayu, the dying Sati vows to be Shiva's wife in every rebirth. Shiva curses Daksha to be reborn in the Chakshusha manvantara as the son of the Prachetases, and Daksha curses Shiva to be worshipped apart from the gods. The yajna that is destroyed is the **later horse-sacrifice of Daksha son of Prachetas**, in the Vaivasvata manvantara. By then Sati has already been reborn as **Uma/Parvati**, and it is she, living, whose distress moves Shiva. (Vayu 1.30.53–80, 1.30.110–121)
  - In the Matsya, before she burns, Sati herself foretells that Daksha will be the only son of ten fathers and that Rudra will wreck his ashvamedha. The chapter closes by saying that in time the Svayambhuva Daksha became Daksha Prachetasa, and the Goddess became Parvati. (Matsya 13.15–16, 13.59)
  - *Variant (Mahabharata 12.274, crit. ed.):* there is no Sati death at all. **Uma, daughter of the mountain-king**, asks Shiva why he does not go to Daksha's horse-sacrifice at Gangadvara, and is pained to learn he has no share. (MBh 12.274.18–29)

## 3. Shiva's Wrath: Virabhadra, Bhadrakali & the Destruction of the Yajna

- **Shiva's grief turns to fury** - Hearing from Narada of Sati's death, Shiva tears out a lock of his matted hair (*jata*) and dashes it on the ground (on the mountaintop, in the Shiva Purana). (Bhagavata 4.5.1–2; Shiva Purana 2.2.32.15–21) In the Shiva Purana the fleeing Ganas bring the news first, and Shiva then summons Narada for the details. (Shiva Purana 2.2.32.4–18)
- **Birth of Virabhadra** - From the lock arises **Virabhadra**, Shiva's wrath in person. In the Bhagavata he is black, sky-high, bright as three suns, thousand-armed and garlanded with men's heads. The Shiva Purana gives him "two thousand" hands (translator's rendering, Motilal tr.). (Bhagavata 4.5.3–5; Shiva Purana 2.2.32.22–23)
  - *Variant (Vayu 1.30.122–138; MBh 12.274 App. I no. 28):* Shiva creates Virabhadra **from his mouth** to soothe the Goddess's anger. The Vayu gives him a thousand heads, eyes and feet. [interpolation] — in the Mahabharata the Virabhadra passage is excluded from the critical text.
  - *Variant (Mahabharata 12.274, crit. ed.):* there is no Virabhadra. Shiva himself, by yoga and with his fearsome followers, wrecks the sacrifice and chases it with his bow. A drop of sweat from his brow becomes a dwarfish red-eyed being, **Jvara (Fever)**, which burns the yajna. Brahma then promises Shiva his share, and Shiva distributes Jvara among all creatures. (MBh 12.274.31–49)
- **Birth of Bhadrakali / Mahakali** - From the other half of the hair-lock arises **Mahakali**, surrounded by crores of goblins; in the fighting she splits the gods and drinks their blood. (Shiva Purana 2.2.32.25, 2.2.37.18) The Devi-Bhagavata has Virabhadra appear "with hosts of **Bhadrakalis**". (Devi-Bhagavata VII.30)
  - *Variant (Vayu 1.30.139–141, 1.30.164; MBh 12.274 App. I no. 28):* **Bhadrakali springs from the fury of the Goddess (Uma/Parvati)**, not from Shiva. In these texts the Goddess is alive and present: "Virabhadra came out of Rudra's anger, Bhadrakali out of the Devi's anger".
- **The army of Ganas destroys the sacrifice** - Virabhadra and Shiva's hordes uproot the sacrificial posts, smash the vessels, defile the fires and rout the gods and priests. (Bhagavata 4.5.13–18; Shiva Purana 2.2.37; Vayu 1.30.142–154; Linga 1.100.11–15)
- **Vishnu opposes Virabhadra** - In the Shaiva texts Vishnu defends the sacrifice and is beaten. In the Shiva Purana Virabhadra fells Vishnu with his trident, freezes his raised discus in his hand and splits his bow into three, and Vishnu withdraws. (Shiva Purana 2.2.36, 2.2.37.31–42) The Linga has the same blows, the frozen discus and the bow broken in three, and adds that Virabhadra severs Vishnu's head; the head is restored afterwards. (Linga 1.100.23–32, 1.100.44) In the Bhagavata, Brahma and Vishnu foresee the disaster and stay away. (Bhagavata 4.6.3)
- **The mutilation of the gods** - The Ganas maim the deities and priests:
  - In the Bhagavata, Virabhadra tears off **Bhrigu**'s moustache, gouges out **Bhaga**'s eyes (Bhaga had moved his eyebrows during Bhrigu's cursing) and knocks out the teeth of **Pushan** and **Daksha** (both had bared them, Daksha in cursing, Pushan in smiling). (Bhagavata 4.5.17–21)
  - In the Shiva Purana, Manibhadra plucks Bhrigu's moustache, Chanda takes Pushan's teeth, Nandin Bhaga's eyes (Bhaga had winked at Daksha while he cursed), and Virabhadra cuts off the nose-tips of **Sarasvati** and **Aditi**. (Shiva Purana 2.2.37.49–55)
  - The Linga has the most detail. Virabhadra freezes **Indra**'s raised hand and later beheads him, grinds **Chandra** under his big toe, cuts off **Agni**'s two hands and tears out his tongue, breaks **Yama**'s staff, and also takes Bhaga's eyes, Pushan's teeth and the nose-tips of Sarasvati and Aditi. (Linga 1.100.15–20, 1.100.38; GRETIL verse count)
  - The Vayu does not narrate the maimings; Bhaga's eyes and Pushan's teeth appear only as epithets in Daksha's hymn. (Vayu 1.30.178, 1.30.253)
  - *Variant (Mahabharata 10.18, Sauptika Parva):* this is the oldest form, with no Daksha, Sati or Virabhadra. The gods hold a sacrifice without a share for Rudra. He pierces it with an arrow and it flees to the sky as a **deer**. With the tip of his bow he breaks **Savitri's arms**, **Bhaga's eyes** and **Pushan's teeth**, and he restores them once pacified. (MBh 10.18.1–23) [scholarly: Sircar traces the Bhaga and Pushan motifs to the Brahmanas' Prajapati-sacrifice legends]
- **Daksha beheaded** - Daksha's head resists Virabhadra's weapons and mantras, so Virabhadra beheads him with the yajna's own animal-slaughtering device and throws the head into the southern fire as an oblation. (Bhagavata 4.5.22–26) In the Shiva Purana, Virabhadra drags Daksha from behind the altar, wrenches off the head when the sword cannot cut it, and throws it into the fire-pit. (Shiva Purana 2.2.37.58–61; Linga 1.100.37)
- **The Sacrifice flees as a deer** - The personified **Yajna** takes the form of a deer and flees skyward; Virabhadra catches and beheads it. (Shiva Purana 2.2.37.45–46; Linga 1.100.34–35; Vayu 1.30.155–157) In the Mahabharata's critical text Shiva himself chases the deer with his bow (MBh 12.274.34–35); in its appendix Bhadrakali cuts off the Sacrifice's head (MBh 12.274 App. I no. 28, lines 109–111).
  - *Variant (Vayu 1.30.156–179; MBh 12.274 App. I no. 28):* **Daksha is not beheaded.** He is kicked on the head and faints. Then, on Virabhadra's advice, he takes refuge with Shiva, who rises out of the fire-pit; Daksha asks that his sacrifice not be wasted and praises Shiva with **1,008 names**. (Tagare's verses 1.30.156–157 read as if Daksha too fled as the deer and was beheaded; Tagare's own note at 157 flags that this contradicts what follows.)

## 4. Restoration: Daksha and the Goat's Head

- **The gods supplicate Shiva** - Brahma tells the gods that their offence lay in excluding Shiva, then leads them, the Pitris and the Prajapatis to **Kailasa** to beg Shiva to relent. (Bhagavata 4.6.4–8, 4.7.1) In the Shiva Purana, the gods go first to Vishnu and then with him to Kailasa. (Shiva Purana 2.2.40.8–22)
- **Daksha revived with a goat's head** - Shiva, pacified, rules on the injuries:
  - Daksha's own head has been burnt to ashes, so he is to have the head of a goat (the sacrificial animal), and Bhrigu the goat's beard. Bhaga will see his share through Mitra's eyes. Pushan will chew through his disciples' teeth or, when alone, eat chickpea-flour dough. Gods who lost arms will use the Ashvins' arms. (Bhagavata 4.7.3–5) The Shiva Purana gives the same goat's head and goat-beard; Bhaga takes his share together with the sun, and Pushan simply stays broken-toothed. (Shiva Purana 2.2.42.7–10, 2.2.42.21–27; Devi-Bhagavata VII.30 for the goat's head)
  - Revived, Daksha praises Shiva, completes the yajna and now gives Shiva his share. In the Bhagavata, Vishnu appears on Garuda as soon as Daksha offers the first oblation of the resumed rite, and teaches the non-difference of Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva. (Bhagavata 4.7.8–19, 4.7.50–56; Shiva Purana 2.2.42–43)
  - *Variant (Linga 1.100.44–49):* Shiva restores every slain god, Indra's and Vishnu's heads included. He fashions a new face for Daksha's "ruined face" from a head, but no goat is specified, and he makes Daksha a lord of the Ganas (*ganapatya*).
- **The doctrine reconciled** - The episode is read as resolving the **Shaiva vs. orthodox-Vedic sacrifice** tension: Shiva must be included among those who receive sacrificial offerings. (Bhagavata 4.6–7; MBh 12.274.43–48) [scholarly]

## 5. The Cosmic Dance of Grief & the Genesis of the Shakti Pithas

This is the narrative origin of the **Shakti Pithas** (Shakta seats), the network of goddess shrines marking where parts of Sati's body fell. Sircar dates this dismemberment layer to roughly the early medieval period and later [scholarly]. Cross-linked in detail at [51 Shakti Peethas](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md) and [Kamakhya](../06-sacred-geography/kamakhya-nilachala-kamarupa.md).

- **Shiva carries the corpse** - After the yajna is restored, Shiva sees Sati's body in the fire, cries "O my Sati!", puts the body on his neck and wanders through many countries "like a madman". (Devi-Bhagavata VII.30) In the Kalika, Shiva first weeps over and caresses the body. **Shani** catches his world-burning tears, which become the river **Vaitarani**. Shiva then takes the body on his shoulder and rushes off eastward. (Kalika Purana 18.1–37, Shastri ed.) The worlds-shaking **Tandava** with the corpse is the popular telling; the word is not in the Devi-Bhagavata or Kalika passages checked here.
  - *Variant (Shiva Purana 2.2.1.41–43):* the Shiva Purana's Sati-khanda has no corpse-carrying. Instead, **a flame from Sati's body** falls on a mountain and is worshipped there as **Jvalamukhi**.
- **Vishnu dismembers Sati** - To free Shiva from his frenzy, the anxious gods turn to **Vishnu**, who cuts the corpse to pieces **with his arrows**; wherever the parts fall, Shiva remains in various forms. (Devi-Bhagavata VII.30)
  - *Variant (Kalika Purana 18.38–54):* **Brahma, Vishnu and Shani (Shanaishchara)** enter the body invisibly by Yogamaya, cut it to pieces from within and let the parts fall. The **feet** fall at **Devikuta**, the **thighs** at **Uddiyana**, and the **yoni** at **Kamagiri (Kamakhya)** in Kamarupa; the **breasts** at **Jalandhara**, the **arms and neck** at **Purnagiri**, and the **head** beyond Kamarupa. The Goddess is also seated as **Dikkaravasini** at the eastern end of Kamarupa (Sircar places the navel there). Wherever a part lies, Shiva stays as a **linga**. (Kalika Purana 18.39–51, Shastri tr.)
  - *Variant (popular iconography and retellings):* Vishnu throws his **Sudarshana Chakra** at the corpse. Sircar records "arrows or discus" in later versions; a textual locus for the discus was not verified here.
  - *Variant (Matsya Purana 13.17–58):* there is no dismemberment. Before burning herself, Sati answers Daksha's question about where she may be worshipped by naming **108 places** with a name of the Goddess for each (Vishalakshi at Varanasi, Lingadharini at Naimisha, Lalita at Prayaga …). These are seats of her presence, not sites where limbs fell.
- **Each landing-spot becomes a Pitha** - Wherever a body-part or ornament fell, the spot became a **Shakti Pitha**, the Goddess enshrined there in a regional form, each paired with a form of Shiva as **Bhairava**. (Pithanirnaya / Mahapithanirupana — [late text], c. 1690–1720 per Sircar) The Devi-Bhagavata calls its 108 places *pithas*; of the fallen parts it names only Gauri's **face**, at Kashi (Vishalakshi), and it names no Bhairavas. (Devi-Bhagavata VII.30) Sircar also cites a Brahmavaivarta passage on siddha-pithas linked to Sati's limbs (4.43.25, via Sircar; not read here), which he dates no earlier than the 14th–15th century. [interpolation]

### Variant counts of the Shakti Pithas (catalog ALL)

- **4 Pithas (Tantric "four seats")** - **Uddiyana (Odra)**, **Jalandhara (Jalashaila)**, **Purnagiri** and **Kamarupa**. (Hevajra Tantra; Kalika Purana 64.43–45, via Sircar) [scholarly]
- **4 "Adi Shakti Pithas"** [regional] - **Kamakhya** (yoni, Assam), **Vimala** at Puri (feet, Odisha), **Tara Tarini** (breasts, Odisha) and **Kalighat / Dakshina Kalika** (right toes or face, Bengal). (Odisha–Bengal temple tradition; no Puranic locus found. Devi-Bhagavata VII.30 lists 108 seats and has no such tetrad, and Sircar notes that Kalighat is rarely named before c. 1690.)
- **7 Pithas** - Devikuta, Uddiyana, Kamagiri, Jalandhara, Purnagiri, the eastern end of Kamarupa (Dikkaravasini), and the head-site. (Kalika Purana 18.42–51)
- **10 Pithas** - the "principal" ten, including the four above plus Varanasi, Mayavati (Haridwar), Madhupuri (Mathura), Ayodhya, Kanchi and others. (Rudrayamala, via Sircar)
- **18 Pithas** - the *Ashtadasha Shakti Pitha* list. The stotra is popularly attributed to **Adi Shankara** [disputed]; Sircar notes that the manuscript is ascribed to "Shankaracharya", probably the 17th-century Bengali Shankara Agamacharya. A different list of eighteen (Uddiyana, Devikotta, Hingula, Jalandhara, Varanasi, Prayaga … Radha, Gauda) is a Rudrayamala passage quoted in the **Kularnava Tantra**. (via Sircar, citing an R.A.S.B. manuscript catalogue; the Kularnava text itself not read)
- **42 Pithas** - siddha-pithas from Mayavati to Vaidyanatha. (Kubjika Tantra, patala VII, via Sircar)
- **50 Pithas** - (Jnanarnava Tantra, via Sircar)
- **51 Pithas** - the most widely cited number, often matched to the 51 letters of the Sanskrit alphabet, with each Pitha paired with a Bhairava. (Pithanirnaya / Mahapithanirupana [late text]; Tantrasara of Krishnananda, 17th c.; Mahabhagavata Purana, locus not verified) [scholarly: Sircar argues the 51 arose when the Tantrasara split one of the Jnanarnava's 50, Merugiri, into two]
- **52 Pithas** - Bengali tradition (a transmission variant of the 51 list). (regional)
- **64 Pithas** - (Brahmanda Purana; later tantric traditions) — locus not verified: a search of the GRETIL Brahmanda found no 64-pitha list; its Lalitopakhyana instead calls the bindu-pitha the embodiment of **50** pithas. (Brahmanda 3.37.47, GRETIL numbering)
- **108 Pithas** - the maximal count, sometimes said to echo the 108-bead mala. (Matsya Purana 13.26–53; Devi-Bhagavata VII.30; the same list recurs in Skanda (Revakhanda) and Padma (Srishti 17), per Sircar)

## 6. The Second Birth: Parvati, Daughter of Himavan

- **The Goddess reborn in the mountains** - Sati, who gave up her body by yoga after her father's insult, takes birth as the daughter of **Himavan** (the personified Himalaya) and queen **Mena**. She is called **Parvati** ("she of the mountain") from her lineage, and later **Uma** because her mother cried "u ma" ("oh, don't!") to stop her tapas. (Kumarasambhava 1.21–26; Shiva Purana 2.3.6, Parvati-khanda; Bhagavata 4.7.58–59; Devi-Bhagavata VII.31; Vayu 1.30.70–71; Linga 1.100–101)
  - *Variant (Devi-Bhagavata VII.31):* the supreme Devi tells the gods that "my Shakti" will be born as **Gauri** in Himavan's house to bear the son who will kill Taraka.
- **The cosmic motive: the demon Taraka** - Brahma's boon has made the asura **Taraka** unconquerable. Brahma tells the gods that only one born of **Nilalohita's (Shiva's) seed** can face him, so Shiva must be drawn to Uma out of his post-Sati austerity. (Kumarasambhava 2.1, 2.55–61; Devi-Bhagavata VII.31; Shiva Purana 2.3.14–16; see [Taraka's slaying](../04-deep-dives/tarakasura-vadha.md))
- **Sage Narada's prophecy** - Seeing the girl beside her father, Narada foretells that she will be Shiva's only wife and take half his body; so Himavan waits, since he cannot offer her unasked. (Kumarasambhava 1.50–52; Shiva Purana 2.3.8)
- **Parvati serves the meditating Shiva** - Shiva does tapas on a Himalayan peak, and Himavan sends Parvati with her companions to tend him. Shiva allows her to serve, though she endangers his concentration. (Kumarasambhava 1.54–60; Shiva Purana 2.3.11–13)

## 7. Kama's Burning & Parvati's Tapas

- **The gods deploy Kamadeva** - **Indra** summons **Kamadeva** (see [Kamadeva](kamadeva-and-minor-deities.md)), who goes to Shiva's hermitage with **Rati** and his friend **Madhu/Vasanta** (spring). As Parvati offers Shiva a rosary of lotus-seeds, Kama fits his *Sammohana* arrow to his flower-bow. (Kumarasambhava 2.63–3.24, 3.65–66; Shiva Purana 2.3.17–18)
- **Kama burned to ash** - Shiva's composure slips a little and his eyes go to Uma's lips. Then he masters himself, looks round for the cause, and sees Kama poised to shoot. Fire leaps from his **third eye** and leaves Kama ash, hence Kama's epithet **Ananga** ("the bodiless"). (Kumarasambhava 3.67–72; Shiva Purana 2.3.19)
  - *What follows (Kumarasambhava 4):* Rati wants to die on Kama's pyre, but a voice from the sky promises that once Shiva marries Parvati he will restore Kama **to his own body**. (Kumarasambhava 4.39–44)
  - *Also (Shiva Purana 2.3.51):* at the wedding festivities Rati pleads with Shiva, and his glance raises Kama from the ashes in his former body, with his bow and arrows. (Shiva Purana 2.3.51.1–15)
  - *Variant (Bhagavata 10.55.1–2):* Kama, burnt by Rudra's anger, merges into Vasudeva and is reborn from Krishna and Rukmini as **Pradyumna**. (Harivamsha also has the Pradyumna birth; locus not verified)
- **Parvati's severe penance (tapasya)** - Resolving to win Shiva by merit, not beauty, Parvati gives up palace comforts for ferocious austerities: fasting, exposure, and finally refusing even fallen leaves. The ancients therefore call her **Aparna** ("not even a leaf"). (Kumarasambhava 5.1–29, esp. 5.28; Shiva Purana 2.3.22–23)
  - *Variant (Matsya Purana 13.8):* **Uma, Ekaparna and Aparna** are three separate ascetic daughters of Mena. Uma is given to Rudra, Ekaparna to Asita, and Aparna to Jaigishavya.
- **The Brahmin-test** - A matted-haired young ascetic (*jatila*, "the first ashrama embodied", i.e. a *brahmachari*) comes to Parvati's grove and disparages Shiva to test her. She defends Shiva fiercely and turns to leave, whereupon he takes his own form and declares himself her slave, "bought by your tapas". (Kumarasambhava 5.30–86; Shiva Purana 2.3.26–28)
  - *Also (Shiva Purana 2.3.25):* before Shiva's own visit, the **Seven Sages** test Parvati's resolve.

## 8. The Wedding & Kumarasambhava ("The Birth of Kumara")

- **Himavan consents; the marriage** - Shiva calls the **Seven Sages (Saptarishi)**, with **Arundhati**, to ask Himavan for Parvati at **Oshadhiprastha**. The wedding at Himavan's city draws all the gods. (Kumarasambhava 6.1–36, 6.91; 7; Shiva Purana 2.3)
- **The honeymoon and the birth of Skanda** - Shiva and Parvati's love-making lasts "a hundred seasons as one night". (Kumarasambhava 8.91) From Shiva comes **Kumara / Skanda / Kartikeya**, the war-god, who slays **Taraka** — the very purpose of the whole rebirth-cycle. (Shiva Purana 2.4, Kumara-khanda; [Taraka's slaying](../04-deep-dives/tarakasura-vadha.md))
  - *Variant (Shiva Purana 2.4; Ramayana 1.35–36, crit. ed.):* Skanda is not born from Parvati's womb. Shiva's seed passes through **Agni** and **Ganga**, and the **Krittikas** nurse the child, hence "Karttikeya".
  - *Variant (Mahabharata 3.213–221, crit. ed.):* Skanda is the son of **Agni and Svaha**; Svaha, Daksha's daughter, takes the forms of the Seven Sages' wives.
  - *Note:* the eight cantos of Kalidasa's **Kumarasambhava** end with the love-making (canto 8). Cantos 9–17, which narrate Kumara's birth and Taraka's death, are generally held to be a later, non-Kalidasan continuation. [scholarly] [interpolation]
- **Birth of Ganesha** - In the Puranic (non-Kalidasa) cycle Parvati also creates **Ganesha** from the **dirt/scurf (*mala*) of her body**; "turmeric/sandal-paste" is a popular gloss. His beheading and elephant-head restoration is a parallel myth. (Shiva Purana 2.4.13.20; see [Ganesha & Kartikeya](ganesha-and-kartikeya.md))
  - *Variant (Brahmavaivarta Purana, Ganapati-khanda):* Ganesha is born as a form of Krishna after Parvati keeps the **Punyaka-vrata**, and his head is lost to **Shani's glance**.

## 9. Parvati's Aspects: Gauri, Annapurna & Beyond

- **Gauri ("the fair/golden one")** - Shiva teases Parvati as **Kali** or "dark". Stung, she leaves for tapas, and **Brahma** grants her a **golden complexion**. Her cast-off dark sheath (*kosha*) becomes a separate goddess, **Kaushiki** (also called Katyayani, or the night-goddess Ekanamsha), who goes to the **Vindhyas** with a lion as her mount, as **Vindhyavasini**. In the Vamana, Indra asks for her as his sister, takes her to the Vindhyas and gives her the lion; in the Matsya, Brahma sends her there, and the lion is born of the Goddess's anger. (Vamana Purana 28.6–28; Matsya Purana 155–157, esp. 157.12–19)
  - *Variant (Devi Mahatmya = Markandeya Purana 85.40–44):* it runs the other way, with no taunt and no tapas. **Ambika/Kaushiki** emerges from Parvati's body-sheath while Parvati is bathing in the Ganga, and Parvati herself turns **black** and is known as **Kalika**, dwelling on the Himalaya.
- **Annapurna ("she who is full of food")** - When Shiva declares the material world *maya* (illusion), Parvati withdraws and food vanishes from the cosmos. She then manifests as **Annapurna** at Kashi (Varanasi), feeding even Shiva — establishing that the Goddess is the sustaining material reality. (Kashi sthala tradition [folk]; Puranic locus not verified) The *Annapurna Upanishad* is a Ribhu–Nidagha Advaita dialogue whose teaching Ribhu credits to the goddess Annapurna; it does not narrate this story.
- **The unified Devi** - As the reborn Shakti, Parvati is identified with the full Goddess spectrum — **Durga, Kali, Uma, Bhavani, Lalita, Ambika** — making this cycle the bridge into the larger [Devi / Shakta theology](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) and the [Devi Mahatmya](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md).
