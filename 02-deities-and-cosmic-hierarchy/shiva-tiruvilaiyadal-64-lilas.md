# The 64 Līlās of Śiva — Tiruviḷaiyāḍal, the Sacred Sports of Sundareśvara at Madurai

> Node in [`02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/`](README.md). The Tamil Śaiva counterpart to the all-India Purāṇic deed-roster: sixty-four *tiruviḷaiyāḍal* ("sacred sports/games," Skt. *divya-līlā*) in which Śiva as **Sundareśvara** ("the beautiful lord," consort of [**Mīnākṣī**](devi-forms-and-navadurga.md)) repeatedly enters the affairs of the Pāṇḍya capital **Madurai (Maturai)**. This file is the full catalogue that replaces the partial ~7-sport treatment in [`shiva-myth-cycle.md`](shiva-myth-cycle.md) §5 (which now cross-links here). It is parallel in treatment to the [`dashavatara.md`](dashavatara.md) descent-list and the [`shiva-avatars-and-forms.md`](shiva-avatars-and-forms.md) form-roster — but where those track avatars/aspects, the *tiruviḷaiyāḍal* track **deeds**, the foundational sthala-purāṇa of the Mīnākṣī-Sundareśvara temple.

---

## The frame — *līlā*, the "two clocks," and the texts

- **The *līlā* doctrine** — the sports are *free, gratuitous "play"* (*viḷaiyāḍal* = Tamil "game/sport"), not acts compelled by cosmic necessity. The framing presents Śiva not as the transcendent Absolute of [`shiva-myth-cycle.md`](shiva-myth-cycle.md) §1 but as an **intimate, locally present deity of Madurai** who sells bangles, hauls earth, judges poetry, and takes a beating for a devotee. [Tamil Śaiva bhakti — living tradition]
- **The "two clocks":** these deeds are **scriptural / mythic time**, not datable history — period `kali-scriptural`. The **texts** that record them are datable: the Tamil *Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam* and the Sanskrit *Hālāsya Māhātmya* are late-medieval compositions (c. 13th–17th c. CE) crystallising older Madurai temple lore. [scholarly — text-dating ≠ event-dating]
- **The recensions (catalogue them, never merge):**
  - *Tamil — Perumpaṟṟappuliyūr Nampi (the older recension):* the earliest extant Tamil *Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam*, enumerating **sixty (60)** sports. [regional / earlier recension]
  - *Tamil — Parañcōti Munivar (c. 16th–17th c.):* the canonical, most-recited Tamil version, enumerating **sixty-four (64)** sports across three *kāṇṭam* (Madurai / Kūṭal / Tirivāṟṟu). The "64" is the standard count. [late text — canonical recension]
  - *Sanskrit — Hālāsya Māhātmya:* the Sanskrit Madurai sthala-purāṇa (attached as a *khaṇḍa* to the *Skanda Purāṇa* tradition), narrating **sixty-four** *līlās* of Sundareśvara ("Hālāsya" = the Sanskritised name of the Madurai shrine). Ordering and naming differ from the Tamil. [late text]
- **Note on the count:** the sets overlap heavily but are **not identical episode-for-episode**; Nampi's 60 vs Parañcōti's 64 vs the Sanskrit 64 diverge in which minor sports are included and in what order. What follows lists the canonical episodes grouped by theme, not a single fixed serial order. [disputed — no single authoritative sequence]

## Founding sports — the city and the goddess

- **The rain of golden lotuses & the founding of Madurai** — Śiva appears to the Pāṇḍya king **Kulaśekhara** (in some tellings via the merchant **Dhanañjaya** who saw Indra worshipping a *svayambhū* liṅga under a *kadamba* tree); nectar/honey-dew (*madhu*) drips from Śiva's matted locks over the new city, naming it **Madhurā / Maturai** ("the sweet city"). The city is laid out as a lotus, the temple at its heart. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam; Hālāsya Māhātmya*)
  - *Variant (Indra's relief):* Indra, freed of the sin of slaying Vṛtra at this spot, raises the first shrine; the Pāṇḍya merely re-founds the worship. (*Hālāsya Māhātmya*)
- **The birth of Taḍātakai / Mīnākṣī from the sacrificial fire** — the childless Pāṇḍya **Malayadhvaja** and queen **Kāñcanamālai** perform a *putra-kāmeṣṭi*; from the fire-altar rises a three-breasted girl, **Taḍātakai** (later **Mīnākṣī**, "fish-eyed"), born already crowned to rule. A voice foretells her third breast will vanish when she meets her lord. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam; Hālāsya Māhātmya*) — see [`devi-forms-and-navadurga.md`](devi-forms-and-navadurga.md) for Mīnākṣī's Śākta identification (Lalitā/Tripurā vs Pārvatī) and [`sati-daksha-and-parvati.md`](sati-daksha-and-parvati.md).
- **The world-conquest and the Tirukalyāṇam (divine wedding)** — Mīnākṣī, ruling and waging *dig-vijaya* (world-conquest), reaches **Kailāsa**; at the sight of Śiva her third breast vanishes and her warrior-fury becomes a bride's modesty. Śiva comes to Madurai as **Sundareśvara** and weds her in the great **Tirukalyāṇam**, with **Viṣṇu (her brother)** giving her away. This marriage is the theological centre of the whole temple. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam; Hālāsya Māhātmya*) [the foundational sport — annual *Cittirai* festival re-enacts it]

## Sports of the marketplace & the city

- **Selling bangles to the women of Madurai (Vaḷaiyal viṟṟa līlā)** — Śiva walks the streets as a bangle-seller to grace the gopī-like cowherd women (and, in tellings, to give the pregnant Mīnākṣī/a devotee a vision); a sport of divine condescension into the marketplace. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam*)
- **Becoming a fish-seller / the cowherd-women episodes** — Śiva takes the guise of a **fisherman/fish-vendor** (paralleling Mīnākṣī's "fish-eyed" name and the Pāṇḍya fish-emblem) to sport among the people. [regional]
- **Vandi-koṇḍa līlā — carrying earth for piṭṭu and taking the cane-blow** *(the most beloved sport)* — to dam the rising **Vaikai** river, the Pāṇḍya conscripts every household to bring earth; the poor old woman **Vandi (Piṭṭu-vāṇicci)**, a piṭṭu-seller (sweet rice-cake), has no labourer, so **Śiva himself** comes as a coolie working for her cakes of *piṭṭu*. He works idly; the king **Varaguṇa** strikes the lazy labourer with his cane — and **the blow is felt by every living being in creation**, since Śiva is the indweller of all. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam; Hālāsya Māhātmya*) [the canonical illustration of *līlā*: God labours for a cake and lets a king beat him]
- **Opening the rock / the Pāṇḍya's command** — Śiva (or a sport tied to the embankment cycle) cleaves a rock or stays the flood for the Pāṇḍya, the river-taming framing the city's deliverance. [regional — ordering varies by recension]

## Sports of the hunt and the beasts

- **Hunting the boar (Pandi / paṉṟi līlā)** — Śiva pursues a great boar through the Pāṇḍya country, a hunt-sport that founds shrines along the chase; in linked tellings the boar's piglets are turned to liṅgas or to ministers. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam*)
- **The hunter and the elephant** — Śiva as a **hunter (Kirāta-like)** subdues a rogue/demon elephant sent against the city or against his devotees, a Madurai recasting of the elephant-demon-slaying motif (cf. Gajāsura in [`shiva-myth-cycle.md`](shiva-myth-cycle.md) §3). [regional]
- **The stone elephant that ate sugarcane** — to confound rivals (in some tellings Jain/Samaṇa opponents, or to vindicate a devotee), Śiva makes a **carved stone elephant come alive and eat sugarcane** — inert matter animated by his grace. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam*) [folk]
- **Turning jackals into horses (and horses back into jackals)** — the devotee-poet **Māṇikkavācakar**, sent by the Pāṇḍya king to buy cavalry horses, spends the treasury on a temple instead; Śiva rescues him by **transforming a pack of jackals into a magnificent troop of horses** delivered to the king — then, the deception done, **turns the horses back into jackals** that scatter through the city, until Śiva intervenes again to vindicate his devotee. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam*; the **Māṇikkavācakar / Tiruvācakam** hagiography) [Tamil Śaiva bhakti — links the *Tiruvācakam* corpus]
  - *Variant:* the flooding of the Vaikai and the **Vandi-koṇḍa earth-carrying** sport above is set within this same Māṇikkavācakar cycle in several recensions. [disputed — episode-linkage varies]

## Sports of Tamil — the Saṅgam and the poets

- **Establishing / convening the Tamil Saṅgam** — Śiva sanctions and presides over the **Madurai Tamil Saṅgam** (academy of poets), legitimising **Tamil** as a sacred language equal to Sanskrit; the cycle repeatedly stages Śiva as the patron and arbiter of Tamil letters. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam*) [Tamil Śaiva cultural charter]
- **The miraculous bench / *palakai* of the Saṅgam poets** — Śiva grants a magical **plank/bench (*caṅka-palakai*)** that floats in the temple tank and **expands or contracts** to seat only true poets, rejecting the unworthy — the divine examiner of poetic merit. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam*) [folk]
- **The contest with Nakkīrar (the verse Śiva himself wrote)** — Śiva, to settle a poet's prize, composes a Tamil verse and submits it; the critic **Nakkīrar** finds a fault in it even after learning the author is Śiva, defiantly insisting "a fault is a fault though Śiva himself stand before me." Śiva opens his **third eye** and scorches/threatens him; Nakkīrar, unbroken on the point of scholarship, is finally pardoned. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam*; the **Nakkīrar** hagiography) [the famous parable of fearless Tamil scholarship]
- **The poet Dharmi and the divine purse / settling the poets' livelihood** — Śiva resolves disputes of patronage among the Saṅgam poets, providing for the poor poet through a god-given gift. [regional — naming varies]

## Sports of grace, miracle, and judgment

- **Sending the seven clouds & the great flood** — Śiva commands the **seven clouds (*sapta-megha*)** to deluge the land (the Vaikai flood that frames the Vandi-koṇḍa earth-carrying), demonstrating mastery of the elements over the Pāṇḍya realm. (*Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam*)
- **Granting children / curing the king / raising the dead-like** — a cluster of grace-sports: Śiva cures the Pāṇḍya of disease, grants heirs, restores a drowned/dead devotee, and answers vows — the everyday miracle-register of a city-god. [regional]
- **Disciplining rival sects / the Jain (Samaṇa) contests** — several sports stage Śiva (and his devotees, e.g. the boy-saint **Tiruñāṉacampantar** in adjacent Tamil hagiography) vindicating Śaivism against **Jain (Samaṇa)** opponents at Madurai — the sectarian-polemic layer of the cycle. [regional — Tamil Śaiva polemic]
- **The marriage-broker, the merchant, and miscellaneous guises** — Śiva appears variously as a brahmin, a labourer, a merchant, an old man, a child, and a *siddha* across the remaining sports, each a local etiology for a shrine, tank, or *tīrtha* within the Madurai sacred geography. [the "long tail" of the 64 — minor sports differ most across recensions]

## Theology — the locally present god

- **Sundareśvara + Mīnākṣī = the temple's whole logic** — every sport radiates from the **Mīnākṣī-Sundareśvara temple**: the deeds are not free-floating myth but *sthala-purāṇa*, each tied to a specific spot, tank (*tīrtha*), gopuram, or festival. The annual **Cittirai** festival dramatises the **Tirukalyāṇam** and Māṇikkavācakar's horse-sport for the whole city. [living tradition]
- **Why "64"?** the number echoes the **64 *kalās* (arts)** and the **64 *tantras*/yoginīs** of pan-Indian sacred numerology — marking the deed-cycle as *complete/total*, the Madurai parallel to the "ten avatars" totality of [`dashavatara.md`](dashavatara.md). [scholarly — numerological framing]
- **Regional counterpart, not subset** — the *tiruviḷaiyāḍal* are the **Tamil Śaiva answer** to the northern Purāṇic Śiva-deeds (Tripurāntaka, Gajāsura, etc. in [`shiva-myth-cycle.md`](shiva-myth-cycle.md)): a few motifs overlap (elephant-slaying, flood-mastery), but the cycle is a self-standing local scripture, asserting Śiva's *presence at Madurai* over his transcendence. [scholarly]
