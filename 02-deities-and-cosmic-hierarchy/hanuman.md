# Hanuman — Vanara Devotee, Chiranjivi, Rudravatara

> Cosmic address: [Deities & Cosmic Hierarchy](README.md) → individual deity. Hanuman acts in the [Treta Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) (Ramayana) and survives into the [Dvapara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) and [Kali Yugas](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) as one of the immortal [Chiranjivi](#chiranjivi--the-deathless-one).

Hanuman (also Anjaneya, Maruti, Bajrangbali, Pavanaputra) is the foremost vanara (forest-dweller) ally of [Rama](dashavatara.md), the paradigm of bhakti (devotion), and among the most widely worshipped deities of living Hinduism. He sits at the meeting point of two great traditions: in the Vaishnava framing he is the perfect *devotee* of Vishnu-as-Rama; in the Shaiva framing he is an *avatara of Shiva himself* (popularly, the 11th Rudra). This file catalogs both, plus his birth cycle, his Sundara Kanda feats, his Mahabharata role, his iconographic forms, and his bhakti cult.

## Birth & Childhood (Anjaneya Cycle)

- **Parentage — Anjana, Kesari, and Vayu** - Born to the apsara-turned-vanari Anjana (cursed to monkey form) and the vanara chief Kesari; conceived through the agency of Vayu, the wind god, making Hanuman *Vayuputra/Pavanaputra* (son of the wind). In Jambavan's telling Anjana is the apsara **Punjikasthala**, reborn by a curse as a shape-shifting vanari, daughter of the vanara Kunjara and wife of Kesari. (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 66.8–10; Uttara Kanda 35, CE 7.35.19–20)
  - *Note (Valmiki, Kishkindha 66):* Vayu, having embraced Anjana on a mountaintop, tells her he has entered her **by the mind** (*manasā*, 66.18) and has not violated her; she bears the child in a cave (66.20). Jambavan then states the double paternity in so many words: Hanuman is Kesari's *kṣetraja* son (born of his wife) and Maruta's *aurasa* (begotten) son (66.29–30). *Aurasa* recurs at Bala 17.16, and Hanuman uses it of himself before Ravana (CE 5.49.14).
  - *Note (Valmiki, Bala Kanda 17):* the begetting is part of the gods' general plan: Brahma tells the devas to father vanara sons to aid Vishnu's incarnation, and Maruta's son Hanuman is "of adamantine frame" (*vajra-saṃhanana*, 1.17.16) — an image that the later epithet *Bajrang* (from *vajra-aṅga*, "thunderbolt-limbed") echoes. Uttara 36 (CE 7.36.44) restates that the gods created Hanuman, Sugriva and the other chiefs for Rama's sake.
  - *Variant (Shiva Purana, Shatarudra Samhita 20.3–7):* Shiva, seeing Vishnu in the Mohini form, lets fall his seed "for Rama's work"; the seven sages keep it in a leaf and pour it **through the ears** of Anjani, here called the daughter of Gautama; Shiva is born of it as the monkey Hanuman. Vayu plays no part in this telling. See [Rudravatara](#the-rudravatara-claim-shaiva).
  - *Variant (Ananda Ramayana):* a kite snatches part of the *payasa* that Dasharatha shares among his queens after the putrakameshti sacrifice; it falls into the hands of Anjana, at penance in the forest, and she eats it and conceives. (Ananda Ramayana, as summarized in Vettam Mani, *Puranic Encyclopaedia*, s.v. Hanuman) [late text]
  - *Variant (Bhavishya Purana, Pratisarga Parva):* Shiva in a fierce form enters Kesari and unites with Anjana, and Vayu afterwards does the same, so the child is born of both; Vayu saves the newborn when Anjana would cast it away. (as summarized in Mani, *Puranic Encyclopaedia*, s.v. Hanuman) [late text]
- **Rahu's grievance and the struck jaw** - The infant, mistaking the rising sun for a ripe fruit, leapt skyward to swallow it — on the very day of the conjunction, when **Rahu** had come for the same orb. Finding "a second Rahu" at his prey, Rahu carried the complaint to **Indra**, who rode out on **Airavata**; the child, seeing the white elephant, sprang for that too, and Indra loosed the *vajra*. It broke the boy's left jaw and cast him lifeless on a mountain. This is **core Valmiki, not a variant reading**: Rahu, the eclipse-day setting, the Airavata lunge and the shattered jaw are all narrated in Uttara Kanda 35 as Agastya tells Rama the vanara's origin. The sun, seeing an innocent child, did not burn him, and Vayu flew alongside to cool him. (Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 35; CE 7.35.23–47, the left jaw at 7.35.47 *vāmo hanur abhajyata*)
- **Why Agastya tells the story** - Rama opens Uttara 35 with a question: if Hanuman was so mighty, why did he not burn Vali to ash when Vali was hounding his friend Sugriva? Agastya's whole account of the childhood, boons and curse is the answer — under the curse Hanuman did not know his own strength, and so he stood by Sugriva in the feud as a mere ally. (Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 35–36; CE 7.35.11–16, 7.36.39–40)
- **The naming** - At the boon-ceremony that follows, Indra himself gives the name: because his own thunderbolt shattered the child's *hanu* (jaw), the boy shall be called **Hanuman**, "one of the [struck] jaw." (Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 36; CE 7.36.11)
  - *Note (Kishkindha 66):* Jambavan's shorter account gives the same etymology — the left jaw broke on the mountaintop, "hence your name Hanuman" (4.66.24) — but does not put the name in Indra's mouth.
- **Vayu's wrath & the boon-ceremony — the itemized roster** - Enraged, Vayu withdrew into a cave with his son and held the winds out of the worlds, so that no creature could breathe or void; the gods came to the wind-god and Brahma restored the child's life. Brahma then called on the assembled powers to endow him, and the grants are **given one by one in the text**: **Indra** — invulnerable to the vajra; **Surya (Martanda)** — a hundredth part of his own effulgence, and, when he is able to study, the shastras that will make him eloquent; **Varuna** — proof against his noose and his waters; **Yama** — immunity from his rod and from disease, and (Shastri's vulgate) never to be slain in battle; **Kubera** — his mace shall never wound him; **Shiva** — his shafts withheld; **Vishvakarma** — proof against all celestial weapons he has forged, and long life; **Brahma** — immunity from his own weapons. Brahma then tells Vayu the boy will be invincible, able to take any form and go anywhere at will. (Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 36, in Shastri's translation of the vulgate; CE 7.36.10–23)
  - *Note (Critical Edition wording):* the CE has the same eight givers but words some grants differently: Yama gives immunity from his rod, perpetual health and **freedom from despondency in battle** (*aviṣādaṃ ca saṃyuge*); Brahma gives immunity from **all brahma-rods** (*brahmadaṇḍa*) **and long life**; Vishvakarma gives immunity to the gods' weapons he made, with no clause on long life. (CE 7.36.10–21)
  - *Note (who is NOT in the roster):* **Vishnu grants nothing** in Valmiki's ceremony, and neither does **Agni** — the two names most often added to the list in retellings. Agni is among the gods Brahma addresses (CE 7.36.8, *mahendrāgnivaruṇa…*), but no grant follows from him. Hanuman's tail not burning is **not** a birth-boon: it is Sita's prayer to Agni answered in the moment, at the burning of Lanka, with Vayu blowing cool alongside the flame — Hanuman himself puts it down to Sita's kindness, Rama's power and his father's friendship. (Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda 53.27–38)
- **The curse of forgetting — core text; the release clause is vulgate wording** - Drunk on the boons, the boy harried the forest hermitages, and the **rishis born in the lines of Bhrigu and Angiras** cursed him: by their curse he would **remain unaware of his own strength for a long time**. In the vulgate as Shastri translates it, the curse goes on to say that **when it is recalled to him he will wield it in full**, which is why Jambavan's reminding works. The Critical Edition's curse-verse — *tad dīrghakālaṃ vettāsi nāsmākaṃ śāpamohitaḥ*, "deluded by our curse, you will not know it for a long time" — lacks the vulgate's release line. Either way the curse is **core Valmiki Uttara Kanda 36**, not folk devotional embroidery. Jambavan's rousing at the southern shore is equally core text, though Kishkindha 66 itself never mentions the curse. (Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 36; CE 7.36.32–34; Kishkindha Kanda 66)
  - *Variant (Ramcharitmanas):* Tulsidas keeps Jambavan's rousing at the shore, at the close of the Kishkindha Kand — "why have you kept silent, O mighty one? … your incarnation was for Rama's work" (*kā cupa sādhi rahehu balavānā … rāma kāja lagi tava avatārā*) — but **narrates no curse**, and the Manas gives no cause for his silence.
- **Education under Surya** - Hanuman studies grammar and the shastras directly from Surya (the sun-god) as guru, facing him and moving backward across the sky **from the eastern mountain to the western** with a great text in hand. That verse is in the Critical Edition as well (CE 7.36.42). The vulgate passage, as Shastri translates it, goes on to say that he became a peerless scholar of the shastras and of prosody, and that he will be a knower of the "nine grammars" (*navavyākaraṇa*), which Shastri renders as "the most recent grammatical systems". (Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 36; CE 7.36.42)
  - *Variant (Shiva Purana, Shatarudra Samhita 20.11):* at his mother's bidding he goes to the sun every day and learns all lore from him.
- **Two Valmiki birth-narrations, not one** - The epic tells the birth **twice**, and the two accounts do not agree. **Kishkindha Kanda 66** (Jambavan's rousing speech) gives the compact version: Anjana-Punjikasthala, Kesari, Vayu's *manasa* begetting, birth in a cave, the leap of **three hundred yojanas** at the sun (66.22), Indra's bolt, Vayu's strike against the worlds, and only two boons — **Brahma**'s immunity to weapons (66.27) and **Indra**'s grant that he shall **die only when he himself chooses** (*svacchanda-maraṇa*, 66.28–29). **Uttara Kanda 35–36** (Agastya to Rama) gives the long version and adds Rahu, Airavata, the itemized eight-god roster, Indra pronouncing the name, the Surya tutelage and the Bhrigu–Angiras curse. The leap (Uttara has "many thousands of yojanas", CE 7.35.29), the birthplace (Uttara puts Kesari's kingdom on **Sumeru**, CE 7.35.19) and the boons all differ between them. The corpus records both; they are not to be merged. (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 66; Uttara Kanda 35–36)

## The Ramayana Role (Treta Yuga)

For the full narrative arc see the [Treta Yuga file](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md). Hanuman's key acts:

- **Minister of Sugriva** - Serving the exiled vanara king Sugriva at Rishyamukha; it is Hanuman who first meets Rama and Lakshmana and brokers the Rama–Sugriva alliance. He goes to them **disguised as a mendicant** (*bhikṣu*), and, still in that guise, tells them he is Sugriva's minister, "a vanara, son of the Wind-god", come in a mendicant's form. Only after Lakshmana has told him their story does he cast off the disguise, take back his own form and carry both brothers **on his back** to Sugriva's mountain — the first time in the epic that he bears Rama. (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 3.2, 3.21–22; 4.34–35)
- **The speech-certificate** - Rama's aside to Lakshmana after that first meeting is the epic's formal warrant for Hanuman's learning: only one who has mastered the **Rig, Yajur and Sama** and studied grammar entire could speak so; the delivery is unhurried, unhesitating, undissonant, pitched in the middle register — and *"though much is said by him, not a single word has gone amiss."* A king with such an envoy, Rama says, succeeds in every undertaking. (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 3.28–35)
- **The search for Sita** - Dispatched south with Angada's party; reaching the ocean's edge, the despairing vanaras learn from Sampati that Sita is in Lanka. Jambavan reminds Hanuman of his forgotten powers, and he grows huge and resolves to leap the sea. (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 56–63, Sampati; 65–67, the leap-ladder, Jambavan's rousing and Hanuman's growth)

### Sundara Kanda — the "Beautiful Book," Hanuman's own canto

The fifth book of the Valmiki Ramayana centers almost entirely on Hanuman; it is recited as a self-contained devotional text.

- **The leap across the ocean** - Expanding to colossal size, Hanuman springs from Mount Mahendra and bounds ~100 yojanas to Lanka. En route he is tested: Mainaka (golden mountain) offers rest; the naga-mother Surasa swallows him (he shrinks and escapes); the shadow-catching demoness Simhika seizes his shadow and he kills her. (Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda 1)
- **Scaling Lanka & finding Sita** - Shrinking at sunset to the size of a cat (*vṛṣadaṃśaka-mātra*, 2.49), he infiltrates Ravana's city by night, searches the palace and Ashoka Vatika, and finally finds Sita captive beneath a shimshapa tree (15); he then watches her refuse Ravana's advances (18–22). (Sundara Kanda 2–22)
- **Lankini, the city's guardian** - At the gate the city of Lanka herself bars his way as a rakshasi. Not wishing to show great anger to a woman, he strikes her with his **left fist**; she falls and tells him of Brahma's old word to her: when a vanara overpowers her, the rakshasas' end is near. She lets him enter. The name *Lankini* belongs to the Ramcharitmanas and other later retellings; Valmiki calls her simply Lanka ("I am the city of Lanka herself", 3.45). (Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda 3.40–51)
- **Choosing his speech** - Before speaking to Sita, Hanuman weighs which language to use. If he speaks Sanskrit "like a twice-born", she may take him for Ravana in disguise and be afraid, so he resolves to speak "meaningful human speech" (*mānuṣaṃ vākyam arthavat*). (Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda 30.17–19)
- **Revealing himself to Sita** - He recites Rama's story and presents Rama's signet ring as a token; offers to carry Sita back, which she refuses (only Rama must rescue her, to uphold his honor). She gives him her *chudamani* (crest-jewel) for Rama. (Sundara Kanda 31–40)
- **His own account of himself** - Pressed by Sita to say who he is, Hanuman gives his fullest account of his parentage. He is the son of **Kesari**, the vanara who went from Mount Malyavan to Gokarna and, at the prayer of gods and seers, slew the demon **Shambasadana** at a shrine by the sea; and he was born to Kesari's wife by the grace of **Vayu**. It is not his only self-introduction: he names himself the Wind-god's son to Rama at their first meeting (Kishkindha 3.21–22) and Maruta's begotten son before Ravana (CE 5.49.14). The passage is genuine Valmiki, and it is the only place this page has found the Kesari–Shambasadana deed. It sits **outside** the two birth-accounts (Kishkindha 66, Uttara 35–36), which is why it is easily mistaken for later tradition. (Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda 35.80–83) — cf. [Vanaras & Rikshas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vanaras-and-rikshas.md).
- **Destroying the Ashoka grove & confronting Ravana** - To provoke a reckoning, Hanuman uproots the grove, slays Aksha (Ravana's son) and many demons, and lets himself be captured by Indrajit's Brahmastra (honoring Brahma's boon). Brought before Ravana, he delivers a warning. (Sundara Kanda 41–51)
- **The burning of Lanka** - Ravana sets Hanuman's tail ablaze; the fire does not burn him — Sita prays to Agni for his welfare, and Vayu blows cool along the flame. Hanuman breaks free and, leaping roof to roof, sets the golden city aflame, sparing **Vibhishana's** house alone, and comes to rest on Trikuta. (Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda 52–54)
  - *Note (quenching the tail in the sea):* the dousing is a matter of **recension**, not folk invention. The vulgate on valmikiramayan.net has no such verse: sarga 54 leaves him blazing on Trikuta (54.44) and ends at 54.50. The Critical Edition, though, prints it: *laṅkāṃ samastāṃ saṃdīpya lāṅgūlāgniṃ mahākapiḥ / nirvāpayām āsa tadā samudre* — having set all Lanka alight, he put out the fire of his tail in the sea (CE 5.52.17). The Shiva Purana (Shatarudra 20.25–26) and the Ramcharitmanas (Sundar Kand, dohā 26, *pūṁcha bujhāi*) also have it. [recensional]
  - *Note (worry for Sita — core text, not a variant):* the scene is in the vulgate itself. Seeing Lanka in ashes, Hanuman reproaches his own anger and fears Sita has burned; omens and his trust in her virtue steady him, and then he overhears the celestial **Charanas** saying that she is unharmed. He goes back to see her once more before leaving. (Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda 55–56)
- **Return with news** - He takes leave of Sita, springs back across the sea from **Mount Arishta** (56.9), roars in triumph on reaching the mainland, and — after the vanaras' revel in **Madhuvana**, Sugriva's honey-grove — reports Sita's location and message to Rama, restoring the prince's hope. (Sundara Kanda 56–68)

### Yuddha Kanda — war feats

- **The first herb-flight — the herb-mountain** - When Indrajit's **brahmastra**, loosed unseen from the sky after his oblation (Yuddha 73), lays Rama, Lakshmana and the host senseless, **Jambavan** sends Hanuman to the Himalaya for four herbs — *mrita-sanjivani*, *vishalyakarani*, *savarnyakarani* and *sandhani*. The mountain lies among the northern peaks, **between Rishabha and golden Kailasa**. Valmiki calls it only the **"herb-mountain"** (*oṣadhi-parvata*, 74.31; CE 6.61.31) and gives it **no proper name**. Unable to pick out the herbs as they hide themselves from him, Hanuman uproots and carries back the **entire summit**. (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda 74.29–33, 74.60–69)
- **The second herb-flight — the same mountain again** - A distinct, later errand to the **same** place. After **Ravana's** Maya-forged *shakti* fells Lakshmana, the physician **Sushena** sends Hanuman to "the herb-mountain that Jambavan described to you before" (*śailam oṣadhiparvatam / pūrvaṃ hi kathito yo 'sau vīra jāmbavatā*). He wants *vishalyakarani*, which grows on its **southern peak**, with the other three herbs. Again Hanuman cannot tell the herbs apart and brings the peak entire, and Sushena draws the herbs himself. (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda 101.31–33; CE 6.89.14–22) — two events, not two tellings of one, but **one mountain**.
  - *Note (the name "Mahodaya"):* the valmikiramayan.net translation glosses Sushena's mountain as "Oushadhi (Mahodaya)". Neither name is a proper name in the Sanskrit. *Oṣadhi-parvata* simply means "herb-mountain", and *Mahodaya* is in neither the vulgate verse nor the Critical Edition's text of the passage; it is the translator's parenthesis.
  - *Note (the name "Dronagiri"):* **Dronagiri is not a Valmiki place-name anywhere in the epic.** It belongs to later and regional tradition, which attaches it to the herb-mountain; the corpus keeps it as tradition's name, not the text's. The herb-roster, the two flights and the "Dronagiri" question in full: [Ramayana Objects & Tokens §V](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/ramayana-objects-and-tokens.md); the mountains from the geography side: [Meru, Kailasa & the Cosmic Mountains](../06-sacred-geography/meru-kailash-and-cosmic-mountains.md).
  - *Variant (Ramcharitmanas / later tradition):* on the return flight he is struck down over Ayodhya by Bharata, who mistakes him for a demon, then is honored on recognizing him. (Ramcharitmanas, Lanka Kand) Not in Valmiki.
  - *Variant (Shiva Purana, Shatarudra 20.31):* Lakshmana is felled by **Meghanada's** (Indrajit's) javelin, not Ravana's, and Hanuman revives him with the herb *sanjivani*.
- **Carrying Rama and Lakshmana on his shoulders** - When Ravana enters the field in his chariot and the brothers fight on foot, Hanuman bears **Rama** aloft on his shoulders so that the fight is met at the chariot's height — the origin of the *Hanuman as Rama's vahana* iconography. In the same sarga he lifts the wounded **Lakshmana** in his arms and carries him back to Rama, a weight Ravana himself could not raise. (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda 59)
- **Bharata's reward at Nandigrama** - Sent ahead from the Pushpaka to break the news of Rama's return, Hanuman is rewarded by **Bharata** with a hundred thousand cows, a hundred prime villages, and **sixteen maidens of noble family** for wives — the epic's largest single grant to him, and a detail retellings almost always drop. (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda 125.44–45)
- **Sita's necklace and the ten virtues** - At the coronation Rama tells Sita to bestow her pearl necklace on whomever she is pleased with and in whom ten named qualities are ever present — strength, valour, fame, skill, capability, conduct, prudence, courtesy, resolution and prowess; she looks over the assembly and puts it on Hanuman. The ten virtues are Rama's wording, the choice hers. (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda 128.79–83) — **not** the occasion of the deathlessness grant; see [Chiranjivi](#chiranjivi--the-deathless-one).
  - *Note:* the coronation gifts in the same sarga include a pearl necklace presented to Rama by **Vayu** at Indra's prompting (128.70–71). The text does not say that this is the string Rama then gives Sita.
- **Rescue from Patala — the Ahiravana episode** - Ahiravana (Mahiravana, Mairavana), a Patala-dwelling sorcerer and Ravana's ally, abducts the sleeping Rama and Lakshmana to the netherworld to sacrifice them to Mahamaya/Bhadrakali. Retellings variously make him Ravana's brother or his friend. Hanuman descends and discovers that Ahiravana's life is split among five lamps in five directions. He assumes his **five-faced (Panchamukha)** form to put out all five at once, and slays him. The episode is absent from Valmiki entirely. Its attestations include the **Shiva Purana**, whose Hanuman chapter says in one verse that he slew Mahiravana and brought Rama and Lakshmana back from his realm; the **Krittivasi Ramayana** (15th c. Bengali); and the Telugu *Mairavana-charitra* (16th c.); it is widespread in the regional Rama-katha. The five lamps and the Panchamukha form belong to the regional versions, not to the Shiva Purana's one-line mention. (Shiva Purana, Shatarudra Samhita 20.34; Krittivasi Ramayana; *Mairavana-charitra*) [late text]
  - *Variant (Makaradhvaja):* in the regional telling Hanuman meets the gatekeeper of Patala, **Makaradhvaja**, who claims to be Hanuman's son. Hanuman overcomes and binds him, and after Ahiravana's death installs him as king of Patala. Popular tradition says he was born of a great fish that swallowed a drop of Hanuman's sweat as he cooled himself in the sea after burning Lanka. [late text / regional; the birth story folk]

## Iconographic Forms

- **Panchamukha Hanuman (five-faced)** - Five faces — Hanuman (east), Narasimha (south), Garuda (west), Varaha (north), Hayagriva (sky/zenith) — with fifteen eyes and ten arms bearing weapons; originates in the Ahiravana rescue. Largely a Tantra-tradition form, attested in iconography from roughly the 15th century CE. (Tantric texts; *Sritattvanidhi*) [late text]
  - *Variant (face attributions):* lists of which face points which direction vary by text and temple.
- **The heart-bearing image (Rama in the chest)** - The beloved image of Hanuman tearing open his chest to reveal Rama and Sita enthroned in his heart — proof that Rama dwells within him — dramatizes total bhakti. The popular tale behind it is usually hung on Sita's necklace gift (Yuddha 128): Hanuman bites the pearls open looking for Rama inside them, and when he is mocked he opens his breast. Neither Valmiki nor the Ramcharitmanas narrates the chest-opening. (popular devotional tale; iconography) [folk tradition]
- **Other forms** - *Veera/Vira Hanuman* (warrior, mace raised); *Dasa Hanuman* (humble servant, hands folded, before Rama); *Suryamukhi / flying Hanuman bearing the mountain*; *Bala Hanuman* (child reaching for the sun). (temple iconography)

## The Mahabharata Role (Dvapara Yuga)

As a Chiranjivi, Hanuman lives on into the next age and intersects the [Mahabharata](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md).

- **Brother of Bhima** - Both Hanuman and Bhima are sons of Vayu (Hanuman in Treta via Anjana, Bhima in Dvapara via Kunti), hence half-brothers. Bhima introduces himself to the old monkey as "the Pandava, son of the Wind" (3.147.3), and the narrator pairs them: "Hanuman, son of the Wind, spoke to the Wind's son" (3.147.4). When the monkey asks who this Hanuman is, Bhima, not knowing whom he addresses, boasts of "my brother, famed in the *Ramayana*" (*rāmāyaṇe 'tivikhyātaḥ*), who crossed the hundred-yojana sea for Rama's wife, and claims to be his equal (3.147.10–13). Only after he is humbled and asks "who are you in monkey form?" does Hanuman reveal himself: born "in Kesari's field, by Vayu" (*kesariṇaḥ kṣetre vāyunā*), the same double paternity as Kishkindha 66. (Mahabharata, Vana Parva 3.147.3–24, BORI)
- **The tail-lifting humility lesson** - Bhima is searching the Gandhamadana slopes for the *saugandhika* flowers Draupadi wants. He finds an aged, sick-seeming monkey (*vyādhinā kleśitaḥ*, 3.147.7) lying across the path in a plantain grove, and demands that it move. The monkey tells him to step over it; Bhima will not, out of respect for the Self that dwells in every body (3.147.8–9). The monkey then asks him to move its tail aside and pass (3.147.16). Bhima tries to lift the tail and cannot stir it even with all his strength (3.147.17–20). Humbled, he asks who this is, and Hanuman tells him the Rama story in brief. He then teaches Bhima the **four yugas** and their decline, and the duties of the varnas and of kings. (Mahabharata, Vana Parva 3.146–149, BORI)
- **The sea-crossing form** - At Bhima's insistence Hanuman shows the colossal body he took to leap the ocean, saying it is the most Bhima can bear to see, and that he can grow larger still; then he shrinks back. (Mahabharata, Vana Parva 3.149.1–9, 3.150.1)
- **The life-span boon, in the Mahabharata's own words** - Hanuman tells Bhima that after Rama was enthroned he asked him for a boon: "may I live as long as the story of Rama endures among the worlds". Rama said, "So be it." Apsarases and gandharvas sing Rama's deeds to him where he lives. A star-passage outside the constituted text adds that divine pleasures attend him there "by the grace of Sita". (Mahabharata, Vana Parva 3.147.37–39; insertion 3.*738, BORI) — a parallel to the Uttara Kanda grant; see [Chiranjivi](#chiranjivi--the-deathless-one).
- **Boon to roar in battle** - Hanuman offers to crush Hastinapura with a rock or to kill the sons of Dhritarashtra himself; Bhima declines and asks only for his favour. Hanuman then promises that when Bhima roars like a lion in the enemy host he will **swell that roar with his own**, and that from **Arjuna's banner** he will let out terrible cries that take the enemies' lives. He then vanishes. (Mahabharata, Vana Parva 3.150.7–15, BORI)
- **Kapidhvaja — the monkey-bannered chariot** - Arjuna's chariot flies an ape banner, hence his epithet *Kapidhvaja* ("monkey-bannered", e.g. Bhagavad Gita 1.20). The chariot and its standard reach him in the Khandava episode, when Varuna, at Agni's request, gives him Gandiva and a chariot bannered with "the best of apes" (*kapipravaraketana*). A divine **vānara** sits on the flagstaff, roaring, among great beings whose cries numb the enemy (Adi Parva 1.216.8–14). The constituted text does **not** name the ape there; a manuscript insertion excluded from the Critical Edition does ("Hanuman by name, wind-born", 1.*2114). The Critical Edition's link between banner-ape and Hanuman is Hanuman's own promise to Bhima (3.150.15). (Mahabharata, BORI Critical Edition)
  - *Note (the end of the chariot):* the war won, Krishna has Arjuna dismount first and then steps down himself. At once the **divine ape vanishes from the standard**, and the chariot, burnt earlier by Drona's and Karna's celestial weapons, bursts into flame and falls to ash. Krishna explains that it had long since been consumed by weapons and had held together only **because he sat on it** (*madadhiṣṭhitatvāt*). The text gives the protection to Krishna, not to the ape. The popular idea that Hanuman on the banner absorbed the weapons' force is later tradition. (Mahabharata, Shalya Parva 9.61.8–19, BORI)
  - *Note:* the [Bhagavad Gita](../04-deep-dives/bhagavad-gita.md) is spoken on this very chariot, and the ape-bannered Arjuna is named in its first chapter. Hanuman as the unseen listener on the standard is devotional tradition.

## The Rudravatara Claim (Shaiva)

- **11th Rudra / avatara of Shiva** - In Shaiva tradition Hanuman *is* Shiva incarnate. The textual anchor is the Shiva Purana's chapter on "the incarnation of Hanumat". Shiva lets fall his seed "for Rama's work"; Hanuman is born of it as "a part of Shiva" and "the incarnation of Shiva", and he serves as minister to Sugriva, "a part of the sun". He goes on to perform the whole Sundara and Yuddha cycle, ending with Mahiravana's death, and "established the cult of devotion to Rama in the world". (Shiva Purana, Shatarudra Samhita 20.1–38) The popular count of Hanuman as **the 11th of the eleven Rudras** is not stated in that chapter, and the corpus has not traced it to a specific text. It is kept here as devotional tradition. [popular / sectarian]
  - *Variant (transmission of the seed):* the tellings differ on how Shiva's power reaches Anjana. In the Shiva Purana the seven sages carry the seed and pour it into her ear, and Vayu has no part (Shatarudra 20.5–6). In the Bhavishya Purana's Pratisarga Parva Shiva unites with her through Kesari and Vayu follows, so the child is born of both (as summarized in Mani, *Puranic Encyclopaedia*). The pudding version (Ananda Ramayana) involves neither Shiva nor Vayu's seed; there the kite's portion of Dasharatha's *payasa* is the cause. Popular retellings often combine these strands — Vayu carrying Shiva's essence, sometimes in the pudding — and that combination is not found in any one of these three texts. Mani's encyclopaedia does report, without naming its source, a telling in which Vayu carries Shiva's embryo from Parvati's womb and deposits it in Anjana, who had been at penance to conceive by Shiva. See [Birth & Childhood](#birth--childhood-anjaneya-cycle).
  - *Note (Valmiki):* Valmiki knows nothing of a Shaiva birth. Shiva appears only as one of the boon-givers at Uttara 36, withholding his shafts.
- **Vaishnava counter-framing (devotee, not deity-incarnate)** - The dominant Ramananda/Tulsidas Vaishnava bhakti stream frames Hanuman not as a god incarnating but as the supreme *dasa* (servant-devotee) of Rama, whose greatness lies entirely in surrender to Rama. Both framings coexist in popular worship.
  - *Reconciliation (common popular view):* "Shiva became the devotee of Vishnu" — Hanuman embodies the unity of Shaiva and Vaishnava streams (Hari-Hara harmony).

## Chiranjivi — the Deathless One

- **Rama's grant — first made at the allies' farewell** - The grant is made **twice** in the Uttara Kanda. It first comes at the leave-taking after the coronation, when Rama sends the vanaras, bears and rakshasas home. Hanuman, bowing, asks that his love and devotion stay fixed on Rama, and that "as long as I hear the story of Rama on earth, so long may life dwell in my body". Rama rises, embraces him and confirms it: as long as his story circulates in the worlds, so long will life remain in Hanuman's body. In Shastri's vulgate Rama also says Hanuman's fame will endure as long, and that for every service Hanuman has done he would give up his life and still remain in his debt; neither line is in the Critical Edition's verses here. Rama then ties his own moon-bright necklace with a **vaidūrya** (cat's-eye) pendant on Hanuman's breast. (Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 40, Shastri; CE 7.39.14–21)
- **Rama's grant — renewed at the Mahaprasthana** - At **Uttara Kanda 108**, about to leave the world, Rama gives his last commands. To Hanuman: hold to your resolve to live and do not break your vow — "as long as my story is told in the world, so long mayest thou be happy" — and in the vulgate Hanuman answers that so long as that history circulates, he will remain on earth; the Critical Edition gives him no reply. "Do not break your vow" (*mā pratijñāṃ vilopaya*, CE 7.98.24) looks back to the earlier promise. Both times it is **Rama's** grant, not Sita's. **Yuddha Kanda 128**, where later tradition often places it, contains only Sita's gift of her pearl necklace, and Rama's own necklace at Uttara 40 is a separate gift. (Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 108; CE 7.98.24–25)
- **The other terms granted in the same scene** - Rama fixes the spans of his other long-lived companions at the same audience. **Vibhishana** is to hold Lanka *as long as people exist… as long as the moon and the sun shine on the earth*; the Critical Edition has only "as long as creatures endure" (CE 7.98.22). In Shastri's vulgate, **Jambavan** is to dwell on earth *until the Kali Yuga begins*. That makes three different terms for three companions in one sarga. The Jambavan clause is **vulgate only**: in the Critical Edition Rama goes straight from Hanuman to "all of you, come with me" (CE 7.98.26). Sugriva, having already installed **Angada**, follows Rama instead of remaining. (Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 108, Shastri; CE 7.98.21–26)
- **Death only at his own choosing** - A different and earlier guarantee is Indra's boon in Kishkindha 66, alongside Brahma's grant of immunity to weapons: Hanuman shall die only when he himself wills it (*svacchanda-maraṇa*). It makes him death-proof at will, not deathless by decree. (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 66.28–29)
- **The Mahabharata witness** - Hanuman tells Bhima that after Rama's enthronement he asked to live as long as Rama's story endures, and that Rama agreed. This parallels the Uttara Kanda grant in a second epic. (Mahabharata, Vana Parva 3.147.37, BORI)
- **The Sita-blessing version** - The widely-told form in which **Sita** confers the deathlessness is later devotional tradition rather than Valmiki's text; the corpus keeps it as such. Its best-known source is the Ramcharitmanas, where Sita, consoled by Hanuman in the Ashoka grove, blesses him: *ajara amara gunanidhi suta hohū*, "my son, be ageless, deathless, a store of virtues". A star-passage of the Mahabharata's Bhima episode (3.*738) also credits Sita's grace for the divine comforts that attend him. (Ramcharitmanas, Sundar Kand, Sita's blessing in the Ashoka grove, in the chaupais after dohā 16 beginning *mana saṃtoṣa sunata kapi bānī*; later Rama-katha tradition)
- **Among the seven (or eight) Chiranjivi** - Hanuman is listed among the immortals in the traditional verse *aśvatthāmā balir vyāso hanūmāṃś ca vibhīṣaṇaḥ / kṛpaḥ paraśurāmaś ca saptaite cirajīvinaḥ* — Ashwatthama, Mahabali, Vyasa, Hanuman, Vibhishana, Kripacharya and Parashurama. A second half-verse adds **Markandeya** as the eighth, and some lists add others. See the broader cosmology in [Devas, Asuras & the Lokas](devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md). (Chiranjivi shloka tradition)
- **Present wherever Rama is praised** - Folk belief holds Hanuman silently attends every recitation of the Ramayana / Rama-katha — a belief that matches the terms of Rama's grant and the Mahabharata's picture of him listening to Rama's deeds sung by apsarases and gandharvas (3.147.39). [folk tradition]

## Bhakti Cult & Living Worship

- **Hanuman Chalisa (Tulsidas)** - A 40-verse (chalisa) Awadhi hymn by Goswami Tulsidas (16th c. CE), the single most-recited Hanuman text; praises his strength, learning, devotion, and protective power (e.g., the *vajra-deha*, leaping the sun, crossing the ocean). (Tulsidas, *Hanuman Chalisa*; companion to the *Ramcharitmanas*) [late text — 16th c.]
- **Ramcharitmanas (Tulsidas)** - Tulsidas's Awadhi Ramayana (begun 1574 CE, by its own dating to Samvat 1631) crystallized North Indian Hanuman bhakti, foregrounding Hanuman's role; the *Sundarkand* of the Manas is recited for protection. [late text]
- **Maruti / Anjaneya regional traditions** - In Maharashtra, Samarth Ramdas (17th c.) established Maruti temples linking Hanuman to strength, celibacy (brahmacharya), and civic vigor; in South India *Anjaneya* worship is widespread, often Panchamukha. (regional bhakti history) [scholarly]
- **Vows & observance** - Tuesday and Saturday are Hanuman's days; offerings of sindoor (vermilion, recalling his smearing himself red out of love for Sita), oil, and *boondi/laddoo*; **Hanuman Jayanti** marks his birth (date varies by region — Chaitra Purnima in the north; other dates in Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, etc.). [folk tradition / regional]
- **Functions in popular devotion** - Remover of fear and obstacles, guardian against evil spirits and Saturn's (Shani's) afflictions, patron of wrestlers and strength, and exemplar of selfless service.
