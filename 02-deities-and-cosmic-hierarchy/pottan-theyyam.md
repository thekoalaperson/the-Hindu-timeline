# Pottan Theyyam — Shiva as the Pulaya who rebukes Shankaracharya

> Where this sits in the hierarchy: `02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/` — node in [Deities & Cosmic Hierarchy](README.md). A North-Malabar **theyyam** in which **Śiva descends as an untouchable Pulaya corpse-bearer** to break the caste-purity claims of the Brahmin ācārya (identified in the *thoṭṭam* as [Śaṅkarācārya](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/adi-shankara.md)); read beside the Kerala hunter-god [Muthappan](muthappan.md), the fierce Keralan [Bhadrakāḷī / Bhagavati](bhadrakali-kerala-bhagavati.md), and the fierce forms of [Śiva's avatars & forms](shiva-avatars-and-forms.md). [folk] [oral tradition] [regional]

**Pottan Theyyam** (Malayalam *Poṭṭan Teyyam*, also *Poṭṭan Daivam*) is one of the most celebrated of the **400+ theyyams** of North Malabar (Kannur–Kasaragod, northern Kerala), performed in *kāvus* (sacred groves) and family shrines during the *Kaliyāṭṭam* season (roughly *Thulam*–*Edavam*, Oct–Jun). It is at once a **fire ritual** — the performer repeatedly throws himself onto a bed of live embers (the *meleri*) — and an **anti-caste charter myth**: its song, the *Poṭṭan thoṭṭam* (the *Pulappoṭṭan* dialogue), stages Śiva in the body of a drunken Pulaya (an "untouchable" caste of Malabar) confronting a high Brahmin on a narrow path and demolishing, line by line, the logic of untouchability. The theyyam is a **deity-possession** performance across caste lines, and its most famous refrain — *if you cut me I bleed, if you cut yourself you bleed the same blood* — is the closest thing Kerala's oral tradition has to a scriptural repudiation of caste hierarchy. This file catalogs the myth, the dialogue, and the ritual on their own terms, preserving variants rather than merging them. [folk] [oral tradition] [regional]

## The myth: Śiva as the Pulaya on the path

- **Śiva takes the Pulaya form** — to test (and to humble) the pride of a great Brahmin scholar, **Śiva descends in the body of a Pulayan** — a toddy-drinking, corpse- and load-bearing outcaste labourer of Malabar — sometimes accompanied by **Pārvatī as the Pulaya woman and Gaṇapati / Subrahmaṇya as the child**, moving along a forest path. (*Poṭṭan thoṭṭam-pāṭṭu*; North Malabar *kāvu* tradition) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The encounter on the narrow way** — the Pulaya meets the Brahmin ācārya face-to-face on a **path too narrow for two**. By the purity code, the untouchable must step aside, break contact, and let the twice-born pass without pollution; the Pulaya **refuses to yield** and instead opens a dispute. (*Poṭṭan thoṭṭam*) [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (identity of the Brahmin):* the *thoṭṭam* names the antagonist as **Śaṅkarācārya** himself — the advaita founder returning from a pilgrimage — making the myth a pointed folk **counter-legend to the Śaṅkara hagiography**. Other tellings leave him a generic **proud Nampūtiri Brahmin** or unnamed *ācārya*; the confrontation is constant, the precise identity is not. (*Poṭṭan thoṭṭam*; regional variants) [folk] [disputed]
  - *Note:* the choice of Śaṅkara is barbed — the Kerala-born formulator of non-dualism, who taught that **one undivided Brahman** underlies all beings, is caught by his own doctrine and made to concede that caste-difference is unreal. [scholarly]

- **The revelation** — after the argument, when the ācārya (in some tellings, chastened; in others, still hostile) presses on, **Śiva reveals his true form** — the Pulayan is unmasked as god, and the Brahmin bows. The deity is thereafter worshipped as **Poṭṭan Daivam** in the *kāvus*, the untouchable's body having become the throne of Śiva. (*Poṭṭan thoṭṭam*) [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (deified Pulaya devotee):* a parallel strand reads Poṭṭan not as Śiva-in-disguise but as a **historical Pulaya cult-hero** — a wronged or martyred outcaste later deified and merged with Śiva; the *theyyam* then carries a real memory of caste violence beneath the divine frame. [folk] [scholarly] [disputed]

## The thoṭṭam dialogue: the anti-caste charter

- **"The blood is the same"** — the core of the *Pulappoṭṭan* song is the Pulaya's rebuttal of pollution by appeal to **shared bodily substance**: *"When you are cut, do you not bleed as I do? When I am cut, red blood flows; when you are cut, is it not the same red blood?"* — caste can claim no basis in the body, for the substance of Brahmin and Pulaya is identical. (*Poṭṭan thoṭṭam-pāṭṭu*) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **"We drink the same water"** — the argument is extended item by item: **both drink the same water**, both are warmed by **the same sun**, both are quenched by **the same toddy**; the Pulaya's toddy and the Brahmin's are one liquor, the leaf-cup and the temple vessel hold the same drink. Difference of rank has no natural ground. (*Poṭṭan thoṭṭam*) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **"You too walk this earth"** — the song presses that **both tread the same ground, breathe the same air, and are born and die alike**; the fences of *tīṇḍal* (untouchability by proximity) and *tōṭṭukūṭāyma* are human inventions laid over a single humanity. (*Poṭṭan thoṭṭam*) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **"Who then is high, who low?"** — the dialogue turns the Brahmin's own logic against him: if the self within all is one (as the ācārya teaches), the distinction of *melajāti* (high caste) and *keezhjāti* (low caste) collapses. The Pulaya, the supposed inferior, out-reasons the master of scripture. (*Poṭṭan thoṭṭam*; read against advaita) [folk] [scholarly]
- **The name "Poṭṭan"** — glossed popularly as **"the deaf/mute one" or "the simpleton/fool,"** the epithet is ironic: the "fool" utters the wisest teaching in the tradition, and the "mute" out-argues the greatest debater. (Malayalam folk etymology) [folk]

## The fire ritual: the meleri and the ordeal

- **Performed on live embers** — Poṭṭan Theyyam's signature is the **meleri**: a great bed of **burning coconut-husk charcoal**. At the climax the possessed performer **repeatedly falls, sits, and rolls on the live embers** (*meleriyil veezhal*), rising unharmed — the fire ordeal read as proof of the indwelling god and as the outcaste's body made invulnerable. (North Malabar *Kaliyāṭṭam* tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The tender-leaf costume** — the Poṭṭan is clad in a distinctive dress of **split areca / betel spathe and tender coconut fronds** (green, perishable, forest material rather than fine cloth), with a large tiered *muṭi* (headdress) and red-and-white face-painting (*mukhattezhuttu*) — an iconography of the labouring, forest-edge outcaste rather than the temple deity. (Theyyam tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **Toddy and non-Brahmanical offering** — consistent with the Pulaya frame, **toddy (*kaḷḷ*) is offered and drunk** in performance, and the rite belongs to the **non-Brahman theyyam communities** (Pulaya, Vaṇṇāṉ / Malayan and allied performer castes) who alone embody the god — the "polluting" body that becomes divine. (Theyyam tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The performer as living god** — as in all theyyam, after the *thoṭṭam* song, face-painting and costume, the placing of the *muṭi* marks **Poṭṭan Daivam entering the dancer**, who is then venerated **across caste lines** — the low-caste performer literally worshipped by the high-caste patron, an inversion enacted annually. (Theyyam / *Kaliyāṭṭam* tradition) [folk] [regional]

## Placement: the "two clocks"

- **Scriptural / cyclic clock** — as a **descent of Śiva** in outcaste form to abolish false hierarchy, Poṭṭan attaches to the **timeless Śaiva manifestation register** (cf. [Śiva's avatars & forms](shiva-avatars-and-forms.md)) — a **cross-yuga** *līlā* of the Lord testing pride, with **no fixed Purāṇic yuga or date**; it belongs to the recurring myth-type of god disguised among the despised. [scholarly]
- **Academic / historical clock** — the *thoṭṭam*'s naming of **Śaṅkarācārya** pins the story's **narrative frame** to the ācārya's traditional floruit (**scriptural/hagiographic** 788–820 CE; **[scholarly]** c. 700 CE), but the **living theyyam** — the *meleri* fire-rite, the *thoṭṭam-pāṭṭu*, the cross-caste worship — is a **late-medieval / early-modern North-Malabar phenomenon**, part of the **pre-Brahmanical Dravidian theyyam substrate** later syncretized with Śaiva Purāṇic myth; its crystallization is **kali-historical and undated by scripture**. [scholarly] [regional]
- **Social reading** — modern scholarship reads Poṭṭan Theyyam as a **subaltern counter-theology**: a ritual in which the lowest caste, once a year, becomes God and lectures the highest on the fiction of purity — a "hidden transcript" of protest voiced in the very grammar (advaita, divine descent) of the dominant tradition. [scholarly]
