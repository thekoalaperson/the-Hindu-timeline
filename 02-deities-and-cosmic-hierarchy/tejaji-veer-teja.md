# Tejaji (Veer Teja) — the Jat serpent-hero who kept his word

> Parent: [Deities & Cosmic Hierarchy](README.md) · Master index: [../README.md](../README.md).
> Deities-and-hierarchy node — the **Tejājī gāthā** as a narrative in its own right: the peasant-warrior **Teja of Kharnal** who pledged his body to a serpent, rescued **Lāchhā Gūjarī's** cattle first, then returned mutilated and offered his **tongue** for the fangs, and was deified as the **snakebite-curing god of the Jats**. Companion to the roster/pīr node [tejaji.md](tejaji.md); parallel to [gugga-gogaji.md](gugga-gogaji.md) and [manasa.md](manasa.md).

---

## 1. Two Clocks — where Tejaji sits

Tejaji has **no scriptural clock**: he appears in no Veda, epic or Purāṇa. He is a hero of the *documented/historical* frame alone, carried by an oral ballad cycle and the shrine tradition of his fairs.

- **Historical clock** — folk-hagiography makes Tejājī a **Jat of the Dhauliyā (Dhaulyā) clan of Kharnal**, a village near Nagaur in Marwar. The traditional reckoning gives **birth Māgha śukla caturdaśī, V.S. 1130 (≈ 1074 CE)** and **death by the serpent's bite Bhādrapada śukla daśamī, V.S. 1160 (≈ 1103 CE)** — hagiographic dates, not documentary. [folk] [disputed]
  - *Scholarly note:* the historical-critical view can affirm only a **medieval Rajasthani hero-cult, c. 11th–12th century CE**, transmitted orally with no contemporaneous record; the precise Vikrama-Saṃvat dates are cult reckoning. [scholarly]
- **Scriptural clock** — **absent**. Tejājī's whole authority is the **Tejājī gāthā** (the *bhopā*-sung ballad cycle of the Teja shrines) and the *Tejā-gīt* / *Tejā-ṭer* sung by Jat cultivators in the monsoon fields. [oral tradition] [scholarly]
- **What he governs** — cure of **snakebite** above all (extended in living practice to dog-bite and other venoms), plus protection of **cattle, crops and the rains**; the pre-eminent deity of the Jat peasantry of Rajasthan, with reach into Malwa (Madhya Pradesh) and Haryana. [folk] [regional]
- **The theological engine — *satya*** — Tejā's snake-power is won not by boon or descent (as with [Gugga](gugga-gogaji.md)) but by **truth-keeping**: he honoured a vow to return and be bitten though it cost his life, and the *nāga*, conquered by that fidelity, decreed that **no venom would harm those who invoke Tejā's name**. Truth is the whole mechanism of the cult. [folk]
- **Divine identification** — many Jat devotees revere Tejā as an **emanation of Śiva**; the identification is devotional, not textual. [folk] [disputed]

## 2. Persons of the gāthā (catalog)

- **Tejājī / Vīr Tejā / Tejā Mahārāj** — the hero; *vīr* marks him as a deified warrior of the *jhuṃjhār / vīr* class of Rajasthan. [regional]
- **Tāhaṛ Dev (Tāhaṛjī)** and **Rāmkuṃvarī** — Teja's father, the Jat headman of Kharnal, and his mother; in the pious telling Teja is born by the grace of the propitiated **Nāga/serpent-deity**, so the serpent bond is present from birth. (Tejājī gāthā) [folk]
- **Līlaṇ (Leelan)** — Teja's inseparable **mare**; his fixed icon is the **sword-bearing rider on Līlaṇ with a serpent striking at his outstretched tongue**, carved on shrine-stones (*devalī*) across the cult region. [regional]
- **Pemal (Pemal De)** — Teja's **bride**, daughter of a chief of **Paner** (the Ajmer–Pushkar tract), married to him in **childhood**; a **blood-feud between the two families at the wedding** left the marriage unconsummated and unspoken of at Kharnal. (Tejājī gāthā) [folk]
- **Lāchhā Gūjarī (Lāchhāṃ)** — the **herdswoman of Paner**, Pemal's companion, whose stolen cattle Teja rides to recover; the pivot of the whole tragedy, and the figure whose distress makes Teja's death inevitable. (Tejājī gāthā) [folk] [regional]
- **The raiders** — in the standard telling the **Mīṇās of Chāṅg** who drive off Lāchhā's herd by night. [folk]
- **Rājal (Rājal Bāī)** — Teja's **sister**, venerated in parts of the cult region as a deified kinswoman. [folk] [regional]

## 3. The core narrative, in order

The ballad is a truth-and-honour tragedy told in four movements — **the taunt, the vow, the cattle-rescue, the tongue-bite** — and turns on the fact that Teja discharges his *worldly* obligation (Lāchhā's cows) **before** his *fatal* one (the serpent). Recensions differ in detail; the through-line is constant.

- **The taunt** — grown to manhood unaware of his childhood marriage, Teja is ploughing when his **bhābhī (elder brother's wife)** brings his meal late and, rebuked, **taunts him to go fetch his own wife from Paner** rather than scold her. Stung, he learns of the feud-broken marriage and rides out at once on Līlaṇ, disregarding his mother's warnings and the ill omens on the road. (Tejājī gāthā) [folk]
- **The serpent in the burning bush, and the vow** — on the way Teja finds a **nāga trapped in a burning bush** and pulls it from the flames. Instead of gratitude the snake declares it **must bite him**. Teja does not refuse; he begs only **leave to finish his errand — to bring his bride home — and vows to return to this very spot to receive the bite**. On his word the serpent releases him. (Tejājī gāthā) [folk]
  - *Variant (the burnt mate):* in the widespread telling the serpent claims the bite because its **mate perished in the same fire** while it alone was saved — Teja's rescue is thus also, in the snake's eyes, an injury. [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (placement):* some recensions set the burning-serpent episode on the **outward** journey to Paner, others on the **return**; the vow-and-return structure is identical either way. [folk] [oral tradition]
- **Lāchhā's cattle carried off** — at Paner, where Pemal's kin (mindful of the feud) resist the reunion, it is **Lāchhā Gūjarī** who aids the couple. That very night **raiders (the Mīṇās of Chāṅg) drive off Lāchhā's herd**, and she cries to Teja for help. (Tejājī gāthā) [folk]
- **The cattle-rescue** — Teja rides alone after the raiders, fights them, and **recovers the whole herd**, taking grievous wounds over his entire body. He puts the herdswoman's need before his own vow — the ethical hinge of the tale. (Tejājī gāthā) [folk]
  - *Variant (the missing calf):* in beloved recensions Teja, having returned the herd, **rides a second time to recover a single calf** still held by the raiders — completing the rescue to the last animal before turning to the serpent. [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The tongue offered for the fangs** — bleeding from every limb, Teja keeps his word and returns to the serpent, which finds **no unwounded spot on his body fit to strike**. Teja therefore **puts out his tongue** — unbloodied — and the *nāga* bites him there. The self-offering of the one clean part of a body given entirely to duty is the cult's central image. (Tejājī gāthā) [folk]
- **The boon of the serpent** — conquered by such truthfulness, the *nāga* grants the dying hero the charter of his cult: **whoever, bitten by a snake, ties a thread (tāṅtī) in Tejā's name and remembers him shall take no harm from the venom**, and snakes will not harm his devotees. (Tejājī gāthā) [folk]
- **Death at Sursurā, and Pemal's satī** — Teja dies of the bite on **Bhādrapada śukla daśamī** at **Sursurā (Ajmer district)**, where his death-shrine stands; the mare **Līlaṇ** is said to carry the news home, and **Pemal becomes satī** on his pyre. (Tejājī gāthā / Sursurā sthala tradition) [folk] [regional]

## 4. The tāṅtī-thread snakebite cult

- **The tāṅtī rite** — the cult's signature act: a **consecrated thread (tāṅtī)** is tied on a snakebite victim (commonly at the wrist or the bitten limb) **in Tejājī's name**, binding the venom by the hero's boon. The victim (or kin) then owes a **vow-visit to a Tejā shrine — ideally on Teja Daśamī — to untie the thread** and offer for the cure. [folk] [regional]
- **Scope of the cure** — in living practice the tāṅtī and Tejā's name are invoked not only for snakebite but for **dog-bite and other venomous bites**, making Teja a general poison-curing god of the countryside. [folk]
- **Shrines (thāns / devrās)** — worshipped at village **thāns** and **devrās** across Rajasthan (densest in Nagaur and Ajmer and the surrounding Jat country), usually as a **hero-stone or image of the rider on Līlaṇ with the serpent at his tongue**; principal seats at **Kharnal** (birthplace), **Sursurā** (death-site), **Parbatsar** (fair-seat) and **Paner** (the bride's home). [regional]
- **Possession-mediums and the whip** — at the shrines and fairs, possession-mediums (the cult's *bhopā*-type functionaries) become vehicles of Teja, dance under possession, and in dramatic displays **flagellate themselves with a whip** as proof of the god's presence while treating snakebite vows. [folk] [regional]
- **Songs of the monsoon** — the **Tejā-gīt / Tejā-ṭer**, sung by Jat cultivators in the fields during the rains (the ploughing-and-snake season), carry the gāthā orally; like the [Gugga-gāthā](gugga-gogaji.md) they are **anonymous, fluid and performative**, with no fixed literary text. [folk] [oral tradition] [scholarly]

## 5. Teja Daśamī and the three fairs

- **Teja Daśamī** — Tejājī's festival falls on the **tenth (daśamī) of the bright fortnight of Bhādrapada (c. August–September)**, his traditional death-day — one day after [Gugga](gugga-gogaji.md)'s dark-fortnight Navamī in the same monsoon snakebite season that governs [Manasā](manasa.md)'s calendar in the east. Devotees fulfil tāṅtī-vows, offer coconuts, *churmā* and thread at the thāns, and processions carry Teja's image. [regional]
- **Kharnal** — the **birthplace** shrine (Nagaur district); the grand temple of Teja stands here, and the day draws Jat pilgrims to the hero's *janma-bhūmi*. [regional]
- **Parbatsar** — around Teja Daśamī, Parbatsar (Nagaur district) holds the annual **Vīr Tejājī cattle fair**, one of Rajasthan's largest livestock fairs, where the **herdsman-protector god presides over the trading of cattle, camels and horses** — the cattle-rescuer honoured with a cattle-market. [regional]
- **Sursurā** — the **death-site** shrine (Ajmer district); pilgrimage and fair mark the spot where Teja died of the bite, the natural terminus of a snakebite vow-journey. [regional]
- **Diaspora observance** — parallel fairs are kept among Jat migrant communities in **Malwa** and **Haryana**, carrying the cult beyond its Marwar heartland. [regional]

## 6. Significance — a peasant's god, twinned with Gugga

- **A cultivator's sanctity** — Tejājī is the outstanding **Jat agrarian hero-cult**: his holiness rests on kept truth, cattle-protection and death in the service of a herdswoman — the values of the ploughing and herding castes — and his cult runs on **hero-stones, threads, possession and song**, outside Brahmanical temple-orthodoxy, like [Gugga](gugga-gogaji.md), [Khaṇḍobā](khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md) and the [village grāmadevatās](gramadevatas-village-deities.md). [scholarly]
- **Two snakebite hero-gods, adjacent feast-days** — Rajasthan carries **two great snakebite-curing hero-gods** with neighbouring Bhādrapada dates: **Gugga**, the Chauhan Rajput of the Bāgar (dark-fortnight Navamī), and **Tejā**, the Jat of Kharnal (bright-fortnight Daśamī). Their cults overlap geographically but stay distinct in **caste base, narrative and rite** — Gugga's power from heroic death and Gorakhnāth's boon, Tejā's from *satya*. [scholarly] [regional]
- **Outside the pāñch pīr** — though of the same class and stature, Tejājī stands **outside** the classical five pīrs (Pābūjī, Haṛbhūjī, [Rāmdev](ramdev-pir.md), Mehājī Māṅgaliyā, Gogājī); the roster and the pīr idiom are treated in the companion node [tejaji.md](tejaji.md). [folk] [regional]
