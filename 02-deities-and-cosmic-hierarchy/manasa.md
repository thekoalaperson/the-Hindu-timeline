# Manasā — the Serpent-Goddess of Eastern India

> Deities-and-hierarchy node. **Manasā** (Bengali *Mā Mansā*, also **Manasā Devī**, **Bishahari**, **Padmāvatī**, **Janguli**) is the pan-eastern serpent-goddess of Bengal, Bihar, Assam, Jharkhand and Odisha — averter of snakebite and giver of fertility, Sanskritized as a daughter of **[Śiva](shiva-myth-cycle.md)** and immortalized in the **Manasāmaṅgal** poem-cycle whose heart is the **Behulā–Lakhindar** tale. ## 1. Two Clocks — Who Manasā Is

The figure has two strata that must be kept distinct: a thin **scriptural** snake-goddess and a thick **historical** Bengali cult-goddess whose great literature is securely datable.

- **Scriptural clock** - the Purāṇic snake-goddess **Manasā / Jaratkāru / Manasādevī**, sister of the cosmic serpent-king **Vāsuki**, appears only as a marginal name in the **Mahābhārata**'s Āstīka-parva snake-genealogies and a handful of late Purāṇic and Tantric lists; she has no developed myth there. (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva; *Brahmavaivarta*, *Devī-Bhāgavata*, *Padma Purāṇa* — late strata) [late text]
- **Historical clock** - the living goddess **Mā Mansā** is a folk-deity of the eastern wetlands whose worship is documented from roughly the medieval period and whose epic literature, the **Manasāmaṅgal**, is dated to the **15th–18th centuries CE** by named, datable poets. This is the goddess actually worshipped. [folk] [scholarly]
- **What she governs** - protection from and cure of **snakebite** (a constant deadly hazard of the Bengal–Assam floodplain), and by extension **fertility, prosperity and child-getting**; she is invoked above all in the monsoon months when snakes are driven into homes. [folk]
- **The core theological engine** - Manasā is a goddess who must **compel recognition**: nearly every narrative about her turns on a human (or god) who *refuses* to worship her, whom she afflicts until they submit. She is the archetype of the new, unestablished deity forcing her way into the pantheon. [scholarly]

## 2. Names and Epithets (catalog)

- **Manasā** - the standard name, popularly etymologized from *manas* ("mind / will") — she who is born of, or grants wishes of, the mind. [folk etymology]
- **Mā Mansā / Mansā Devī** - the everyday Bengali devotional name.
- **Bishahari / Viṣaharī** - "remover of poison (*viṣa*)," her functional epithet, dominant in Bihar and among Maithil/Bhojpuri communities (the **Bishahari pūjā**). [regional]
- **Padmā / Padmāvatī** - "she of the lotus," because she is said to be enthroned on or to dwell in a lotus; a major Bengali name (the river **Padmā** is folk-linked to her). [regional]
- **Janguli / Jāṅgulī** - the snake-poison-curing goddess of Buddhist Tantra (a form of Tārā), widely held by scholars to be a Buddhist parallel/source of Manasā. (Buddhist Sādhanamālā) [scholarly] [disputed identification]
- **Jaratkāru / Jagatgaurī / Nityā / Nāgeśvarī / Sarpamātā** - further Sanskritizing names in the *maṅgal* invocations and late Tantric lists.
- **Astika-mātā** - "mother of Āstīka," after her son (§4). (Mahābhārata)

## 3. Sanskritization — Daughter of Śiva and the Excluded One

The *maṅgal* poets graft the folk goddess onto the Śaiva family, but pointedly as an **outsider within it** — a half-belonging that mirrors her cult's struggle for status. Never merge the variant birth-stories; the poets disagree.

- **Birth from Śiva's seed/mind** - in the dominant *Manasāmaṅgal* frame, Manasā is born when **[Śiva](shiva-myth-cycle.md)**'s semen (or the heat of his desire) falls upon a lotus / a clay snake-image in the netherworld and quickens; she is thus Śiva's daughter but **motherless from Pārvatī**, conceived without her. (Manasāmaṅgal — Bijay Gupta; Ketakādāsa)
  - *Variant (Vāsuki's sister, Source: Mahābhārata):* in the Sanskrit substrate she is simply the **sister of the nāga-king Vāsuki**, with no Śaiva parentage at all — the daughter-of-Śiva genealogy is a Bengali *maṅgal* innovation. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Kadrū's line):* some lists make her a daughter of the sage **Kaśyapa** and **Kadrū**, the mother of all serpents — assimilating her to the standard nāga genealogy. [late text]
- **Rivalry with Caṇḍī / Pārvatī** - the *kāvya*'s most vivid scenes: **[Pārvatī](sati-daksha-and-parvati.md)** (here **Caṇḍī**), Śiva's wife, hates this co-wife's-rival daughter; she insults and strikes her, and in one episode **destroys one of Manasā's eyes** with a flower or a glance, leaving Manasā **one-eyed (Kāṇī)** — a marked, folk feature of her iconography. (Manasāmaṅgal — Ketakādāsa Kṣemānanda)
- **Marriage to Jaratkāru** - Manasā is married to the ascetic sage **Jaratkāru** (Bengali *Jarātkāru / Jaratkārū*), who abandons her on their wedding night over a trivial slight; her fury at this desertion is part of her dangerous temperament. She bears him the sage **Āstīka**. (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva; Manasāmaṅgal)
- **Āstīka the peace-maker** - their son **Āstīka** halts King **Janamejaya**'s **Sarpa-satra** (snake-sacrifice) that was annihilating the serpent race — the Mahābhārata's own snake-rescue myth, which the *maṅgal* tradition absorbs to credit Manasā as protectress of all nāgas. (Mahābhārata, Āstīka-parva)
- **Neera / Netā the confidante** - the *maṅgal*s give Manasā a created companion **Netā / Neto** (a dhobin/washerwoman fashioned from Śiva's body-dirt) who advises her and helps revive the dead — a folk-narrative device. [folk]

## 4. The Manasāmaṅgal Kāvya Cycle

The **Manasāmaṅgal** (or **Manasā-vijaya**, "the triumph of Manasā") is one of the great **maṅgal-kāvya** — Bengali narrative-devotional poems (15th–18th c.) celebrating a deity's forcing of worship onto an unwilling devotee. It is the single most important source for the goddess.

- **The genre** - *maṅgal-kāvya* are long verse-cycles sung in village performance over multiple nights; each glorifies a popular deity (Manasā, Caṇḍī, Dharma, Śītalā) by narrating how that deity established their *pūjā* against human resistance. (Bengali literary history) [scholarly]
- **The named poets (the "historical clock" anchors):**
  - *Variant (Vipradāsa Pipilāi, c. 1495 CE):* his **Manasā-vijaya** is among the earliest datable recensions (composed under Sultan Hussain Shah's reign). [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Bijay Gupta / Vijaya Gupta, c. 1494 CE):* the most celebrated and widely sung recension, fixing the standard plot. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Ketakādāsa Kṣemānanda, 17th c.):* a later, literarily admired recension that develops Caṇḍī's cruelty and Behulā's grief. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Nārāyaṇdeb / Nārāyaṇ Dev):* the recension dominant in **Assam and East Bengal** (the *Padmā-purāṇa* / *Suknani* tradition). [regional]
- **Reliability note** - because the *maṅgal*s have named authors and (often) named patrons and rulers, they are datable **historical** texts about a **folk** goddess — a rare case where the cult's literary history is firm even though the deity is non-Vedic. [scholarly]

## 5. The Behulā–Lakhindar Narrative (the central tale)

The emotional core of every recension is the duel between Manasā and the proud merchant **Chand Sadagar**, resolved by the heroism of his daughter-in-law **Behulā**. Events in narrative order:

- **Chand's refusal** - **Chānd Sadāgar (Chand the merchant)**, a wealthy Śiva-devotee of Champaknagar, **refuses to worship Manasā**, holding that he will not bow to a low-born upstart goddess; he is a *siddha* protected by the boon-spell **Mahājñāna** taught him by Śiva. (Manasāmaṅgal)
- **Escalating affliction** - Manasā destroys Chand's beloved flower-garden, kills his friend, wrecks his merchant fleet at sea, and **kills his six elder sons** one by one — Chand still refuses. (Manasāmaṅgal)
- **The doomed seventh son** - Chand's seventh son **Lakhindar (Lakṣmīndara / Lakhāi)** is born; the gods reveal he is fated to **die of snakebite on his wedding night** — for he and his bride are two cursed *gandharva*-class celestials (see [../08-beings-and-bestiary/gandharvas-and-kinnaras.md](../08-beings-and-bestiary/gandharvas-and-kinnaras.md)) reborn to play out Manasā's revenge. (Manasāmaṅgal)
- **Behulā the bride** - he is married to **Behulā (Behulā / Bipulā)**, a paragon of wifely virtue (*satī*). (Manasāmaṅgal)
- **The iron bridal chamber** - knowing the prophecy, Chand builds an **impenetrable iron nuptial chamber (*lohā-bāsar / bāsar-ghar*)** with no gap for any serpent. The divine architect **Viśvakarmā**, coerced/tricked by Manasā, deliberately **leaves a needle-fine hole** in the iron. (Manasāmaṅgal — Bijay Gupta)
- **Lakhindar's death** - on the wedding night Manasā sends the serpent **Kālnāgini (Kālanāginī)** through the pinhole; **Lakhindar is bitten and dies** beside the sleeping Behulā. (Manasāmaṅgal)
- **The raft-journey** - by custom a snakebite victim is not cremated but set afloat (in case of revival); Behulā refuses to part from him and floats **down-river on a raft with her husband's decaying corpse**, enduring months of horror, decomposition and the assaults of men, to reach the gods. (Manasāmaṅgal)
- **The dance before the gods** - Behulā arrives at the **abode of the gods** (via Netā the washerwoman, §3), and **dances so beautifully before Śiva and the assembled devas** that they grant her boon. (Manasāmaṅgal)
- **The bargain and revival** - Manasā agrees to **restore Lakhindar and Chand's six other sons to life and his sunken wealth** — on the condition that Behulā secure **Chand's worship**. (Manasāmaṅgal)
- **Chand's left-handed submission** - Chand finally yields, but, still a Śaiva, **offers the pūjā-flower with his left hand while looking away** — a famous image of grudging, half-given devotion; nonetheless Manasā's worship is thereby **established among humankind**. (Manasāmaṅgal)

## 6. Iconography and Worship

- **Iconography** - a golden or yellow goddess seated on a **lotus** or standing on a **snake**, **canopied by a hood of (often seven) cobras**, a serpent in hand, sometimes a child on her knee; frequently shown **one-eyed (Kāṇī)** per the Caṇḍī episode (§3). (Bengali icon tradition; *maṅgal* dhyānas) [regional]
- **Aniconic forms** - very commonly worshipped **without an image**: in a branch of the *sij* (cactus-like euphorbia) plant, a *ghaṭa* (pot), a snake-image, or a painted **clay-mound / mancā**, especially in village and household rites. [folk]
- **Manasā-pūjā** - her festival falls chiefly on the last day of the month of **Śrāvaṇa (Āṣāṛh–Śrāvaṇa, c. July–August)** — the height of the monsoon and the snake season; celebrated across **Bengal, Assam, Bihar, Jharkhand, Tripura and parts of Odisha**. [regional]
- **The Bishahari / Maithil rite** - in Mithilā and the Bhojpuri belt she is **Bishahari**, worshipped in groups (the "five sisters" *Bishahari* set) for protection against poison. [regional]
- **No mainstream temple-orthodoxy** - she is largely a **domestic and folk** goddess; her cult sits outside the great Brahmanical temple establishment even where she is acknowledged as Śiva's daughter — a status mirrored in her own myth. [scholarly]

## 7. The Puranic Snake-Goddess Strata Behind Her

- **Nāga-worship substrate** - Manasā crystallizes a far older, pan-Indian **serpent (nāga) cult**; she is the eastern, feminine focus of veneration that elsewhere takes the form of **Nāga-pañcamī** and male nāga-kings. [scholarly]
- **Late Purāṇic naming** - a goddess **Manasā / Manasādevī** appears in late layers of the **Brahmavaivarta**, **Devī-Bhāgavata** and **Padma** Purāṇas, usually slotted into the Prakṛti / minor-goddess lists and given the Śiva-daughter or Kaśyapa-Kadrū genealogy after the fact. [late text] [disputed]
- **Tantric Janguli link** - scholars connect her to the Buddhist snake-poison goddess **Jāṅgulī** (a Tārā emanation in the *Sādhanamālā*), suggesting a shared eastern-Indian poison-curing goddess substratum that surfaced in both Buddhist and Hindu dress. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Roster cross-reference** - her single-line entry as a grāmadevatā is in [devi-forms-and-navadurga.md](devi-forms-and-navadurga.md); this file is the first-tier expansion of that line.
