# Jyotiba (Kedarling) of Wadi Ratnagiri — demon-slayer guardian of Kolhapur

> Where this sits in the hierarchy: `02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/` — node in [Deities & Cosmic Hierarchy](README.md). A Deccan **folk-Śaiva warrior-guardian god** of western Maharashtra, worshipped on the **Wadi Ratnagiri** hill above Kolhapur as **Kedārliṅg / Kedāreśvara**, held to be a **combined descent of Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva** (with a Jamadagni-aṃśa in some tellings) sent to free the **Karavīra** country of demons. He is the **male counterpart** of the enthroned Goddess of [Kolhapur — Mahālakṣmī / Ambābāī (Karavīra Kṣetra)](../06-sacred-geography/kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md), and a Deccan cousin of [Khaṇḍobā / Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava](khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md). [folk] [regional]
>
> Parent: [Deities & Cosmic Hierarchy](README.md)

**Jyotibā** (Marathi *Jyotibā*, also **Jotibā**, **Kedārliṅg / Kedāreśvara**, **Vāḍī-Ratnāgirīcā Jyotibā**, **Dākhaṇcā Rājā** "king of the Deccan," honorific **Śrī Jyotibā**) is a hill-god of **Wadi Ratnagiri**, a spur roughly **18 km north-west of Kolhapur** in Maharashtra. His *sthala* tradition — carried chiefly in the Marathi devotional narrative **Kedāra Vijaya** and in the **Karavīra-māhātmya** cycle attached to Kolhapur — makes him the martial protector who cleared the Karavīra region of the demons **Kolhāsura**, **Ratnāsura** and **Raktabhoja** so that the Goddess **Mahālakṣmī** could sit in peace. His cult is defined by its distinctive **pink/rose gulāl** (the cry *"Cāṅg bhalā!"*), setting it apart from Khaṇḍobā's yellow turmeric. This file catalogs his identity, myth, consorts, festivals and placement, cataloging variants rather than merging them.

---

## Identity & names

- **Kedārliṅg / Kedāreśvara** — the Sanskritizing name identifies Jyotibā with **Śiva as the liṅga of Kedāra** (cf. the Himalayan Kedārnāth), transplanting the Kedāra-Śiva onto the Wadi Ratnagiri hill; the shrine's central image is worshipped as a **svayambhū liṅga / Kedāreśvara**. (*Kedāra Vijaya*) [regional]
- **Jyotibā / Jotibā** — the everyday Marathi name, popularly derived from *jyoti* ("light, flame"), marking him as a **god born of / manifest as radiant light** at the sages' need; the affectionate *-bā* is the same honorific seen in Khaṇḍobā, Viṭhobā. [regional] [folk]
- **Dākhaṇcā Rājā** — "King of the Deccan (Dakhan)," a royal-guardian epithet asserting sovereignty over the Karavīra country he freed. [regional]
- **The Trimūrti-aṃśa god** — the defining theological claim of the tradition: Jyotibā is a **single deity compounded of the aṃśas (portions) of Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Maheśa (Śiva)** — a rare **fused-Trimūrti** manifestation (see [Trimūrti & Tridevī](trimurti-and-tridevi.md)) produced by the three gods together to answer the sages' appeal. (*Kedāra Vijaya*) [regional]
  - *Variant (with a fourth aṃśa):* some tellings add the aṃśa of the sage **Jamadagni** (father of Paraśurāma; consort of [Reṇukā](renuka-yellamma.md)), making the god a **fourfold** compound and tying him to the Paraśurāma-Reṇukā Deccan complex. [folk] [disputed]
  - *Variant (a form of Śiva alone):* in the plainest devotional register Jyotibā is simply **Kedāreśvara-Śiva** himself descended as a warrior — the Trimūrti reading being an elaboration; the underlying stratum is a **folk-Śaiva hill deity** later Brahmanized, structurally parallel to Khaṇḍobā. [scholarly] [disputed]

## Birth to sage Paugaṇḍa (Pougaṇḍ)

- **Born to the ṛṣi Paugaṇḍa** — the *Kedāra Vijaya* frame has the god take birth as the **son of the sage Paugaṇḍa (Pougaṇḍ)** and his wife (named **Vimalāmbujā** in some recensions), appearing to the childless ascetic as a **radiant infant / flame-child** — hence *Jyoti*-bā. The birth is a **theophany answering the sages' prayer**, not an ordinary generation. (*Kedāra Vijaya*) [regional]
  - *Variant (self-manifest, no human parent):* other tellings dispense with a human father and have Jyotibā **manifest directly as light from the Trimūrti** at the sacrifice of the harassed ṛṣis, later adopting the Wadi Ratnagiri hill; the Paugaṇḍa-parentage is a devotional framing of an essentially **svayambhū** god. [folk]
- **Purpose of the descent** — like the [Devī Māhātmya](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) goddess and like [Khaṇḍobā](khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md), Jyotibā is **produced by the gods for a single mandate**: to destroy the demons oppressing the sages and the Goddess and to establish a standing protective seat over Karavīra. (*Kedāra Vijaya*) [regional]

## The demon-slayings: Ratnāsura, Kolhāsura and Raktabhoja

- **The tyranny over Karavīra** — the *asuras* infesting the Karavīra (Kolhapur) country oppress the sages and gods and menace the Goddess herself; the harassed devotees appeal for a deliverer, and the Trimūrti-born Jyotibā takes up the war. The demons named across the cycle are **Kolhāsura**, his associate/kin **Ratnāsura** (whence **Ratnāgiri**, the hill), and **Raktabhoja** (var. **Raktabhīja / Raktabhoj**, "blood-devourer"). (*Kedāra Vijaya*; *Karavīra-māhātmya*) [regional]
- **Ratnāsura and the naming of Ratnagiri** — Jyotibā slays the demon **Ratnāsura** on the hill that thereafter bears the name **Ratnāgiri** ("jewel-mountain"), where the god then settles — the shrine village **Vāḍī-Ratnāgiri** taking its name from the victory-site. (*Kedāra Vijaya*) [regional]
- **Kolhāsura — the shared demon of the Kolhapur charter** — the demon **Kolhāsura** who gives Kolhāpura its name is slain in this cycle; in the Goddess's own charter (the [Karavīra-māhātmya](../06-sacred-geography/kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md)) the killing of Kolhāsura is credited to **Mahālakṣmī**, while the Jyotibā *sthala* tradition credits (or shares) the deed to **Jyotibā as the Goddess's guardian-general** — the **two shrines narrating the same demon-war from complementary sides**. (*Kedāra Vijaya*; *Karavīra-māhātmya*) [regional] [disputed]
  - *Variant (division of labour):* the reconciling telling has **Mahālakṣmī slay Kolhāsura** and **Jyotibā slay Ratnāsura / Raktabhoja**, the god acting as the Goddess's **commander (sarsenāpati)** who clears the outlying demons so she can dispatch the chief — this is the sense in which Jyotibā is called the **male counterpart / protector of Ambābāī**. [regional] [folk]
- **Raktabhoja / Raktabīja motif** — the demon **Raktabhoja** ("blood-eater") echoes the **Raktabīja** of the *Devī Māhātmya* (whose every blood-drop spawns a clone); the Deccan retelling reuses this famous *Śākta* demon-motif for the Karavīra war, a sign of the cult's absorption of the pan-Indian Goddess mythology. (*Kedāra Vijaya*; cf. *Devī Māhātmya*, Śumbha–Niśumbha cycle) [scholarly] [regional]
- **Freeing Karavīra & taking the hill** — with the demons dead, Jyotibā **liberates the Karavīra country** and establishes himself permanently on **Wadi Ratnagiri** as its guardian, from where he watches over Kolhapur below; the Goddess's peace and the god's mountain-seat are the twin results of the war. (*Kedāra Vijaya*) [regional]

## Consorts, retinue and iconography

- **Yamāī (Jogāī) — the consort** — Jyotibā's principal goddess-consort is **Yamāī** (also **Jogāī / Yogeśvarī**), whose own shrine stands on the hill near his; she is worshipped as his **Śakti**, aligning the pair with the Śiva–Śakti / Bhairava–Yoginī pattern. (regional tradition) [regional] [folk]
- **Retinue** — the god is attended by subordinate deities and guardians on the hill, and his **palanquin (pālkhī)** and **sāsan-kāṭhī** (the tall ceremonial poles/staves carried in procession) are central cult objects, paralleling the Deccan warrior-god processional style. [regional] [folk]
- **The mounted-warrior icon** — Jyotibā is depicted as a **martial deity**, often **mounted and armed**, in the equestrian warrior-god idiom of the Deccan shared with [Khaṇḍobā](khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md) and the *vīra* / *kāval* guardian gods; the shrine's inner image, however, is the aniconic **Kedāreśvara liṅga**. [regional]

## The pink gulāl and the Caitra yātrā

- **Gulāl, not turmeric** — the signature of Jyotibā worship is the **pink/rose gulāl** (*gulāl* powder) hurled over the deity, the palanquins and the crowds — the hill and its pilgrims turn **pink**, exactly as Jejuri turns **yellow** with Khaṇḍobā's *bhaṇḍāra*. The two Deccan warrior-gods are thus a matched pair distinguished by their **colour-substance** (rose *gulāl* vs. turmeric *bhaṇḍāra*). [regional] [folk]
- **The Caitra yātrā** — the great annual pilgrimage falls on **Caitra Pūrṇimā** (the full moon of Caitra, roughly March–April), when huge crowds and hundreds of **sāsan-kāṭhī** processions from surrounding villages converge on Wadi Ratnagiri; the air fills with gulāl and the cry **"Cāṅg bhalā!"** ("all good / hail the good one!"). A second major gathering falls around **Krṣṇa/Vaiśākha** and on **ṣaṣṭhī / other lunar dates**. (regional practice) [regional] [folk]
- **"Cāṅg bhalā!"** — the god's characteristic acclamation, invoked with the throwing of gulāl; it functions as Jyotibā's equivalent of Khaṇḍobā's *"Yelkoṭ! Jai Mālhār!"* [regional] [folk]

## Male counterpart of the Kolhapur Goddess

- **The charter-pair of Karavīra** — Jyotibā (guardian on the hill) and **Mahālakṣmī / Ambābāī** (enthroned in the city) form a **single sacred charter of the Karavīra country**: the Goddess is the seated sovereign power, Jyotibā the **martial guardian who secured her seat** by clearing the demons. Kolhapur pilgrims traditionally pair the two *darśanas* — the Goddess below and her guardian-god above. See the Goddess's own charter in [Kolhapur — Mahālakṣmī (Karavīra Kṣetra)](../06-sacred-geography/kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md). [regional]
- **Shared demon, complementary myths** — because **Kolhāsura** appears in *both* the Goddess's *māhātmya* and the god's *sthala* tradition, the two shrines preserve the **same demon-war told twice**; the corpus records this as **complementary regional variants**, not a contradiction to be resolved. [regional] [disputed]

## Placement — the "two clocks"

- **Scriptural / cyclic clock** — as a **Trimūrti-born demon-slaying descent** with no yuga fixed in the texts, Jyotibā's war belongs to the **cross-yuga** class of Śaiva–Śākta *līlās* (like the Maṇi-Malla slaying of [Khaṇḍobā](khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md) or the Kolhāsura myth of the Goddess): a deed of the deep Purāṇic past establishing a seat that **stands active through the present [Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/README.md)**. The tradition treats Karavīra, like the [Śakti-pīṭha](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) seats, as a persisting power-center rather than a dated event. [regional] [late text]
- **Academic / historical clock** — the *cult* of Jyotibā is a **regional Deccan warrior-god tradition** whose Wadi Ratnagiri temple and festival are documented **medieval-to-modern**: the standing shrine and its patronage are associated with the **later medieval / Maratha era** (renovations credited to local chiefs and to the Kolhapur state), and the **Kedāra Vijaya** narrative that Sanskritizes the folk god is a **late devotional text**, on the same Brahmanizing pattern as the *Mallāri Māhātmya* for Khaṇḍobā. The god's underlying layer is read by folklorists as a **pastoral/warrior hill deity** later fused with Kedāreśvara-Śiva. [scholarly] [late text]

## Sources

- **Kedāra Vijaya** — the Marathi *sthala* narrative of Jyotibā / Kedāreśvara of Wadi Ratnagiri: the birth to sage Paugaṇḍa, the Trimūrti (and Jamadagni) aṃśa, and the slaying of Ratnāsura, Kolhāsura and Raktabhoja to free Karavīra. [regional] [late text]
- **Karavīra-māhātmya** (attached to the *Padma Purāṇa* / *Skanda Purāṇa* recensions) — the Kolhapur *kṣetra-māhātmya* narrating the Kolhāsura myth and the Goddess's enthronement, the complementary charter that shares the demon-war with the Jyotibā tradition (see [kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md](../06-sacred-geography/kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md)). [regional] [late text]
- **Devī Māhātmya** (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa) — source of the **Raktabīja** blood-clone motif reused for the Karavīra "Raktabhoja"; comparandum for the deity-born-to-slay-a-demon pattern. [scholarly]
- Regional Marathi devotion & practice — the Wadi Ratnagiri temple cult, the consort **Yamāī / Jogāī**, the **sāsan-kāṭhī** processions, the pink **gulāl**, the **Caitra Pūrṇimā yātrā**, and the acclamation **"Cāṅg bhalā!"**. [regional] [folk] [oral tradition]
- Comparative scholarship on Deccan warrior-gods (Sontheimer and others on Khaṇḍobā/Mailāra and pastoral tutelary deities) — for the reading of Jyotibā as a folk hill-god Brahmanized into Kedāreśvara-Śiva, and for the Jyotibā–Khaṇḍobā (gulāl vs. turmeric) pairing. [scholarly]
