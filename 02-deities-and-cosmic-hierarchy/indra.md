# Indra — King of the Devas, Wielder of the Vajra

> Where this sits in the hierarchy: `02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/` — **Indra** (Śakra, Devendra,
> Purandara, Maghavan) is the *rājan* of the [devas](devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md), sovereign of
> **Svarga** and ruler of the **east**. Once the supreme, most-hymned god of the
> [Ṛgveda](../90-literature-corpus/), he is progressively demoted across the Purāṇic period beneath
> the [Trimūrti](trimurti-and-tridevi.md) and the avatāras of [Viṣṇu](vishnu-forms-and-vyuhas.md). His
> deeds cluster in the eternal [Devāsura wars](devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md) and recur every
> [manvantara](../01-manvantaras/), since **"Indra" is an office, not a person**. See also his vajra in
> [`../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/`](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/) and the master
> [TIMELINE](../TIMELINE.md).

---

## Identity, parentage & domain

- **Indra / Śakra / Devendra** — king of the devas, god of thunder, lightning, rain, rivers and war;
  guardian (*lokapāla*) of the **east**; rides the white elephant **Airāvata** and the chariot/horse
  context, wields the **vajra** (thunderbolt) and a bow seen as the rainbow (*indradhanus*). His city
  is **Amarāvatī**, his garden **Nandana**, his hall **Sudharmā**, his court graced by the
  [apsarās and gandharvas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/). (Ṛgveda; Purāṇas)
- **Parentage [variants]** — the texts do **not** agree on his birth.
  - *Variant (Ṛgveda):* son of **Dyaus** (Sky-father) and **Pṛthivī** (Earth-mother), or of an
    unnamed father he is said to have slain; born already mighty and immediately drinking soma.
  - *Variant (Purāṇic, standard):* son of the sage **Kaśyapa** and **Aditi**, eldest/foremost of the
    twelve **Ādityas**, making him brother of [Viṣṇu-Vāmana](dashavatara.md), Sūrya, Varuṇa, etc.
  - *Variant:* in some lists the son of **Tvaṣṭṛ** or kin to him — a thread that darkens once Indra
    later slays Tvaṣṭṛ's son Triśiras/Viśvarūpa (below).
- **Consort** — **Śacī / Indrāṇī / Pulomajā**, daughter of the asura Puloman; goddess of wrath and
  jealousy in her own right. Sons include **Jayanta**, and (by adoption/myth) **Arjuna** of the
  Pāṇḍavas and the monkey **Vālī** of the [Rāmāyaṇa](../04-deep-dives/) are called his offspring.
- **Epithets (a partial roster — open-ended)** — *Śakra* (the mighty), *Maghavan* (the bountiful),
  *Purandara* (city-/fort-breaker), *Vṛtrahan* (Vṛtra-slayer), *Vajrapāṇi/Vajrī* (vajra-handed),
  *Sahasrākṣa* (thousand-eyed), *Devarāja/Surapati* (king of gods), *Meghavāhana* (cloud-rider),
  *Ākhaṇḍala*, *Vāsava*, *Gotrabhid* (cleaver of the cow-pen/mountain). [roster open]

## Part I — The Vedic Indra (zenith)

Roughly **one quarter of the Ṛgveda's ~1,028 hymns address Indra** — by far the most-invoked deity,
the warrior-king who props apart heaven and earth, releases the waters and the dawn, and is the
divine model of the Ārya chieftain. *(scholarly note: the Vedic Indra and the later Purāṇic Indra are
the same name across very different theologies — a clear case of the "two clocks," scripture-internal
continuity vs. academic religious history.)*

### The Vṛtra slaying (*Vṛtrahatya*) — the central deed

- **Vṛtra the obstructor** — the great serpent/dragon (*ahi*) coils round the mountain and holds the
  cosmic **waters (the seven rivers)** captive, choking the world in drought; he is *vṛtra*, "the
  encloser." (Ṛgveda 1.32, the *locus classicus*)
- **The drink and the blow** — fortified by **soma** pressed by the gods, Indra hurls the **vajra**
  forged by **Tvaṣṭṛ**, smashes the dragon's "ninety-nine fortresses," splits his head and belly, and
  the pent rivers rush "like cattle to their stalls." This act is presented as nothing less than the
  ordering of the cosmos. (Ṛgveda 1.32)
- **Reward and the office** — by this victory Indra becomes king of the gods; soma, the Maruts and the
  storm are his perpetual companions. (Ṛgveda)
  - *Variant (mythic doublet):* the same feat is told of releasing the **Dawn (Uṣas)** and the Sun, so
    Vṛtra is read both as drought-demon and as the dark that swallows light. [scholarly]

### The Vala cave & the Aṅgirasa cows

- **The Paṇis and the Vala** — the niggardly **Paṇis** steal/hide the cosmic **cows** (light, dawn,
  truth, wealth) inside **Vala**, a stone enclosure. (Ṛgveda)
- **Saramā the divine hound** — Indra sends **Saramā**, his messenger-bitch, who tracks the cows and
  parleys with the Paṇis (refusing their bribe). (Ṛgveda 10.108)
- **The breaking-open** — aided by the **Aṅgirasas** and **Bṛhaspati**, whose chant/roar cracks the
  rock, Indra splits Vala and drives out the cows, the dawn and the waters — a second great
  cosmogonic release paralleling Vṛtra. (Ṛgveda 3, 10)
- **Other Vedic exploits [roster open]** — slaying **Śambara** (99 forts) for Divodāsa, killing
  **Pipru**, **Śuṣṇa**, **Namuci** (by a foam-trick, since he could be killed by neither wet nor dry,
  day nor night), aiding mortal kings in the **Battle of the Ten Kings (Dāśarājña)**, and the soma
  raids that mark him "drinker of the soma." (Ṛgveda)

## Part II — The vajra & Dadhīci (Purāṇic refoundation)

- **A second Vṛtra, a new weapon** — the Purāṇas retell Vṛtra-slaying with a fresh aetiology for the
  vajra. A Vṛtra (often the son of **Tvaṣṭṛ**, born to avenge a slain brother — see below) cannot be
  killed by any ordinary metal weapon. (Bhāgavata, Devī Bhāgavata, Mahābhārata)
- **Viṣṇu's counsel** — Viṣṇu/Brahmā reveal the demon can be slain only by a weapon made from the
  **bones of a sage** who freely surrenders them. (Purāṇas)
- **Dadhīci's sacrifice** — the ascetic **Dadhīci (Dadhyañca)**, son of Atharvan, gladly yields up his
  own body; he enters yogic absorption and abandons it so the devas may take his bones. (Bhāgavata
  Purāṇa; Mahābhārata)
- **Viśvakarmā forges the vajra** — the divine artificer **Viśvakarmā** fashions the **Vajrāyudha** from
  Dadhīci's **spine** (with other weapons from his other bones), and Indra at last fells Vṛtra with it.
  (Purāṇas) → cross-ref [`../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/`](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/).
  - *Variant (Ṛgvedic):* no Dadhīci at all — the vajra is simply **made by Tvaṣṭṛ** and given to Indra
    before the first Vṛtra fight. The bone-relic origin is a **Purāṇic expansion**. [late text]
  - *Variant:* Dadhīci had earlier taught the "Madhu-vidyā" through a horse's head (the Aśvin myth),
    independent of the vajra story; the Purāṇas weld the two reputations together. [scholarly]

## Part III — The thousand eyes & Ahalyā

- **The seduction** — Indra, smitten with **Ahalyā**, wife of the sage **Gautama**, takes the sage's
  form (in the earliest telling) or comes by a ruse (cock-crow at midnight to send Gautama to the
  river) and lies with her. (Bālakāṇḍa of the [Rāmāyaṇa](../04-deep-dives/); Purāṇas) — see also
  [`saptarishi.md`](saptarishi.md).
- **Gautama's curse on Indra** — the returning sage curses the offender. The form of the curse
  **varies sharply by text**:
  - *Variant (Rāmāyaṇa, Bālakāṇḍa):* Gautama curses Indra to **lose his testicles** (*vṛṣaṇa*); the
    devas later fit him with a ram's, and he is mocked as *gata-/vṛṣaṇa*-less.
  - *Variant (Brahma Purāṇa):* Indra is cursed to bear **a thousand vulvae (yoni/bhaga) over his body**
    as a mark of his lust; bathing in the **Gautamī (Godāvarī)** the marks turn into **eyes**, whence
    *Sahasrākṣa*, "the thousand-eyed."
  - *Variant (Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa):* the thousand vulvae turn to eyes when Indra worships
    **Sūrya**; the curse is mitigated rather than lifted.
  - *Variant:* a milder strand simply grants/curses him "a thousand eyes" outright as omniscient
    surveillance. [folk]
- **Ahalyā's fate & redemption** — Gautama also curses Ahalyā (to invisibility / to be a stone / to
  ash-bed penance) until **Rāma's** foot/dust releases her in the [Treta Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md);
  variants on her culpability (knowing vs. deceived) are catalogued under [`saptarishi.md`](saptarishi.md). [variant]

## Part IV — The Brahmahatyā, exile & restoration (the recurring fall)

The signature Purāṇic theme: Indra **repeatedly loses and regains** his throne — a king perpetually
humbled, the mythic engine that subordinates him to higher powers and to merit.

- **The sin of Vṛtra/Viśvarūpa** — because the Purāṇic Vṛtra (and his brother **Triśiras/Viśvarūpa**,
  Tvaṣṭṛ's son and a *purohita* of the gods) is a **brāhmaṇa**, killing him incurs **Brahmahatyā**,
  the gravest sin. (Bhāgavata; Mahābhārata, Udyoga/Śānti Parva)
- **Indra in hiding** — crushed by the sin (or fearing it), Indra **shrinks into the fibres of a lotus
  stalk** in Lake **Mānasa** and vanishes; Svarga is left kingless and chaos looms. (Purāṇas)
- **Expiation** — Indra is purified by distributing the Brahmahatyā in four parts onto **earth (it
  becomes saline cracks), water (foam), trees (sap/gum), and women (menstruation)** — each granted a
  boon in exchange — and by the **Aśvamedha** horse-sacrifice. (Mahābhārata; Purāṇas)
- **Nahuṣa the usurper** — during Indra's absence the devas crown the merit-rich **mortal king
  Nahuṣa** as regent of Svarga. Arrogant, he lusts after **Śacī** and demands she come to him; Śacī
  stalls. Forcing the saptarṣis to bear his palanquin, Nahuṣa kicks the sage **Agastya** (crying
  *"sarpa, sarpa!"* — "go faster"), who curses him to fall as a **serpent** for ten thousand years.
  Indra is restored. (Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva; Purāṇas) — see [`saptarishi.md`](saptarishi.md).
- **Other displacements [roster open]** — Indra is repeatedly bested or supplanted: by **Meghanāda**
  (Rāvaṇa's son) who captures him and earns the name **Indrajit** ([Rāmāyaṇa](../04-deep-dives/)); by
  asura emperors **Bali** (until [Vāmana](dashavatara.md) restores the three worlds) and **Hiraṇyakaśipu**;
  and by ascetics whose tapas threatens his seat, prompting his stock counter-move — sending an
  **apsarā** (Menakā to **Viśvāmitra**, Rambhā, Tilottamā) to break their penance. (Purāṇas; Epics)

## Part V — The Govardhana humbling

- **The interrupted yajña** — at **Vraja/Vṛndāvana** the cowherds prepare their annual **Indra-pūjā**
  for rain. The boy **[Kṛṣṇa](dashavatara.md)** argues their true sustenance is **Mount Govardhana**
  and the cows, not Indra, and persuades them to worship the hill instead. (Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Canto
  10.24–25)
- **Indra's wrath** — enraged at the lost offering, Indra looses the apocalyptic **Sāṃvartaka
  storm-clouds** to drown Vraja in seven days of deluge. (Bhāgavata 10.25)
- **The lifted mountain** — Kṛṣṇa raises **Govardhana on his little finger** as a vast umbrella,
  sheltering all the people and cattle for seven days until Indra, defeated, withdraws the storm.
  (Bhāgavata 10.25)
- **Indra's submission (Govinda-paṭṭābhiṣeka)** — Indra descends with **Surabhi** the cow and
  **consecrates Kṛṣṇa as "Govinda,"** bathing him with celestial/cow's milk and confessing his pride —
  the theological centerpiece of Indra's demotion beneath Viṣṇu. Commemorated as **Govardhan Pūjā /
  Annakūṭa**. (Bhāgavata Purāṇa)
  - *Variant (Braj oral tradition):* details of the days, the gopas' speeches and Surabhi's role vary
    across regional Vaiṣṇava retellings. [folk]

## Part VI — "Indra" as an office across the manvantaras

- **The post, not the person** — Purāṇic cosmology fixes that **each [manvantara](../01-manvantaras/)
  has its own Manu, set of devas, Saptarṣis, a Viṣṇu-aṃśa, and its own Indra**. The title rotates;
  countless beings have held and will hold it. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa; Bhāgavata Purāṇa)
- **Current holder** — the Indra of our **7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara** is **Purandara**, "subduer of
  the asuras' pride." (Viṣṇu/Bhāgavata Purāṇa) — see
  [`../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/`](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/).
- **Past & future Indras [roster open]** — names given per manvantara include **Yajña / Viśvabhuk /
  Suśānti / Śibi / Vibhu / Manojava / Purandara** (current) and successors; the **lists differ across
  Purāṇas**, so the roster should be treated as variant and incomplete. [disputed]
- **Earned by merit** — mortals and ascetics can ascend to Indrahood through tapas and **a hundred
  Aśvamedhas** (hence Indra's vigilance over rival sacrificers, and his alias **Śatakratu**, "of a
  hundred rites"); the seat is thus contestable, explaining the chronic fall-and-restoration pattern.
  (Purāṇas) [variant]

## Part VII — Vedic supremacy vs. Purāṇic decline (two clocks)

- **Scriptural continuity** — within the texts Indra is *always* the king of Svarga; his
  subordination to Viṣṇu/Śiva is framed as his proper place in a hierarchy he himself acknowledges
  (e.g. Govardhana), not a contradiction. *(scripture clock)*
- **Academic trajectory** — historians of religion read a clear **demotion arc**: the dominant,
  martial, soma-drinking sky-warrior of the Ṛgveda becomes, by the epic-Purāṇic age, a **flawed,
  fearful, lustful, frequently-defeated** monarch whose victories require sages' bones and gods'
  rescue — a mirror of the rise of bhakti theism around Viṣṇu, Śiva and the
  [Goddess](devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md). *(academic clock)* [scholarly]
- **Character of the decline** — recurring tropes encode it: the **Brahmahatyā** stain, **cowardice**
  in war (fleeing asuras, needing avatāras), **sexual transgression** (Ahalyā), **pride punished**
  (Govardhana, Nahuṣa), and **insecurity** about his throne (disrupting tapas). [scholarly]
- **Comparative note** — the name and storm-king profile parallel the wider Indo-European thunder-god
  (cf. the *Vajra*/thunderbolt motif); in Buddhist texts he survives as **Śakra (Sakka)**, lord of the
  Tāvatiṃsa heaven. [scholarly]
