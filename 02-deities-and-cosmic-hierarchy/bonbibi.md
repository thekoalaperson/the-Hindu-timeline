# Bonbibi — the Forest-Goddess of the Sundarbans

> Deities-and-hierarchy node. **Bonbibi** (also **Banbibi**, **Banabibi**, **Bonbibi Mā**, "Lady of the Forest"; honorifics **Bandurga**, **Bibi-mā**) is the tutelary guardian-goddess of the **Sundarbans** mangrove delta of southern West Bengal and Bangladesh — protector of the **mauleys** (honey-gatherers), **bāwālis** (woodcutters), fisherfolk and crab-collectors who enter the tiger-forest, and a rare **Hindu–Muslim shared frontier deity** worshipped by both communities at the same shrine. ## 1. Two Clocks — Who Bonbibi Is

Bonbibi has **no scriptural clock at all**: she is absent from every Sanskrit text and belongs wholly to the "historical-folk" register of the Bengal delta frontier. What stands in for a chronology is her one named narrative text and the documented spread of her cult.

- **Scriptural clock** — **none.** Bonbibi has no Vedic, epic or Purāṇic footprint; she is a purely **regional, post-classical folk deity** of the agrarian-and-forest frontier, with no Brahmanical sanction and no Sanskrit *dhyāna*. [scholarly]
- **Historical clock** — her cult is tied to the **clearing and settlement of the Sundarbans** under Mughal, zamindari and colonial-era reclamation (roughly **17th–19th centuries CE** onward), when forest-workers entering tiger country needed a guardian; her text the **Bonbibi Jahuranama** is a printed *puthi* of the **19th century**, though its oral matter is older. [folk] [scholarly] [kali-historical]
- **What she governs** — safe passage in the **jungle**: protection from **tigers** (above all the man-eating Royal Bengal tiger), crocodiles, snakes and the dangers of honey-collecting and wood-cutting; she licenses and limits human extraction from the forest. [folk]
- **The theological engine** — Bonbibi is a goddess of **just measure**: the forest belongs to the tiger-spirit **Dakṣiṇ Rāy**, and Bonbibi does not destroy him but **partitions the jungle** so that those who enter with pure heart and modest need are spared. She is invoked to keep human greed and animal violence in balance, not to conquer the wild. [scholarly]

## 2. Names, Idiom and the Syncretic Frame

- **Bonbibi / Banbibi** — "**Lady (bibi) of the forest (bon/ban)**," her standard Bengali name; *bibi* is an Indo-Persian honorific for a lady, signalling her Islamic-inflected dress. [regional]
- **Banadurgā / Bandurga** — "Durgā of the forest," the **Hindu** reading that assimilates her to the Devī (see [devi-forms-and-navadurga.md](devi-forms-and-navadurga.md)); used by Hindu worshippers who see her as a form of the Mother-goddess. [regional]
- **Bibi-mā / Mā Bonbibi** — the everyday devotional address, "Mother."
- **The dobhāṣī idiom** — her text and liturgy are in **dobhāṣī** (*musalmāni* / "mixed-language") **Bengali**, a register saturated with **Persian and Arabic** loan-words (*Allāh*, *pīr*, *fakir*, *jahura*); the goddess is given a Muslim genealogy and Arabian birthplace even as she is worshipped with Hindu-style ritual — the linguistic emblem of her **syncretism**. [scholarly]
- **A shared deity, not a converted one** — Bonbibi is venerated by **Muslim and Hindu forest-workers alike** at the same shrines; she is not "really" Hindu or "really" Muslim but a genuine **frontier cult** of the delta, one of the clearest living examples of folk Hindu–Muslim religious sharing in South Asia. [scholarly] [regional]

## 3. The Bonbibi Jahuranama (the source text)

The single authoritative narrative is the **Bonbibi Jahuranama** ("the glory-tale / *jahura-nāmā* of Bonbibi"), a Bengali *puthi* whose best-known printed recension is ascribed to **Munshi Mohammad Khater** (often dated to the late 19th century, **c. 1880**), with an earlier version attributed to **Bayanuddin**. It functions for Bonbibi much as the **Manasāmaṅgal** does for [Manasā](manasa.md) — a deity-glorifying narrative that establishes her worship. [folk] [scholarly]

- **Two-part structure** — the text divides into the **birth-and-mission story** of Bonbibi (her Arabian origin and arrival in the "country of eighteen tides," *āṭhāro bhāṭir deś*) and the **Dukhe narrative** (the contest with Dakṣiṇ Rāy that proves her power). (Bonbibi Jahuranama)
- **Read right-to-left in book form** — printed copies are conventionally **opened and read from the back / right**, in imitation of an Arabic-Persian *kitāb*, a physical marker of the dobhāṣī, Islamicate framing. [regional]
- **Performance** — the *Jahuranama* is **sung and dramatized** as **Bonbibi pālā / Bonbibi-r jātrā** in delta villages before the work-season in the forest. [folk]

## 4. Birth and Mission of Bonbibi (narrative order)

The *Jahuranama* gives Bonbibi a wholly **Islamicate** origin myth, set in Arabia, then transplants her to the Bengal delta. Catalog of its episodes:

- **Born in Mecca / Arabia** — Bonbibi and her twin **brother Śāh Jaṅgalī** (Shāh Jongoli, "the forest-king / lord of the jungle") are the children of a **fakir, Ibrāhīm**, by his second wife **Gulālbibi**; the first wife had demanded the child be abandoned in the forest. (Bonbibi Jahuranama) [folk]
- **Abandoned and reared in the forest** — Gulālbibi, pregnant with twins, is left in the wild; she keeps **Bonbibi** and the infant is suckled by a **deer**, growing up a child of the forest, while **Śāh Jaṅgalī** is taken away and later reunited. (Bonbibi Jahuranama)
- **Divine commission** — the twins are summoned to **Medina**, blessed by the **Prophet** and the saints, and **commissioned by Allāh** (in the text's idiom) to go and make habitable the **"land of eighteen tides"** — the Sundarbans — for the sake of humankind. (Bonbibi Jahuranama) [regional]
- **Arrival in the delta** — Bonbibi and Śāh Jaṅgalī travel to the mangrove country and sound the **Muslim call (āzān)**, by which act the wild domain is claimed for the just rule of the goddess. (Bonbibi Jahuranama)
- **The dual brother** — **Śāh Jaṅgalī** is her constant companion-protector, the male warrior-figure who fights at her side; the cult is effectively of the **two siblings together**, though Bonbibi is dominant. [regional]

## 5. The Contest with Dakṣiṇ Rāy and the Dukhe Narrative

The heart of the text is the confrontation between Bonbibi and **Dakṣiṇ Rāy** ("King of the South"), the **tiger-demon / lord of the tigers** who claims the forest and demands **human flesh** as tribute. Events in narrative order:

- **Dakṣiṇ Rāy's claim** — **Dakṣiṇ Rāy**, a powerful tiger-spirit who rules the jungle and appears as a Brahmin or in tiger form, regards the men who enter his forest as his rightful prey and rivals Bonbibi's authority. (Bonbibi Jahuranama)
  - *Variant (independent Hindu cult, Source: Bengali folk tradition):* Dakṣiṇ Rāy is also worshipped on his own as a **forest-and-tiger deity** with shrines (and the figure **Kālu Rāy**), so the *Jahuranama* in effect dramatizes the **subordination of an older Hindu tiger-god to the newer syncretic goddess**. [regional] [scholarly]
- **The poor man Dukhe** — a poor boy named **Dukhe** ("Sorrow") is taken into the forest by his greedy uncle / employer **Dhonā (Dhona Mauley)**, a honey-and-wood merchant leading an expedition of seven boats. (Bonbibi Jahuranama)
- **The bargain for honey** — Dhonā can get no wax or honey; **Dakṣiṇ Rāy appears in a dream** and promises to fill the boats **only if Dhonā abandons the boy Dukhe** to him as a human offering. Dhonā agrees and **leaves Dukhe behind** on the mud-flat of **Kedokhali**. (Bonbibi Jahuranama)
- **The cry to Bonbibi** — as the tiger (Dakṣiṇ Rāy in tiger form) closes in, terrified Dukhe **calls out "Mā Bonbibi"** for rescue. (Bonbibi Jahuranama)
- **Bonbibi's rescue** — **Bonbibi hears and saves him**, sending **Śāh Jaṅgalī** to fight Dakṣiṇ Rāy; the tiger-king is **defeated** and flees. (Bonbibi Jahuranama)
- **The mediation of Bara Khān Gāzī** — Dakṣiṇ Rāy takes refuge with the Muslim pīr-saint **Bara Khān Gāzī (Gāzī Pīr)**; the two come to Bonbibi, and a **settlement** is reached. (Bonbibi Jahuranama) [regional]
- **The partition of the forest** — Bonbibi, victorious but merciful, does **not annihilate** Dakṣiṇ Rāy; she **accepts him as a "son"** and **divides the forest's bounty** — honest, needy entrants are under her protection, and the forest is shared rather than seized. This *compromise* is the cult's central ethic. (Bonbibi Jahuranama) [scholarly]
- **Dukhe enriched and returned** — Bonbibi sends Dukhe home **laden with wealth** (and in some tellings a crocodile-spirit **Seko / Sekher** ferries him); Dukhe is restored to his mother, and the **worship of Bonbibi is established** among the forest people as the price and proof of her power. (Bonbibi Jahuranama)

## 6. Iconography and Worship

- **Iconography** — Bonbibi is shown as a **young woman in distinctly Muslim-coded dress** — *salwar* / trousers, cap or crown, sometimes booted — often **seated on or flanked by a tiger**, holding the rescued child **Dukhe** on her lap or hip, with her brother **Śāh Jaṅgalī** beside her; clay images pair her with Dakṣiṇ Rāy's tiger. (Sundarbans shrine tradition) [regional]
- **Shrines (*than / āsthāna*)** — simple mud-and-thatch or brick **forest-edge shrines** scattered through the delta villages of South 24 Parganas and the Bangladesh Sundarbans; no grand temple, in keeping with a working-people's cult. [folk]
- **Worship before entering the forest** — **mauleys, bāwālis and fishermen perform Bonbibi pūjā** and recite or hear the *Jahuranama* **before each expedition**, vowing to take only what they need; the rite is **led by either Hindu or Muslim** practitioners, both communities sharing the same shrine and **Bawali / *bonojibi*** ("forest-living") identity. [regional] [scholarly]
- **Festival** — her chief worship falls around **Māgh (January–February)**, at the start of the honey and forest season. [regional]
- **No image-Brahmanism** — like [Manasā](manasa.md), she sits **outside the Brahmanical temple establishment**; her authority is the lived danger of the tiger-forest, not Sanskrit liturgy, and her priests are often **non-Brahmin forest-workers**. [scholarly]

---

## 7. Why Bonbibi Is Unique

- **The clearest shared Hindu–Muslim folk deity** — few South Asian cults so completely **dissolve the Hindu/Muslim line**: an Islamic genealogy and dobhāṣī liturgy fused with goddess-worship and Hindu ritual, accepted by both communities as **the same protector**. [scholarly]
- **An ecology-goddess** — her myth encodes a **conservation ethic** (take only your need; the forest is shared with its non-human lord), which is why she figures so prominently in modern Sundarbans environmental and anthropological writing. [scholarly]
- **A frontier deity** — she is the religious expression of the **agrarian frontier**: the cult arose precisely where settled human society pressed against an untamed, tiger-ruled mangrove wilderness. [scholarly]
