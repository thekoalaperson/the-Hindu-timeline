# Dharma Ṭhākur — the Aniconic God of Bengal

> Deities-and-hierarchy node. **Dharma Ṭhākur** (Bengali *Dharma Ṭhākur / Dharmarāj*, also **Buṛā Ṭhākur**, "the Old Lord") is the uniquely Bengali **aniconic folk god** — worshipped not as an image but as a rough **stone**, often shaped or read as a **tortoise / kūrma** — who presides over **justice (dharma), fertility, child-getting and the cure of disease**, above all among the **lower-caste and once-untouchable communities** (Ḍom, Bāuri, Bāgdi, Hāḍi, Sundī) of the western Rāṛh districts of Bengal. ## 1. Two Clocks — Who Dharma Ṭhākur Is

Dharma Ṭhākur has **no scriptural clock of his own**: he is absent from all Sanskrit śāstra and belongs wholly to the historical-folk register of western Bengal. His name **Dharma** collides with the Sanskrit god of justice, but the cult is independent.

- **Scriptural clock** — **none in his own right.** There is no Vedic, epic or Purāṇic Dharma Ṭhākur; the Sanskrit **Dharma / Dharmarāja** (a name of [Yama](yama.md), and the deified personification of righteousness) is a *separate* figure with whom the folk god was only later identified by Sanskritizing priests. [scholarly]
- **Historical clock** — the cult is a **post-classical regional development of the Rāṛh** (western West Bengal: Bāṅkuṛā, Birbhum, Bardhamān, Medinīpur), best documented from the medieval period onward; its great literature, the **Dharma-maṅgal**, is dated by named poets to roughly the **14th–18th centuries CE**, with the cult itself older. [folk] [scholarly] [kali-historical]
- **What he governs** — **dharma in the plain sense of right order and justice**, plus the concrete boons his low-caste devotees seek: **offspring (especially sons), recovery from disease and leprosy, rain and good harvest, and success in ordeal/oath**. He is sworn-by as the witness of truth. [folk]
- **The theological engine** — Dharma Ṭhākur is the **god of the disprivileged**: a deity whose priesthood (the **Ḍom paṇḍit / deāsī**) is itself non-Brahmin, who is worshipped **without an anthropomorphic image**, and whose festival inverts caste order by giving ritual primacy to the lowest. He is the male axis of the same agrarian, non-Brahmanical Bengal piety that produced [Manasā](manasa.md) and the *maṅgal* literature. [scholarly]

## 2. Names, Form and the Aniconic Cult

- **Dharma Ṭhākur** — "Lord Dharma," the standard name; *ṭhākur* is the Bengali honorific for a god or a Brahmin. [regional]
- **Dharmarāj** — "King Dharma," the more Sanskritized devotional name, the form under which Brahmanical assimilation links him to the cosmic lord of justice (see [yama.md](yama.md)). [regional]
- **Buṛā Ṭhākur / Buṛo-Rāy** — "the Old Lord," an affectionate village name stressing his antiquity. [folk]
- **The aniconic stone** — Dharma Ṭhākur is **almost never an anthropomorphic idol**. His normal embodiment is an **unhewn or roughly rounded stone**, frequently **black or reddish**, smeared with vermilion and oil and set under a tree or in a small open shrine; some are natural boulders, some shaped slabs. [folk] [regional]
- **The tortoise / kūrma form** — very characteristically his stone is identified as, or carved as, a **tortoise (kūrma / kacchapa)**; the tortoise-shaped emblem is one of the cult's signatures and is read as a symbol of stability, longevity and the supporting base of the world. [regional]
  - *Variant (other emblems, Source: Bengali folk tradition):* in some shrines he is a **footprint-slab, a metal pot or a horse-figure (the *ghoṛā* offerings)**; terracotta and clay **horses** are a common votive gift to him. [folk]
- **No fixed iconography** — because the god *is* the stone, there is **no canonical dhyāna-image**; what varies from shrine to shrine is the stone and its tree, not a painted form. This **aniconism** is the single most distinctive feature of the cult. [scholarly]

## 3. Dharma-pūjā, the Gājan and the Ghaṭ Rites

The worship of Dharma Ṭhākur centres on the **Dharma-pūjā / Dharma-gājan**, a spring–summer festival (commonly in **Baiśākh–Jyaiṣṭha / Āṣāṛh**, April–July) marked by ascetic vows, ordeal and lower-caste ritual leadership.

- **The Ḍom paṇḍit / deāsī priesthood** — the officiant is **not a Brahmin** but a **Ḍom (or other low-caste) "paṇḍit," also called deāsī / bhakta**; the cult is one of the clearest cases in Bengal of a **non-Brahmanical priesthood** serving a major god. [scholarly] [regional]
- **The ghaṭ (the consecrated pot)** — the god is invoked into a **ghaṭ**, a water-filled **pot** that serves as his temporary seat for the festival; the **ghaṭ-sthāpana** (establishing the pot) opens the rite, and the *bhaktas* worship the pot-borne god through the days of the gājan. [folk]
- **The gājan and its devotees** — the **gājan** is the body of **vow-taking ascetic devotees (sannyāsīs / bhaktas)** who, for the festival, take on temporary renunciation, fasting and self-mortification to honour the god. [regional]
- **Ordeals and self-mortification** — characteristic gājan austerities include **fire-walking, piercing the body or tongue (*bāṇ-phoṛā*), swinging from a hook (*caṛak*-style hook-swinging), walking on thorns and the *jhāp* leap** — bodily ordeals offered to win the god's boon; the **Caṛak / gājan complex** overlaps the worship of [Śiva](shiva-myth-cycle.md) as well — for the Śaiva **Gajan–Charak Puja** of Chaitra Sankranti (the Bengali year's end), of which this Dharma-er Gājan is the Rāṛh counterpart, see [Regional Temple Festivals](../05-concepts-and-dharma/regional-temple-festivals.md). [folk] [regional]
- **Fertility and disease vows** — childless couples and the sick **vow service to Dharma Ṭhākur** in exchange for a son or a cure, repaying with **clay horses, ghaṭ-offerings and gājan participation**; the fertility function is central and is why the cult thrives among agrarian and labouring castes. [folk]
- **The Sanskritizing overlay** — where Brahmins have entered the cult, Dharma Ṭhākur is **re-read as Dharmarāj / Yama-Dharma or even as a form of [Śiva](shiva-myth-cycle.md) or Viṣṇu/Buddha**, and Brahmanical mantras are grafted on; but the **core rite remains the low-caste gājan**. [scholarly]

## 4. The Dharma-maṅgal Kāvya and the Lausen Cycle

The cult's great literature is the **Dharma-maṅgal** (also *Dharmamaṅgal / Dharmer-gān*), one of the major **maṅgal-kāvya** genres of medieval Bengal alongside the Manasā- and Caṇḍī-maṅgals. It is a **deity-glorifying narrative** that establishes Dharma Ṭhākur's worship through the heroic career of his devotee **Lausen**.

- **The maṅgal-kāvya frame** — like the [Manasāmaṅgal](manasa.md), the Dharma-maṅgal is a long Bengali narrative poem whose purpose is to **propagate the god's worship** by showing his power to reward devotees and crush those who deny him. (Dharma-maṅgal) [scholarly]
- **The named poets** — the cycle survives in several recensions by datable authors, the most celebrated being **Ghanarām Cakravartī** (early 18th c.), with earlier poets **Mayūrabhaṭṭa, Rūpram Cakravartī, Mānikrām Gāṅgulī, Sahadeva Cakravartī, Rāmdās Ādak** and others — anchoring the text to roughly the **14th–18th centuries CE**. (Dharma-maṅgal recensions) [scholarly] [kali-historical]
- **The hero Lausen** — the heart of the poem is the **Lausen (Lāusen) cycle**: Lausen, son of **Karṇasena** of Maina (Mayanā) and his wife **Rañjāvatī**, is born by the grace of Dharma Ṭhākur after his mother's severe austerities, and becomes the god's supreme champion. (Dharma-maṅgal) [folk]
  - *Variant (the birth boon, Source: Dharma-maṅgal, Rañjāvatī episode):* Rañjāvatī performs the extreme **hook-swinging / self-offering ordeal to Dharma** — even, in some tellings, **offering her own flesh / preparing to immolate herself** — until the god grants her the son Lausen; this episode is the mythic charter of the gājan ordeals. [folk]
- **The villain Mahāmada / Mahudyā** — the cycle's antagonist is the wicked minister **Mahāmada (Mahudyā)** of King **Gauṛeśvar**, who repeatedly plots Lausen's destruction; Lausen's trials at court and in war drive the plot. (Dharma-maṅgal) [folk]
- **The great miracle — Lausen makes the sun rise in the west** — the cycle's signature episode: to prove the power of Dharma, **Lausen offers his own severed head/body and worships until Dharma Ṭhākur makes the sun rise in the west (*paścime sūrya-uday*)**, the supreme sign of the god's might, after which the god restores him. (Dharma-maṅgal) [folk] [regional]
- **The campaign at Kāmrūp / Hākanda and Kālu-Ḍom** — Lausen's wars (including against **Kāmrūp / the fort of Hākanda**) and his loyal low-caste companion **Kālu Ḍom** are sung at length; the prominence of the **Ḍom hero** mirrors the Ḍom priesthood of the living cult. (Dharma-maṅgal) [regional]
- **Function** — the poem ends, *maṅgal*-fashion, with the **establishment and propagation of Dharma-pūjā**: Lausen's devotion is the model the worshipper imitates in the gājan. (Dharma-maṅgal) [scholarly]

## 5. The Buddhist-Survival Thesis (debated)

A long-running scholarly debate holds that Dharma Ṭhākur is a **vestige of Buddhism** — a survival of the **Mahāyāna / Vajrayāna** that flourished in Bengal under the **Pāla dynasty** (8th–12th c.) and was driven underground after Brahmanical and Islamic ascendancy.

- **The Sashibhusan Dasgupta / Haraprasād Śāstrī thesis** — early-20th-c. scholars (notably **Haraprasād Śāstrī** and **Sashibhusan Dasgupta**) argued that **Dharma = the Buddhist *Dharma* (the *Dharma-ratna* of the Triratna)** or the **Ādi-Buddha / Śūnya** principle, surviving as a folk god after organized Buddhism collapsed in Bengal. (Buddhist-survival scholarship) [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Supporting clues cited** — the cult's **aniconism**, the worship of **bare stones**, the term **Śūnya / Niraṅjan** ("the Void / the Stainless") used for Dharma in the *Śūnya-Purāṇa* and *Dharma-pūjā-vidhāna*, ritual formulae echoing Buddhist usage, and the cult's location precisely in the **old Pāla Buddhist heartland of the Rāṛh**. (Śūnya-Purāṇa of Rāmāi Paṇḍit; Dharma-pūjā-vidhāna) [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The Śūnya-Purāṇa** — a key liturgical text of the cult, ascribed to **Rāmāi Paṇḍit**, presents a **creation account from Śūnya / Niraṅjan-Dharma** and contains the famous **"Niraṅjaner Ruṣmā"** passage (read by some as describing Brahmin oppression and even a Muslim turn); it is the chief evidence marshalled for the Buddhist-survival reading. (Śūnya-Purāṇa) [late text] [disputed]
- **The counter-view** — many later scholars **reject or heavily qualify** the thesis, arguing that Dharma Ṭhākur is better explained as an **autochthonous tribal/aboriginal Sun-and-stone cult** (the sun-in-the-west miracle, the kūrma-stone) **Hinduized over time**, with the "Buddhist" features being **superficial verbal survivals** rather than a continuous Buddhist church; the name *Dharma* may simply mean "the moral law / the just god." [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Net assessment** — the question is **genuinely unresolved**: the cult shows a layered **tribal + Buddhist + Brahmanical** sediment, and the relative weight of each layer is still argued. The Buddhist-survival idea is **historically influential but not settled fact**. [scholarly] [disputed]

## 6. Why Dharma Ṭhākur Is Unique

- **The great aniconic god of Bengal** — almost alone among major Hindu cult-deities, he is worshipped **without an anthropomorphic image**, *as a stone / tortoise*; the god is the rock itself. [scholarly]
- **A low-caste god with a low-caste priesthood** — his **Ḍom / non-Brahmin priesthood** and gājan inversions of caste order make him a rare institutionalized deity of the disprivileged. [scholarly]
- **A four-layered figure** — he fuses an **aboriginal sun-and-stone cult**, a possible **Buddhist Śūnya-Dharma survival**, a **Sanskritized Dharmarāj/Yama identification** (see [yama.md](yama.md)), and a fully developed **maṅgal-kāvya mythology** (the Lausen cycle) into one living western-Bengal cult. [scholarly]
