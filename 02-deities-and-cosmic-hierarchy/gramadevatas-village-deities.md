# Grāmadevatās — the Village-Deity System and Its Goddesses

> Deities-and-hierarchy node. The **grāmadevatā** ("village deity") complex is the largest living stratum of Hindu worship — the fierce guardian gods and village mothers who hold the field, the boundary and the body, beneath and before the Purāṇic pantheon. **Grāmadevatās** (Sanskrit *grāma-devatā*, "village deity"; Telugu *grāmadēvata*, Tamil *kāval teyvam* "guardian deity") are the tutelary divinities of a specific village, hamlet or locality — overwhelmingly **goddesses** (the "village mothers," *ammā / amman / amma*), flanked by **male guardian gods** of the boundary and the gate. They are the **substrate** of Hindu religion: the oldest, densest and most widely worshipped layer, largely outside the Brahmanical Sanskrit pantheon, onto which Purāṇic figures (Durgā, Kālī, Pārvatī, Śiva-Bhairava) were later mapped. Their study is anchored in **Henry Whitehead's *The Village Gods of South India* (1916)** and a century of *grāmadevatā* ethnography.

> **Two clocks.** The grāmadevatā system is a **kali-historical** living phenomenon — shrines, festivals, possession-cults and Sanskritizing myths attested in recent centuries and in the present, with no fixed scriptural yuga-date. Where a village mother is given a Purāṇic pedigree (Reṇukā, Dakṣa's daughter, a Mātṛkā), that *myth* is borrowed onto a separate clock to legitimize a cult that is itself archaeologically and textually undatable.

## The system — guardians, mothers and the boundary

- **The village as a sacred enclosure** — each village is conceived as a bounded body needing **protection at its perimeter and gate**; the deities sort into **boundary/guardian gods** (who repel disease, demons and outside evil at the *ellai*/edge) and **mother goddesses** (who are the village's own life, fertility and fever) (Source: Whitehead, *Village Gods of South India*; grāmadevatā studies [scholarly]).
- **Aniconic and open-air shrines** — the typical shrine is **not** a roofed Agamic temple but a plain stone, a daubed **termite-mound (puttu)**, a trident, a pot, a neem tree, or a simple platform under the open sky at the village edge; many have **no anthropomorphic image and no enclosing structure** (Source: Whitehead; village-shrine ethnography [regional]).
- **Non-Brahmanical priesthood** — worship is conducted by **non-Brahmin pūjārīs** drawn from cultivator, artisan, washer, potter or so-called "low" castes — *pambala*, *asadi*, *potha-raju* lineages, oracles and possession-mediums — not by Vedic priests; this is a hallmark distinguishing grāmadevatās from temple gods (Source: Whitehead; grāmadevatā ethnography [scholarly]).
- **Blood sacrifice (bali)** — the defining offering is **animal blood**, above all the **buffalo** and the **goat/cock/sheep**, given to the goddess at her annual festival; the buffalo-sacrifice re-enacts the goddess's slaying of a buffalo-demon and "feeds" her heat — the very rite that reform-Sanskritization later tries to abolish (Source: Whitehead; festival ethnography [scholarly]).
- **Possession and oracle** — the goddess "comes onto" (mounts) chosen devotees who shake, dance and prophesy; the **jātara/festival** centres on possession, drumming and the carrying of pots (Source: grāmadevatā possession-cult studies [scholarly]).
- **The fierce, unmarried, sovereign mother** — the village goddess is characteristically **independent and unwed** (or her consort is subordinate, absent, or beheaded); she is *sovereign in her own right*, hot, virgin-fierce and dangerous — the opposite of the docile married Purāṇic consort (Source: grāmadevatā studies [scholarly]).

## The Seven Sisters pattern (Sapta-Mātṛkā / Saptakannimār)

- **The seven mothers** — village mothers cluster as a **group of seven sisters** — Telugu *Saptamātṛkā / Yedugurakkalu* ("seven sisters"), Tamil *Saptakannimār / Ēḻu Kanniyar* ("seven virgins"), Kannada *Ēḻu Akkagaḷu* — worshipped together as a set of related, often quarrelsome goddesses guarding the village (Source: Whitehead; Sapta-Mātṛkā village-mother studies [scholarly]).
  - *Variant (Brahmanical overlay):* the village "seven sisters" are equated with the Purāṇic **Sapta-Mātṛkā** — Brāhmī, Māheśvarī, Kaumārī, Vaiṣṇavī, Vārāhī, Indrāṇī/Aindrī, Cāmuṇḍā — the śakti-mothers born to drink Andhaka's/Raktabīja's blood; this is a **Sanskritizing equation**, not the folk origin (Source: *Devī-Māhātmya* matrix mapped onto folk sevens [disputed]).
  - *Variant (open count):* the "seven" is often a **conventional, fluid number** — local rosters run to eight, nine or "the sisters and their brother," with names varying village to village (Source: grāmadevatā ethnography [regional]).

## Poturāju — the buffalo-guardian and executioner-brother

- **Poturāju / Pōtu-rāju** ("buffalo-king," Telugu *pōtu* = male buffalo) — the **male guardian who accompanies the seven mother-goddesses** as their **brother and protector**; he is the dark, fierce, bare-chested figure who **escorts, controls and executes** for the mothers (Source: grāmadevatā ethnography; Pothuraju traditions [scholarly]).
  - *His festival role:* at Bonalu and village jātaras a man becomes Poturāju — **vermilion-smeared, garlanded, cracking a whip, a turmeric-rope tied to his body**, leading the procession, controlling the possessed and presiding over the **buffalo-sacrifice** as the goddess's executioner (Source: Bonalu/Pothuraju festival ethnography [regional]).
  - *Variant (the sacrificed buffalo-demon):* in some readings Poturāju is the **buffalo-demon himself**, subdued and made the goddess's doorkeeper — the local cognate of the Mahiṣa whom Durgā slays, here demoted to the mothers' servant-brother (Source: grāmadevatā studies [disputed]).
  - *Pambala / Pothuraju lineages:* hereditary non-Brahmin ritual specialists who serve the village mothers and embody Poturāju (Source: Telugu grāmadevatā ethnography [regional]).

## Bonalu, jātara and the festival complex

- **Bonalu** (Telugu *bōnālu* < *bhōjanālu*, "meals/feasts") — the great Telangana village-mother festival (Hyderabad–Secunderabad), held in **Āṣāḍha (Jul–Aug)**; women carry **decorated pots of cooked rice, jaggery and curd (bōnam)** balanced on the head to the goddess (Mahākālī, Pochamma, Maisamma, etc.), accompanied by drumming, possession-dance and Poturāju (Source: Bonalu festival ethnography [regional]).
- **Jātara** — the generic name for a periodic village-goddess fair/festival across the Deccan; centres on procession, possession, animal sacrifice and the renewal of the village's bond with its mother (Source: grāmadevatā festival studies [scholarly]).
- **Sammakka–Sāralamma jātara of Medaram** — the biennial (every second **Māgha**, Jan–Feb) jātara of the mother-goddesses **Sammakka** and her daughter **Sāralamma** at **Medaram** (Mulugu district, Telangana), reckoned **India's largest tribal religious gathering** — attendance in recent editions is counted in the tens of millions — conducted entirely in the grāmadevatā register: forest goddesses, aniconic emblems, non-Agamic rites (Source: Medaram jātara studies; Telangana festival ethnography [scholarly/regional]).
  - *The tiger-forest foundling:* **Koya** oral tradition holds that Sammakka was found as an **infant in the forest amid tigers**, radiant and unharmed; the Koya chief who found her raised her as his own, and she grew into a healer-queen wed to the Medaram chief **Pagiḍḍa Rāju**, a feudatory of the Kākatīyas (Source: Koya oral tradition [folk/oral tradition]).
  - *The stand at Sampenga Vagu:* when drought made the tribute impossible and the **Kakatiya tax-armies** marched on Medaram, Sammakka's family fought them at the **Sampenga Vagu** stream — her husband, her daughter Sāralamma and her son **Jampanna** falling in the battle — and Sammakka herself took the field as a warrior-mother against the imperial force (Source: Koya oral tradition [folk/oral tradition]; the Kakatiya framing ties an undatable cult to a c. 12th–13th-c. dynasty — two clocks).
  - *The disappearance and the vermilion casket:* wounded, Sammakka cursed the Kākatīyas and **walked into the Chilakala Gutta hill and vanished**; searchers found only a **vermilion casket (kumkuma bharaṇi)** — the aniconic form in which she promised to **return to her people every two years**, and in which the goddesses are still brought from the forest to the jātara platforms (*gaddelu*) (Source: Koya oral tradition [folk/oral tradition]).
  - *Bangāram — jaggery as gold:* the signature offering is **bangāram** ("gold") — **jaggery**, often weighed against the devotee's own body, heaped on the goddesses in place of metal gold; a poor-cult offering-logic parallel to the bōnam pot (Source: Medaram festival ethnography [regional]).
  - *Koya priesthood:* the rites are performed exclusively by **non-Brahmin Koya priests**, who fetch Sāralamma from Kannepalli and Sammakka from the hill in procession — the grāmadevatā hallmark of non-Vedic priesthood at its largest scale (Source: Medaram festival ethnography [regional]).
  - *Jampanna Vagu:* the stream where her son fell is renamed **Jampanna Vagu**, and bathing in it — as pilgrims do before approaching the goddesses — honours his sacrifice and cleanses the bather, a folk-tīrtha in the fullest sense (Source: Koya oral tradition [folk/oral tradition]).
- **Pot, neem, turmeric, fire** — recurring ritual matter: the **karagam/bōnam pot** (the goddess's cooling presence), **neem leaves**, turmeric, blood-bali, and **firewalking** to "spend" the goddess's heat — shared with the Māriyammaṉ repertoire (Source: festival ethnography [regional]).

## A roster of the principal village mothers

Open-air or aniconic shrines, non-Brahmin priests, blood-offering and a fierce unmarried character are shared by all; each governs a specific protective domain.

- **Pochamma (Pōcamma)** — the Telangana/Andhra **goddess of smallpox, pox and protective fertility**, the most beloved of the Telugu village mothers; she *sends and cures* the eruptive fevers and guards children, worshipped at a stone or neem-tree shrine with bōnam-pots, turmeric and bali; the Telugu sister of **[Māriyammaṉ](mariamman.md)** and **[Śītalā](shitala.md)** in the heat-and-cooling theology (Source: Telugu grāmadevatā ethnography [regional]).
- **Pōleramma (Poleramma)** — the **boundary/guardian mother** (*pōli* = border, edge); she stands at the village limit and turns back epidemic, demon and outside evil; a chief recipient of buffalo-sacrifice and one of the "seven sisters" (Source: Whitehead; Telugu village-deity studies [regional]).
- **Maisamma (Maisamma / Mahiṣamma)** — **boundary and tank/water guardian**, often sited at the village edge, gate or irrigation-tank bund; her name links to *mahiṣa* (buffalo), tying her to the buffalo-sacrifice and to Poturāju (Source: Telangana grāmadevatā ethnography [regional]).
- **Aṅkālamman (Ankalamman / Aṅkāḷaparamēcuvari)** — the fierce **grief-and-cremation mother** of Tamil Nadu; her central myth makes her the goddess who **carries Brahmā's severed skull** (or relieves Śiva of the Brahmā-skull/*Brahmahatyā* sin), associated with the cremation-ground, with vengeful protection and with all-night possession-festivals (*Aṅkāḷamman tiruviḻā*); read as a local Kālī/Bhairavī (Source: Tamil grāmadevatā tradition [folk]).
- **Ellamma / Mutyālamma (Mutyalamma)** and kin — **Ellamma** ("mother of all," the boundary-and-all mother) and **Mutyālamma** ("pearl mother," a pox-and-protection goddess whose pustules are "pearls") head a large kin-group of Telugu/Kannada village mothers — **Gangamma, Nookalamma, Peddamma, Balamma, Dilli Polasi** and others — each a sister-aspect of the one village Mother (Source: Deccan grāmadevatā ethnography [regional]).
  - *Variant (Ellamma = Reṇukā):* across the Karnataka–Maharashtra border, **Ellamma/Yellammā is identified with Reṇukā of Saundatti**, the most elaborated of all village mothers — treated in **[renuka-yellamma.md](renuka-yellamma.md)** (Source: Deccan folk tradition [folk]).

## Selective Sanskritization into Durgā / Kālī / Pārvatī

- **Mapping onto the Great Goddess** — village mothers are progressively **identified with Purāṇic Devī forms** (Durgā the buffalo-slayer, Kālī, Cāmuṇḍā, Pārvatī, Ādi-Parāśakti) to legitimize and "upgrade" the cult; the local fierce mother becomes "a form of Durgā who descended to this village" (Source: grāmadevatā studies; Whitehead [scholarly]).
- **The buffalo-sacrifice equation** — the village buffalo-bali is read as the goddess's **Mahiṣāsura-mardana**, fusing the folk rite with the **[Devī-Māhātmya](devi-forms-and-navadurga.md)** myth (Source: comparative studies [scholarly]).
- **Reform and contestation** — Sanskritizing temple managements replace **blood-sacrifice with vegetarian Agamic worship** and install Brahmin priests, contested by village shrines that retain bali, possession and non-Brahmin pūjārīs — a documented, ongoing tension (Source: grāmadevatā studies [scholarly/disputed]).
- **The most elaborated instances** — two village goddesses outgrew the local frame into pan-regional cults with their own pilgrimage centres: **[Gangamma of Tirupati](gangamma.md)** (the fierce guardian sister of the Tirupati region with her *jātara* and guise-taking) and **[Reṇukā–Yellammā of Saundatti](renuka-yellamma.md)** (the beheaded-and-rejoined Deccan mother); both are treated in dedicated files (Source: regional cult studies [scholarly]).
