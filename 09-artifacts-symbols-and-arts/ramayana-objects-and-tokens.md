# Rāmāyaṇa Objects & Tokens

> Sits in [`09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/`](README.md) — the **object-set of the Rāmāyaṇa**, gathered in one place because the section's other rosters are Mahābhārata-weighted and churning-weighted and leave the epic's own relics scattered across seven kāṇḍa files.
> Up-link: section index [`README.md`](README.md). Weapons proper: [`divyastras-and-divine-weapons.md`](divyastras-and-divine-weapons.md) · the pan-Hindu treasure-roster and the Puṣpaka's owner-chain: [`divine-objects-and-treasures.md`](divine-objects-and-treasures.md) · the sandals at full length: [`paduka-and-the-regency-symbol.md`](paduka-and-the-regency-symbol.md).

The Mahābhārata's objects are mostly **instruments of force** — armour that cannot be pierced, a bowl that cannot be emptied, a gem that yields gold. The Rāmāyaṇa's are mostly **instruments of proof and of standing**: a ring that says who sent the messenger, a jewel that says the wife is alive, a pair of sandals that says who is king. Almost every object below does its work by being *recognised* rather than by being used, which is why the epic can hinge a whole book on a piece of jewellery and a whole regency on footwear. Sarga numbers throughout are vulgate / Southern recension.

## I. The recognition-tokens (*abhijñāna*)

The epic's technical term for these is **abhijñāna** — a thing carried so that a stranger can be believed. Vālmīki builds an exact chain of them across the middle three kāṇḍas: outbound proof, inbound proof, and a private memory that cannot be forged at all.

- **Aṅgulīya — the signet ring** - Rāma's ring, **"shining forth with his own name engraved"** (*sva-nāmāṅka*), handed to **Hanumān** as the southern search-party leaves Kiṣkindhā, so that Sītā will trust a monkey who claims to come from her husband. Rāma singles Hanumān out for it in front of the whole muster — the first sign in the epic that he has read which vānara will actually reach her. (Source: *Ramayana*, Kiṣkindhā 44.12)
  - **The delivery in the Aśoka-vāṭikā** - Hanumān produces the ring after his recital of Rāma's story has already half-convinced her; Vālmīki's Sītā, who has just been shown a counterfeit severed head of her husband, receives the ring as the first thing in her captivity that cannot lie, and her face is said to clear "like the moon released by Rāhu." (Source: *Ramayana*, Sundara 36) — see [Aśoka-vāṭikā & the Laṅkā trail](../06-sacred-geography/lanka-ramayana-trail-ashoka-vatika.md)
  - *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas):* the much-loved staging in which Hanumān, hidden in the śiṃśapā, **lets the ring fall** from the branch into Sītā's lap before speaking is Tulsīdās's; Vālmīki has him come to her and show it. [late text]
  - *Variant (folk):* the tale of Hanumān sent to the nether world after a fallen ring, finding there a whole mound of identical rings — one for every Rāma of every past cycle — is a widespread devotional folk episode outside the Sanskrit epic, and one of the tradition's sharpest images of cyclic time. [folk] [oral tradition]
- **Cūḍāmaṇi — the crest-jewel** - Sītā's return-token, and the object with the fullest provenance-chain in the epic: **found in the waters**, given by **Indra** to Janaka in pleasure at a sacrifice, given by Janaka to his daughter when she became a bride, and bound into her hair at the wedding. In the grove she unties it **from her garment**, not from her hair — she has kept it knotted in her clothes through the captivity. (Source: *Ramayana*, Sundara 38, 66)
  - **Why Rāma weeps over it** - Receiving it, Rāma says that in seeing this jewel he is as surely seeing **Janaka and his own father Daśaratha** as he is seeing Sītā. The token works backwards up two lineages at once: it is simultaneously proof of life, a father-in-law's gift, and the last surviving object of the wedding. (Source: *Ramayana*, Sundara 66)
- **The kākāsura story — the token that is not an object** - With the jewel Sītā sends a **memory**: the day at Citrakūṭa when a crow (Indra's son in crow-form) tore her, and Rāma loosed a blade of *kuśa*-grass charged as a **brahmāstra** that pursued the bird through all the worlds until it surrendered an eye. She names it explicitly as a thing to be told "as a token," because only Rāma can know it. It is the epic's purest recognition-proof — unforgeable precisely because it has no physical existence. (Source: *Ramayana*, Sundara 38)
- **The ornament bundle — the jewel-trail** - The gems and upper garment Sītā knots together and drops from the flying chariot onto the **Ṛṣyamūka** hill, gathered up by the vānaras and produced for Rāma. They are **Anasūyā's** gifts, given at Citrakūṭa and never spent, which is why she is wearing them on the day of the abduction. The identifying verse — *nāhaṃ jānāmi keyūre*, "I do not know the armlets, I do not know the earrings; I know only the anklets, from bowing every day at her feet" — is **Lakṣmaṇa's**, not Rāma's. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 118; Araṇya 52–54; Kiṣkindhā 6.22–23) — see [Atri & Anasūyā](../04-deep-dives/atri-anasuya.md)
  - *Note on the verse [disputed]:* traditional editors themselves flag these lines as possibly absent from the oldest manuscripts — the corpus's most-quoted Rāmāyaṇa verse is also one of its least secure. (commentarial tradition)
- **The pearl necklace at the coronation** - After the consecration Rāma gives Sītā a **string of pearls set with fine gems, shining like a beam of moonlight**, and tells her to bestow it on whoever pleases her in whom sharpness, firmness, renown, dexterity, competence, modesty, prudence, virility, prowess and intelligence all live together. She gives it to **Hanumān**, who wears it "like a mountain silvered by a white cloud." This is the whole of what Yuddha 128 contains — the *cirañjīvi* grant belongs elsewhere, at Uttara 108. (Source: *Ramayana*, Yuddha 128)
  - *Variant (folk):* the beloved episode in which Hanumān bites the pearls open one by one looking for Rāma inside them, and is challenged to show where Rāma is in his own body, belongs to later devotional retelling, not to the sarga. [folk]

### Counterfeit tokens

- **The illusory head and bow** - With Rāma's host encamped on **Suvela**, Rāvaṇa has the sorcerer **Vidyujjihva** conjure a severed head and a bow, presents them to Sītā as her husband's, and adds a fabricated account of a night-massacre of the vānara army — a deliberate forgery of exactly the kind of proof Hanumān had brought her. **Saramā** names it as *māyā* to Sītā one sarga later, and undoes it. (Source: *Ramayana*, Yuddha 31; 33.14) — see [Suvela](../06-sacred-geography/suvela.md), [Saramā](../04-deep-dives/characters/sarama.md)
  - *Not the same Vidyujjihva:* the war-book's conjurer shares a name with the Kālakeya dānava **Vidyujjihva**, Śūrpaṇakhā's husband, whom Rāvaṇa killed at *Uttara* 23, long before the siege. Two bearers, one name; the distinction is held at [Śūrpaṇakhā](../04-deep-dives/characters/shurpanakha.md). [disputed]
- **The māyā-Sītā** - **Indrajit** conjures a phantom Sītā and beheads her in front of Hanumān's troops to break the army's morale — and, as Vibhīṣaṇa immediately grasps, to buy himself an uninterrupted hour at the **Nikumbhilā** altar. The fake token is itself a feint. (Source: *Ramayana*, Yuddha 80–84) — see [Nikumbhilā](../06-sacred-geography/nikumbhila.md)

## II. Regalia, and objects that constitute authority

- **The Pādukā — the sandals** - Rāma's gold-adorned wooden sandals, which **Bharata** asks for in place of Rāma himself, carries home, and installs on the throne as sovereign, ruling as their servant from **Nandigrāma** for fourteen years. The corpus's single clearest case of an object made into a constitutional instrument; full treatment, including the doctrine and the later pādukā-cults, in the dedicated file. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 112, 115; Yuddha 127) — see [The Pādukā & the regency symbol](paduka-and-the-regency-symbol.md)
- **The white parasol and the two cāmaras** - The **śveta-cchatra** (white state umbrella) and the pair of white yak-tail whisks with golden handles are Ayodhyā's working insignia of kingship. Bharata holds them over the *sandals*, not over himself, and carries them out to Nandigrāma unused for fourteen years to hand back at the reunion — the insignia doing in miniature what the whole regency does. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 115; Yuddha 127)
- **Rāvaṇa's crown** - From the summit of **Suvela**, **Sugrīva** springs unaided onto the gateway where Rāvaṇa stands, tears the bright crown from his head and throws it to the ground before grappling with him. Nothing tactical is won; what is destroyed is the display of sovereignty, in front of both armies, by a vānara. (Source: *Ramayana*, Yuddha 40)
- **Vālī's kāñcana-mālā — the golden garland** - Indra's gift to his son. Vālmīki's stated property is not strength-transfer but **residency**: the *dying* Vālī tells Sugrīva to take the chain from him at once, because **the bounteous goddess of triumph who dwells in it will leave it at his death** rather than touch a corpse. It passes to Sugrīva with Tārā and the kingdom. The popular "the garland gave Vālī half his opponent's strength" clause is grafted on; Vālmīki does not state it. (Source: *Ramayana*, Kiṣkindhā 22.16, 25) [folk]
- **The krodhāgāra — the anger-chamber** - Not an object but a court **institution** the epic handles like one: a bare room a queen withdraws into in formal displeasure, stripping off her ornaments and lying on the floor, which obliges the king to come to her and ask what she wants. **Kaikeyī**, coached by Mantharā, uses it to extract the two boons — the whole exile turns on a piece of palace procedure. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 9–10) — see [Kaikeyī](../04-deep-dives/characters/kaikeyi.md)

## III. Made things, and the vehicles

- **The Setu (Nala-setu)** - Not a floating causeway in Vālmīki. **Nala's** boon from his father **Viśvakarman** is **craft equal to Viśvakarman's own**, and the sea-god **Sāgara himself bears** the stones and trees the vānaras cast down; the bridge is ten yojanas broad and a hundred long, laid in five days. **Nothing floats.** (Source: *Ramayana*, Yuddha 22) — see [Rāmeśvaram & Setu-bandha](../06-sacred-geography/rameswaram-setu-bandha.md)
  - *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas):* the stones float because Rāma's name is inscribed on them. *Variant (folk):* the squirrel who carries sand in her fur and is stroked into stripes. Neither is Vālmīki. [late text / folk]
- **The golden mule-yoked chariot** - The vehicle of the abduction: a **golden car drawn by mules**, never named Puṣpaka anywhere in the text. **Jaṭāyu** destroys it in the air — car, driver and mules — after which Rāvaṇa completes the flight **bodily**, carrying Sītā in his arms. (Source: *Ramayana*, Araṇya 35, 49, 51–52) — see [Jaṭāyu & Sampāti](../04-deep-dives/characters/jatayu-and-sampati.md)
- **The Puṣpaka Vimāna** - The self-moving flying palace, Viśvakarman-made, held in turn by Brahmā, Kubera and Rāvaṇa, and finally flown home by Rāma; it is a **Laṅkā** object throughout and has no part in the abduction. Full owner-chain and the Kubera re-gift clause are catalogued with the pan-Hindu treasures. (Source: *Ramayana*, Sundara 7–8; Yuddha 121–125; Uttara 3, 14–15, 41) — see [Divine Objects & Treasures](divine-objects-and-treasures.md)
- **Indrajit's chariot and the inexhaustible quivers** - Won, not made: from the seven sacrifices completed at **Nikumbhilā** under Śukra's officiating, Meghanāda receives from Brahmā a **celestial chariot that goes at will through the sky**, the **māyā that darkens a battlefield so that he fights unseen**, **two inexhaustible quivers**, a **bow no weapon can shatter**, and a great arrow. Every one of these is an object with a ritual condition attached to it, which is why the war can be won by interrupting a fire-rite. (Source: *Ramayana*, Uttara 25; cf. Uttara 29–30) — see [Indrajit](../04-deep-dives/characters/indrajit-meghnad.md), [Nikumbhilā](../06-sacred-geography/nikumbhila.md)

## IV. Blades and gifts from the gods

- **Candrahāsa ("laughter of the moon")** - The sword **Śiva** gives Rāvaṇa at the close of the **Kailāsa** episode, together with the name *Rāvaṇa*, "he who makes the worlds cry out" — the reward for the thousand-year song sung from beneath the mountain the boaster had tried to uproot. Object and name are granted in the same breath, which is the point of the sarga: the demon leaves with a blade and an identity, both of them Śiva's. (Source: *Ramayana*, Uttara 16)
  - *Variant (later tradition):* the clause that the blade turns on a wielder who uses it unjustly is **not stated at Uttara 16**; it is later devotional gloss. [late text]
- **Agastya's gift-set in the forest** - Before Rāma settles at Pañcavaṭī the sage **Agastya** arms him: the great **bow of Viṣṇu** made by Viśvakarman, **two inexhaustible quivers**, a **sword** in a golden sheath, and an arrow — the forest years' whole armoury, given by a brahmin to a prince who has renounced kingship. (Source: *Ramayana*, Araṇya 12) — see [Agastya](../04-deep-dives/agastya.md) and [Divyāstras & Divine Weapons](divyastras-and-divine-weapons.md)
- **The Brahmā-arrow that kills Rāvaṇa** - The shaft **Agastya** gives Rāma on the field, prompted by **Mātali** when nothing else will end the duel: Brahmā's own arrow, wind in its feathers, fire and sun in its head, Meru and Mandara in its weight. It **tears open Rāvaṇa's heart**, and returns of itself to the quiver. There is no amṛta and no navel anywhere in the sarga. (Source: *Ramayana*, Yuddha 108) — full weapon-entry in [Divyāstras & Divine Weapons](divyastras-and-divine-weapons.md)

## V. The herbs, and the vessel that begins the epic

- **The four Sañjīvanī herbs** - The epic's healing-relics are four **named** herbs, not one, and Vālmīki stages the herb-flight **twice**, with different speakers, different casualties and different sargas. **First flight (Yuddha 74):** after Indrajit's brahmāstra fells both brothers, **Jāmbavān** names *mṛta-sañjīvanī* (restores the dead), *viśalya-karaṇī* (draws out embedded weapons and closes the wound), *suvarṇa-karaṇī* (restores the body's colour) and *sandhānī* (knits severed limb and broken bone), growing on a blazing herb-mountain **between Mount Ṛṣabha and the golden peak of Kailāsa**, which the sarga leaves unnamed. **Second flight (Yuddha 101–102):** after Rāvaṇa's Maya-made śakti fells Lakṣmaṇa, the physician **Suṣeṇa** sends Hanumān to a mountain the text *does* name — **Oṣadhi**, also called **Mahodaya**. Both times Hanumān cannot tell one herb from another and brings back the whole summit. (Source: *Ramayana*, Yuddha 74, 101–102)
  - *Note on the name:* **"Droṇagiri" is not a Vālmīki place-name** — it appears nowhere in the epic. It belongs to later Purāṇic and Rāmcaritmānas-era retelling and to the sthala-purāṇa of the Uttarākhaṇḍ village that claims the torn-out peak. **Gandhamādana**, which Vālmīki does use — both for a mountain and for a vānara commander — is likewise never the herb-source of either flight. [folk/regional]
- **The golden vessel of pāyasa** - The object that starts everything: from **Daśaratha's** *putrakāmeṣṭi* fire rises a radiant being bearing a **golden vessel of pāyasa** (sweet milk-rice) and instructs the king to divide it among his queens. Kausalyā takes half, Kaikeyī half the remainder, and **Sumitrā two portions** — which is why she bears twins, and why the tradition reads Lakṣmaṇa as attaching to Rāma and Śatrughna to Bharata. (Source: *Ramayana*, Bāla 15–16) — see [Ṛṣyaśṛṅga](../04-deep-dives/rishyashringa.md), [Sumitrā](../04-deep-dives/characters/sumitra.md), [Kausalyā](../04-deep-dives/characters/kausalya.md)
