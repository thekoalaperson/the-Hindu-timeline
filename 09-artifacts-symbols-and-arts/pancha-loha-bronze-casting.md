# Pañca-loha and the lost-wax bronze mūrti (the śilpin's sacred metallurgy)

> Sits in [`09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/`](README.md) — the **material counterpart to iconometry**: where [`murti-lakshana-and-iconometry.md`](murti-lakshana-and-iconometry.md) gives the proportional canon (*tālamāna*) that lays out the icon, this file gives the **metal and the fire** that cast it — the five/eight-metal alloys (*pañca-loha*, *aṣṭadhātu*) and the lost-wax (*madhūcchiṣṭa-vidhāna*) process by which a wax model becomes a consecrated bronze deity. Up-link: section index [`README.md`](README.md) · master index [`The Hindu Timeline`](../README.md).

The same *bera-lakṣaṇa* / *tālamāna* that governs the carved stone icon also governs the **cast metal image** (*dhātu-pratimā*) — but metal adds its own *śāstra*: which metals, in what proportion, are auspicious for which deity, and how the *sthapati* (master image-maker) turns wax into a single seamless pour. The supreme historical achievement of this craft is the **Cōḻa bronze** — the Naṭarāja of the **Ānanda-Tāṇḍava** chief among them (the icon itself read in [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md) and the Śiva dance-myth in [`../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md`](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md)). **Two clocks:** on the **scriptural/āgamic clock** the metals and rules are revealed canon (the *Mānasāra*, *Mayamata* and *Manasollāsa* present *dhātu-pratimā* as fixed *śilpa*); on the **historical clock** this is a **dated, continuously-living guild craft** — the Viśvakarma *sthapati* lineages of Svāmimalai/Thañjāvūr still casting today, and the great surviving bronzes datable to the recorded (kali-historical) Cōḻa centuries (c. 9th–13th c. CE). [scholarly]

---

## I. The Sacred Alloys — Pañca-loha & Aṣṭadhātu

- **Pañca-loha — the five metals** — the canonical alloy for a sacred image is the **"five metals,"** conventionally **gold (*svarṇa/hema*), silver (*rajata*), copper (*tāmra*), zinc/brass (*yaśada/rītī*) and lead or iron (*sīsa/loha*)** — copper forming the dominant base into which the others are added in prescribed measure. The mixture is held to be more *prāsādika* (grace-bearing) and more *maṅgala* than any single metal. (Source: *Śilpa Śāstra* tradition; *Śukranīti* 4) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (the five named differently):* the constituents are **not perfectly fixed across traditions** — some lists give gold, silver, copper, **brass** and **tin/lead**; others substitute **iron** for lead, and many South Indian *sthapati* castings are in practice **high-copper bronze with small additions of silver/gold** rather than a literal equal five. (Source: South Indian *sthapati* practice; Sivaramamurti) [regional/disputed]
- **Aṣṭadhātu — the eight metals** — a richer alloy of **eight** metals (the five plus further additions — variously **gold, silver, copper, zinc, lead, tin, iron and mercury/*pārada*** in the common reckoning) used for especially auspicious or potent images; the term is widely applied to revered temple bronzes. (Source: *Śilpa Śāstra* tradition) [scholarly]
- **Auspicious metal per deity** — the texts key **metal to deity and end**: gold and silver for the highest/wealth-giving forms, copper as the all-purpose sacred base, specific metals enjoined or avoided for particular gods. The *Śukranīti* (4.4) prescribes the proper *dhātu* for an image fit for *pūjā*, and treats stone, metal, wood, clay and jewel as graded media. (Source: *Śukranīti* 4) [late text]
- **Manasollāsa on metal images** — the **Abhilaṣitārtha-cintāmaṇi** (*Mānasollāsa*) of the Cālukya king **Someśvara III** (12th c.) — itself a **dated royal encyclopedia** — treats *dhātu-pratimā* and the proportions/ornament of cast images among the kingly arts, a *śāstric* witness standing on the historical clock. (Source: *Mānasollāsa* of Someśvara III) [scholarly]
- **Mānasāra & Mayamata on dhātu-pratimā** — the two great *śilpa/vāstu* manuals carry the **bera-lakṣaṇa** that explicitly covers **metal as a medium** alongside stone and stucco, the metal image sharing the *tālamāna* of the carved one but adding alloy and casting rule. (Source: *Mānasāra*; *Mayamata*) [scholarly]

## II. Madhūcchiṣṭa-vidhāna — The Lost-Wax (Cire-Perdue) Process

The South Indian bronze is **solid-cast by the lost-wax (*cire-perdue*) method in a single pour** — a *madhu-ucchiṣṭa* ("bees-wax leavings") technique whose name records its core: a **wax model that is melted away and replaced by metal.** Each casting is **unique** — the mould is destroyed to release the image, so there are no two identical bronzes. (Source: Dehejia, *Chola: Sacred Bronzes*; Sivaramamurti, *South Indian Bronzes*) [scholarly]

- **1. Madhūcchiṣṭa — the wax model** — the *sthapati* first models the full image in **beeswax** (mixed with resin/*dhūpa* and a little oil for workability), shaping it *to the tālamāna canon* — every limb, attribute, ornament and the eyes worked in wax exactly as the finished bronze must stand. The wax is the true sculpture; the metal merely copies it. (Source: *sthapati* practice; Sivaramamurti) [scholarly]
- **2. Clay investment** — the wax is coated in successive layers of **fine clay** (often with charred husk/sand and cow-dung temper) to capture the finest detail, then thicker clay to build a strong **mould (*mṛt-pātra*)**, with wax sprues/vents left for pouring and venting. (Source: *sthapati* practice; Dehejia) [scholarly]
- **3. De-waxing & firing** — the clay-cased model is **heated**; the wax **runs out** (the "loss" of *cire-perdue*), leaving a hollow cavity that is the exact negative of the image, and the clay is **fired hard** to a ceramic mould. (Source: *sthapati* practice) [scholarly]
- **4. The single pour** — the alloy (*pañca-loha*/bronze) is **melted in a crucible and poured in one continuous pour** into the heated mould to fill the cavity; the solid-cast image must take the metal **completely in that single act** — a failed or interrupted pour ruins the icon. (Source: Dehejia; Sivaramamurti) [scholarly]
- **5. Breaking out & chasing** — the clay mould is **broken away** (destroying it forever), the raw casting freed, sprues cut, and the surface **chased, filed and finished (*tᾱkṣaṇa*)** by hand — the fine ornament, jewellery and facial detail refined cold, and a **patina** developed. (Source: *sthapati* practice; Sivaramamurti) [scholarly]
- **The Svāmimalai / Thañjāvūr sthapatis** — the living transmitters are the **Viśvakarma *sthapati* (śilpin) lineages** of **Svāmimalai** and the Thañjāvūr (Kāveri delta) region of Tamil Nadu, hereditary bronze-casters who trace their craft and *gotra* to **Viśvakarman**, the divine artisan, and still cast *pañca-loha* mūrtis by the same lost-wax rite. (Source: Svāmimalai *sthapati* tradition; Dehejia) [regional/scholarly]

## III. Utsava-mūrti vs Mūla-mūrti — Why Metal at All

- **The fixed stone main icon** — the **mūla-/dhruva-bera** (root/immovable icon) in the *garbhagṛha* ("womb-house") is typically of **stone**, permanently installed, the seat of the deity's primary presence (the shrine that houses it → [`temple-architecture-nagara-dravida-vesara.md`](temple-architecture-nagara-dravida-vesara.md)). (Source: *Āgama* / Vaikhānasa tradition) [scholarly]
- **The movable festival bronze** — the **utsava-mūrti** (also *kautuka-/bhoga-bera*) is the **portable metal image** that **leaves the sanctum** to be bathed, processed through the streets on festival days, and to receive the *bhoga* (enjoyment) offerings on the deity's behalf — hence cast in **bronze**, light and durable enough to be carried, where stone cannot move. The **Vaikhānasa Āgama** fixes this **dhruva/utsava distinction by distinct measures** (cross-ref [`murti-lakshana-and-iconometry.md`](murti-lakshana-and-iconometry.md)). (Source: *Vaikhānasa Āgama*; Dehejia) [scholarly/sectarian]
- **The bronze as the god in the world** — because the bronze **goes out among the people**, the great processional icons became the focus of patronage and artistry — the metal image is where temple, festival and royal donation meet (the symbol-grammar of these emblems → [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md); the painted counterpart → [`chitra-kala-painting-traditions.md`](chitra-kala-painting-traditions.md)). (Source: Dehejia, *Chola: Sacred Bronzes*) [scholarly]

## IV. The Cōḻa Bronze — The Supreme Achievement

The bronzes of the **Cōḻa empire** (Tamil Nadu, c. 9th–13th c. CE) are the high-water mark of the craft worldwide — and stand squarely on the **historical clock**, datable by **inscription and reign**.

- **Naṭarāja — the Ānanda-Tāṇḍava** — the **dancing Śiva** within the ring of fire (*prabhā-maṇḍala*) is the **iconic Cōḻa bronze**: the *atibhaṅga* dance-flexion, *ḍamaru* and *agni*, *abhaya-* and *gaja-hasta*, trampling **Apasmāra**, embodying the **pañcakṛtya** (five cosmic acts). The full icon-reading sits in [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md); its myth in [`../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md`](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md). The Cōḻa atelier perfected the form as cast bronze. (Source: Sivaramamurti; Dehejia) [scholarly]
- **Rajarāja I & the Bṛhadīśvara commissions** — **Rājarāja I Cōḻa** (r. c. 985–1014 CE) built the great **Bṛhadīśvara (Rājarājeśvaram) temple at Thañjāvūr**, and its **stone inscriptions record numerous metal images (*tirumēni*) donated to the temple** — solid-cast bronzes of Śiva, Devī and the saints, gifted with their weight in metal specified — among the **earliest dated, inscriptionally-attested bronzes**. (Source: Thañjāvūr / Bṛhadīśvara Cōḻa inscriptions; Dehejia) [scholarly]
- **Sembiyaṉ Mahādevī — the queen-patron** — the Cōḻa queen **Sembiyaṉ Mahādevī** (10th c.), aunt/grand-patron of the dynasty, was a **prolific donor of temples and bronze icons**, her commissions a key dated horizon in the bronze sequence; her patronage and that of the **Tiruvārūr** and other delta temples seeded the great processional images. (Source: Dehejia, *Chola: Sacred Bronzes*; Cōḻa inscriptions) [scholarly]
- **Inscriptional patronage as the historical record** — Cōḻa temple inscriptions (Thañjāvūr, **Tiruvārūr**, and across the Kāveri delta) record **donors, the metal weight, and the very names of the images** cast — so that the bronze tradition, unlike the timeless *śāstra*, is **dated to reign and benefaction**. (Source: South Indian Cōḻa inscriptions; Sivaramamurti) [scholarly]

## V. Shared Canon & Consecration — From Cast Metal to Living God

- **Bera-lakṣaṇa / tālamāna shared with iconometry** — the cast bronze obeys the **same proportional canon** as the carved stone: *daśa-/nava-/aṣṭa-tāla* schemes, *aṅga-pratyaṅga* sub-measures, *āsana–mudrā–āyudha–vāhana* identity-grammar — modelled first in the wax (full system in [`murti-lakshana-and-iconometry.md`](murti-lakshana-and-iconometry.md)). Metal changes the *medium*, not the *measure*. (Source: *Mānasāra*; *Mayamata*; *Viṣṇudharmottara* III, *Citrasūtra*) [scholarly]
- **Prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā — life-installation** — a perfectly cast bronze is still inert metal until the **prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā** rite "installs the life-breath," invoking the deity to **descend and dwell** in the image, making it a fit object of *pūjā*. (Source: *Āgama* *kriyāpāda*; *Śilpa Śāstra*) [scholarly]
- **Netra-unmīlana — opening the eyes** — the culminating act is the **eye-opening (*netra-unmīlana / nayanonmīlana*)**: the *śilpin* or priest **carves/paints open the icon's eyes** (often with a golden needle and honey/ghee), the moment at which the image **first "sees"** and becomes the living god — held so charged that the first gaze is shielded or directed at a mirror or auspicious object. (Source: *Āgama* / *śilpa* consecration tradition) [scholarly]
- **The śilpin's sacred discipline** — across these steps the *sthapati* works under **vrata** (vow), purity and *dhyāna*, "seeing" the deity inwardly before shaping it — the *Śukranīti* insisting the maker follow the *dhyāna* and *māna*, not a living model, so the image is fit to be **animated** at all. (Source: *Śukranīti* 4) [late text]
