# Rangoli, Kōlam & the Sacred Floor-Art Tradition

> Sits in [`09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/`](README.md) — the **living sacred floor-art** of the Hindu household: the threshold-drawing that turns swept ground into a daily-renewed maṇḍala of welcome, auspiciousness, and the invitation of Śrī-Lakṣmī.

This file expands a single line of the [64-Kalā roster](sixty-four-kalas.md) — Vātsyāyana's #7, **Taṇḍula-kusuma-balivikāra** ("offering-designs from coloured rice and flowers") — into a full treatment of the **pan-Indian sacred floor-drawing tradition**: at once a **classical fine art** (a *kalā*), a **daily domestic rite** of auspiciousness and threshold-protection, and a **folk-geometric cousin of the yantra/maṇḍala**. Per repo policy, **every regional name, material and ritual use is catalogued to source**; the tradition is genuinely **plural** — each region carries its own vocabulary, medium and grammar, **never merged**.

---

## I. The classical anchor — the 64th Kalā

- **Taṇḍula-kusuma-balivikāra** — the **7th of Vātsyāyana's sixty-four arts**: the making of *bali* (offering) **designs from *taṇḍula* (rice) and *kusuma* (flowers)**, laid on the ground as ornament and oblation. This is the single classical text-anchor for the whole living tradition. (Source: *Kāmasūtra* 1.3, *Vidyā-samuddeśa*; see [`sixty-four-kalas.md`](sixty-four-kalas.md) #7) [scholarly]
  - *Note — the art's place in the roster:* it sits among the **decorative/auspicious arts** (alongside #5 *Ālekhya* painting, #6 *Viśeṣaka-cchedya* face-marking, #8 *Puṣpāstaraṇa* flower-spreading), confirming that floor-decoration was reckoned a cultivated skill, not merely a chore. (Source: *Kāmasūtra* 1.3)
- **Etymology of *rangoli*** — from Sanskrit ***raṅgāvalī*** ("a row/creeper of colour"), *raṅga* = colour + *āvalī* = row/series; popularly glossed as "a colourful expression of the soul." (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*) [scholarly]
- **Etymology of *kōlam*** — Tamil *kōlam* (கோலம்) = "form, beauty, adornment, guise"; the floor-design that gives the threshold its daily "form." (Source: Wikipedia, *Kolam*)
- **A purāṇic origin-legend** — one widely-told tale (cited via the *Citralakṣaṇa*, the earliest Indian treatise on painting) makes floor-drawing the **origin of painting itself**: when a priest's/king's son died, **Brahmā** bade the grieving father draw the boy's likeness on the ground so He could breathe life back into it — the first painted image. (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*, citing the *Chitralakshana*) [late text][disputed]

## II. The regional forms — one art, many vocabularies

The same practice carries a different name, medium and idiom in each region. **Each is catalogued separately and not merged.**

- *Rangoli (north & west India):* the umbrella/Hindi-Marathi term for the **coloured-powder floor-art** of Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka and the north; typically **free-hand, filled-in and polychrome**, drawn at the doorstep and in the courtyard, peaking at **Diwali**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*) [regional]
- *Kōlam (Tamil Nadu):* the **white rice-flour line-drawing** of Tamil households, drawn fresh **every dawn** on the swept, cow-dung-or-water-washed threshold; characteristically **geometric, looping and continuous**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kolam*) [regional]
  - *Pulli-kōlam (dot-grid kōlam):* the signature Tamil **dot-lattice** method — a grid of *puḷḷi* (dots) is laid first, then a **single unbroken line** is woven **around and between** the dots to close every loop. The mathematically richest form, studied as **array/picture grammars and knot-theory loops**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kolam*; Gift Siromoney, *kōlam picture-languages*) [scholarly]
  - *Sikku / kambi kōlam:* the "twisted/wire" line-kōlam, a continuous looping line that must **return to its start** without lifting the hand. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kolam*) [regional]
- *Muggu (Telugu — Andhra & Telangana):* the Telugu floor-art, likewise dot-based (*chukkala muggu*), drawn in rice-flour at the threshold; the **Saṅkrānti/Pongal-season muggulu** are especially elaborate, often centred on a *gobbemma* (cow-dung-and-flower mound). (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*/*Muggu*) [regional]
- *Ālpana / Alpona (Bengal & Assam):* the eastern form, painted in a **white rice-paste** (*piṭuli/* rice-flour batter) applied with the fingers or a cloth wick — **flowing, vegetal, free-hand floral and creeper motifs** rather than geometry; drawn for *vratas* and festivals such as **Lakṣmī-pūjā** and the *Brata* rites. Often derived from Sanskrit *ālimpana* ("plastering/anointing"). (Source: Wikipedia, *Alpana*/*Rangoli*) [regional]
- *Maṇḍana (Rajasthan & Madhya Pradesh):* the tribal/folk **wall-and-floor painting** of Rajasthan, executed in **white chalk/lime (khaḍiya) over a red-ochre (geru) ground**, with motifs of peacocks, *cauk* squares, the svastika and footprints of Lakṣmī; drawn for festivals, weddings and protection of the home. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mandana*) [regional]
- *Other regional names (catalogued, not merged):* **Aripana** (Mithila/Bihar), **Jhoti / Chita** (Odisha), **Chowkpurana** (Uttar Pradesh), **Sona/Sathiya** (Gujarat), **Aripan/Aipan** (Kumaon), **Hase / Rangavalli** (Karnataka). (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*) [regional]

## III. Meaning & ritual use — why the floor is drawn

- **Daily threshold-auspiciousness** — the kōlam/muggu is renewed **at dawn** on the doorstep (*vāsal*), the liminal boundary between street and home; the act sanctifies the household's day and is read as a sign that the home is awake, ordered and **welcoming**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kolam*) [folk]
- **Invitation of Lakṣmī (prosperity)** — the auspicious design is an **invitation to Śrī-Lakṣmī to enter and reside**; an ill-kept or undrawn threshold is held to repel the goddess of fortune. The motif of **Lakṣmī's footprints** (*Lakṣmī-pada*) drawn **walking into** the house is widespread, especially at Diwali. (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*; festival custom) [folk] — Lakṣmī ↔ [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md)
- **Feeding the small lives (ahiṃsā)** — the **rice-flour** medium is deliberately edible: the kōlam **feeds ants, birds and insects**, so the day begins with an act of giving to the smallest creatures — the *bali* (offering) sense preserved from *Taṇḍula-bali*. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kolam*) [folk]
- **Threshold-protection / boundary** — the closed, unbroken loops of the *sikku*/*pulli* kōlam are read as a **protective trap** that **demons/evil cannot cross** without becoming snared, sealing the home's perimeter. (Source: kōlam folk lore; Wikipedia, *Kolam*) [folk][regional]
- **Mourning and abstention** — the kōlam's **daily renewal is suspended** during household death-pollution and certain inauspicious days; its **presence or absence itself signals** the home's ritual state. (Source: Tamil household custom) [folk]

## IV. Festival & vrata designs (the ritual calendar)

- *Diwali / Deepāvalī (pan-India):* the high season of **coloured rangoli**, drawn large and polychrome at the doorstep to welcome **Lakṣmī** on the night of her pūjā; paired with **dīpa (lamp)** rows. (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*; festival custom) [folk]
- *Mārgaḻi / Mārgaśīrṣa kōlam (Tamil, Dec–Jan):* the auspicious month of **Mārgaḻi** sees the **largest, most elaborate dawn kōlams** of the year, drawn through the cold pre-dawn; associated with the **Tiruppāvai/Āṇṭāḷ** devotional season. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kolam*; Tamil custom) [regional]
- *Pongal / Saṅkrānti (mid-Jan harvest):* doorways and cattle-yards carry festive kōlam/muggu around the **boiling-over of the Pongal pot** and the *gobbemma* mounds of Andhra; the harvest-thanksgiving design. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kolam*/*Muggu*; festival custom) [regional]
- *Vrata & women's rites:* **ālpana** for **Lakṣmī-pūjā** and Bengali *brata* vows; the **maṇḍana** *cauk* for weddings and **Govardhan/Annakūṭa**; floor-*cauk*s laid as the **seat (āsana/pīṭha)** on which the deity or the worshipper sits during pūjā. (Source: Wikipedia, *Alpana*, *Mandana*; vrata custom) [regional][folk]

## V. Materials & medium

- **Rice flour (*taṇḍula-piṣṭa*)** — the classical and most widespread medium for the white line-work (kōlam, muggu, ālpana batter); edible, biodegradable, and the literal *taṇḍula* of the kalā's name. (Source: *Kāmasūtra* 1.3; Wikipedia, *Kolam*)
- **Coloured powders** — for polychrome rangoli: dry pigments, **dyed rice-flour, ground/coloured rice, dried-leaf and flower powders, turmeric (yellow), kumkum/sindūr (red), charcoal, brick-dust, and coloured sand/sawdust**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*)
- **Flowers (*kusuma*)** — petals and whole blooms laid as floral rangoli (*puṣpa-rangoli* / *pookkalam*), the *kusuma* of the kalā; **marigold, rose, jasmine and chrysanthemum** dominate (Kerala's **Onam pookkalam** is the great floral-carpet form). (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*) — flowers ↔ [`sacred-flora-and-fauna.md`](sacred-flora-and-fauna.md)
- **Ground & priming** — the surface is **swept, washed, and primed**: south-Indian thresholds are wiped with **cow-dung/water** (purifying, the kōlam's traditional ground), Rajasthani maṇḍana over a **red-ochre (geru)** wash. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kolam*, *Mandana*) [regional]

## VI. Link to maṇḍala / yantra sacred geometry

- **Floor-art as folk-yantra** — the dot-grid kōlam and the square *cauk* share the **maṇḍala's concentric, centred, bounded grammar**: a **bindu/centre**, an enclosing boundary, and a symmetric in-filling — the household-level, daily-renewed counterpart of the priestly **yantra/maṇḍala**. (Source: tradition; cf. [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md) §II) [scholarly]
- **Shared auspicious motifs** — the **svastika**, **lotus (padma)**, **śatkoṇa (hexagram)**, **conch**, and **Lakṣmī-footprints** recur across both the formal yantra and the doorstep rangoli, linking domestic floor-art to the wider **Aṣṭamaṅgala** sign-system. (Source: Wikipedia, *Rangoli*; cf. [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md)) [folk/iconographic]
- **The two clocks** — *the tradition keeps the scriptural-vs-historical distinction:* on the **scriptural clock** the art is fixed in *kalpa*-old auspiciousness lore and the timeless 64-Kalā curriculum; on the **historical clock** it is a **continuously-practised living folk art** of the recorded (kali-historical) period, attested in classical text but surviving as everyday household ritual into the present. (Source: repo "two-clocks" convention) [scholarly]
