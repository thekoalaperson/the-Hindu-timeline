# The Pādukā & the Regency Symbol

> Sits in [`09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/`](README.md) — the **pādukā (sandals)** as an object of doctrine: the one thing in the Hindu epics that is handed over *instead of* a person and then governs a kingdom for fourteen years.
> Up-link: section index [`README.md`](README.md). The epic's wider object-set: [`ramayana-objects-and-tokens.md`](ramayana-objects-and-tokens.md) · the feet-motif as a pan-Hindu emblem: [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md) · the place they ruled from: [`../06-sacred-geography/nandigrama.md`](../06-sacred-geography/nandigrama.md).

A **pādukā** is the plainest possible object — a wooden sole with a toe-knob, the footwear of ascetics because it is made without leather and touches the ground at two points. The Rāmāyaṇa takes that object and does something no other Indian narrative does with a thing: it makes it the **holder of sovereignty**. For fourteen years the kingdom of Kosala has a king who is not present, a regent who refuses the title, and a pair of sandals on the throne under a white parasol, to which the orders of state are read out before they are issued. Everything the later tradition does with pādukā — guru-worship, the Śrīvaiṣṇava theology of the feet, the Vārkarī palanquins — traces its warrant back to this. Sarga numbers are vulgate / Southern recension.

---

## I. The object itself

- **What Bharata asks for** - At Citrakūṭa, having failed by every argument to move Rāma back to Ayodhyā, **Bharata** asks instead for Rāma's **sandals**. Rāma **places his feet in them** and hands them over — the gesture matters, because what is transferred is not footwear but the impression of the feet. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 112)
- **Wooden, gold-adorned** - Vālmīki describes them as **wooden sandals adorned with gold** (*pāduke hema-bhūṣite*) that will "bestow gain and security upon all the people" — the phrasing of a royal grant, applied to a pair of shoes. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 112, 115)
- **Bharata's terms** - He takes them on two conditions of his own making: he will wear **matted locks and bark** for the fourteen years, living exactly as Rāma lives; and **if Rāma is not there on the day after the fourteenth year ends, he will enter the fire**. The regency is time-bounded by a suicide clause. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 112)
- **Carried home on an elephant's head** - Bharata sets the sandals **on the head of a fine elephant** for the return journey and worships them on the road — the sandals travel in the position a king travels in, and the king's brother walks. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 112)

## II. What the sandals actually do — the mechanics of the regency

- **Enthroned, not merely kept** - At **Nandigrāma** Bharata sets the sandals **on the royal throne** and orders the **state canopy held over them**, "these wooden sandals, which have been accepted as the symbols of the feet of Rāma." The white parasol and the two golden-handled cāmaras — Ayodhyā's working insignia — are used over the object, never over the man. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 115) — see [Nandigrāma](../06-sacred-geography/nandigrama.md)
- **The kingdom held in trust** - Bharata's own formula to Vasiṣṭha and the ministers is legal, not sentimental: his brother "has given me this kingdom **as a trust**," and he will preserve it until his return. He is not a king with a modest style; he is a **custodian of someone else's property**. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 115)
- **Orders reported to the sandals** - The operative detail, and the one retellings drop: Bharata **reports all royal orders to the wooden sandals** and refers every matter of consequence to them before acting. Petitions and offerings are presented to the sandals first. The sandals are not a memento on a shelf; they are the office through which business passes. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 115)
- **The regent lives as the exile does** - Bark robes, matted hair, the ground for a bed, ascetic fare, and residence outside the capital. Bharata governs Kosala without once entering Ayodhyā's palace or wearing its dress; **Śatrughna** shares the vigil with him for the whole period. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 115) — see [Bharata & Śatrughna](../04-deep-dives/characters/bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md)
- **The hand-back** - At the reunion Bharata — emaciated by fasting, in bark and black-antelope skin, carrying the unused white parasol and the two whisks — takes the sandals and **sets them himself beneath Rāma's feet**. The object is not retired, revered or enshrined; it is put back on the man it was standing in for, and the regency ends in the same gesture that began it. (Source: *Ramayana*, Yuddha 127)

## III. The doctrine the object carries

- **Sovereignty is not in the ruler** - The pādukā-regency states, in a form no treatise states it, that kingship inheres in a **rightful claim** rather than in occupancy of the throne, and that the claim can be represented by a token. Bharata's refusal is not modesty; it is a constitutional position — an usurped throne would be void whether or not the usurper governed well. (epic doctrine) [scholarly]
- **The token as the limit on the regent** - Because the sandals are *there*, and orders are read to them, the arrangement is also a check: it gives Bharata's ministers, and posterity, a fixed point against which the regent's conduct can be measured. This is why the fourteen years read as **Rāma-rājya before Rāma returns**. [scholarly]
- **Contrast within the epics** - The Mahābhārata's succession crises turn on who occupies the seat — a blind king, a regent-grandsire bound by a vow, a throne played away at dice. The Rāmāyaṇa answers the same question by **emptying the seat and filling it with an object**, and the tradition has treated Bharata's solution ever since as the ideal case rather than the strange one. [scholarly]
- **Why footwear and not a crown** - The chosen token is the one that touches the feet — the lowest and, in the tradition's ranking of reverence, the most worshipful part of the sovereign. A crown would have transferred *rank*; the sandals transfer **subordination**, which is exactly what Bharata means to claim for himself. [scholarly]

## IV. Pādukā after the epic — cult, theology and practice

- **Rāma-pādukā and the pādukā-shrine** - The sandals themselves become an object of worship in **Rāmānandī** and wider Rāma-bhakti practice, with pādukā enshrined and processed as the Lord's presence-in-absence; the [Nandigrāma / Bharatkuṇḍ](../06-sacred-geography/nandigrama.md) site keeps a pādukā shrine on the ground of the regency. (devotional tradition) [regional]
- **The Pādukā-sahasram** - **Vedānta Deśika's** thousand-and-eight verses in praise of **Raṅganātha's sandals** — composed, by tradition, in a single night to answer a rival poet's challenge, and containing a *citra-paddhati* chapter of picture-pattern verses. Its whole theological warrant is Ayodhyā: the pādukā that ruled a kingdom are read as the Lord's *ācārya*, the intermediary through which he is approached. (Source: Wikipedia, *Paduka Sahasram*) [traditional] — see [Vedānta Deśika & the later Śrīvaiṣṇava ācāryas](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/vedanta-desika-and-later-srivaishnava-acharyas.md)
- **The guru's pādukā** - In *guru-śiṣya paramparā*, worship of the guru's sandals (**pādukā-pūjā**) stands for the guru when he is absent or dead, and the *pīṭha* passes with them; the **Guru-pādukā stotra** is recited across sampradāyas. The logic is Bharata's exactly — the sandals hold the office. (pan-Hindu devotional practice) — see [Guru-śiṣya paramparā](../05-concepts-and-dharma/guru-shishya-parampara.md)
- **The Vārkarī pālkhīs** - In Maharashtra the **pālkhī** palanquins that walk to Pandharpur each Āṣāḍhī carry not images but the **pādukā of the saints** — Jñāneśvar's from Āḷandī, Tukārām's from Dehu — so that the saints themselves are held to make the pilgrimage. (Vārkarī practice) [regional] — see [Pandharpur & the Wārī](../06-sacred-geography/pandharpur-vitthala-wari.md), [Jñāneśvar](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/jnaneshwar.md), [Tukārām](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/tukaram.md)
- **Viṣṇu-pāda and the footprint** - The pādukā belongs to a wider **feet-emblem** family — Viṣṇu-pāda at [Gayā](../06-sacred-geography/gaya-pitru-tirtha.md), the *caraṇa-cihna* footprints of saints and tīrthaṅkaras, the *caraṇāmṛta* of the foot-wash — in all of which the feet stand for the whole and surrender is offered to them. (Source: pan-Hindu emblem tradition) — see [Sacred Symbols & Emblems](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md)

## V. Variants and later staging

- *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas):* Tulsīdās stages the giving of the sandals as the emotional summit of the **Bharata-milāp**, with Bharata's plea and Rāma's refusal drawn out at far greater length than in Vālmīki and the sandals begged for as a last resort before Bharata's threatened fast-unto-death. The object and its function are unchanged; the pathos is Tulsīdās's. [late text]
- *Variant (folk, Citrakūṭa):* the **Bharat Milāp Mandir** at [Citrakūṭa](../06-sacred-geography/chitrakoot-kamadgiri.md) shows stone "footprints" said to have been sunk into the rock by the weight of the brothers' embrace — a local materialisation of the same episode, and the reason the site is a pādukā-tīrtha in its own right. [folk] [regional]
- *Note on where the sandals are given:* Vālmīki sets the handing-over at **Citrakūṭa**, at the end of the Bharata embassy, and the enthroning at **Nandigrāma** on Bharata's return — two places, two acts. Retellings that stage both at Nandigrāma collapse the gift and the installation into one scene. (Source: *Ramayana*, Ayodhyā 112 vs. 115)
