# Sacred musical instruments (Vādya)

> Parent: [Artifacts, Symbols & Arts](README.md) — the **sounding objects of the cosmos**: the *vādya* (instruments) of the threefold art *saṅgīta* (*gīta*–*vādya*–*nṛtya*). Up-link: section index [`README.md`](README.md) · master index [`The Hindu Timeline`](../README.md).
> Where [`sangita-classical-music-svara-raga-tala.md`](sangita-classical-music-svara-raga-tala.md) treats the *science of sound* (svara, śruti, rāga, tāla) and [`natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md`](natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md) the *performance-theology*, this file catalogs the **instruments themselves** — their fourfold taxonomy, their divine owners, and the origin-myths attached to each. *Vādya* is roster-item **#2** of the [Sixty-Four Kalās](sixty-four-kalas.md).

**Vādya** (Skt. "that which is sounded, an instrument") is instrumental music and, by extension, the physical instruments. Two clocks govern the material below. The **scriptural clock** treats instruments as eternal divine attributes — Sarasvatī's vīṇā, Kṛṣṇa's flute, Śiva's ḍamaru — sounded across all yugas. The **historical clock** [scholarly] follows a codified organology from the *Nāṭya Śāstra* (c. 200 BCE–200 CE, ch. 28, the *atodya-vidhāna*) through the *Saṅgīta-ratnākara* (13th c., ch. 6, *vādyādhyāya*) to the living Hindustani and Carnatic instrumentaria. Never collapse the two: the flute *as Kṛṣṇa's* is timeless; the flute *as a keyed bāṃsurī* is a documented craft-history.

---

## I. The fourfold classification (caturvidha vādya)

- **Bharata's four classes** — the *Nāṭya Śāstra* (ch. 28) divides all instruments into **four** according to their **sounding body**: **(1) Tata** (तत, "stretched") — stringed / chordophones, the vīṇā class; **(2) Avanaddha** (अवनद्ध, "covered") — skin-covered / membranophones, the drum class; **(3) Ghana** (घन, "solid") — struck solids / idiophones, cymbals and bells; **(4) Suṣira** (सुषिर, "hollow") — hollow / aerophones, flute and conch. This is the *caturvidha vādya*, the base taxonomy of all Indian organology. (Source: *Nāṭya Śāstra* 28) [scriptural]
- **The śarīra-vīṇā and the dāru-vīṇā** — Bharata pairs the wooden vīṇā (*dāravī / dāru-vīṇā*) with the **body-vīṇā** (*śārīrī vīṇā*), the human throat: the two are declared of equal worth, the instrument a mirror of the voice. This is why śruti and svara are demonstrated on the vīṇā (see the two experimental vīṇās / *sāraṇā*). (Source: *Nāṭya Śāstra* 28) [scholarly]
- **Continuity of the scheme** — Śārṅgadeva's *Saṅgīta-ratnākara* (13th c., ch. 6) keeps the identical fourfold division, adding detailed making, tuning and playing-technique (*vādana*) for each class; the same four names survive in modern musicology (chordophone / membranophone / idiophone / aerophone). (Source: *Saṅgīta-ratnākara* 6, *Vādyādhyāya*) [scholarly]

## II. Tata — stringed instruments and their divine owners

- **Vīṇā — the archetype** — the **vīṇā** is the queen of Indian instruments and the oldest named; in the Vedas *vīṇā* is a generic for any stringed instrument, and the term later specializes. It is the fixed **attribute of Sarasvatī**, goddess of speech, learning and music, whose vīṇā is titled **Kalānidhi** ("treasury of the arts"). (Source: Vedic usage; Purāṇic iconography) [scriptural]
- **Kacchapī — Sarasvatī's vīṇā** — Sarasvatī's own instrument is named **Kacchapī** ("the tortoise-formed," from *kacchapa*, tortoise), after its resonator's shape; the goddess seated on the white lotus or swan holds it across her body. Because she is *Vīṇāpustaka-dhāriṇī* (bearer of vīṇā and book), music and letters share one patron. (Source: Purāṇic tradition; iconographic texts) [scriptural]
- **Mahatī — Nārada's vīṇā** — the sage **Nārada**, divine wanderer between the worlds and reputed transmitter of music to humankind, carries the vīṇā **Mahatī** (महती, "the great one"). Nārada is the archetypal *vaiṇika* (vīṇā-player); tradition makes his careless playing an occasion for humility — in one Vaiṣṇava tale he learns from Viṣṇu that the rāgas and rāgiṇīs he has mangled lie wounded, correcting his pride. (Source: Purāṇic / Vaiṣṇava tradition) [scriptural]
- **Rudra-vīṇā (bīn) — Śiva's instrument** — the great fretted stick-zither **rudra-vīṇā** is attributed to **Śiva**: a widespread [folk] etiology says Śiva fashioned it in contemplation of **Pārvatī's** reclining body — the two large gourds her breasts, the fretted stick her form — so that the instrument itself is an icon of the goddess. It is the classical *bīn* of Hindustani dhrupad. (Source: oral / iconographic tradition) [folk]
- **Rāvaṇa the vīṇā-master** — **Rāvaṇa**, ten-headed king of Laṅkā and supreme devotee of Śiva, is remembered as a peerless vīṇā-player; the *Rāma­yaṇa* tradition shows him singing the **Sāmaveda** and playing to Śiva. Iconography of Rāvaṇa propitiating Śiva on Kailāsa (the *Rāvaṇānugraha* scene) often gives him a vīṇā, and one legend has him tear out his own sinews for strings when the instrument failed — the devotion that wins Śiva's grace. (Source: *Rāmāyaṇa* tradition; Śaiva iconography) [scriptural / folk]
- **Ravaṇahattā (ravanahattha) — the bowed folk fiddle** — a spike-fiddle with a coconut resonator and horsehair bow, played across Rajasthan, Gujarat and (as the *rāvaṇahasta vīṇā*) far into Southeast Asia; [folk] tradition credits its invention to **Rāvaṇa**, made to worship Śiva. It is held to be among the oldest bowed instruments and an ancestor of the fiddle family. (Source: regional oral tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The Nāṭya Śāstra vīṇā-types** — Bharata names graded string-instruments: the **Citrā** (seven-stringed, plucked with the fingers), the **Vipañcī** (nine-stringed, plucked with a plectrum / *kōṇa*), and the **Kacchapī** and **Ghoṣakā** among others; the later **Kinnarī vīṇā** takes its name from the Kinnaras, celestial musicians. (Source: *Nāṭya Śāstra* 29) [scholarly]

## III. Avanaddha — the drums (membranophones)

- **Ḍamaru — Śiva's hourglass drum and the fourteen sounds** — the small waisted **ḍamaru**, struck by knotted cords as it is twirled, is held in Śiva's upper-right hand in the **Naṭarāja** form; its beat marks the pulse of creation in the cosmic dance (*tāṇḍava*). At the dance's end Śiva sounded the ḍamaru **fourteen times**, and from those fourteen strokes issued the **Māheśvara Sūtras** (*akṣara-samāmnāya*) — the fourteen aphorisms that order the Sanskrit sounds and from which **Pāṇini's** grammar is derived. The famous verse *nṛttāvasāne naṭarāja­rājo nanāda ḍhakkāṃ navapañcavāram* ("at the close of the dance the King of Dancers sounded the drum fourteen times") records this. (Source: Nandikeśvara's *Kāśikā* on the Māheśvara Sūtras; Pāṇinīya tradition) [scriptural] — Pāṇini himself is dated [scholarly] c. 5th–4th c. BCE. The ḍamaru is catalogued among the deity hand-attributes in [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md).
- **Mṛdaṅga — the "clay-limbed" drum** — the barrel-drum **mṛdaṅga** (*mṛt-aṅga*, "clay body," from its original clay shell) is the classical percussion of gods and men. Tradition seats it at Śiva's *tāṇḍava* played by **Nandi**, Śiva's bull-attendant and chief of the *gaṇas*, whose drumming is so wondrous it names the *nāndī* prelude of drama; hence Nandi is titled *Mṛdaṅga-vādaka*. (Source: Śaiva tradition) [scriptural] — Nandi's fuller dossier: [`../08-beings-and-bestiary/nandi-and-shaiva-mounts.md`](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nandi-and-shaiva-mounts.md).
  - *Variant (origin-myth):* one tradition credits the mṛdaṅga's making to **Brahmā** (or to **Gaṇeśa**) to accompany the victory-dance of Śiva after the burning of **Tripura**, the three asura-cities. [folk]
  - *Variant (Gauḍīya):* in Bengal-Vaiṣṇava *kīrtan* the two-headed clay drum is the **khol / śrī-khol**, and the wooden North-Indian form is the **pakhāvaj** ("firm-sided"), the dhrupad drum descended directly from the mṛdaṅga. (Source: Gauḍīya tradition) [regional]
- **Dundubhi — the war-drum of the gods** — the **dundubhi** (kettledrum) is the *deva-dundubhi* sounded in the heavens at every victory of the *devas* — at the slaying of asuras, the descent of avatāras, the churning of the ocean; its beating from the sky is the standard sign of divine approval in Purāṇic narrative. (Source: Purāṇic / epic tradition) [scriptural]
- **Epic battle-drums** — the *Mahābhārata* and *Rāmāyaṇa* field an array of *avanaddha* instruments of war: **bherī** (kettledrum), **paṭaha** (war-drum), **ānaka** (large drum, whence Kṛṣṇa's father **Ānakadundubhi** = Vasudeva, at whose birth these drums resounded), **muraja** and **paṇava**. (Source: *Mahābhārata*; *Rāmāyaṇa*) [scriptural]
- **Tablā — the split of the pakhāvaj** — the paired North-Indian drums **tablā** carry a persistent [disputed] legend that the poet-musician **Amīr Khusrau** (13th–14th c.) cut a pakhāvaj in two to create them; historically the instrument is documented only from the 18th c. [scholarly], the legend being an origin-story rather than a record. (Source: musicological survey) [disputed]

## IV. Ghana — the idiophones

- **Tāla — the cymbals that keep time** — the **tāla** (hand-cymbals; also **jhāñjh**, **jālrā**, **kartāl**, **kaṃsyatāla**) are the idiophones that mark the metric cycle; the word *tāla* for the **rhythmic cycle itself** derives from this clashing timekeeper. Paired cymbals accompany temple singing and dance everywhere. (Source: *Nāṭya Śāstra* 28; performance tradition) [scriptural]
- **Ghaṇṭā — the ritual bell** — the **ghaṇṭā** (hand-bell) is the core *pūjā* idiophone, rung at *āratī* and each stage of worship; its sound (*ghaṇṭā-nāda*) is read as **nāda / Om made audible**, driving off inauspiciousness and summoning the deity. (Source: āgama / ritual tradition) — fuller note under the ritual emblems in [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md). [scriptural]
- **Ghungharū and jayaghaṇṭā** — the **ghungharū** (ankle-bells) worn by dancers and the great **jaya-ghaṇṭā** (temple gong) round out the *ghana* class. (Source: performance / temple tradition) [regional]

## V. Suṣira — the wind instruments

- **Veṇu / Muralī / Bāṃsurī — Kṛṣṇa's flute** — the transverse bamboo flute is the fixed attribute of **Kṛṣṇa** as *Veṇu-gopāla* / *Muralīdhara* ("flute-bearer"); its call across Vraja draws the *gopīs* from their homes in the night, the theme of the **Veṇu-gīta** ("Song of the Flute," *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.21), where the cows, birds, rivers and even the peacocks are entranced by the sound. (Source: *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.21) [scriptural] — full treatment: [`../04-deep-dives/venu-gita.md`](../04-deep-dives/venu-gita.md).
- **Śaṅkha — the conch as instrument** — the **śaṅkha** (conch), a *suṣira* sounded by blowing, is **Viṣṇu's Pāñcajanya**; blowing it produces the primeval **Om / nāda**. In the *Mahābhārata* each hero owns a named conch — Kṛṣṇa's **Pāñcajanya**, Arjuna's **Devadatta**, Bhīma's **Pauṇḍra**, Yudhiṣṭhira's **Anantavijaya**, and the twins' **Sughoṣa** and **Maṇipuṣpaka** — blown to open the Kurukṣetra war (*Bhagavad Gītā* 1.15–18). (Source: *Bhagavad Gītā* 1.15–18) [scriptural] — the conch's relic-lore: [`divine-objects-and-treasures.md`](divine-objects-and-treasures.md); its emblem-meaning: [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md).
- **Śṛṅga, kombu and turya** — the curved horn **śṛṅga** (originally an animal horn), the South-Indian metal **kombu**, and the straight trumpet **turya / tūrya** and **kāhala** are the martial and processional aerophones of epic and temple. (Source: epic / temple tradition) [scriptural / regional]
- **Nāgasvaram and the temple śahnāi** — the great double-reed **nāgasvaram** (nadaswaram) of South India and the North-Indian **śahnāi** are the *maṅgala-vādya*, "auspicious instruments," whose sound must open every wedding, procession and temple rite; they belong to the temple **periya mēḷam** ensemble. (Source: temple performance tradition) [regional]

## VI. The concert of the gods and temple ensembles

- **The divine orchestra** — Purāṇic and iconographic tradition sets a **tableau of gods each with an instrument** around Śiva's dance: **Sarasvatī** the vīṇā, **Nārada** the vīṇā Mahatī, **Nandi / Gaṇeśa** the mṛdaṅga, **Brahmā** the cymbals (keeping *tāla*), **Viṣṇu** the drum, **Indra** the flute, while the **Gandharvas** and **Kinnaras** (celestial musicians) and the **Apsarases** dance. The heavenly source of all music is *gāndharva*, and music is classed as the **Gāndharvaveda**, upaveda of the Sāmaveda. (Source: Purāṇic tradition; *Nāṭya Śāstra* 28) [scriptural]
- **Ektārā — the drone of the bhakti saints** — the single-stringed **ektārā** ("one-string"), a gourd with a plucked drone, is the emblematic instrument of the wandering **bhakti** and **Bāul** singers — carried in the iconography of **Mīrābāī**, **Kabīr**, **Narsiṃha Mehtā** and the Bengali Bāuls — its one unwavering note a figure of single-minded devotion to the one God. (Source: bhakti hagiography; Bāul tradition) [regional] [folk]
- **Naubat / naqqārkhāna — the temple drum-house** — great temples maintain a **naubat** (also *nagārā-khānā*): a chamber over the gateway where the **naqqārā** (twin kettledrums) and **śahnāi** sound the watches of the day and announce the deity's services — famously the **naubat** at the Jagannātha temple of Purī and at many North-Indian shrines. (Source: temple custom) [regional]
- **Pañcavādyam and the temple ensembles** — Kerala temples keep the **pañcavādyam** ("five instruments": *timila*, *maddalam*, *ilattāḷam*, *kombu*, *edakka*) and the **cĕṇṭa mēḷam**; South-Indian temples the **periya mēḷam** (nāgasvaram + *tavil*); and the **pañcamukha-vādyam**, the rare five-faced temple drum of Tiruvārūr sacred to Śiva. Each ensemble is a *vādya* liturgy in its own right. (Source: temple performance tradition) [regional]
