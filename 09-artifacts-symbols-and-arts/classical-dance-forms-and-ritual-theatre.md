# Classical Dance Forms & Ritual Dance-Theatre

> Sits in [`09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/`](README.md) — the **living descendants of the Nāṭyaveda**: the eight recognized classical dance forms and the great ritual dance-theatres, each carrying a mythic origin-claim and a temple lineage. Up-link: section index [`README.md`](README.md) · master index [`The Hindu Timeline`](../README.md). The parent theory (rasa, abhinaya, tāṇḍava–lāsya) is in [`natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md`](natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md); dance among the 64 arts → [`sixty-four-kalas.md`](sixty-four-kalas.md); the folk/festival dances (garbā, rāsa) → [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/garba-and-dandiya-raas.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/garba-and-dandiya-raas.md).

Every classical form tells time on **two clocks**: a **mythic clock** (dance descends from Śiva's Tāṇḍava, Pārvatī's Lāsya, Kṛṣṇa's rāsa-līlā, or Viṣṇu's Mohinī form) and a **documented clock** — temple inscriptions, court patronage, colonial-era suppression, and 20th-century revival [scholarly]. This file keeps both distinct throughout. The "eight classical dances" is itself an administrative canon: the **Sangeet Natak Akademi** (est. 1953) recognized the forms progressively, Sattriya last in **2000** [scholarly].

## I. Bharatanāṭyam — sadir, the devadāsīs & the Tanjore Quartet

- **Mythic claim** — the Tamil tradition holds its dance to be the very art Brahmā gave Bharata and Śiva demonstrated: the **108 karaṇas of Nāṭya Śāstra ch. 4 are sculpted on the gopuras of Cidambaram**, where Naṭarāja dances the Ānanda-tāṇḍava — the form's icon and patron. (Source: *Nāṭya Śāstra* 4; Cidambaram temple sculpture) — see [`natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md`](natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md) §VI and [`sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md`](sacred-symbols-and-emblems.md).
- **The devadāsī institution** — women ritually **dedicated ("married") to the temple deity** (the *poṭṭukkaṭṭu* tali-tying rite), maintained by temple lands, who danced and sang in daily and festival ritual; regarded as *nityasumaṅgalī* — ever-auspicious, never widowed, because wed to the god. (Source: South Indian temple custom; āgamic temple-service tradition) [scholarly]
  - *Inscriptional anchor:* **Rājarāja I Cōḻa settles 400 dancing-women** (*taḷicceri peṇḍugaḷ*) around the Bṛhadīśvara temple, Thanjavur (c. 1004–1010 CE), with named individuals, houses and land allowances — the largest epigraphic record of the institution. (Source: Thanjavur temple inscriptions) [scholarly]
  - *Contested readings:* whether the devadāsī order "degenerated" into concubinage, or whether that narrative is largely a **colonial–reformist construction** flattening a complex ritual profession, is disputed among historians (anti-nautch campaigners vs. later revisionist scholarship). [disputed] [scholarly]
  - *Abolition:* the **Madras Devadasis (Prevention of Dedication) Act, 1947** outlaws dedication; temple dance had already been legislated out of Tamil temples in the anti-nautch decades. [scholarly]
- **Sadir → Bharatanāṭyam** — the devadāsīs' solo art was called **sadir / dāsīāṭṭam**. At the Maratha court of **Serfoji II of Tanjore (r. 1798–1832)**, the **Tanjore Quartet** — the brothers **Chinnayya, Ponnayya, Sivanandam and Vadivelu** — codified the concert format (*mārgam*: alārippu → jatīsvaram → śabdam → varṇam → padam → tillānā) that still defines the recital. (Source: Tanjore court tradition) [scholarly]
- **The 20th-century revival** — **E. Krishna Iyer** campaigns for the art in the 1920s–30s; **Rukmini Devi Arundale** founds **Kalakshetra** (Madras, 1936), renames sadir "**Bharatanāṭyam**," and re-frames it as a spiritualized, "sanitized" concert art — while hereditary artists like **T. Balasaraswati** defend the devadāsī aesthetic, including its śṛṅgāra. The revival-vs-appropriation question remains an open scholarly debate. [scholarly] [disputed]

## II. Odissi — mahārīs, gotipuas & the Jagannātha service

- **Scriptural claim** — the Nāṭya Śāstra itself names **Oḍra-Māgadhī** as one of the four regional styles (*pravṛttis*, with Āvantī, Dākṣiṇātyā, Pāñcālī), which Odissi claims as its charter. (Source: *Nāṭya Śāstra* ch. 14) [scriptural]
- **Archaeological claim** — the **Mañcapurī cave of Udayagiri** (Bhubaneswar), of **King Khāravela's** era (c. 2nd–1st c. BCE), carves a dance-and-music scene, and the Hāthīgumphā inscription credits Khāravela with staging performances — cited as the oldest witness of the lineage. [scholarly]
- **The mahārī lineage** — the temple dancers of **Jagannātha at Puri** (*mahārī*, glossed as *mahān nārī*, "great woman"), who danced in the deity's daily ritual and at the *bhoga* offerings — the Odia counterpart of the devadāsī. Tradition associates the institution's establishment with the Gaṅga kings who built the present temple (Coḍagaṅga Deva, 12th c.). (Source: Puri temple tradition) [scholarly] — see [`../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md`](../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md).
  - *Gīta Govinda mandate:* the singing of **Jayadeva's Gīta Govinda** was fixed in the temple's dance-service — a Jagannātha temple inscription of the Gajapati era (late 15th c.) orders that only the Gīta Govinda be performed before the deity; the mahārī repertoire is steeped in it. [scholarly] — see [`../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/jayadeva-and-the-gita-govinda.md`](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/jayadeva-and-the-gita-govinda.md).
- **The gotipua counter-lineage** — from about the **16th century**, with Vaiṣṇava reform and the decline of the mahārīs, **boys dressed as girls** (*gotipua*, "single boy") danced devotional pieces outside the sanctum in the *akhāḍās*; their acrobatic *bandha-nṛtya* fed directly into modern Odissi. [scholarly] [regional]
- **Reconstruction** — mid-20th-century gurus (**Kelucharan Mohapatra, Pankaj Charan Das, Deba Prasad Das**), several of them former gotipuas or mahārī-trained, rebuilt Odissi's concert grammar (*maṅgalācaraṇa → sthāyī/baṭu → pallavi → abhinaya → mokṣa*) from temple sculpture, the *Abhinaya Candrikā* of Maheśvara Mahāpātra, and surviving practice. [scholarly]

## III. Kathak — the kathākāras of the north

- **The storyteller origin** — *kathak* from **kathākāra/kathaka**, the hereditary **temple storytellers** of the Gangetic plain and Braj who recited purāṇic *kathā* with song, mime and gesture — remembered in the proverb *"kathā kahe so kathak"* ("who tells the story is a Kathak"). The core repertoire remains **Rādhā–Kṛṣṇa līlā** mime. (Source: north Indian oral-professional tradition) [oral tradition] [scholarly]
- **Court transformation** — under the Mughal and Nawabi courts the temple art absorbed Persianate presentation (the *salāmī*, the *ghungru*-driven pure-dance of *tatkār*, *tukṛā*, *chakkar* spins), producing the **gharānās**: **Lucknow** (patronized by **Wajid Ali Shah of Awadh**, r. 1847–56 — himself a composer; its great house the line of **Bindadin Maharaj** and Kalka Prasad, ancestors of Birju Maharaj), **Jaipur** (rhythm-dominant) and **Banaras** (Janaki Prasad). [scholarly]
- **Two clocks note** — the dancers' own memory runs the line back to the epic-reciting *kathakas* of purāṇic assemblies; documented Kathak as a named concert art is early-modern. [scholarly]

## IV. Kuchipudi — Siddhendra Yogī & the Bhāmākalāpam

- **The founding legend** — **Siddhendra Yogī**, saved from drowning (in one telling, while crossing a river to his bride) after vowing himself to Kṛṣṇa, renounces, and composes the dance-drama **Bhāmākalāpam** — Satyabhāmā's jealous love for Kṛṣṇa as the soul's longing for God. He binds the **brahmin boys of Kuchipudi village (Kuchelapuram, Andhra)** to perform it, decreeing that every male of the families enact **Satyabhāmā** at least once — hence the hereditary **all-male, brahmin dance-drama** tradition in which men played the heroines. (Source: Kuchipudi village tradition) [oral tradition]
  - *Dating variant:* Siddhendra Yogī is placed anywhere from the 14th to the 17th century by different accounts; the tradition's own chronology is unfixed. [disputed]
- **The Golconda grant** — the Qutb Shahi sultan **Abul Hasan Tana Shah grants the village of Kuchipudi to the performing brahmin families** (copper-plate, **1678**), reportedly after watching a performance — the form's firmest early document. [scholarly]
- **Repertoire** — *kalāpam* dance-dramas (Bhāmākalāpam, Gollakalāpam) plus solo *daruvus*; signature *tarangam*, danced on the rim of a brass plate to Nārāyaṇa Tīrtha's *Kṛṣṇa-līlā-taraṅgiṇī*. [scholarly]

## V. Kathakali — from Rāmanāṭṭam to the green-room of the gods

- **Rāmanāṭṭam, the seed** — the **Rāja of Kottarakkara (Kottarakkara Thampuran)**, mid-**17th century**, composes **Rāmanāṭṭam**, the Rāmāyaṇa danced in a cycle of plays in Malayalam-Sanskrit.
  - *The rivalry legend (Kerala oral tradition):* the Kottarakkara Rāja asked the **Zamorin of Calicut** to send his **Kṛṣṇanāṭṭam** troupe for a festival; the Zamorin refused (in some tellings with the taunt that the southern court could not appreciate it), and the slighted Rāja created Rāmanāṭṭam in answer. [oral tradition]
- **Kathakali proper** — Rāmanāṭṭam's stagecraft is broadened (notably by the **Kottayam Thampuran's** four Mahābhārata plays, and later Kalluvaḻi-school codification) into **Kathakali**: the *āṭṭakathā* play-literature; the color-coded *veṣam* make-up types — **pacca** (green, noble heroes), **katti** (knife, arrogant anti-heroes like Rāvaṇa), **tāḍi** (bearded: red, white — Hanumān —, black), **kari** (black, demonesses), **minukku** (polished, women and sages); the all-night temple-yard performance with *ceṇḍa* and *maddaḷam*. (Source: Kerala āṭṭakathā tradition) [regional] [scholarly]
- **Revival** — the poet **Vallathol Narayana Menon** founds **Kerala Kalamandalam (1930)**, saving Kathakali (and Mohiniyāṭṭam) from collapse under colonial-era neglect. [scholarly]

## VI. Mohiniyāṭṭam — the dance of the Enchantress

- **Mythic namesake** — the dance of **Mohinī**, Viṣṇu's female form: the enchantress who distributes the amṛta at the Churning and who destroys **Bhasmāsura** *through dance itself*, luring him to place his boon-hand on his own head — the only classical form named for an avatāra, and the one whose charter-myth is a dance. (Source: Bhāgavata/Viṣṇu Purāṇa Mohinī cycle; Bhasmāsura tale) — full myth-cycle in [`../04-deep-dives/mohini-avatara.md`](../04-deep-dives/mohini-avatara.md).
- **Character** — Kerala's **lāsya**-dominant solo for women: swaying torso (the palm-and-paddy aesthetic), white-and-gold costume, *cāri* glides — the feminine pole of the tāṇḍava–lāsya polarity ([`natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md`](natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md) §VI). [regional]
- **Documented lineage** — the name appears in Kerala texts of the early-modern period (17th–18th c.) [scholarly]; the shaping patronage is **Svāti Tirunāḷ of Travancore (r. 1829–46)**, whose court hosted **Vadivelu of the Tanjore Quartet** — a direct Bharatanāṭyam graft. After decline and early-20th-century Travancore restrictions on temple/court dancers, **Vallathol's Kalamandalam** (with Kalyanikutty Amma's generation) rebuilt it. [scholarly]

## VII. Manipuri — the Rāsa-līlā embodied

- **Mythic claims** — Meitei tradition holds that **Śiva and Pārvatī danced in the Manipur valley**, and Vaiṣṇava Manipur identifies the land with the epic **Maṇipura of Citrāṅgadā and Arjuna** — an identification modern scholars dispute. [regional] [disputed]
- **The Lai Haraoba substrate** — the pre-Vaiṣṇava Meitei ritual dance-festival for the *umang lai* deities, whose movement vocabulary underlies the classical form. [regional] [oral tradition]
- **King Bhāgyacandra's dream** — Maharaja **Bhāgyacandra (Ching-Thang Khomba)**, Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava convert, sees **Kṛṣṇa's rāsa in a dream** and choreographs the **Rāsa-līlā dances (first staged 1779)** for the **Śrī Govindajī temple** at Imphal, designing the drum-shaped *kumil* skirt from the vision — the charter of classical Manipuri. (Source: Manipuri court chronicle tradition, *Cheitharol Kumbaba*) [regional] [oral tradition] — the Kṛṣṇa rāsa itself → [`../04-deep-dives/rasa-lila.md`](../04-deep-dives/rasa-lila.md).
- **Form** — the rounded, veiled, uninterrupted grace of the rāsa dances (lāsya) with the leaping drum-dance **puṅg cholom** and *saṅkīrtana* (tāṇḍava side); Nata-saṅkīrtana was inscribed by UNESCO (2013). [scholarly]

## VIII. Sattriya — the monastery dance of Assam (summary)

- **Śaṅkaradeva's liturgy** — created by **Śrīmanta Śaṅkaradeva** (1449–1568) as part of the *ekaśaraṇa* bhakti complex: the **aṅkīyā nāṭ** one-act Kṛṣṇa plays, their **bhāonā** staging, **borgīt** songs, and the dance nurtured for five centuries inside the **sattra monasteries** by celibate monks — recognized as India's eighth classical dance (**Sangeet Natak Akademi, 2000**). [scholarly] Full treatment → [`../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/sankaradeva-and-assamese-vaishnavism.md`](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/sankaradeva-and-assamese-vaishnavism.md).

## IX. Ritual dance-theatre — where the play is the pūjā

- **Rāmlīlā (north India; Ramnagar)** — the autumn enactment of Rāma's story, text-bedded in **Tulsīdās's Rāmcaritmānas**; tradition credits **Megha Bhagat**, remembered as Tulsīdās's disciple, with staging the first Mānas-based Rāmlīlā [oral tradition]. The grandest is the **Ramnagar Rāmlīlā** across the Gaṅgā from Varanasi — instituted by the Maharajas of Banaras (Udit Narayan Singh's line, early 19th c.), a **31-day** peripatetic cycle in which the whole town becomes Ayodhyā, Janakpur and Laṅkā, boy *svarūpas* are worshipped as the deities themselves, and the Maharaja attends as Śiva's representative; UNESCO proclaimed Rāmlīlā an Intangible Heritage masterpiece (2005/2008). [scholarly] [regional] — see [`../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/tulsidas.md`](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/tulsidas.md), [`../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md`](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md), [`../06-sacred-geography/kashi-varanasi.md`](../06-sacred-geography/kashi-varanasi.md).
- **Kūṭiyāṭṭam (Kerala)** — the **only surviving Sanskrit theatre**, performed by the hereditary **Cākyār** (actors) and **Naṅgyār** (actresses) with **Nambiar** drummers on the great copper **miḻāvu**, inside temple theatres (**kūttampalam**); tradition claims some two millennia of continuity, with a formative reform attributed to the Cera king **Kulaśekhara Varman** (c. 10th–11th c.) [scholarly]; a single act, elaborated through *āṭṭaprakāram* manuals and the *nirvahaṇam* flashback, can run many nights; UNESCO's first Masterpiece of Oral & Intangible Heritage from India (**2001**). [regional] [scholarly]
- **Kṛṣṇanāṭṭam (Guruvayur)** — the Zamorin **Mānaveda** composes the Sanskrit **Kṛṣṇagīti (1650s)**, danced as a cycle of **eight plays over eight nights** (Avatāram → Svargārohaṇam) at the **Guruvayur temple**, offered as votive worship (a ninth night repeats *Avatāram*, since ending with Kṛṣṇa's departure is inauspicious); legend says Mānaveda glimpsed the child Kṛṣṇa through the sage Vilvamaṅgalam and caught only a peacock feather — enshrined in the crown of the Kṛṣṇa-actor. [oral tradition] [regional] — see [`../06-sacred-geography/guruvayur-krishna-kshetra.md`](../06-sacred-geography/guruvayur-krishna-kshetra.md).
- **Yakṣagāna (coastal Karnataka)** — the all-night **bayalāṭa** (open-field play): a **bhāgavata** singer-narrator with *caṇḍe/maddaḷe* drums (himmēḷa) drives costumed dancer-actors (mummēḷa) through purāṇic *prasaṅgas* (Devī-Mahiṣāsura, Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa episodes), in the towering-headgear **baḍagutiṭṭu** and **teṅkutiṭṭu** styles; the current form is documented from the early-modern centuries [scholarly], performed as temple-festival offering. [regional] [folk]
- **Terukkūttu (Tamil Nadu)** — "street theatre": all-night Mahābhārata cycles performed at village **Draupadī-Amman festivals**, where the play *is* the ritual — culminating in episodes like Draupadī's disrobing and vow, and Arāvāṉ's battlefield self-offering (*kaḷappali*), tied to firewalking and possession rites; Draupadī is worshipped as the village goddess. [folk] [regional]
- **Boundary note** — the festival circle-dances **garbā/ḍāṇḍiyā-rās** and the rāsa-maṇḍala folk traditions are cataloged separately → [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/garba-and-dandiya-raas.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/garba-and-dandiya-raas.md); Kṛṣṇa's own rāsa → [`../04-deep-dives/rasa-lila.md`](../04-deep-dives/rasa-lila.md).
