# Sagara — The Ocean-Namesake Emperor of Ayodhyā

> *Up to: [Lineages (Vaṃśa)](./README.md) · a restorer-emperor of the senior [Sūryavaṃśa / Ikṣvāku line of Ayodhyā](./suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) in the present (28th) Mahāyuga of the Vaivasvata Manvantara — born in exile after the [Haihaya–Tālajaṅgha](./yadava-vamsha.md) overthrow of his father Bāhu, he retakes the empire, brands the foreign hosts, and fathers the 60,000 sons whose ashes summon [Gaṅgā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganga.md) down from heaven.*

**Sagara** ("with poison," *sa-gara*) is the Ikṣvāku emperor whose name the tradition hears in the very word for the ocean (*sāgara*). His story falls into two great halves. The first — the dynasty's darkest hour and recovery: his father **Bāhu** dethroned by the **Haihayas and Tālajaṅghas**, the posthumous poison-birth in the āśrama of the Bhārgava sage **Aurva**, the reconquest, and the famous imposition of distinguishing marks on the **Śakas, Yavanas, Kāmbojas, Pāradas and Pahlavas** — is told in the Purāṇas and *Harivaṃśa* and is treated in full here. The second half — the **Aśvamedha**, the digging of the ocean, **Kapila's** burning of the 60,000 sons, and the generations-long labor that ends in **Bhagīratha's** descent of the Gaṅgā — is only summarized here; its canonical homes are [Gaṅgā — the goddess & the Descent](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganga.md), [Gaṅgāsāgar — Sāgara-dvīpa & Kapila's āśrama](../06-sacred-geography/gangasagar-sagara-dvipa-kapila-ashram.md), and [Kapila & Devahūti](../04-deep-dives/kapila-devahuti.md).

## 1. Place in the Ikṣvāku king-list

- **Solar-line emperor of Ayodhyā.** Sagara belongs to the senior Sūryavaṃśa branch seated at **Ayodhyā** in Kosala, several generations below the [Triśaṅku](../04-deep-dives/trishanku.md)–[Hariścandra](../04-deep-dives/hariscandra.md) cluster and above the Gaṅgā-descent kings: **… Hariścandra → … → Bāhu (Bāhuka) → Sagara → Asamañja → Aṃśumān → Dilīpa I → Bhagīratha …** (full spine at [suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md §1a](./suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)). (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3–4; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.8)
  - *Variant (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa 70):* in Vasiṣṭha's recitation of the line at Rāma's wedding, Sagara's father is named **Asita**, not Bāhu — the same overthrow-and-exile story is attached to Asita. Most Purāṇas read Bāhu/Bāhuka; the figure is the same slot under two names. [disputed]
- **Counted among the exemplary emperors.** Sagara is invoked in the *Mahābhārata* among the great righteous kings of old whose deaths prove that even *cakravartins* pass — part of the "sixteen kings" consolation-eulogy genre — and is repeatedly cited as a model sacrificer and world-ruler. (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 106–107; Droṇa/Śānti Parva eulogies)
- **The ocean bears his name.** Because his sons excavated the earth down to the sea in search of the sacrificial horse, the ocean is called **sāgara**, "of Sagara" — the tradition's own etymology, treated with the full Gaṅgā arc at [gangasagar-sagara-dvipa-kapila-ashram.md](../06-sacred-geography/gangasagar-sagara-dvipa-kapila-ashram.md). (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 38–44)

## 2. The fall of Bāhu — the Haihaya–Tālajaṅgha overthrow

The prelude to Sagara's life is the collision of the Solar line with the **Haihaya** power of Māhiṣmatī — the same Yādava sub-branch whose rise and fragmentation is charted at [yadava-vamsha.md §2](./yadava-vamsha.md).

- **Bāhu dethroned.** King **Bāhu (Bāhuka)** of Ayodhyā — in the Purāṇic telling a king grown addicted to pleasure — is defeated and driven from his kingdom by the **Haihayas and Tālajaṅghas**, the post-Kārtavīrya remnant-clans of the Yādava Haihaya branch. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Harivaṃśa* 13–14; *Brahmāṇḍa* and *Vāyu Purāṇa* genealogies)
- **The five foreign confederates.** The Haihaya host comes supported by the **Śakas, Yavanas, Kāmbojas, Pāradas and Pahlavas** — the "five hosts" (*pañca-gaṇa*) of frontier peoples who thereby enter the dynastic story and whose fate Sagara will later decide. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Harivaṃśa* 13–14; *Vāyu Purāṇa* 88)
- **Exile and death in the forest.** Bāhu flees to the forest with his wives and dies there, an old broken king, near the āśrama of the Bhārgava sage **Aurva** (on the Bhṛgu line and its fire-wrath motif, see [bhrigu.md](../04-deep-dives/bhrigu.md)). (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Harivaṃśa* 13–14)
- **Link to the Paraśurāma cycle.** In the Purāṇic sequence these marauding Haihayas–Tālajaṅghas are the *survivors* of [Paraśurāma's](../04-deep-dives/parashurama.md) extermination of the Kṣatriyas (see [jamadagni-parashurama.md](../04-deep-dives/jamadagni-parashurama.md)); Sagara's war is thus the Solar line's answer to the same cycle of violence, and the tradition remembers him as the king who finished what the Bhārgava began. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3, IV.11; *Vāyu/Brahmāṇḍa* genealogies) [scholarly]

## 3. The poison-birth at Aurva's āśrama — why "Sa-gara"

- **The poisoned queen.** Bāhu's pregnant chief queen (named **Yādavī** in several Purāṇic tellings) had been given **poison (gara)** by a jealous co-wife to destroy the unborn child; by the poison's power the fetus is held unborn in her womb — for **seven years** in the Purāṇic account. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Harivaṃśa* 13–14; *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa*)
- **Aurva prevents the satī.** When Bāhu dies, the queen prepares to ascend his funeral pyre; the sage **Aurva** stops her, declaring that she carries in her womb a **universal sovereign (cakravartin)** who will slay his enemies and rule the earth. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Harivaṃśa* 13–14)
- **Born "with the poison."** The child is born in the āśrama together with the poison that had confined him — whence Aurva names him **Sa-gara**, "he who was born with the *gara*." (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.8.4; *Harivaṃśa* 13–14)
  - *Variant (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa 70):* the exiled king is **Asita**, the rescuing sage is the Bhārgava **Cyavana** (see [cyavana-sukanya.md](../04-deep-dives/cyavana-sukanya.md)), and the mother is named **Kālindī**; the poison-birth and the *sa-gara* etymology are the same. [disputed]
- **Fostered and armed by the Bhārgava.** Aurva performs the boy's rites, teaches him the Vedas and the science of arms, and bestows on him the **Āgneyāstra**, the Bhārgava fire-weapon — the instrument of his coming reconquest. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3)
- **The vow.** Learning from his mother why they live in exile, Sagara **vows to exterminate the Haihayas–Tālajaṅghas** and recover his ancestral kingdom. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Harivaṃśa* 14)

## 4. Reconquest — and the imposed marks on the Śakas, Yavanas & Pahlavas

- **The Haihayas nearly annihilated.** Armed with the Āgneyāstra, Sagara destroys almost the whole Haihaya–Tālajaṅgha power and retakes Ayodhyā and the imperial dignity of the Ikṣvākus. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Harivaṃśa* 14; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.8.5)
- **Vasiṣṭha's intercession.** As Sagara turns to exterminate the foreign confederates — Śakas, Yavanas, Kāmbojas, Pāradas, Pahlavas — they take refuge with **Vasiṣṭha**, the Ikṣvāku family priest, who declares them "as good as slain" and makes Sagara renounce the killing. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Vāyu Purāṇa* 88; *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa*)
- **The marks instead of death.** In place of slaughter Sagara strips them of their dharma and imposes visible marks of degradation: the **Yavanas** must shave their heads entirely, the **Śakas** half their heads, the **Pāradas** must wear their hair long and loose, and the **Pahlavas** must wear beards; barred from Vedic recitation and the *vaṣaṭ*-call, and abandoned by their Brahmin preceptors, they become **mlecchas**. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.3; *Harivaṃśa* 14; *Vāyu Purāṇa* 88 — the assignment of mark to people varies slightly across the parallel Purāṇic texts) [disputed]
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa IX.8.5–7):* it is his guru **Aurva**, not Vasiṣṭha, on whose instruction Sagara spares them; the marked peoples there include **Tālajaṅghas, Yavanas, Śakas, Haihayas and Barbaras**, some left shaven, some mustached, some loose-haired, some half-shaved, some deprived of under- or over-garments.
- **What the episode is doing** — the names are those of real historical frontier peoples: Śakas = Scythians, Yavanas = Ionians/Greeks, Pahlavas = Parthians, with the Kāmbojas on the Iranian borderlands. Modern scholarship reads the passage as a retrojected **ethnogenesis myth** — an explanation of foreigners' distinctive grooming and their exclusion from Vedic rites, composed after these peoples entered India's political horizon (broadly post-2nd century BCE), and pinned onto a deep-past emperor. It cannot be used as evidence for events of Sagara's scriptural era. [scholarly]

## 5. The two queens, Bhṛgu's boon, and the two kinds of posterity

- **Keśinī and Sumati.** Sagara's queens are **Keśinī**, daughter of the king of Vidarbha, and **Sumati**, daughter of **Ariṣṭanemi (Kaśyapa)** and sister of **Garuḍa**. Long childless, the king performs austerities for a hundred years at **Bhṛguprasravaṇa** in the Himālaya. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 38)
- **Bhṛgu's boon — one son or sixty thousand.** **Bhṛgu** grants that one wife will bear a **single son who continues the dynasty**, the other **sixty thousand sons** of vast renown; Keśinī chooses the one, Sumati the sixty thousand. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 38)
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva 106):* the boon is given by **Śiva**, propitiated at Kailāsa; the wives are daughters of **Vidarbha** and of **Śibi**, and the sixty thousand are foretold to be proud heroes who will all **perish together**. [disputed]
- **The gourd-birth.** Keśinī bears **Asamañja**; Sumati delivers a **gourd (tumba)**, from which sixty thousand embryos emerge, nurtured in **jars of ghee** until they grow to youth — Sagara's vast second posterity. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 38; *Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 106–107)
- **Attribution of mothers varies.** Which queen bears the single heir and which the sixty thousand is swapped in some Purāṇic tellings (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.8 gives Asamañjasa to Keśinī; some texts reverse the assignments). [disputed]

## 6. Asamañja — the exiled heir

- **The cruel prince.** **Asamañja** (Asamañjasa), Sagara's only dynastic son, grows into a public terror: he seizes the children of the townsmen and **throws them into the river Sarayū**, laughing as they drown. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 38; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.4)
- **Exiled by his father.** For this cruelty Sagara **banishes his own heir** from the kingdom — an act the tradition remembers as the extreme proof that Ikṣvāku kings put dharma above blood. Asamañja's virtuous son **Aṃśumān** remains and becomes the effective successor. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 38; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.4)
- **The remembered precedent.** Generations later, when Rāma's own exile is demanded, Asamañja's banishment is cited at the Ayodhyā court as the **sole precedent** for casting out a royal son — and is answered that Asamañja was wicked where Rāma is blameless. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/2-ayodhya-kanda.md) 36)
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa IX.8.15–21):* Asamañjasa is secretly a **fallen yogī** of a former life feigning odiousness to detach himself from the world; when exiled, he **restores the drowned boys to life** by yogic power and departs, revealing the cruelty as a mask. [late text]

## 7. The Aśvamedha, Kapila, and the ocean — the arc told elsewhere

*(Summary only — full treatments at [ganga.md](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganga.md), [gangasagar-sagara-dvipa-kapila-ashram.md](../06-sacred-geography/gangasagar-sagara-dvipa-kapila-ashram.md), [kapila-devahuti.md](../04-deep-dives/kapila-devahuti.md), and [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md).)*

- **The horse-sacrifice and Indra's theft.** Sagara undertakes the **Aśvamedha**; **Indra**, fearing the merit of the rite, steals the horse and hides it beside the meditating sage **Kapila** in the netherworld. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 38–40; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.8)
- **The digging and the burning.** The **60,000 sons** excavate the earth to the sea — hollowing the bed of the future ocean, whence **sāgara** — find the horse by Kapila, insult him, and are **burnt to ash by his glance**. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 39–41; *Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 107; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.8)
- **Aṃśumān recovers the horse.** Sagara's grandson **Aṃśumān** appeases Kapila, retrieves the horse so the sacrifice can be completed, and learns that only the celestial **Gaṅgā** can redeem the un-rited ashes. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 41–42)
- **The long redemption.** Neither Sagara, nor Aṃśumān, nor Dilīpa achieves the descent; it falls to **Bhagīratha**, whose tapas brings Gaṅgā down through **Śiva's** locks to the sea and over the ashes at **Gaṅgāsāgar** — the full Gaṅgāvataraṇa is at [ganga.md](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganga.md). (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 42–44)
- **End of the reign.** Having ruled for **thirty thousand years** by the epic's reckoning, Sagara installs **Aṃśumān** and departs to heaven, the redemption of his sons still unfinished — a rare Purāṇic portrait of an emperor whose greatest work is completed only by his great-great-grandson. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 42; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.4)

## 8. The "two clocks"

- **Scriptural clock.** Sagara reigns in the **present (28th) Mahāyuga of the Vaivasvata Manvantara**, in the deep generations of the Ikṣvāku line well above Rāma (traditionally placed around the **Satya-yuga / early-Tretā** stretch of the line; this corpus's Gaṅgā-cycle files carry the Sagara events in the Satya-yuga band). His Haihaya war is synchronized by the Purāṇas with the *aftermath* of the Kārtavīrya–Paraśurāma cycle — the same synchronism carried at [yadava-vamsha.md](./yadava-vamsha.md) — and the exact yuga-placement of the whole cluster is not fixed uniformly across texts. [disputed]
- **Historical / text-critical clock.** On the academic clock there is no datable king Sagara; the Śaka–Yavana–Pahlava episode names peoples of the **c. 2nd century BCE – early CE** frontier and is read as a late, retrojected charter-myth grafted onto the genealogies, while the Aśvamedha–Gaṅgā arc belongs to the epic-Purāṇic literary strata (*Rāmāyaṇa* Bāla Kāṇḍa and *Mahābhārata* Vana Parva onward). The genealogical Sagara and the ethnological just-so story must not be collapsed into one chronology. [scholarly]
