# The Vaivasvata king-tales of the Mārkaṇḍeya — Rājyavardhana, Nābhāga, Vatsaprī, Khanitra

> *Parent: [Lineages (Vaṃśa)](README.md) · four self-contained episodes strung along the **Diṣṭa branch** of Vaivasvata Manu's descendants in the present (7th) [Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md), 28th Mahāyuga. They survive almost only in the **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa**'s genealogical block (Pargiter, Cantos ~106–118) and are the reason that Purāṇa's dynastic section is worth reading whole.*

The Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa carries the descent of **Vaivasvata Manu** through his son **Diṣṭa** in a run of cantos that most other Purāṇas compress to a bare king-list. Four of its kings get real stories, and none of the four is duplicated at length anywhere else in the corpus: **Nābhāga**, who marries out of his order and *accepts* the demotion; **Vatsaprī**, who goes down to Pātāla to kill a Daitya wielding a Prajāpati-forged club; **Khanitra**, whose fratricidal sorcery-attack is turned back by nothing but his own accumulated merit; and **Rājyavardhana**, whose subjects' devotion wins him ten thousand years of youth — and who discovers that youth alone, without company, is a punishment. They are cataloged here in genealogical order, with each king's Pargiter canto flagged.

---

## 0. Where these four sit in the Diṣṭa line

- **The spine.** **Vaivasvata Manu → Diṣṭa → Nābhāga → Bhalandana → Vatsaprī → Prāṃśu → … → Prajāti → Khanitra → … → Karandhama → Avīkṣit → Marutta → Nariṣyanta → Dama → Rājyavardhana → Sudhṛti.** This is the *Diṣṭa* (or Nediṣṭha) branch — one of the collateral houses of Vaivasvata Manu, distinct from the great **Solar (Ikṣvāku)** and **Lunar (Aila)** trunks. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 111, "The guide to the genealogies"; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1) See the [Sūrya- & Candra-vaṃśa overview](suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
  - *Name of the progenitor [disputed: spelling]:* the son of Manu who heads this branch appears as **Diṣṭa**, **Nediṣṭha**, **Riṣṭa**, **Ariṣṭa**, and **Dhṛṣṭa** across recensions; the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* (IV.1) prefers **Nediṣṭha**. The corpus records the variance, not a single correct form.
- **Two of the four are *ends* of the same story.** **Rājyavardhana** stands genealogically far down the list as the son of **Dama** (*Mārkaṇḍeya* 136), yet his tale is told back near the top of the block, inside the "Majesty of the Sun" cantos (109–110), because it is used there to *illustrate solar bhakti* rather than to advance the pedigree. Pargiter's own footnote at Canto 136 flags the displacement. This is a genuine feature of the Mārkaṇḍeya's non-linear arrangement, not an error.
- **The line's continuation** — Karandhama, Avīkṣit, Marutta, and Dama — is treated in [Karandhama, Avīkṣit, Marutta & Dama](../04-deep-dives/karandhama-avikshit-marutta-dama.md). For the Mārkaṇḍeya's other great dynastic set-piece (Kuvalayāśva and Madālasā, from the earlier cantos of the same Purāṇa) see [Kuvalayāśva & Madālasā](../04-deep-dives/kuvalayashva-madalasa.md).

## 1. Nābhāga and the accepted caste-fall (Cantos 113–115)

- **The desire across varṇa.** **Nābhāga**, kṣatriya son of King **Diṣṭa**, becomes enamoured of "a most surpassingly charming **vaiśya maiden**" (the girl and her merchant father are left unnamed in the text) and asks for her hand. The father, unwilling to give a vaiśya daughter to a prince without leave, defers the matter to Diṣṭa. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 113)
- **The ṛṣis' counsel refused.** The family ṛṣis rule that a kṣatriya must **first take a wife of his own order** before a vaiśya woman may lawfully be added; Nābhāga rejects the counsel outright and **carries the maiden off by the Rākṣasa form of marriage** (marriage by seizure), declaring the deed openly. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 113–114)
- **Father and son at war.** Diṣṭa takes the field against his own son over the abduction; the two armies clash. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 114)
- **The wandering muni's verdict — and the acceptance.** A passing **muni** halts the battle and pronounces the dharma-śāstra rule plainly: *whoever weds an "excluded" woman after neglecting union in his own caste becomes himself a participator in that woman's caste.* By his choice Nābhāga has **fallen to vaiśya-hood** — and, crucially, he does not repent or seek reversal; he keeps the wife and wears the demotion. His descendants are counted a vaiśya branch of the royal house. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 114–115) See [varṇa, āśrama & saṃskāras](../05-concepts-and-dharma/varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md).
  - *Doctrinal point [scholarly]:* the episode is one of the Purāṇas' clearest narrative charters for **pratiloma** consequence — that varṇa can be *lost* by marriage-choice and that the loss is lawful, not tragic. Nābhāga's calm acceptance is the moral, against the more usual Purāṇic pattern of a fall being cursed and later undone.
  - *Name-collision warning [disputed]:* this **Nābhāga** (son of Diṣṭa, vaiśya-fallen) is **not** the more famous **Nābhāga/Nabhaga son of Vaivasvata Manu** of the Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa / *Bhāgavata* IX.4 (the boy who serves the Aṅgirasas and receives the sacrificial leavings, and whose son is Ambarīṣa). The two share a name and a Manu-adjacent slot and are routinely conflated; the Mārkaṇḍeya's Nābhāga is the *Diṣṭa*-line king, father of Bhalandana.

## 2. Vatsaprī, the Daitya Kujṛmbha, and the club Sunanda (Canto 116)

- **The succession.** Nābhāga having **declined the kingdom** (a fitting close to his story), his son **Bhalandana** (also **Bhananda / Bhanandana**) reigns; Bhalandana's son is **Vatsaprī** (Vatsaprīti). (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 116)
- **Kujṛmbha and the gaping club.** The Daitya king **Kujṛmbha** — his name from *ku-* (evil) + *jṛmbha* (gape, yawn, expand) — owns a **club named Sunanda, fashioned by a Prajāpati**, whose property is to **rend the earth open** and bore passages down to **Pātāla** (the "yawning weapon" of the file's title: a mace that gapes the ground apart). (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 116) See the [asura-wars roster](../08-beings-and-bestiary/asura-wars-and-roster.md).
- **The abduction of Mudāvatī.** King **Vidūratha** has two sons, **Sunīti** and **Sumati**, and a daughter, **Mudāvatī**. Kujṛmbha seizes Mudāvatī from a pleasure-grove and drags her down to Pātāla; Vidūratha's two sons pursue and are **captured and bound** by the Daitya. The king proclaims his daughter's hand to whoever will slay Kujṛmbha and free the captives. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 116)
- **Vatsaprī's descent, and the club's secret.** Vatsaprī goes down to Pātāla with **scimitar and bow**. The club Sunanda carries a fatal flaw — **on any day it is touched by a woman it loses its power**. Mudāvatī, knowing this, **repeatedly touches the club under pretence of paying it reverence**, so that when Kujṛmbha finally swings it in battle its blows fall harmlessly. Vatsaprī beats down the demon's remaining weapons and kills him with his **fire-weapon (āgneya)**. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 116)
- **The naming — precise, not merged.** After the kill the serpent-king **Śeṣa (Ananta)** takes possession of the club; because Mudāvatī had handled the *Sunanda* club so often, Śeṣa **gives the princess the new name Sunandā**, derived from the club (Saunanda). *The maiden Vatsaprī rescues is therefore* ***Mudāvatī***, *renamed* ***Sunandā*** *after the weapon — the wife's proper name and the club's name are the same by this gift, and must not be collapsed into "a woman called Sunanda who was abducted."* (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 116) See [nāgas & serpents](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md).
- **The reign.** Vatsaprī weds Sunandā, succeeds Bhalandana when his father retires to the forest, rules righteously, and (in the genealogists' tally) begets twelve sons, **Prāṃśu** continuing the line. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 116; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1)

## 3. Khanitra and the recoiling abhicāra (Cantos 117–118)

- **The beneficent king and his four brothers.** **Khanitra**, son of **Prajāti**, is chief of five sons and a famously benevolent, prowess-renowned sovereign; his four brothers are **Śauri, Udāvasu, Sunaya, and Mahāratha**, whom he holds as sub-kings under his overlordship. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 117)
- **The minister's plot.** **Viśvavedin**, minister to the brother **Śauri**, talks Śauri into rebelling against Khanitra's suzerainty, brings the other three brothers under his own control, and sets them to **spells (abhicāra) aimed at destroying Khanitra**. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 117)
- **Four purohitas, one kṛtyā.** Each rebel brother's family priest is enlisted: **Suhotra** (for Śauri, of the Atri line), **Kusāvartta** (for Udāvasu, of Gautama's line), **Pramati** (for Sunaya, a Kāśyapa), and a **Vāsiṣṭha** (for Mahāratha). Day after day they perform "an exceedingly arduous magical incantation," raising a **four-fold female deity (kṛtyā)** to strike Khanitra dead. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 117)
- **The recoil.** The kṛtyā, launched at Khanitra, is **repelled by the king's own store of merit (puṇya)** — he takes no counter-action whatever, offers no counter-rite; his accumulated righteousness alone deflects the weapon. Cast back, the four-fold deity **falls upon the purohitas and Viśvavedin themselves and destroys them all**. The sorcery kills only its own authors. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 117–118)
  - *Doctrinal point [scholarly]:* this is a textbook Purāṇic statement of the abhicāra "boomerang" — a hostile rite (a kṛtyā) that finds its target morally impregnable **turns on the sender**. Khanitra's *passivity* is the whole point; merit, not counter-magic, is the defence.
- **The grief and the renunciation.** Khanitra, far from triumphant, is **stricken with grief** that his kinsmen and their priests have died on his account; he **resigns the kingdom** to his son and withdraws to the forest with his queen — a beneficent king undone in spirit by a victory he never sought. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 118)

## 4. Rājyavardhana's ten-thousand-year youth (Cantos 109–110)

- **The grey hair.** Far down the same line, **Rājyavardhana** (son of **Dama**) has guarded the earth **seven thousand years** when he finds a single **grey hair** and, reading it as the summons of age, resolves to renounce the throne and retire to the forest. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 109) The tale is set inside the Purāṇa's "**Majesty of the Sun**" cantos and functions there as a proof of solar bhakti.
- **The subjects' austerity.** His people, unwilling to lose him, **perform austerities to propitiate the Sun (Sūrya)** for three months; the Sun appears in bodily form and asks their wish. They petition that their king **"live ten thousand years, free from sickness, victorious over his enemies, rich in his treasury, and with firmly-enduring youth."** The Sun grants it. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 109–110) See [Sūrya & his family](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/surya-and-his-family.md).
  - *Variant [folk/retelling]:* popular retellings assign the boon to the **[Aśvins](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ashvins.md)** rather than to Sūrya, framing it as "may our king live ten thousand years… with unwavering youth." Pargiter's Mārkaṇḍeya text places the grant with the **Sun** inside the Sun-majesty cantos; the Aśvin version is a secondary telling.
- **The loneliness — the heart of the tale.** The boon *dismays* the king. His queen **Māninī** asks why he shows no joy, and he explains the horror the wish overlooked: **he alone will endure**, watching wife, servants, sons, and every subject die around him across the millennia. Deathless youth *without shared time* is not a gift but a solitary sentence. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 110)
- **The corrected boon.** Rājyavardhana and Māninī withdraw to a mountain sanctuary and perform severe penance (~fourteen months); the Sun, pleased again, **extends the long life to all of them together** — the king's dependants, citizens, subjects, and sons alike. Returning, the king rules in joy, gives lavishly, offers many sacrifices, and **"remained continuously youthful," rejoicing with sons, grandsons, and subjects for the full ten thousand years.** The episode closes as an exemplum of "the power of faith in the Sun." (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 110)
  - *The moral [scholarly]:* the two-stage boon makes the tale a meditation on longevity as a *social*, not individual, good — the corpus's sharpest statement that a lifespan is worth nothing without a shared world to live it in. Compare the Purāṇic king-lists' usual bare "he reigned N years" with this rare interior view of what a superhuman reign would *feel* like.

## 5. The two clocks

- **Scriptural clock.** All four kings belong to the collateral **Diṣṭa house of Vaivasvata Manu**, in the present **Vaivasvata (7th) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga**. As descendants of Manu's own son they sit at the **dawn of the current manvantara** — the corpus places this Manu's-sons stratum in the early **Satya (Kṛta) band** of the current Mahāyuga, well above the Tretā of Rāma and the Dvāpara of Kṛṣṇa. The Purāṇa does not yuga-stamp each reign; the placement is the standard genealogical inference. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* 111; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1)
- **Academic clock.** The **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa** is reckoned among the **oldest Purāṇas**; its genealogical (*vaṃśa*) block belongs to the archaic pañcalakṣaṇa stratum that Pargiter mined for the reconstructed "Purāṇic genealogical tradition." The older portions are dated **[scholarly] c. 3rd–6th century CE**, distinct from the interpolated **Devī-Māhātmya** (c. 5th–6th c. CE) embedded earlier in the same book. **F. E. Pargiter's** English translation (Bibliotheca Indica) appeared in **1904** and remains the standard canto-numbered reference used here. None of these four kings has Vedic attestation; they are creatures of the Purāṇic dynastic tradition. The two frames are not to be collapsed.
