# Jyāmagha — the Henpecked King and the Bride for the Unborn Son

> *Hierarchy: [`03-lineages-vamsha/`](README.md) — a single hinge-node of the **Candravaṃśa** (Lunar dynasty), **Yadu → Kroṣṭṛ branch**: see the [Yādava overview](yadava-vamsha.md) §3. Jyāmagha is the Purāṇic genealogies' one openly comic king — the sonless exile so afraid of his queen **Śaibyā** that he dares not remarry, who blurts that a captive girl is "the wife of your son yet unborn," and is taken at his word: the aged queen bears **Vidarbha**, ancestor of Rukmiṇī's house and (through Kratha's line) of Kṛṣṇa's own Vṛṣṇis. Scriptural clock: early Vaivasvata-manvantara dynastic time, many generations before the Dvāpara-end Kṛṣṇa cycle.*

---

## 0. Where Jyāmagha sits in the tree

- **Genealogical spine (Kroṣṭṛ branch).** **Yadu → Kroṣṭṛ (Kroṣṭu) → Vṛjinīvat → … → Citraratha → Śaśabindu → Pṛthuśravas → … → Uśanas → (Jyāmagha's father) → Jyāmagha**. This is the *southern*, non-Haihaya fork of the Yādavas — the line that will eventually yield Sātvata, the Vṛṣṇis, and Kṛṣṇa. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.23; *Matsya Purāṇa* 44; *Harivaṃśa*, Harivaṃśa-parvan Yadu-vaṃśa chapters) See [Sūrya- & Candravaṃśa](suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) and the [Yādava-vaṃśa file](yadava-vamsha.md).
- **Śaśabindu the cakravartin.** Jyāmagha's most storied ancestor: a universal emperor "lord of the fourteen great gems," credited with a fantastic multitude of wives and sons, of whom the *pṛthu*-named sons (Pṛthuśravas etc.) are the remembered heirs. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Bhāgavata* IX.23)
  - *Synchronism:* Śaśabindu's daughter **Bindumatī** marries the Solar cakravartin **Māndhātṛ** — the standard Purāṇic dovetail pinning this stretch of the Yadu line to Māndhātṛ's era. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.2) → [Māndhātṛ](../04-deep-dives/mandhata.md).
- **Uśanas of the hundred horse-sacrifices.** A nearer forefather, remembered solely for performing **one hundred aśvamedhas**. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Bhāgavata* IX.23)
- **Father and brothers — the lists diverge.** [disputed: names]
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.12):* the father is **Parāvṛt** (son of Rukmakavaca), with five sons — **Rukmeṣu, Pṛthurukman, Jyāmagha, Pālita, Harita**.
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata IX.23.34):* the father is **Rucaka**, with five sons — **Purujit, Rukma, Rukmeṣu, Pṛthu, Jyāmagha**. The two rosters share little beyond Rukmeṣu and Jyāmagha; the traditions are cataloged, not harmonized.

## 1. The exile — a kingdom carved on the Narmadā

- **Displaced by his brothers.** In the fuller Matsya/Vāyu/Harivaṃśa telling, on the father's death **Rukmeṣu** takes the ancestral throne (with Pṛthurukman remaining at his side), and **Jyāmagha departs** — the disinherited/withdrawing brother who must live by his bow. (*Matsya Purāṇa* 44; *Vāyu Purāṇa* Yadu-vaṃśa section; *Harivaṃśa*; the *Viṣṇu* and *Bhāgavata* compress or omit the exile frame)
- **Mount Ṛkṣavat and Mṛttikāvatī.** The exile turns **south**: Jyāmagha settles on the **Ṛkṣavat** mountain range and establishes himself at **Mṛttikāvatī** in the **Narmadā** country, ruling as a frontier king. (*Matsya Purāṇa* 44; *Vāyu Purāṇa*; *Harivaṃśa* — details collated in H. H. Wilson's notes to *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12) → the Narmadā's sacred geography: [Narmadā parikramā](../06-sacred-geography/narmada-parikrama.md).
- **Śuktimatī taken.** He then advances and takes **Śuktimatī** — the city the epic later knows as the **Cedi** capital — so the exile's conquests pre-draw the map of the Cedi–Vidarbha cousin-kingdoms his descendants will hold. (*Matsya Purāṇa* 44; *Vāyu Purāṇa*; *Harivaṃśa*)
  - *[scholarly]* Pargiter and later historians read this episode as tradition's memory of a **Yādava migration/expansion southward across the Narmadā** into Vidarbha (the Berar region) — legendary charter, not datable history.

## 2. The henpecked husband — Śaibyā

- **"Foremost of husbands obedient to their wives."** The Purāṇas themselves flag Jyāmagha with a proverb: among **bhāryā-vaśya** ("wife-ruled") husbands he is the exemplar — the genealogists' one deliberate joke in a thousand names. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Harivaṃśa*; echoed *Matsya Purāṇa* 44)
- **Sonless, and afraid to remarry.** His queen **Śaibyā** (a Śibi princess by her name) is childless; by every norm of Purāṇic kingship a sonless king takes another wife — but Jyāmagha, **"out of fear of Śaibyā" (śaibyā-patir bhayāt)**, does not dare. (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.35; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12)
  - *Note:* Śaibyā is simultaneously drawn as a **pativratā** (devoted wife) in the *Bhāgavata*'s wording — the texts let her be both formidable and faithful; the comedy is at the king's expense, not hers. (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.35)

## 3. The captive girl and the blurted line — the central scene

- **A girl brought from the enemy's house.** Old and still sonless, Jyāmagha returns from a campaign bringing a **captive maiden** — seated, fatally, **on the queen's place in his chariot**. (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.35–36 "śatru-bhavanād … kanyām ahāraṣīt"; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Matsya Purāṇa* 44)
  - *Variant (reading of "bhojyāṁ kanyām," Bhāgavata IX.23.35) [disputed]:* commentators split on the word — a **princess of the Bhoja clan**, or simply "a maiden (to be cared for/enjoyed)." The girl is **never named** in any of the core texts; she enters the lineage as pure narrative function.
- **Śaibyā's challenge.** Seeing the girl on her seat, the queen confronts him: *"Who is this, you cheat (kuhaka), that you have set upon my place in the chariot?"* (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.36; the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* gives the same confrontation in indirect speech)
- **The blurt: "She is your daughter-in-law" (snuṣā tava).** Cornered, the king improvises the one answer that is neither co-wife nor concubine. (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.36; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Matsya Purāṇa* 44)
- **The queen's riposte.** *"I am barren, and you have no other wife — daughter-in-law to which son of yours?"* (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.37 "ahaṁ bandhyāsapatnī ca"; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12)
  - *Variant (tone):* in the *Bhāgavata* Śaibyā asks this **smiling** (*smayantī*) — the scene tips toward wry domestic comedy; the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*'s queen speaks in jealous anger. Same words, two temperatures — cataloged, not merged.
- **The king doubles down: bride of the unborn son.** *"She shall be the wife of the son whom you will bear, O queen" (janayiṣyasi yaṁ rājñi tasyeyam upayujyate).* An impossible promise from an aged, barren couple — spoken, in effect, as an oath. (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.37; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12)
- **The word is ratified above.** The **Viśvedevas and the Pitṛs approve** the utterance (*anvamodanta*), converting the king's face-saving improvisation into destiny — the classic Purāṇic logic that a king's solemn word (**satya-vāc**), once spoken, must be made true. (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.38)
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.12):* the *Viṣṇu* credits the outcome more diffusely — the girl's own conduct/merit and the force of the declaration — without the divine-assent verse; the mechanism differs, the result is identical.

## 4. Vidarbha — the son born to make the word true

- **Aged Śaibyā conceives.** "In time" (*kāle*) the old queen conceives and bears an auspicious son, **Vidarbha** — the miracle child whose whole reason for existing is his father's blurt. (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.38; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Matsya Purāṇa* 44)
- **Vidarbha marries the waiting bride.** Grown, Vidarbha **weds the very captive girl** long since accepted as the household's *snuṣā* — the texts call her *satī*, the virtuous bride who waited. The daughter-in-law-before-the-son becomes daughter-in-law in fact. (*Bhāgavata* IX.23.38–39; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12)
- **The eponymous kingdom.** Vidarbha gives his name to the southern realm of **Vidarbha** (the Berar/Kundina country of the epics) — the dynasty seeded by an exile's conquest and a domestic improvisation. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Bhāgavata* IX.24; *Matsya Purāṇa* 44)
- **Vidarbha's sons — the lists diverge.** [disputed: names]
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.12):* **Kratha, Kaiśika, Romapāda (Lomapāda)** — the Mahābhārata knows the Vidarbha people precisely as the **"Kratha-Kaiśikas."**
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata IX.24.1):* **Kuśa, Kratha, Romapāda** — Kaiśika's slot taken by Kuśa; Romapāda is styled "delight of the Vidarbha line."

## 5. Downstream — Rukmiṇī's house, the Caidyas, and Kṛṣṇa's own line

- **The Vidarbha king-line → Bhīṣmaka → Rukmiṇī.** From Vidarbha's sons descend the Vidarbha kings; in epic times **Bhīṣmaka** of Kuṇḍina fathers **Rukmiṇī** — whom Kṛṣṇa carries off from her betrothal to Śiśupāla — and her brother **Rukmī**. Jyāmagha's blurt is thus the founding charter of **Rukmiṇī's house**. (*Bhāgavata* IX.24, X.52–54; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12, V.26; *Harivaṃśa*) → [Rukmiṇī-haraṇa](../04-deep-dives/rukmini-harana.md).
- **Damayantī of Vidarbha.** The earlier celebrated princess of the line: **Damayantī**, daughter of King Bhīma of Vidarbha, heroine of the Nala tale. (*Mahābhārata* Vana Parva, Nalopākhyāna) → [Nala & Damayantī](../04-deep-dives/nala-damayanti.md).
- **The Cedi (Caidya) kings.** The Chedi royal house is also run out of Vidarbha's sons — whence, in the epic age, **Śiśupāla** of Chedi, Rukmiṇī's intended and Kṛṣṇa's slain cousin-rival; Jyāmagha's earlier seizure of **Śuktimatī**, the Cedi capital, foreshadows the connection. (*Bhāgavata* IX.24 — via Romapāda's descendants → Uśika → **Cidi**, "from whom the Caidya kings"; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Matsya Purāṇa* 44) → [Śiśupāla](../04-deep-dives/shishupala.md).
- **Kṛṣṇa's own ancestry passes through this marriage.** Both master genealogies continue the main Yādava stem **through Vidarbha's son Kratha**: Kratha → Kunti → … → Daśārha → … → **Madhu** → … → **Sātvata**, from whom the Vṛṣṇi–Andhaka–Bhoja clans and ultimately **Kṛṣṇa** descend. The nameless captive girl and old Śaibyā are therefore ancestresses of Kṛṣṇa himself — and the [Rukmiṇī-haraṇa](../04-deep-dives/rukmini-harana.md) is a marriage **back within Jyāmagha's own posterity**, Yādava rejoining Yādava cousin-line. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12–13; *Bhāgavata* IX.24; *Harivaṃśa*) See [Yādava-vaṃśa](yadava-vamsha.md) §3, §6.
- **Why the genealogists kept the joke.** Amid bare name-chains, the Jyāmagha vignette does real work: it explains the **Vidarbha–Cedi–Vṛṣṇi kinship triangle** that powers the Rukmiṇī–Śiśupāla–Kṛṣṇa plot, models the inviolability of a king's spoken word (even a panicked one), and gives the tradition its gentle proverb about wife-ruled husbands — comedy with genealogical consequences. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.12; *Bhāgavata* IX.23–24) [scholarly: reading of narrative function]

## 6. Two-clocks note

- **Scriptural clock.** Jyāmagha belongs to the deep dynastic stretch of the **Vaivasvata (7th) Manvantara**, generations after Yadu (→ [Yayāti](../04-deep-dives/yayati.md)) and roughly pegged, via the Śaśabindu–Māndhātṛ marriage-synchronism, a few generations after [Māndhātṛ's](../04-deep-dives/mandhata.md) era — many king-generations before the Dvāpara-end Kṛṣṇa cycle in which his line's two branches (Vṛṣṇi and Vidarbha) rejoin. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.2, IV.12; *Bhāgavata* IX.23)
- **Historical clock.** [scholarly] No historicity is claimable for Jyāmagha; Pargiter treated the episode as legend preserving a memory of Yādava expansion south of the Narmadā into Vidarbha/Berar. The recording texts date (academic estimates): *Harivaṃśa* c. 1st–3rd c. CE; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* c. 4th–5th c. CE; *Matsya Purāṇa* core c. 3rd–6th c. CE; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* c. 8th–10th c. CE.
