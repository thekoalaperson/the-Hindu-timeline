# Kuśanābha's Hundred Daughters, Vāyu's Wrath, and the Birth of Brahmadatta

> *Hierarchy: [`03-lineages-vamsha/`](README.md) — an ancestral episode of the **Kuśika** branch of the Lunar line, one generation above [Gādhi](gadhi-and-the-caru-swap.md) and two above Viśvāmitra. Scriptural clock: **Tretā Yuga** of the current (28th) Mahāyuga — told by Viśvāmitra himself, en route to Mithilā, as the origin-story of his own house and of the city that tradition calls **Kanyākubja** ("the maidens' hunch," Kanauj).*

---

## 0. The frame — Viśvāmitra's fireside genealogy at the Śoṇa

- **Where the telling happens.** After the destruction of Mārīca and Subāhu at Siddhāśrama, Viśvāmitra leads Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa north-east toward Janaka's sacrifice at Mithilā. The party halts for the night on the banks of the river **Śoṇa** (Sona); Rāma asks whose pleasant, prosperous country this is, and Viśvāmitra answers with the history of his own ancestors — the material of **Bāla Kāṇḍa sargas 32–34**. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 31–32)
- **Why it matters for the lineage shelf.** This is the *Rāmāyaṇa's* own account of the **Kuśa → Kuśanābha → Gādhi → Viśvāmitra** descent — the fullest narrative version of the spine that [Gādhi and the Caru-Swap](gadhi-and-the-caru-swap.md) carries only as a bare genealogical line. The next morning the princes cross the Śoṇa and reach the Gaṅgā, where the embedded-legend cycle continues (Kārttikeya, Sagara, the Gaṅgā's descent) — see the [Bāla Kāṇḍa deep-dive](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md).

---

## 1. Kuśa and the four city-founders (sarga 32)

- **Kuśa, the Brahmā-born king.** Viśvāmitra opens with **Kuśa**, a great ascetic king described as born of Brahmā (*brahmayoni*), firm in vows and honoring the good. He marries the princess of **Vidarbha**, and by her has four sons "like himself": **Kuśāmba, Kuśanābha, Ādhūrtarajasa** (read **Amūrtarajasa** in some versions [disputed: name-reading]) **and Vasu**. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32)
  - *Variant (Purāṇic genealogies):* the [Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.7 / Bhāgavata IX.15 spine](gadhi-and-the-caru-swap.md) reaches Kuśa through the Lunar line (Purūravas → Āyu → … → Kuśa) rather than directly from Brahmā, and the [Bhāgavata threads the branch through Jahnu](jahnu.md) — the *Rāmāyaṇa's* "Brahmā-born Kuśa" is the compressed, more archaic-sounding telling. [disputed: king-order]
- **Four sons, four cities.** Kuśa charges his sons to rule and foster dharma; each founds a capital: **Kuśāmba builds Kauśāmbī**, **Kuśanābha builds Mahodaya**, **Ādhūrtarajasa builds Dharmāraṇya**, and **Vasu builds Girivraja**. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32)
- **Vasu's land is the land they camp in.** Girivraja lies ringed by **five hills**; the lovely river **Māgadhī (Sumāgadhī)** winds garland-like among them — this is **Magadha**, Vasu's domain, and Viśvāmitra tells the princes that the fertile country around their Śoṇa campsite is Vasu's. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32)

---

## 2. The hundred daughters and Vāyu's wrath (sarga 32)

- **A hundred daughters by an apsarā.** **Kuśanābha**, the righteous king of Mahodaya, begets **one hundred daughters, unmatched in beauty, on the apsarā [Ghṛtācī](../08-beings-and-bestiary/apsaras.md)** — the same celestial nymph who mothers dynastic and sage lines across the epics. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32)
- **Lightning in the rain-clouds.** Grown to radiant youth, the hundred girls go out to the gardens adorned like streaks of **lightning amid monsoon clouds**, singing, dancing, and playing instruments — the picture of unbroken good fortune. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32)
- **Vāyu's proposition.** **[Vāyu](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vayu.md), the all-pervading wind-god (sarvātmaka)**, sees them and desires them all: "Abandon the human state and be my wives — take long life; fleeting is human youth, but as mine you will be ageless, immortal." (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32)
- **The maidens' refusal — the father is our lord.** The girls laugh off the god's advance with a pointed answer: "We know you move within all beings, and we know your power; and by our own tapas we could even hurl you from your godhood — but we guard our austerity. Never, O dull-witted one, shall we choose a husband for ourselves and dishonor our truthful father. **Our father is our lord, our supreme deity**; he alone will give us to whomever he chooses." (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32)
- **The twisting.** Enraged at the refusal, **Vāyu enters their limbs and breaks/bends them** — the hundred daughters are twisted into **hunchbacks (kubjā)**, their beauty deformed. Ashamed and weeping, they enter their father's palace. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32)
- **They report the assault, not a bargain.** To the anguished king they explain: Vāyu, resorting to a dishonorable course, disregarded their answer — "we told him we have a father; ask him for us" — and struck them down even as they held to dharma. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32)

## 3. Kuśanābha's teaching — forbearance is the ornament (sargas 32–33)

- **The kṣamā speech.** Kuśanābha does not rage at the god; he praises his daughters for their restraint: "**Forbearance (kṣamā) you have shown, and acted as one** — this befits my house. Forbearance is the ornament of women as of men; forbearance is gift, forbearance is truth, forbearance is sacrifice, forbearance is glory, forbearance is dharma — **on forbearance the universe is established**." (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 33)
- **From injury to alliance.** Dismissing the daughters, the king sits in counsel with his ministers on the question of their **giving-in-marriage (kanyādāna)** — the wound inflicted by a god will be healed by a rightly-made marriage, which the narrative now sets up. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 33)

---

## 4. Cūlī, Somadā, and the mind-born Brahmadatta (sarga 33)

- **The ascetic Cūlī.** At that time a sage of brahmic conduct named **Cūlī** is engaged in austerities. A **gandharvī — Somadā, daughter of Ūrmilā** — serves and attends him with perfect piety; see the [gandharvas & kinnaras roster](../08-beings-and-bestiary/gandharvas-and-kinnaras.md) for her class of being. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 33)
- **The boon of a son without a husband.** Pleased with her long service, Cūlī offers a boon. Somadā answers that she is **without a husband**, and asks that the sage, radiant with brahmic power, grant her **a son endowed with brahmic merit — by his tapas**, not by marriage. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 33)
- **Brahmadatta, the mind-born (mānasa) son.** Cūlī consents and brings forth for her a **mind-born son, Brahmadatta** — "given by brahman/Brahmā," true to his name and to the manner of his begetting. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 33)
- **King in Kāmpilya.** Brahmadatta rules as king from the city **Kāmpilya** in supreme prosperity, "as the king of the gods rules heaven." (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 33)

---

## 5. The healing marriage (sarga 33)

- **Kuśanābha's resolve.** Learning of Brahmadatta, Kuśanābha determines to give him **all one hundred daughters**, invites him with honor, and performs the wedding. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 33)
- **Touch of the hand undoes the god's wrath.** As Brahmadatta **takes each daughter's hand in turn (pāṇigrahaṇa), the crookedness leaves her at the very touch** — freed of the deformity (*vikubjā*), the hundred shine again in full beauty. The marriage-rite itself is the cure. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 33)
- **Two households rejoice.** Kuśanābha, seeing his daughters released from Vāyu's affliction, rejoices as at a festival and sends them off with their new lord; **Somadā**, delighted, receives her hundred daughters-in-law with honor, praising Kuśanābha. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 33)

---

## 6. The putrakāmeṣṭi and the birth of Gādhi (sarga 34)

- **A son to continue the line.** His daughters married but himself sonless, Kuśanābha undertakes a **son-yielding sacrifice (putreṣṭi / putrakāmeṣṭi)**. During the rite his father **Kuśa**, the Brahmā-born, declares to him: "You shall have a son named **Gādhi**, supremely righteous; through him you will win everlasting fame in the world." (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 34)
- **Gādhi is born — Viśvāmitra names himself.** In time Gādhi is born, and Viśvāmitra closes the circle for Rāma: "That Gādhi is my father; I am **Kauśika**, born in the line of Kuśa." From Gādhi the story runs on into the caru-swap, Satyavatī–Ṛcīka, Jamadagni, and Paraśurāma — the canonical fork treated in [Gādhi and the Caru-Swap](gadhi-and-the-caru-swap.md), with Viśvāmitra's own arc in [Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha](../04-deep-dives/vishwamitra-vasishtha.md). (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 34)
- **Satyavatī becomes the Kauśikī.** Viśvāmitra adds that his elder sister **Satyavatī**, wife of Ṛcīka, followed her husband to heaven **in her very body** and became the great Himalayan river **Kauśikī (Kosi)**, holy and sweet-watered; out of love for her he dwells by her banks on the Himālayan slopes, and has settled at **Siddhāśrama** for the perfection of the rite. With this "I have told you my origin and my clan," the night being half spent, all sleep — and the march to the Gaṅgā resumes at dawn. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 34)

## 7. Kanyākubja — the etymology — and the Brahmadatta name-tangle

- **"The maidens' hunch."** The city's later, famous name **Kanyākubja** (whence **Kanauj**) is traditionally derived from this very episode: *kanyā* (maidens) + *kubja* (hunchbacked) — the town of the hundred bent daughters. In the received Vālmīki text Kuśanābha's city is called **Mahodaya**; the identification Mahodaya = Kanyākubja is made by the commentators and by later tradition rather than unambiguously by the sarga itself. [disputed: whether the naming-verse is original] (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32–33 with commentaries)
  - *Corroboration (Purāṇas):* the Purāṇic genealogies flatly call Kuśanābha's and Gādhi's house the kings **of Kānyakubja** (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.7; *Bhāgavata* IX.15), sealing the identification within tradition — see the [Candravaṃśa overview](suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **The other Brahmadatta of Kāmpilya [disputed: identity].** Epic-Purāṇic literature knows a second, more famous **Brahmadatta of Kāmpilya** — the Pāñcāla king, son of **Aṇuha** and of **Kīrtimatī, daughter of Śuka** (Vyāsa's son), remembered as a yogically gifted ruler (*Harivaṃśa* genealogies; *Mahābhārata*, Śānti Parva tales set at Kāmpilya) — see [Śuka](../04-deep-dives/shuka-shukadeva.md). The shared throne-city invites conflation, but the texts keep the two figures distinct: the Rāmāyaṇa's Brahmadatta is a gandharvī's mind-born son with no Pāñcāla pedigree. [late text: Harivaṃśa material]
- **Buddhist stock-king Brahmadatta.** The Pāli **Jātaka** corpus opens tale after tale with "when **Brahmadatta** was reigning in Vārāṇasī" — a formulaic royal name showing how widely the name circulated, with no narrative connection to Kuśanābha's son-in-law. (Pāli Jātakas) [folk]
- **Not the arrow.** Unrelated by everything but spelling: in the Araṇya Kāṇḍa, Agastya gives Rāma a divine arrow that some renderings call "Brahmadatta" — i.e. simply "**given by Brahmā**," an epithet, not the king — see [Araṇya Kāṇḍa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md). (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Araṇya 12)

## 8. Two-clocks note

- **Scriptural clock.** The episode is ancestral memory recited **within Tretā Yuga** (Viśvāmitra narrates it during Rāma's youth); the events themselves sit two to three generations above Viśvāmitra in the Kuśika line, with Kuśa reaching back to Brahmā himself in the Rāmāyaṇa's telling. Tradition keeps the whole Kuśika–Bhārgava complex within the Tretā frame. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla Kāṇḍa 32–34)
- **Historical clock [scholarly].** The place-names are real early-historic cities: **Kanauj (Kanyākubja)**, **Kauśāmbī** (major excavated site near Prayagraj), **Kāmpilya** (Kampil, Farrukhabad district), and **Girivraja/Rājagṛha** (Rajgir) all flourish in the first millennium BCE and later — Kanauj most famously as Harṣa's seventh-century CE capital. Modern scholarship reads the sarga as an **etiological city-name legend** attached to a genealogy, not datable history; the Bāla Kāṇḍa's embedded-legend block is itself widely held to be among the epic's later strata. [scholarly] [late text]
