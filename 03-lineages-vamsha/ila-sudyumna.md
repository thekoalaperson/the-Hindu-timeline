# Ilā / Sudyumna — the gender-shifting hinge of the Lunar dynasty

> *Hierarchy: [Lineages (Vaṃśa)](README.md) — the single junction-node where the **Sūryavaṃśa** (Solar line of Vaivasvata Manu) grafts onto the **Candravaṃśa** (Lunar line of Soma). Ilā/Sudyumna is Manu's gender-alternating child who, **as a woman**, mothers [Purūravas](../04-deep-dives/pururavas-urvashi.md) by [Budha](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/chandra-soma.md), and **as a man**, fathers three regional kings. The Lunar dynasty is therefore matrilineal at its very root.*

Ilā/Sudyumna is one of the strangest and structurally most important figures in the genealogical literature: a child of the Solar patriarch **Vaivasvata Manu** who oscillates between the sexes, and through whom the entire **Lunar dynasty** of Purūravas, Nahuṣa, Yayāti, the Kurus, the Pāṇḍavas and Kṛṣṇa's kin descends. Because the founding ancestor of the Candravaṃśa human line is *born of Manu's line but bears children by the Moon's son (Budha)*, the corpus's own summaries note that the Lunar dynasty is "matrilineal at its hinge" — grafted onto the Moon's house through Manu's daughter-son. See the [Sūrya- & Candra-vaṃśa overview](suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md). The figure is also a palimpsest: a Vedic offering-goddess (**Iḍā**) overlaid, in the Purāṇas and the *Rāmāyaṇa*, with a full sex-changing myth that the older texts do not know.

---

## 0. Where Ilā/Sudyumna sits — the Solar-Lunar weld

- **A child of Vaivasvata Manu.** In the Purāṇic reckoning Ilā/Sudyumna is counted among the ten-plus children of **Vaivasvata Manu** (Śrāddhadeva), alongside **Ikṣvāku** (who takes the Solar throne of Ayodhyā) and his brothers. So Sudyumna is *Solar by birth* yet becomes the *root of the Lunar line* — the one place the two dynasties physically touch. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1) See the [Vaivasvata Manvantara roster](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md) and the [Satya-Yuga event file](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/01-satya-yuga.md).
- **The matrilineal graft.** Purūravas, first mortal Lunar king, is Ilā's son *as a mother* — so the Candravaṃśa descends through a female phase of a male-born prince. Tradition calls Purūravas and his line **Aila**, "descendant of **Ilā**" (feminine), not "descendant of Sudyumna." The patronymic itself records that the line is traced through the *mother*. (*Ṛgveda* X.95 colophon tradition; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6) [scholarly]
- **Name-and-sex table.** As **Sudyumna** (Skt. *su-dyumna*, "of good splendour") the figure is male and a king; as **Ilā / Iḍā / Iḷā** (from *iḍ/iḷ*, "libation, refreshment, nourishment") the figure is female. The two names are the two phases of one being. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1)
  - *Variant (which sex is original) [disputed]:* the **Bhāgavata** and **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** make the child *born female* (Ilā), then turned male (Sudyumna), then female again in the grove; the **Rāmāyaṇa**'s Ila is *born male* and only later feminised. The corpus catalogs both — the "original sex" is genuinely unfixed across traditions.

---

## 1. The Vedic root — Iḍā, Manu's offering-daughter (Śatapatha)

The oldest stratum knows no sex-change at all. There, **Iḍā/Iḷā** is a *ritual personification* — the poured libation, the sacrificial cow, the goddess of nourishment invoked in the Āprī hymns — and the figure who becomes "Sudyumna" is a later narrative growth on that Vedic root.

- **Iḷā the libation-goddess.** In the *Ṛgveda*, **Iḷā/Iḍā** is the deified oblation and the "mother of herds," invoked with Sarasvatī and Bhāratī in the Āprī hymns; she personifies the *iḍā* portion of the offering (butter, milk, food). This is the pre-genealogical, purely ritual Iḍā. (*Ṛgveda*, Āprī sūktas) [scholarly]
- **Iḍā, Manu's flood-daughter.** In the **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa** account of the deluge, **Manu** — sole survivor — offers into the waters (ghee, sour milk, whey, curds); after a year a **woman, Iḍā (Iḷā), rises** from the offering. She calls herself Manu's own, "produced from the oblation," and through her Manu *generates his race* — "this race of Manu." **Mitra and Varuṇa** claim her, but she declares she was made for Manu and stays with him. (*Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* I.8.1) [scholarly]
- **No transformation here.** The Śatapatha Iḍā is a *daughter/consort-progenitrix*, not a man-turned-woman; there is no Śiva-grove, no monthly alternation, no Budha. The gender-shifting Sudyumna is an entirely Purāṇic-epic overlay onto this Vedic offering-woman — the single most important interpretive point about the figure. (*Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* I.8.1) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Mitra–Varuṇa claim) [scholarly]:* the Brāhmaṇa's contested paternity (Manu vs. Mitra-Varuṇa) is later re-used by the Purāṇas, where it is precisely **Mitra-Varuṇa** whose boon converts the daughter Ilā into the son Sudyumna — the Purāṇic authors seem to have read the old Mitra-Varuṇa claim forward into a new sex-change plot.

---

## 2. The Purāṇic birth — a daughter by a flawed rite, made a son

- **A childless Manu, a botched yajña.** Wanting a son, **Vaivasvata Manu** commissions his priest — usually **Vasiṣṭha**, with the hotṛ officiating — to perform a sacrifice to **Mitra and Varuṇa** for male issue. The officiant, or the queen **Śraddhā**'s covert wish for a daughter, corrupts the invocation, and a **daughter, Ilā**, is born instead of the wanted son. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1)
  - *Variant (whose fault) [disputed]:* the **Bhāgavata** blames the **hotṛ's flawed intonation** of the vaṣaṭkāra; other strands blame **Śraddhā's silent prayer** for a girl during the rite. The outcome — a daughter where a son was sought — is constant.
- **Ilā made Sudyumna.** Distressed, Manu appeals again to **Vasiṣṭha**, who prays to **Mitra-Varuṇa** (in the Bhāgavata, to **Hari/Viṣṇu**); pleased, they transmute the girl **Ilā into a boy, Sudyumna**. Sudyumna grows into a prince and rides out to hunt. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1.16–17; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1) See [Vasiṣṭha's role across the lineages](../04-deep-dives/vishwamitra-vasishtha.md).

---

## 3. The Śiva-grove transformation — the Śaravaṇa reed-forest

- **The forbidden grove at Meru's foot.** Hunting with his companions, **Sudyumna** enters a grove (variously **Sukumāra-vana** / a reed-thicket **Śaravaṇa**) at the base of **Mount Meru**, where **Śiva** is dallying with **Pārvatī**. Because Śiva — to please Pārvatī, or having himself taken a female form — has decreed that *every male thing that enters the grove shall become female*, Sudyumna, his companions, and even his horse are instantly turned female. Sudyumna becomes **Ilā** once more. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1.23–26; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1)
  - *Variant (naming of the grove) [disputed]:* the reed-forest **Śaravaṇa** (śara = reed) is elsewhere the birthplace of Kārttikeya; some retellings simply call it "Śiva's pleasure-grove" or **Sukumāra** without a fixed name. The transformation-by-trespass is constant; the toponym is not.
  - *Variant (the standing curse) [scholarly]:* the reason for the emasculating decree ranges from Śiva honouring a promise that no male would intrude on their love-making, to Śiva's own gender-ambiguity (Ardhanārīśvara motif) leaking into the grove. Texts differ on the *why*; all agree on the *what*.
- **The horse becomes a mare.** The detail that Sudyumna's **stallion is turned into a mare** (and later must itself be restored) recurs in the fuller Purāṇic tellings and is echoed in the equine motifs of the wider Aila legend. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1)

---

## 4. Budha, Ilā, and the mothering of Purūravas

- **Budha sees Ilā.** Wandering the grove as a woman, **Ilā** is seen by **Budha** (the planet Mercury), son of **Soma/Candra** by **Tārā** — the child of the Moon's abduction of Bṛhaspati's wife in the Tārakāmaya war (see [Chandra (Soma)](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/chandra-soma.md)). Budha, doing austerities near the grove, is smitten and unites with her. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1.34; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1)
- **Purūravas born.** From the union of **Budha** and **Ilā** is born **Purūravas**, first mortal king of the Lunar dynasty, who will reign from **Pratiṣṭhāna** (Prayāga) and love the apsarā **Urvaśī**. Thus the Candravaṃśa's founder is grandson of the **Moon** on his father's side and great-grandson of the **Sun** (Manu ← Vivasvān) on his mother's — a literal welding of the two royal houses. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6) Full arc in [Purūravas & Urvaśī](../04-deep-dives/pururavas-urvashi.md).
- **The oldest human king by one reckoning.** Because Purūravas is the first *named mortal* of either dynasty to hold a throne and love story (the Ṛgvedic X.95 dialogue is older than the epics), Ilā's motherhood is the true dynastic zero-point of the Lunar line. (*Ṛgveda* X.95) [scholarly]

## 5. Vasiṣṭha's intervention & the monthly alternation

- **The ministers' distress.** With their king now a woman living in a forest, Sudyumna's ministers and the sage **Vasiṣṭha** are grieved — a kingdom cannot be ruled by a sex-changed forest-dweller. Vasiṣṭha, the family purohita, intercedes with **Śiva**. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1.37–38)
- **The compromise: a month a man, a month a woman.** Unable wholly to revoke his own decree, **Śiva** grants a compromise — Sudyumna will be **male one month and female (Ilā) the next**, alternating for life; in each phase he/she has no recollection of the other. Sudyumna, in his male months, returns to rule. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1.38–40; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1)
  - *Variant (whom Vasiṣṭha propitiates) [disputed]:* the **Bhāgavata** has Vasiṣṭha propitiate **Śaṅkara (Śiva)** directly; older Purāṇic strands route the boon back through **Mitra-Varuṇa** — the same deities who first made Ilā into Sudyumna.
- **A part-time king.** The alternation makes Sudyumna structurally unfit for continuous sovereignty — his subjects do not favour a ruler absent (as a woman) half of every year — which sets up his eventual abdication. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1)

## 6. The *Rāmāyaṇa* version — Ila of Bāhlī and the kiṃpuruṣa compromise

The **Uttara Kāṇḍa** tells an independent recension in which the figure is not Manu's child at all, and the retinue's transformation seeds an entire race of beings.

- **Ila of Bāhlī, son of Kardama.** Here **Ila** is a king of **Bāhlī/Bāhlīka**, son of the Prajāpati **Kardama** — *born male*, no botched-rite backstory. Hunting near **Kailāsa**, he enters Śiva's grove and is turned into a woman, along with his whole army and horse. (*Rāmāyaṇa*, Uttara Kāṇḍa — the Ila-upākhyāna) [late text]
- **The kiṃpuruṣa origin.** Budha, dwelling by the grove, weds the woman **Ilā**; and to dispose of Ila's transformed **retinue**, Budha turns them into **kiṃpuruṣas** (kinnara-like mountain beings) and settles them in the hills to live on roots — the *Rāmāyaṇa*'s etiology for the **kiṃpuruṣa people**. This "kiṃpuruṣa compromise" is distinctive to the epic version and absent from the Bhāgavata/Viṣṇu accounts. (*Rāmāyaṇa*, Uttara Kāṇḍa) [late text] [regional]
- **Monthly alternation, then the aśvamedha restoration.** Budha first arranges the **month-on, month-off** alternation (so that Ilā bears **Purūravas** in the female phase); later, to end the affliction permanently, Ila performs an **Aśvamedha** in Śiva's honour, and **Rudra**, pleased, restores him to **permanent maleness**. Ila then founds/settles **Pratiṣṭhāna**, giving it to **Purūravas**, and returns to rule Bāhlī. (*Rāmāyaṇa*, Uttara Kāṇḍa) [late text]
  - *Variant (permanent vs. lifelong alternation) [disputed]:* the **Rāmāyaṇa** *ends* the alternation via the horse-sacrifice (Ila becomes fully male); the **Bhāgavata/Viṣṇu** keep Sudyumna alternating *for life* and never restore him permanently. A clean, un-mergeable divergence.

---

## 7. Sudyumna's three sons and his retirement

- **Three sons as a father.** In his male (Sudyumna) months he begets three sons who become eponymous regional kings: **Utkala**, **Gaya**, and **Vinatāśva** — the standard **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** trio. Utkala gives his name to **Utkala** (Odisha), Gaya to **Gayā**, and Vinatāśva rules the **western** quarter. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1)
  - *Variant (third son's name) [disputed]:* the **Bhāgavata** (IX.1.42) names the trio **Utkala, Gaya, and Vimala**; other lists give **Haritāśva** for the third. The corpus records Vinatāśva / Vimala / Haritāśva as parallel namings of the same son, not three separate princes.
- **Purūravas keeps the central throne; the sons take the marches.** Because Purūravas (Ilā's son) inherits the Lunar throne at **Pratiṣṭhāna**, the three Sudyumna-sons are settled on the outlying regions — the dynastic estate is divided along the mother-line/father-line seam that runs through the whole figure. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1; *Bhāgavata* IX.1)
- **Abdication and liberation.** Judged unfit for kingship by reason of his alternating nature, Sudyumna finally makes over the realm to **Purūravas**, retires to the forest, takes up **Nārada**'s teaching / goddess-worship, and attains liberation. Manu's central sovereignty thereafter continues through the Solar sons (Ikṣvāku and his brothers). (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1)

## 8. Significance and the two clocks

- **The one weld between Sun and Moon.** Structurally, Ilā/Sudyumna is the *only* genealogical point where the Sūryavaṃśa and Candravaṃśa physically join: a Solar-born prince who, as a woman, bears the Lunar line's founder. Every later Lunar king — Yayāti, Bharata, the Kauravas and Pāṇḍavas, Kṛṣṇa's Yādavas — hangs from this androgynous hinge. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1, IV.6) [scholarly]
- **A Vedic ritual-word grown into a myth.** The [scholarly] reading is that the whole sex-change legend is a Purāṇic-epic *elaboration* of the Vedic **Iḍā/Iḷā** — the offering-goddess and Manu's flood-daughter of the **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa** — with the old **Mitra-Varuṇa** contest over her ownership re-read as a boon that changes her sex. The figure is thus a case study in how ritual personification hardens into narrative genealogy.
- **Scriptural clock.** The birth, transformation and alternation are set in **Satya (Kṛta) Yuga** at the opening of the current **7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara**, 28th Mahāyuga; Purūravas, the fruit of Ilā's motherhood, is placed at the head of the **Tretā Yuga**. The two events straddle the Satya→Tretā seam of the present great-age.
- **Historical clock.** The Vedic Iḍā belongs to the **[scholarly] c. 1200–900 BCE** Ṛgvedic/Brāhmaṇa layer; the **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa** redaction is **[scholarly] c. 900–700 BCE**; the fully developed Sudyumna sex-change is Purāṇic (**Bhāgavata** **[scholarly] c. 9th–10th c. CE**; **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** somewhat earlier), and the *Rāmāyaṇa*'s **Uttara Kāṇḍa** version is a recognised **[late text]** appendix to the epic. Never collapse the cyclic Satya-Yuga placement into these composition dates.

## Sources

- **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa I.8.1** — the deluge and the rising of **Iḍā (Iḷā)** from Manu's offering; she becomes his progenitrix-daughter, contested by Mitra-Varuṇa. The oldest, non-sex-changing stratum. (Eggeling tr., SBE XII)
- **Ṛgveda** — **Iḷā/Iḍā** as the libation- and food-goddess in the Āprī hymns; **X.95**, the Purūravas–Urvaśī dialogue that anchors Ilā's son at the head of the line.
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa (Śrīmad Bhāgavatam) IX.1** — the fullest Purāṇic account: Manu's flawed rite, the daughter Ilā made Sudyumna, the Śiva-grove transformation, Budha and the birth of Purūravas, Vasiṣṭha's propitiation of Śiva and the **monthly alternation**, the three sons (**Utkala, Gaya, Vimala**), and Sudyumna's abdication and liberation.
- **Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.1** — parallel account opening the dynastic books; names the three sons **Utkala, Gaya, Vinatāśva**; IV.6 carries the **Aila** line forward through Purūravas.
- **Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa** — the independent **Ila-upākhyāna**: Ila of Bāhlī (son of Kardama), the **kiṃpuruṣa** origin, and the **Aśvamedha** to Śiva that restores permanent maleness. A recognised [late text] recension.
- **Mahābhārata**, Ādi Parva (Sambhava-parvan) — the **Aila** genealogy tracing the Lunar kings from Ilā/Purūravas. (Critical Edition; cf. Ganguli tr.)
- **Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa** — a Śākta retelling of the Sudyumna sex-change with goddess-worship framing. [late text]
