# Māyā & Avidyā

> **Where this sits:** A cross-cutting doctrine of the *concepts-and-dharma* layer — the "two veils" that explain *why the One appears as the many*, and why the bound self mistakes the world (and itself) for what it is not. This file maps the **epistemology of illusion/ignorance** that the [Ātman, Brahman & the Six Darśanas](atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md) file presupposes whenever it speaks of "the empirical level."
> Parent: [../README.md](../README.md) · Sibling concepts: [./README.md](./README.md). See also the rebirth/bondage framework in [Puruṣārthas & Karma–Saṃsāra–Mokṣa](purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md), the jñāna path in [Yoga — Paths & Patañjali](yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md), and the Upanishadic creation accounts in [../00-time-and-cosmology/cosmogony-creation-accounts.md](../00-time-and-cosmology/cosmogony-creation-accounts.md).

If Brahman is the sole reality and is non-dual (*advaita*), the entire timeline — kalpas, manvantaras, yugas, gods, and selves — demands an account of *how* the plural world *appears* without compromising that unity. The classical Hindu answer turns on a cluster of terms: **māyā** (the cosmic power of appearance), **avidyā** (the individual ignorance that misperceives), **adhyāsa** (superimposition), **anirvacanīya** (the indescribable ontological status of the appearance), and the rival causal theories **vivarta** (apparent transformation) vs **pariṇāma** (real transformation). The schools of Vedānta diverge precisely on how literally to take these terms — Advaita makes māyā the linchpin of the whole system; Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita attack that linchpin head-on. [scholarly: even *within* Advaita the term's meaning shifted between Śaṅkara and his later commentators — see §8.]

---

## 1. The word *māyā* — a shifting signifier

The term is far older than Advaita and carries different weights across the corpus; cataloging the senses prevents the common error of reading the late Advaita meaning back into the Veda.

- **Vedic māyā — "power, craft, magic"** - In the Ṛgveda, *māyā* is the wonder-working power by which gods (and demons) accomplish marvels; Indra assumes many forms "through his māyās" (Ṛgveda 6.47.18, quoted in Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.5.19). Not yet "illusion" but creative/transformative potency. [scholarly]
- **Māyā as the Lord's creative power** - In the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad the personal Lord wields *māyā* as his ground-stuff: "Know prakṛti as māyā, and the great Lord (Maheśvara) as the wielder of māyā (*māyin*)" (Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad 4.10). Here māyā ≈ prakṛti, the material matrix.
- **Māyā as the Gītā's veiling power** - Kṛṣṇa: "Veiled by my yoga-māyā, I am not manifest to all" (Bhagavad Gītā 7.25); and the divine māyā "made of the guṇas" is "hard to cross" except by those who take refuge in him (Bhagavad Gītā 7.14).
- **Māyā as cosmic illusion (classical Advaita)** - The mature technical sense: the beginningless, indescribable power that makes the one Brahman *appear* as the manifold world without any real change in Brahman. This is the sense §3–§8 develop.
- **Māyā as Devī / Mahāmāyā** - In the Śākta stream the power is *personified* as the Goddess whose deluding power binds and whose grace releases; Viṣṇu's cosmic sleep is induced by Yoganidrā/Mahāmāyā (Devī Māhātmya 1.). See the Goddess theology in [../03-puranic-age/](../01-manvantaras/README.md) for the devotional inversion where māyā is worshipped, not merely negated. [folk/Śākta emphasis]

## 2. *Avidyā* — ignorance, and how it relates to māyā

- **Avidyā as primal nescience** - The "not-knowing" that is the root cause (*mūla-kāraṇa*) of bondage: forgetting one's true nature as Brahman, the self takes itself to be a finite, suffering jīva. Avidyā is the *subjective/epistemic* face; māyā the *cosmic/ontological* face — but the two are often used interchangeably.
- **Two avidyās of the Upaniṣads** - Distinguished early: *vidyā* (knowledge of the Self) vs *avidyā* (ritual/empirical knowledge); "into blind darkness enter those who worship avidyā" (Īśa Upaniṣad 9–11; Muṇḍaka 1.2). Note this older sense is *not* yet the metaphysical nescience of Advaita.
- **The māyā / avidyā relation — three positions:**
  - *Variant (synonymy):* Many Advaita texts treat **māyā = avidyā**, two names for one beginningless principle (the dominant simplifying reading).
  - *Variant (cosmic vs individual):* Māyā = the *collective/cosmic* power conditioning Īśvara (the upādhi of saguṇa Brahman); avidyā = the *individual* nescience conditioning each jīva. (Associated with the Pañcadaśī of Vidyāraṇya and later doxography.)
  - *Variant (pure vs impure):* Māyā as *śuddha-sattva*-predominant (the medium of Īśvara, undeluded) vs avidyā as *malina*/impure (the deluding nescience of the jīva). [scholarly: a later systematizing distinction.]
- **Two functions of avidyā/māyā** - It works by two powers: **āvaraṇa-śakti** (the *concealing* power that hides Brahman) and **vikṣepa-śakti** (the *projecting* power that throws up the appearance of the world). The rope is hidden (āvaraṇa) *and* a snake is projected (vikṣepa).

## 3. *Adhyāsa* — superimposition, the engine of error

- **The opening of the Brahma Sūtra Bhāṣya** - Śaṅkara begins his entire commentary not with Brahman but with the problem of *adhyāsa*: the "natural" (*naisargika*) confusion whereby we mix the real (*sat*) and unreal, the Self and the not-Self, saying "I am this body," "this is mine" (Śaṅkara, *Adhyāsa-bhāṣya*, preamble to Brahma Sūtra 1.1.1).
- **Definition of adhyāsa** - "The apparent presentation, in the form of memory, of something previously seen, in some other thing" (*smṛti-rūpaḥ paratra pūrva-dṛṣṭāvabhāsaḥ*) — i.e., projecting a remembered datum onto a present substrate (Śaṅkara, *Adhyāsa-bhāṣya*).
- **The structure "there is a snake"** - The bare *"there is"* (existence, *sat*) belongs to the real rope/Brahman; *"a snake"* is the unreal particular mentally projected. Existence is borrowed from the substrate; the false form is superimposed onto it.
- **Mutual superimposition (*itaretarādhyāsa*)** - Self and not-Self are superimposed *on each other*: the Self's reality/consciousness lends the world its apparent being, while the world's properties (agency, suffering, limitation) are falsely ascribed to the Self.

## 4. The rope–snake (*rajju-sarpa*) and sister analogies

The standard teaching images, each isolating a different feature of illusion:

- **Rope–snake (*rajju-sarpa*)** - In dim light a rope is mistaken for a snake: the snake never *existed*, yet was *experienced* (with real fear) until light reveals the rope. So the world is mistaken for real until knowledge reveals Brahman. The snake's "being" was always only the rope's. [Origin: developed in Gauḍapāda's *Māṇḍūkya Kārikā*; popularized by Śaṅkara's bhāṣyas.]
- **Mother-of-pearl as silver (*śukti-rajata*)** - Nacre glimpsed as silver: positive misperception corrected by closer knowledge; favored for analyzing *what kind of error* perception is.
- **Mirage water (*marīci-jala*)** - Water seen in a desert: appears, draws action, never quenches — appearance with practical force but no substance.
- **Dream (*svapna*)** - Within the dream, dream-objects are fully real; on waking they are sublated. Gauḍapāda presses this hardest in his *vaitathya* (unreality) chapter, edging toward *ajāta-vāda* (no-origination). See the three-states analysis at [atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md §5](atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md).
- **Rope coiled / single moon as double** - The "second moon" (timira, a sight defect) and the snake-in-rope are paired as examples of seeing one thing as another.
- **Space in pots (*ghaṭākāśa*)** - One space *appears* divided by jars; break the jars and the division vanishes — illustrating how the one Self *appears* as many jīvas (the **avaccheda / limitation** model; see §6).

## 5. *Anirvacanīya* — the "indescribable" status of māyā

The most distinctive and most attacked Advaita claim: the appearance is *neither real nor unreal nor both* (*sad-asad-vilakṣaṇa*).

- **Not real (*sat*)** - Because it is *sublated* (*bādhita*): on the dawn of knowledge it ceases, as the snake vanishes. Only the never-sublated (Brahman) is truly real.
- **Not unreal (*asat*)** - Because it *is experienced* and *has effects* (*vyāvahārika satya*, empirical reality), unlike a sky-flower or a square circle (*tuccha*, the utterly non-existent) which is never experienced.
- **Therefore "indescribable" (*anirvacanīya*)** - Falling outside the exhaustive real/unreal dichotomy, its ontological status cannot be fixed in those terms — hence *anirvacanīya-khyāti*, the Advaita theory of error.
- **Three orders of being (*sattā-traya*)** - The framework that houses this: *pāramārthika* (absolute — Brahman alone), *vyāvahārika* (empirical — the shared waking world, including Īśvara), *prātibhāsika* (merely apparent — the dream snake, private and instantly sublated). Māyā operates the lower two.
  - *Variant (theories of error among schools):* Advaita = *anirvacanīya-khyāti*; Nyāya = *anyathā-khyāti* (one real thing misperceived *as* another real thing); Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā = *akhyāti* (non-apprehension of a distinction, "no error, only incomplete cognition"); Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā = *viparīta-khyāti*; Yogācāra Buddhism = *ātma-khyāti*; Madhva/Viśiṣṭādvaita realists ≈ *sat-khyāti / yathārtha-khyāti* (all cognition apprehends something real). The rope-snake debate is really a proxy war over *which theory of error is correct*.

## 6. Where does avidyā *reside*? — the locus problem (*āśraya*)

A genuine internal fault-line, which Ramanuja later weaponizes (§9). If avidyā is to do work it must inhere somewhere; both candidates are awkward.

- **The dilemma** - It cannot rest in **Brahman** (which is pure, self-luminous knowledge — ignorance contradicts its nature), nor straightforwardly in the **jīva** (which is itself a *product* of avidyā — circularity: avidyā produces the jīva that is supposed to house avidyā).
- **Avaccheda-vāda (limitation)** - The one Self is "limited/delimited" by the upādhi of the inner organ (antaḥkaraṇa), as one space is delimited by pots; each delimited portion is a jīva. (Associated with the **Bhāmatī** school of Vācaspati Miśra.)
- **Pratibimba-vāda (reflection)** - The jīva is a *reflection* (*pratibimba*) of Brahman (the *bimba*, prototype) in the mirror of avidyā/antaḥkaraṇa; Īśvara is the reflection in māyā. (Associated with the **Vivaraṇa** school of Prakāśātman.)
  - *Variant (ābhāsa-vāda):* the reflection is a mere *semblance* (ābhāsa), even less real than a true reflection — a still more deflationary reading.
- **Bhāmatī's answer to the locus** - Avidyā's *locus (āśraya)* is the **jīva**, while its *object (viṣaya)* is **Brahman** (jīva is ignorant *of* Brahman). Multiple avidyās, one per jīva (*jīva-avidyā*).
- **Vivaraṇa's answer to the locus** - Both locus *and* object of avidyā is **Brahman** itself; there is one root-nescience (*mūla-avidyā*), and Īśvara is Brahman as conditioned by it. Bites the bullet that ignorance "rests on" Brahman, arguing self-luminosity is not contradicted because avidyā is its *object*, not its modification.

## 7. *Vivarta* vs *pariṇāma* — apparent vs real transformation

The causal heart of the matter: when Brahman "becomes" the world, is the change *real* or merely *apparent*?

- **Pariṇāma-vāda (real transformation)** - The cause *actually transforms* into the effect: milk into curd, gold into ornaments, Brahman into world. The effect is a real modification of the cause; the world is *as real as* Brahman, being Brahman modified. (Sāṃkhya holds this of prakṛti; Vedānta-realists hold it of Brahman.)
- **Vivarta-vāda (apparent transformation)** - The cause *appears* as the effect *without itself changing*: the rope appears as a snake, Brahman appears as the world, but no real modification occurs. The classic line: *vivarta* = appearance of one thing as another *of a lower order of reality*; *pariṇāma* = appearance of one thing as another *of the same order of reality*.
- **The standard Advaita position** - The world is a *vivarta* of Brahman, not a *pariṇāma*; Brahman is the *non-transforming* (*vivarta-upādāna*) material cause. This protects Brahman's immutability (*kūṭastha-nitya*).
  - *Variant — was vivarta-vāda Śaṅkara's own view?* [disputed] Several modern scholars (Hacker, Ingalls, and esp. Andrew Nicholson) argue Śaṅkara's own language is closer to **pariṇāma/satkāryavāda**, and that strict **vivarta-vāda** was crystallized later — the **13th-c. Prakāśātman** (Vivaraṇa school) is credited with giving *vivarta* its sharp technical sense. On this reading "the world is illusory" is a post-Śaṅkara hardening, not the master's first word. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (dṛṣṭi-sṛṣṭi-vāda):* "Perception-is-creation" — a radical sub-Advaita view (Prakāśānanda, *Vedānta-siddhānta-muktāvalī*) that objects exist *only* as perceived, collapsing even the vyāvahārika order; close to subjective idealism. [late text]
  - *Variant (ajāta-vāda):* Gauḍapāda's "no-origination" — nothing is *ever* really born at all; even the framework of cause-and-effect is provisional. The most uncompromising terminus of the vivarta logic.

## 8. The two Advaita sub-schools — Bhāmatī vs Vivaraṇa

Post-Śaṅkara Advaita split over the mechanics of avidyā; both are orthodox Advaita but disagree on locus, number, and the role of perception.

- **Bhāmatī school** - Founded on **Vācaspati Miśra's** *Bhāmatī* (commentary on Śaṅkara's Brahma Sūtra Bhāṣya, ~9th–10th c.). Holds: avidyā's *locus* is the **jīva**; avidyā is **plural** (one per soul); favors **avaccheda-vāda** (limitation); the mind reaches out to objects (*vṛtti* via the senses); Brahman is the *object* of ignorance, jīva the seat. Sometimes called *jīva-āśrita-avidyā-vāda*.
- **Vivaraṇa school** - Founded on **Prakāśātman's** *Pañcapādikā-vivaraṇa* (~13th c., a sub-commentary on Padmapāda's *Pañcapādikā*). Holds: avidyā's locus *and* object is **Brahman**; one **mūla-avidyā** (root-nescience); favors **pratibimba-vāda** (reflection); sharpened the technical sense of *vivarta*. The more influential of the two in later Advaita.
- **Why it matters** - These intramural differences are exactly the cracks Ramanuja and Madhva pry open: "you cannot even agree where ignorance lives or how many there are."

## 9. Viśiṣṭādvaita critique — Ramanuja's *Saptavidha-anupapatti* ("Seven Untenables")

In the *Śrī-bhāṣya* (commentary on the Brahma Sūtras), Ramanuja launches the most famous demolition of the Advaita avidyā, the **seven great untenables** — seven respects in which *avidyā/māyā* is allegedly incoherent. (Catalog per the Viśiṣṭādvaita tradition; John Grimes, *The Seven Great Untenables*, gives the standard English reconstruction.)

- **1. Āśraya-anupapatti (locus)** - There is no possible *seat* for avidyā: not Brahman (self-luminous, knowledge by nature — ignorance cannot reside in it), not the jīva (a *product* of avidyā — circular).
- **2. Tirodhāna-anupapatti (concealment)** - Avidyā cannot *conceal* Brahman, since Brahman is self-luminous consciousness; to veil it would be to destroy its very nature, which is impossible.
- **3. Svarūpa-anupapatti (nature)** - The very *being* of avidyā is incoherent: it is said to be neither real nor unreal (*anirvacanīya*) — but "indefinable" is itself a covert contradiction; whatever exists is either *sat* or *asat*.
- **4. Anirvacanīyatva-anupapatti (indescribability)** - The doctrine of indescribability itself is untenable: to call something "neither real nor unreal" violates the law of excluded middle and is empty.
- **5. Pramāṇa-anupapatti (means of knowledge)** - There is **no valid pramāṇa** that establishes avidyā: perception gives reals, inference needs a sound ground, scripture (rightly read) teaches a real world and a qualified Brahman — none delivers a positive cosmic nescience.
- **6. Nivartaka-anupapatti (removal)** - There is no coherent *remover* of avidyā: a *nirguṇa*, contentless, attribute-free knowledge cannot stand opposed to and cancel ignorance the way a determinate cognition cancels a determinate error.
- **7. Nivṛtti-anupapatti (cessation)** - The *cessation* of avidyā is untenable: if liberating knowledge is itself within the empirical order (a vṛtti), it too is a product of avidyā and cannot finally end it — and if it is Brahman, Brahman is ever-present, so bondage should never have arisen.
- **Ramanuja's positive alternative** - Reject anirvacanīya avidyā altogether. The world and souls are **real** and form the **body (śarīra)** of Brahman (Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa); Brahman is **saguṇa** with infinite auspicious qualities; bondage is real *karma*, not illusion; liberation is by *bhakti/prapatti*, not by dissolving an unreal veil. See the school summary at [atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md §6](atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md).
  - *Variant (how decisive?):* [disputed] Advaitins (e.g. later Vivaraṇa authors, and the *Advaita-siddhi* of Madhusūdana Sarasvatī answering Madhva) reply that the Seven Untenables conflate the *vyāvahārika* and *pāramārthika* standpoints — that objections framed in empirical logic cannot bind a doctrine about what *transcends* that logic. The debate is unresolved and tradition-internal.

## 10. Dvaita critique — Madhva's realism

Madhva (Ānanda Tīrtha) rejects māyā/avidyā even more flatly, founding a thoroughgoing realism and pluralism (*tattva-vāda*).

- **The world is real (*satya*), not illusory** - Difference (*bheda*) is the irreducible fact of reality — the famous **five-fold real difference (*pañca-bheda*)**: God–soul, God–matter, soul–soul, soul–matter, matter–matter are all *eternally and truly* distinct. No appearance to dissolve.
- **No anirvacanīya** - Whatever is, is *sat*; the "neither-real-nor-unreal" is rejected as meaningless. Cognition is intrinsically valid (*yathārtha-khyāti*): error is misperception of *real* things, not the projection of an indescribable third category.
- **Re-reading the identity texts** - Where Advaita reads *tat tvam asi* as bare identity, Madhva preserves difference — even glossing problematic phrases to keep soul and God eternally distinct (he reads some as *atat tvam asi*, "thou art *not* That"). See the mahāvākya variants at [atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md §3](atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md).
- **Pratibimba turned realist** - Madhva *keeps* the reflection (*bimba-pratibimba*) image but inverts its meaning: the soul is an *eternal, real* reflection-image of God (a permanent dependence-relation, *paratantra*), **not** a passing appearance produced by avidyā that vanishes at liberation. Even in mukti the soul remains a distinct, God-dependent reflection.
- **Bondage and liberation** - Bondage is real and beginningless, lifted by God's grace through *bhakti*; souls are intrinsically graded (*svarūpa-bheda*), and Madhva uniquely teaches *eternal damnation* (*tamo-yogya*) for some — impossible if all difference were mere māyā. [late text relative to Śaṅkara: Madhva, 13th c.]
- **The literary fallout** - The Advaita–Dvaita polemic over māyā produced a celebrated chain of debate texts: Vyāsatīrtha's *Nyāyāmṛta* (Dvaita) → Madhusūdana Sarasvatī's *Advaita-siddhi* (Advaita) → Rāmācārya's *Taraṅgiṇī* (Dvaita rejoinder) — among the most technical exchanges in Indian philosophy. [scholarly]

## 11. Other Vedānta readings of māyā (briefly)

- **Bhedābheda / Achintya-bhedābheda** - Bhāskara and Nimbārka (bhedābheda) and the Gauḍīya school (Caitanya, *achintya-bhedābheda*, "inconceivable difference-and-non-difference") treat the world as a **real** transformation (*pariṇāma*) / real *śakti* of Brahman, **not** an illusion; māyā is God's real deluding *power*, affecting souls but never the Lord. Identity *and* difference both hold, inconceivably.
- **Śuddhādvaita (Vallabha)** - "Pure non-dualism": the world is a *real* manifestation of Brahman (Kṛṣṇa); Vallabha explicitly *rejects* Śaṅkara's māyā as a falsifying veil — hence "pure" (un-adulterated by māyā). Brahman really becomes the world by its own will (*āvirbhāva*). [late text]
- **Kashmir Śaivism (Pratyabhijñā)** - Reframes māyā as one of the 36 *tattvas* and as the Lord's *svātantrya* (autonomy/free power) by which pure Consciousness (Śiva) freely contracts itself into limited subjects — *ābhāsa-vāda*, "doctrine of self-manifestation." Illusion here is **real self-limiting play**, not error to be negated. (Outside Vedānta proper but a major rival theory of appearance.) [scholarly]
- **Śākta worship of Māyā** - As in §1, the Goddess-traditions *personify and venerate* Mahāmāyā: the power that binds is the very power that, propitiated, releases. The metaphysical "veil" becomes a soteriological *Mother*.

## 12. Practical upshot — how the veil is lifted

- **Knowledge alone removes ignorance (Advaita)** - Since bondage is *avidyā*, only *jñāna* (Self-knowledge, triggered by the mahāvākyas under a qualified teacher) can end it — "as light dispels darkness, not action." Karma and bhakti purify the mind toward this. See [Yoga — Paths & Patañjali](yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md).
- **Jīvanmukti** - One in whom avidyā is destroyed is *liberated while living*; the body persists by spent karma (*prārabdha*) like a potter's wheel spinning after the push, but is no longer binding.
- **Devotional inversion (theist schools)** - For Ramanuja, Madhva, Vallabha, and the bhakti streams, māyā is *crossed by grace*, not dissolved by gnosis: "those who take refuge in Me cross over this māyā" (Bhagavad Gītā 7.14). Bondage being real, only the Lord can release.
