# The Four Aims (Purusharthas) & Karma–Samsara–Moksha

> Cross-cutting doctrine of Hindu life and the rebirth engine. This sits in the **Concepts & Dharma** layer of the timeline — it is the "physics" of the individual soul (jiva) moving through the cosmic clock described in [00-time-and-cosmology](../00-time-and-cosmology/README.md).
> Up: [Master Index](../README.md)

These two doctrines answer the two great questions of classical Hindu thought: *what is a human life for* (the four purusharthas) and *what drives a soul through endless births inside the manvantara/yuga machinery* (karma → samsara → moksha). Both are pan-Hindu but disputed in detail across schools; all major variants are cataloged below.

---

## I. The Purusharthas — the Four Aims of Human Life

The **purushartha** ("object/goal of a human being") is the classical fourfold scheme of legitimate ends. The first three form the **trivarga** ("three categories"), the worldly/social set; **moksha** is the fourth, the transcendent end, sometimes counted *within* the four and sometimes treated as standing *beyond* the trivarga altogether.

- **Dharma** - Righteousness, duty, cosmic and social order; the regulating aim that constrains the other two. The proper conduct fitting one's varna, ashrama, and circumstance. Canonical corpus: the **Dharmashastras** (Manusmriti, Yajnavalkya Smriti, Gautama/Apastamba/Baudhayana Dharmasutras) — see [agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md](../90-literature-corpus/agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md).
- **Artha** - Material prosperity, wealth, political power, economic security; the means of worldly success. Canonical text: **Kautilya's Arthashastra** (statecraft, economy, law), plus the Nitishastra tradition.
- **Kama** - Desire, pleasure, aesthetic and sensual enjoyment, love; legitimate when not in conflict with dharma. Canonical text: **Vatsyayana's Kamasutra** (and broader Kamashastra), which itself ranks dharma > artha > kama.
- **Moksha** - Liberation/release from the cycle of rebirth (samsara); the supreme spiritual goal. Corpus: **Upanishads**, **Bhagavad Gita**, **Brahma Sutras**, **Yoga Sutras**, and the moksha-dharma sections of the Mahabharata (Shanti Parva). See [smriti-itihasa-epics.md](../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md), [shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md).

### History & textual layering

- **Trivarga first, moksha added later** - The early Vedic Samhitas/Brahmanas/Aranyakas operate chiefly with the trivarga (dharma–artha–kama); moksha emerges as a distinct fourth aim with the renunciatory thought of the **Upanishads** (Vedic to Upanishadic shift). By the epics and Dharmashastra, the integrated fourfold (chaturvarga) is standard. ([Wikipedia: Puruṣārtha])
- **The trivarga / moksha divide** - Scholars read the split as social/worldly (trivarga) vs. personal/transcendent (moksha): the first three concern engaged householder life, the fourth concerns renunciation and release. ([Wikipedia: Puruṣārtha])

### The ranking debate (catalog of positions)

- **Dharma-supremacy (Dharmashastra default)** - Order of priority is **dharma > artha > kama**; any artha or kama that violates dharma is to be abandoned. Manusmriti and Kamasutra both subordinate wealth and pleasure to righteousness.
- *Variant (Arthashastra):* Kautilya gives **artha** practical primacy for the king, since artha (a secure, prosperous state) is the precondition for both dharma and kama to be possible at all — a pragmatic statecraft inversion of the priority list.
  - *Variant (Vatsyayana, Kamasutra):* affirms the dharma > artha > kama order in principle, but argues each has its proper time of life and must each be cultivated as a genuine science.
- **Moksha as the supreme/fourth aim (Vedanta default)** - The Upanishads, Gita, and Vedanta schools treat **moksha** as the *parama-purushartha* (highest aim) that relativizes the trivarga; the worldly aims serve, at best, as preparation.
  - *Variant [scholarly/disputed]:* Some thinkers hold moksha is **not a fourth purushartha at all** but the *negation/transcendence* of all aims — since purushartha presupposes desire and agency, while moksha is the cessation of desire-driven action. On this reading the genuine human ends are the trivarga, and moksha lies *outside* the scheme. ([Wikipedia: Puruṣārtha])
  - *Variant (Mimamsa, ritualist):* the Purva-Mimamsa school historically emphasized **dharma** (correct Vedic ritual action) as the operative human aim and was reluctant about moksha; liberation becomes central only in **Uttara-Mimamsa / Vedanta**.

## II. Karma — the Law of Action

**Karma** ("action") is the moral causality binding deed to consequence across lifetimes: every intentional act leaves a residue (samskara / apurva / adrishta) that ripens into future experience. It is the mechanism that *fills* and *empties* the rebirth cycle.

### The standard fourfold classification of karma

- **Sanchita karma** - The total *accumulated* store of all karmic impressions amassed across all past lives — the latent reservoir not yet ripened. ([Wikipedia: Karma in Hinduism])
- **Prarabdha karma** - The *allotted* portion of sanchita selected to ripen in the present life; it has "begun to fruit" and shapes this body, birth, and lifespan. Held to be unavoidable — it must be exhausted by experience (even by the liberated; see jivanmukti).
- **Kriyamana karma** (a.k.a. **vartamana**) - Karma *being made now*, by present thought, intention, and action; the only karma fully under one's control. Part yields immediate fruit; the remainder is deposited.
- **Agami karma** - *Coming/future* karma — the consequences of present actions destined to manifest in subsequent lives; agami flows into the sanchita store, perpetuating the cycle. ([Shortform: Four Types of Karma])
  - *Note [scholarly]:* Many texts use a **threefold** scheme (sanchita, prarabdha, kriyamana/agami), treating agami and kriyamana as the same forward-flowing category; the fourfold list simply splits "action now" from "its future fruit."

### Karma in the Bhagavad Gita

- **Nishkama karma (karma-yoga)** - Act according to dharma while renouncing attachment to the *fruits* of action ("you have a right to action, never to its fruits," Gita 2.47). Desireless action does not generate binding karma, so one can act fully yet not accumulate fresh bondage. ([Wikipedia: Karma yoga])
- **Three margas to moksha** - The Gita lays out **karma-yoga** (selfless action), **jnana-yoga** (knowledge/discrimination), and **bhakti-yoga** (loving devotion/surrender) as complementary paths to liberation, suited to different temperaments. See deep file [../04-deep-dives](../04-deep-dives) (Gita).

## III. Samsara — Transmigration and the Two Paths

**Samsara** ("wandering through") is the beginningless cycle of birth–death–rebirth (**punarjanma**) driven by karma and ignorance (avidya). The jiva (embodied soul) takes successive bodies until liberated.

### The doctrine of the two after-death paths (gati)

The **Panchagni Vidya** ("doctrine of the five fires") in **Chandogya Upanishad 5.3–5.10** — nearly identical to **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 6.2** — sets out where the soul goes at death, sorted by knowledge and conduct. ([Wikipedia: Chandogya Upanishad])

- **Devayana** ("path of the gods" / path of light) - For knowers of Brahman and practitioners of meditation/wisdom: the soul travels through realms of flame, the day, the bright fortnight, the northward sun, to Brahman's world — and **does not return** to rebirth (the "path of no return," archiradi-marga). ([hinduwebsite: Devayana])
- **Pitryana** ("path of the fathers" / path of smoke) - For those devoted to ritual works, charity, and worldly desire: the soul goes via smoke, the night, the dark fortnight, the southward sun, to the **world of the Moon**, enjoys the fruits of merit until that merit is exhausted, then **definitely returns** to a new birth (Brihadaranyaka 6.2.16). ([Wikipedia: Chandogya Upanishad])
- **The third state** - Those of neither path (the wicked / those without knowledge or proper works) are said to take repeated birth as small creatures — "be born and die" continually, the "third place." (Chandogya 5.10.8)
  - *Variant (Bhagavad Gita 8.23–26):* the same two paths are termed **shukla-gati** (bright, no-return) and **krishna-gati** (dark, returning); the yogi who knows them is not deluded.

### Rebirth across the cosmic scale

- The cycle of births plays out *inside* the yuga/manvantara/kalpa clock (see [yugas-and-mahayuga.md](../00-time-and-cosmology/yugas-and-mahayuga.md), [manvantara-system.md](../00-time-and-cosmology/manvantara-system.md)). At **pralaya** (cosmic dissolution) un-liberated jivas are not destroyed but reabsorbed in latent (avyakta) form, carrying their karmic seeds into the next creation — see [kalpa-and-pralaya.md](../00-time-and-cosmology/kalpa-and-pralaya.md). Samsara thus spans not just lifetimes but world-cycles.

## IV. Moksha — Liberation (variant goals by school)

**Moksha** (also mukti, kaivalya, apavarga, nirvana) is release from samsara — the cessation of binding karma and rebirth. *What* liberation consists of, and *when* it is attainable, differs sharply by darshana. All major positions are cataloged.

### Vedanta variants

- **Advaita Vedanta (Shankara)** - Moksha = realization of the non-dual identity of **atman = Brahman**; ignorance (avidya), not karma per se, is the root bondage, dissolved by knowledge (jnana). Liberation is the discovery of an already-existing fact, not a future attainment.
  - **Jivanmukti** - Liberation **while still living**: the knower (jivanmukta) is free *now*; the body persists only until **prarabdha karma** is spent, after which **videhamukti** (disembodied liberation) follows. ([Wikipedia: Moksha])
  - The liberated atman is not "going" anywhere — Brahman is omnipresent; talk of "reaching Brahman's world" (krama-mukti via devayana) is, for Advaita, a graded path for meditators on the qualified (saguna) Brahman.
- **Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja)** - The liberated jiva retains its **individuality**, never merging into Brahman; moksha is eternal, blissful, conscious **service to God in Vaikuntha**. Attained only **after death (videhamukti)** — there is **no jivanmukti**. Means: **bhakti** and **prapatti** (total self-surrender). ([Wikipedia: Vishishtadvaita])
- **Dvaita (Madhva)** - Strict dualism; post-mortem liberation only, by God's grace through bhakti. Four graded states of moksha, lowest to highest: **salokya** (sharing God's world) → **samipya/sannidhya** (nearness) → **sarupya** (resembling God's form) → **sayujya** (intimate union/communion, the highest). Souls are intrinsically ranked; some are eternally bound [disputed across schools]. ([Wikipedia: Moksha])

### Non-Vedanta darshanas

- **Samkhya** - Liberation = **kaivalya** ("isolation"): the discriminative realization (viveka) that **purusha** (pure consciousness) is wholly distinct from **prakriti** (matter/mind). No God required (classical Samkhya is nir-ishvara). Accepts **jivanmukti**.
- **Yoga (Patanjali, Yoga Sutras)** - Same goal of **kaivalya**, reached by the eightfold path (ashtanga) culminating in samadhi, stilling the chitta-vrittis (Yoga Sutra 1.2–1.3); the seer then "abides in its own nature." Theistic in flavor (Ishvara as aid), method-focused. See [upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md](../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md).
- **Nyaya–Vaisheshika** - Moksha (apavarga) = total cessation of all experience including pleasure — the soul (atman) freed of all qualities, a state of absence of suffering, attained through right knowledge of the categories. [Some critics call this a "stone-like" liberation.]
- **Purva-Mimamsa** - Originally action/ritual-centric and lukewarm on moksha; later Mimamsakas accommodate liberation as the natural result of exhausting karma without generating new karma.

### Cross-tradition contrasts [comparative]

- **Buddhist contrast** - **Nirvana** is the extinguishing of craving and the cycle of suffering, but framed on **anatta** (no-self) and **dependent origination** — rejecting any permanent atman that is liberated; rebirth without a transmigrating soul.
- **Jain contrast** - **Moksha/kaivalya** = the jiva sheds all accreted **karmic matter** (conceived as literal subtle particles) and rises to the **siddhashila** at the top of the universe, omniscient and eternally free; achieved by ascetic purification and non-violence (ahimsa). Karma is substance, not just moral law.
