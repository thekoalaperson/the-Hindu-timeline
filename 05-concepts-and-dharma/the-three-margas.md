# The Three Mārgas — Karma, Bhakti, Jñāna

> Cross-cutting doctrine within [05-concepts-and-dharma](./README.md) of *The Hindu Timeline*. The **three (or four) paths to liberation** — action, devotion, knowledge, and meditation — as a soteriological *system*: how each is defined, how the [Bhagavad Gītā](../04-deep-dives/bhagavad-gita.md) braids them, and which schools privilege which. Back to the [master index](../README.md).
> Companion files: [Yoga — Paths & Patañjali](yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md) (the practitioner's-eye view of each yoga + the eight limbs), [Puruṣārthas & Karma–Saṃsāra–Mokṣa](purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md) (mokṣa as the goal), [Ātman, Brahman & the Six Darśanas](atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md) (the metaphysics the paths serve).

The Sanskrit *mārga* ("road, path") and *yoga* ("yoking, discipline") are used interchangeably for a route to *mokṣa*. The classical triad — **karma-mārga** (action), **bhakti-mārga** (devotion), **jñāna-mārga** (knowledge) — is the *trimārga* introduced and reconciled in the Bhagavad Gītā; a fourth, **rāja-yoga** (meditation), is bolted on in later and modern framings. This file is the *comparative-doctrinal* node: it catalogs how each path is defined, the disputes over which is supreme and whether they combine, and the sectarian schools that elevate one over the others. For the lived practice of each (techniques, limbs, sub-yogas) see the companion [Yoga file](yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md), which this file deliberately does not duplicate.

## 1. The Three Paths Defined

- **Karma-mārga — the path of action** - Liberation through *dharmic* action performed without attachment to its **fruits** (*phala*); work itself offered as sacrifice. The watchword: *"karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana"* — "your right is to action alone, never to its fruits" (Bhagavad Gītā 2.47). Action is unavoidable — *"na hi kaścit kṣaṇam api jātu tiṣṭhaty akarmakṛt"*, no one exists even a moment without acting (Gītā 3.5) — so the answer is not inaction but *desireless* action. Suited to the **active** temperament.
  - *Variant (Gītā 5.2):* **niṣkāma karma** (desireless action) is judged *superior to* **saṃnyāsa** (outright renunciation of action) — doing-without-attachment beats not-doing.
  - *Variant (Mīmāṃsā reading):* the older Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā school reads *karma* narrowly as **Vedic ritual action** (*yajña*), holding that correct performance of enjoined rites is itself the means to heaven/well-being — a ritualist root that the Gītā universalizes into *all* duty. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Lokasaṃgraha):* karma-yoga reframed as action "for the holding-together of the world" (*lokasaṃgraha*, Gītā 3.20–25) — the sage acts to set an example, a reading foregrounded by modern interpreters (Tilak, Gandhi) as a gospel of selfless social service. [late text] [scholarly]

- **Bhakti-mārga — the path of devotion** - Liberation through loving, single-minded surrender (*prapatti*, *śaraṇāgati*) to a personal deity (Ishvara — Kṛṣṇa, Viṣṇu, Śiva, Devī), whose **grace** (*prasāda*, *kṛpā*) carries the devotee across. Kṛṣṇa promises: *"sarva-dharmān parityajya mām ekaṃ śaraṇaṃ vraja"* — "abandoning all dharmas, take refuge in Me alone; I will free you from all sins" (Gītā 18.66, the *carama-śloka*). The devotee is "dearest" to God (Gītā 12). Suited to the **emotional/devotional** temperament.
  - *Variant (navadhā bhakti):* nine modes of devotion — *śravaṇa* (hearing), *kīrtana* (chanting), *smaraṇa* (remembering), *pāda-sevana* (serving the feet), *arcana* (ritual worship), *vandana* (bowing), *dāsya* (servitude), *sakhya* (friendship), *ātma-nivedana* (total self-offering) — codified at Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.5.23. See [../04-deep-dives/krishna-lila.md](../04-deep-dives/krishna-lila.md).
  - *Variant (rāgānugā vs vaidhī):* later Gauḍīya theology distinguishes **vaidhī bhakti** (devotion governed by scriptural injunction) from **rāgānugā bhakti** (spontaneous, passion-led devotion modeled on the Vraja-*gopīs*), with the *bhāva*/*rasa* taxonomy of Rūpa Gosvāmī's *Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu*. [late text]
  - *Variant (Śaiva/Śākta):* parallel devotional streams toward [Śiva](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) (Nāyaṉārs, Śaiva Siddhānta) and the [Goddess](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) — bhakti is not Vaiṣṇava-exclusive.

- **Jñāna-mārga — the path of knowledge** - Liberation through *discriminative* wisdom (*viveka-jñāna*): the direct realization that bondage is rooted in *avidyā* (ignorance), and that the *ātman* is — depending on school — identical to, or distinct-yet-dependent on, *Brahman*. The classical method is the threefold *śravaṇa* (hearing the teaching) → *manana* (reflecting on it) → *nididhyāsana* (sustained meditative assimilation), preceded by the *sādhana-catuṣṭaya* (the four qualifications: *viveka*, *vairāgya*, the six virtues, *mumukṣutva*). Rooted in the [Upaniṣads](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md) and Advaita Vedānta. Suited to the **contemplative/intellectual** temperament.
  - *Variant (mahāvākyas):* in Advaita the trigger of realization is meditation on the "great sayings" — *tat tvam asi* ("That thou art", Chāndogya 6.8.7), *ahaṃ brahmāsmi* ("I am Brahman", Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.4.10), *prajñānaṃ brahma*, *ayam ātmā brahma*. See [atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md](atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md).
  - *Variant (jīvanmukti):* jñāna is held to liberate **here and now** — the knower becomes a *jīvanmukta* (liberated-while-living), the body persisting only by spent *prārabdha karma* — whereas bhakti/karma traditions often defer full liberation to *videhamukti* (after death). [scholarly]

## 2. Rāja-Yoga — the Fourth Path

- **Rāja-yoga / dhyāna-mārga — the path of meditation** - The "royal" path of stilling the mind through concentrated meditation, in later usage essentially Patañjali's eight-limbed (*aṣṭāṅga*) system. The Gītā's chapter 6 (*Dhyāna* / *Ātma-saṃyama Yoga*) is its compact charter — posture, gaze, regulated breath, the steadied mind "like a lamp in a windless place" (Gītā 6.19). Suited to the **mystical/meditative** temperament. For the eight limbs and the Yoga Sūtras themselves, see [yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md §3](yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md) and [../04-deep-dives/yoga-sutras-of-patanjali.md](../04-deep-dives/yoga-sutras-of-patanjali.md).
  - *Variant (late label):* "Rāja-yoga" as a name for Patañjali's system is **medieval** — the *Haṭha-yoga-pradīpikā* (15th c.) frames *haṭha* as preparation *for* rāja-yoga (HYP 1.1–2), and the term generalized only as "yoga" came to mean "spiritual path." [scholarly] [late text]
  - *Variant (is it a separate mārga?):* classically the triad is **three**, not four; rāja/dhyāna is often folded into jñāna-mārga (meditation as the *nididhyāsana* phase) rather than counted independently. The four-path scheme is largely a modern systematization (see §4). [scholarly]

## 3. The Gītā's Synthesis

The Bhagavad Gītā is the single text that holds all three paths together; its 18 chapters are each colophoned a "yoga," presenting the whole dialogue as a graded curriculum rather than a single recipe. [04-deep-dives/bhagavad-gita.md](../04-deep-dives/bhagavad-gita.md) treats it chapter-by-chapter; here is how it *reconciles* the paths.

- **Action as the gateway, not the obstacle** - The Gītā opens Arjuna's crisis as a clash between *action* (fight) and *renunciation* (withdraw). Kṛṣṇa dissolves it: renunciation means renouncing the **fruit**, not the deed (Gītā 2.47–51, ch. 3). Karma-yoga is the entry path because all embodied beings must act.
- **Karma purifies, jñāna culminates** - Disinterested action **purifies the mind** (*sattva-śuddhi*), making it fit for knowledge; thus karma-yoga is propaedeutic to jñāna-yoga (Gītā 4.38, *"na hi jñānena sadṛśaṃ pavitram iha vidyate"* — "nothing in the world purifies like knowledge"; 5.6). Action and knowledge are not rivals but a sequence.
- **Devotion as the integrator and supreme disclosure** - Chapters 7–12 turn to bhakti; the cosmic theophany of ch. 11 (*Viśvarūpa-darśana*) and ch. 12 (*Bhakti Yoga*) present loving surrender as the easiest and most intimate path, culminating in the *carama-śloka* (18.66). Many read the Gītā's *final* word as privileging bhakti while *containing* karma and jñāna within it.
- **All three converge** - The three are framed as complementary routes for different temperaments and stages, all reaching the same *mokṣa* — distinct but mutually reinforcing, not mutually exclusive ([Three Yogas — Wikipedia]).
  - *Variant (which path "wins"):* the Gītā's own emphasis is **disputed by design** — different chapters foreground different paths, and the *commentarial* tradition splits sharply (see §5). The text supports karma-primary, jñāna-primary, *and* bhakti-primary readings, which is precisely why all three schools claim it. [scholarly] [disputed]
  - *Variant (sthitaprajña ideal):* the Gītā's portrait of the *sthitaprajña* — the "person of steady wisdom" (2.54–72) — fuses all three: acting without attachment (karma), unshaken in knowledge (jñāna), and devoted to the Lord (bhakti). The integrated sage, not the specialist, is the goal.

## 4. Vivekananda's "Four Yogas" — the Modern Frame

- **The fourfold temperament scheme** - Swami Vivekananda (1890s, esp. the lectures collected as *Karma-Yoga*, *Bhakti-Yoga*, *Jñāna-Yoga*, *Rāja-Yoga*) popularized the now-ubiquitous **four paths for four temperaments**: karma for the *active*, bhakti for the *emotional*, jñāna for the *rational/philosophical*, rāja for the *mystical/meditative* — the four faculties of will, feeling, intellect, and psyche ([Vivekananda's four yogas]). [scholarly]
- **Acknowledged as modern systematization** - This neat 4×4 mapping is a **19th-century synthesis**, not an ancient doctrine; the classical sources give the *trimārga* and treat the paths as overlapping, not as a tidy personality test ([Three Yogas — Wikipedia]). [scholarly]
- **Universalist gloss** - Vivekananda (following Ramakrishna) presented the four as *equally valid* and ultimately convergent — "as many faiths, so many paths" — feeding the modern "all paths lead to the same summit" framing of Hinduism. [scholarly]

## 5. Schools That Privilege Each Path

The same Gītā and Brahma-Sūtras are read very differently by the great *ācāryas*; their commentaries (*bhāṣyas*) are essentially arguments over which mārga is the *direct* means (*sākṣāt-sādhana*) to liberation. See [../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/README.md](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/README.md).

- **Jñāna privileged — Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara, 8th c.)** - For Śaṅkara, **knowledge alone** (*jñāna*, *vidyā*) is the direct and sufficient means to *mokṣa*; action and ritual can only purify the mind and *qualify* one for knowledge, never produce liberation. Reasoning: liberation is *eternal*, but anything *produced* by action is perishable, so a finite act cannot yield an eternal result ([Advaita path to liberation]). Bhakti is accepted as a preparatory aid (*saguṇa* worship), subordinate to *nirguṇa* realization.
  - **The jñāna-karma-samuccaya debate** - Śaṅkara's signature polemic is the *rejection* of *jñāna-karma-samuccaya* — the doctrine that **knowledge and ritual action combined** are needed for liberation. Knowledge and action are opposed in nature (one reveals what *is*, the other *produces*), so they cannot cooperate as the means to the same end. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (opponents):* **Bhāskara** (Bhedābheda, ~9th c.), a near-contemporary, *upheld* jñāna-karma-samuccaya — acts and knowledge as mutually reinforcing, not exclusive ([Bhedābheda — Britannica]). **Maṇḍana Miśra** (*Brahma-siddhi*) defended the combined-means / *prasaṃkhyāna* (repeated meditative affirmation) view that Śaṅkara opposed. **Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā** (Kumārila, Prabhākara) held ritual action paramount outright. [scholarly] [disputed]

- **Bhakti privileged — Viśiṣṭādvaita (Rāmānuja, 11th–12th c.)** - Rāmānuja reads the Gītā's master-theme as **bhakti-yoga** as the direct means to the Supreme; karma-yoga and jñāna-yoga are *preparatory rungs* that mature into devotion. Liberation is the eternal, loving, conscious service and beatific vision of the personal Brahman (Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa), never the impersonal merger of Advaita ([Ramanuja — IEP]).
  - **Prapatti / śaraṇāgati** - Total self-surrender to God as an alternative (or completion) of bhakti, open to all regardless of qualification; the basis of devotion in the **Śrī (Sri Vaiṣṇava) Sampradāya** ([Sharanagati — Wikipedia]).
  - *Variant (cat vs monkey — the post-Rāmānuja split):* the Śrī Vaiṣṇava tradition divided over **grace vs effort**. The **Vaḍakalai** ("northern") school = *markaṭa-nyāya*, the **monkey** principle: the baby monkey must *cling* to its mother, so the soul must make positive effort (prapatti as a deliberate act) to win grace. The **Teṉkalai** ("southern") school = *mārjāra-nyāya*, the **cat** principle: the kitten is carried *passively* in the mother's mouth, so grace (*kṛpā*) is spontaneous and unconditional and the soul need do nothing but not-reject it ([Vadakalai and Tenkalai]). [scholarly]

- **Bhakti privileged (dualist) — Dvaita Vedānta (Madhva, 13th c.)** - Madhva makes *bhakti* essential by *metaphysics*: ātman and Brahman are eternally **distinct** (*tattva-vāda*, fivefold difference), so the soul can never merge into God — it can only *worship* the supreme, independent Viṣṇu. Devotion plus correct knowledge of the difference is the path; identity-doctrines (Advaita) are rejected precisely because they would undermine Viṣṇu's supremacy ([Madhva — IEP]).
  - *Variant (taratamya):* Madhva uniquely teaches a **gradation of souls** (*jīva-traividhya*: eternally liberation-bound, ever-transmigrating, and damned) — liberation is not universally available, a sharp departure from the other Vedāntas. [scholarly] [disputed]

- **Karma / ritual privileged — Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā** - The ritual-exegetical school holds that correct performance of Vedic *karma* (yajña) is the operative power; *dharma* is defined as what the injunctions (*codanā*) enjoin. The most action-centric of the orthodox schools, though its aim is classically *abhyudaya* (prosperity/heaven) more than *mokṣa*. See [../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md](../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md). [scholarly]

- **Synthesis schools** - **Caitanya / Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism** (16th c.) makes *prema-bhakti* (ecstatic love) the highest of all goals, *above* even liberation. **Bhakti movement** poet-saints across India (Mīrābāī, Tukārām, Kabīr, the Āḻvārs and Nāyaṉārs) carried devotion to the masses regardless of caste, effectively democratizing the bhakti-mārga. See [../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/bhakti-movement-and-saints.md](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/README.md) [open-ended roster — saint lists vary widely by region and sect].

## 6. Cross-Cutting Disputes (quick reference)

- **Are the paths a hierarchy or a menu?** - "Menu by temperament" (Vivekananda, modern Hinduism) vs "hierarchy/sequence" (Rāmānuja: karma→jñāna→bhakti) vs "one direct means, rest preparatory" (Śaṅkara: jñāna alone). [disputed] [scholarly]
- **Can means be combined?** - *Samuccaya* (combination) accepted by Bhāskara, Maṇḍana, much of bhakti-Vedānta; rejected by Śaṅkara for jñāna+karma. [disputed]
- **Effort vs grace** - Self-effort (*sva-prayatna*, jñāna and Vaḍakalai prapatti) vs unconditional grace (Teṉkalai *mārjāra-nyāya*, Gauḍīya *kṛpā*). [disputed]
- **Liberation now or later?** - *Jīvanmukti* (Advaita, liberated-while-living) vs post-mortem service/vision (Viśiṣṭādvaita, Dvaita). [scholarly]
