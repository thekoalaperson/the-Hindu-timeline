# Papa, Punya and Prayaschitta — sin, merit and expiation

> Cross-cutting doctrine of the moral economy of Sanātana Dharma: the accounting of *pāpa* (demerit) and *puṇya* (merit) that fills the [karma–saṃsāra machinery](purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md), and the *prāyaścitta* (expiation) system by which incurred sin is discharged before it ripens into hell.
> Parent: [Concepts & Dharma](README.md)

**Pāpa** ("evil, demerit, sin") and **puṇya** ("merit, virtue, the good") are the two moral substances that intentional action deposits in the karmic account. They are not mere ethical labels but *quasi-physical residues* (adṛṣṭa, apūrva) that must be *exhausted by experience* — puṇya ripening into pleasure and heavenly (svarga) reward, pāpa into suffering and hell (naraka). **Prāyaścitta** ("that which sets the mind aright," from *pra-√i* + *citta*) is the ritual-penitential technology of the Dharmaśāstra for *destroying* accumulated pāpa in this life, so it does not have to be worked off through rebirth or in the [narakas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/narakas-and-afterlife.md). The locus classicus is **Manusmṛti, Adhyāya 11** (the entire chapter on sin and expiation), paralleled by **Yājñavalkya-smṛti III** (Prāyaścitta-adhyāya), the Prāyaścitta sections of the Dharmasūtras, and the medieval digests (*Prāyaścitta-viveka*, *Prāyaścittendu*). See [agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md](../90-literature-corpus/agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md).

## I. Pāpa and Puṇya — the two ledgers

- **The Vedic root — ṛta, ṛṇa and enas** — the oldest layer frames sin not as pāpa but as **énas** (guilt/transgression) against the cosmic order (**ṛta**), a *debt* (**ṛṇa**) owed above all to **Varuṇa**, the sky-god who binds transgressors with his noose (*pāśa*). Vedic hymns beg release from énas incurred knowingly or unknowingly. See [rta-and-cosmic-order.md](rta-and-cosmic-order.md). (Ṛgveda 7.86–89, to Varuṇa)
- **Pāpa as substance, not just wrong** — in classical Hindu thought pāpa/puṇya are treated as *dravya-like* accretions that adhere to the jīva and *must* fruit; morality is a physics. Puṇya buys svarga and good rebirth; pāpa buys naraka and degraded rebirth. Both, crucially, are **binding** — puṇya is a "golden shackle," pāpa an "iron shackle" — and only their joint transcendence yields [mokṣa](purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md).
- **Sources of puṇya** — dāna (charity), yajña (sacrifice), tapas (austerity), vrata (vows), tīrtha-yātrā (pilgrimage), sevā, ahiṃsā, satya, and pativratā/dharma-fidelity. Merit is transferable and storable (it can be gifted, e.g. to ancestors, or "spent" as svarga-residence).
- **The gradation of sin** — the Dharmaśāstra ranks transgressions on a descending scale: **mahāpātaka** (mortal/grievous sin) → **anupātaka** (near-mortal) → **upapātaka** (secondary sin) → **jāti-bhraṃśakara** (caste-degrading) → **saṃkarīkaraṇa, apātrīkaraṇa, malāvaha** (progressively minor defilements). (Manusmṛti 11.55–71)

## II. The Five Mahāpātakas — the great sins

The **pañca-mahāpātaka** are the gravest offences, each nominally punished by loss of caste (*patana*, "falling") and a hell-term, requiring the heaviest expiation. (**Manusmṛti 11.55**; cf. 9.235)

- **Brahmahatyā** — killing a **brāhmaṇa**; the archetypal and gravest sin, the standard against which all others are weighed. (Manu 11.55)
- **Surāpāna** — drinking **surā** (intoxicating liquor), specifically forbidden to the twice-born; a defilement of the very body that bears the Veda. (Manu 11.55)
- **Steya** (**suvarṇa-steya**) — **theft of gold**, especially a brāhmaṇa's gold; the paradigm case of theft as mortal sin. (Manu 11.55)
- **Guru-talpa-gamana** — violating the bed of the **guru** (the teacher's/elder's wife); incest-adjacent betrayal of the preceptor. (Manu 11.55)
- **Mahāpātaki-saṃsarga** — **prolonged association** with one who has committed any of the above four; the sin is contagious, and the companion "falls" with the fallen. (Manu 11.55)
  - *Note:* Manu 11.56–59 appends **anupātakas** ("almost-mortal" sins) rated equal in gravity — e.g. forging (false) coinage, slandering the great, betraying a friend, killing a woman/śūdra/vaiśya/kṣatriya — and treats certain acts (killing a cow, learning forbidden lore) as their equivalents.

### The prescribed austerities for the mahāpātakas

- **Brahmahatyā** — a **twelve-year** *mahāvrata*: living in a forest hut on alms, carrying a staff and the **skull of the slain** (or a skull-topped staff, *kāpālika* banner) and the foot of a bed, publicly proclaiming the deed, eating little, bathing thrice daily. Alternatively, dying for a brāhmaṇa in battle, or offering one's body three times into fire. (Manu 11.73–90)
- **Surāpāna** — drinking the liquor **boiling-hot** (or hot water, milk, ghee, or cow's urine) until death; or the twelve-year vow. (Manu 11.91–92)
- **Suvarṇa-steya** — approaching the king with a **club (musala)** and confessing; the king strikes the thief once — whether he dies or is spared, he is purified. (Manu 11.100–101)
- **Guru-talpa** — self-mutilation and death: embracing a red-hot iron image of a woman, or severing one's own organ and walking southward to death; or confession and the mahāvrata. (Manu 11.104–107)

## III. Upapātakas — the secondary sins

The **upapātakas** are a long, open-ended catalogue of grave-but-not-mortal offences (Manu 11.60–67 lists dozens); they degrade but are more readily expiated.

- **Representative upapātakas** — **govadha** (killing a cow), performing sacrifice for the unworthy, adultery (other than guru-talpa), selling oneself, **abandoning parents, guru, or the sacred fires**, giving up Vedic recitation, neglecting a son, usury, cutting green trees for firewood, killing insects/birds, eating forbidden food, atheism/reviling the Veda, subsisting by a wife's earnings, and many more. (Manusmṛti 11.60–67)
- **Caste-degrading and lesser classes** — below the upapātakas: **jāti-bhraṃśakara** (acts that expel from caste, e.g. injuring a brāhmaṇa, smelling forbidden things), **saṃkarīkaraṇa** (mixing-causing), **apātrīkaraṇa** (rendering unfit for gifts), and **malāvaha** ("defilement-bearing," minor). Each has its own scaled penance. (Manu 11.68–71)
- **The "signs of past sin" doctrine** — where sin is *not* expiated, its residue surfaces in the next birth as **disease, deformity, or degraded status**: the brahmin-killer becomes consumptive, the liquor-drinker gets black teeth, the gold-thief diseased nails, the guru-bed violator a skin disease, and so on — the Dharmaśāstra's most explicit weld of prāyaścitta to karma. (Manusmṛti 11.49–53) See [tapas-boons-and-curses.md](tapas-boons-and-curses.md).

## IV. The vows of expiation — krīcchra, cāndrāyaṇa, pañcagavya

The Dharmaśāstra prescribes a graded toolkit of **penitential fasts and purifications**, calibrated to the sin's gravity. The two backbone regimens are the **krīcchra** (a fixed twelve-day austerity) and the **cāndrāyaṇa** (a lunar fast).

- **Prājāpatya krīcchra** — the standard "arduous" penance of **twelve days**: eat only in the morning for three days, only in the evening for three days, then three days eating only food received **unsolicited**, then a total **fast** for three days. (Manusmṛti 11.212)
- **Atikrīcchra** — "extra-arduous": the same four triads, but eating only **one mouthful** per meal in the first three phases, then fasting. (Manu 11.213–214)
- **Krīcchrātikrīcchra** — the severest of the series: subsisting on **water alone** for twelve days. (Manu 11.214)
- **Sāntapana krīcchra** — the "heating" penance of consuming for one day the **pañcagavya** plus kuśa-grass water; the "hot" variant (**tapta-krīcchra**) takes hot water, hot milk, hot ghee, and inhaled hot air on successive days. (Manu 11.213)
- **Parāka** — a **twelve-day total fast** (no food at all), the extreme austerity prescribed for the heaviest reductions. (Yājñavalkya-smṛti III; Dharmaśāstra digests)
- **Cāndrāyaṇa** — the **"lunar course"**, the signature vow whose food-intake **waxes and wanes with the moon**. In the standard form the penitent eats a number of mouthfuls (*grāsa*) equal to the moon's digits: decreasing by one mouthful each day of the **dark fortnight** (from a starting quota) down to a **total fast on new-moon (amāvāsyā)**, then increasing by one each day of the **bright fortnight** to the full-moon peak. (Manusmṛti 11.217–222)
  - *Variant (yava-madhyama, "barley-middle"):* begins on new-moon with a fast, swells to the full-moon, and wanes again — full in the middle, thin at the ends, like a barley-grain. (Manu 11.220)
  - *Variant (pipīlikā-madhyama, "ant-middle"):* begins on full-moon and wanes to the new-moon fast, then waxes — thin in the middle like an ant's waist. (Manu 11.220–221)
  - *Variant (yati- / śiśu-cāndrāyaṇa):* later Smṛtis add fixed-ration forms — e.g. eating a steady **eight mouthfuls** daily throughout the month (the ascetic's cāndrāyaṇa). [late text] (Yājñavalkya-smṛti III)
- **Pañcagavya** (**brahma-kūrca**) — the **five products of the cow** — milk (kṣīra), curd (dadhi), ghee (ghṛta), urine (gomūtra), and dung (gomaya) — mixed and consumed as an internal purificatory draught, integral to sāntapana and to routine re-admission after defilement. The compound drunk with kuśa-water is the **brahma-kūrca**. (Manu 11.212–213; Parāśara-smṛti)
- **Vedic recitation and japa as expiation** — Manu also credits **repetition of sacred texts** (the Gāyatrī, Aghamarṣaṇa hymn, Praṇava, the Vedic Saṃhitās), **prāṇāyāma**, tīrtha-snāna, and dāna as sin-effacing — a bridge from ritual austerity toward the later mantra/nāma path. (Manusmṛti 11.226–266)

## V. Prāyaścitta and the karma doctrine — how they interlock

- **The mechanism** — prāyaścitta is held to **actually destroy** the pāpa-residue (not merely signal contrition): the completed penance *burns off* the demerit so that its otherwise-inevitable fruit (naraka, degraded rebirth) does not manifest. Sin left unexpiated *must* fruit; expiated, it is annulled. (Manu 11.53–54)
- **Two functions, sometimes in tension** — scholars distinguish (i) prāyaścitta as **social re-integration** (restoring the outcaste to ritual/caste fellowship, a this-worldly purification enforced by the *pariṣad* of learned brāhmaṇas who prescribe the penance) from (ii) prāyaścitta as **karmic annulment** (deleting the invisible demerit). The Dharmaśāstra runs both together. [scholarly]
- **Prārabdha resists** — a hard limit from the karma side: **prārabdha karma** (the portion already "begun to fruit," shaping the present body and lifespan) is generally held *unavoidable* even by expiation or by knowledge; prāyaścitta and even mokṣa-realization annul the *storehouse* (sañcita) and *future* (āgāmi) sin, but the ripened prārabdha must be exhausted by experience. See [purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md](purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md).
- **Intentional vs. unintentional** — the Śāstra doubles the penance (or bars expiation) for **deliberate** (*kāma-kāra*) sin versus **inadvertent** (*ajñāna*) sin; for the very gravest deliberate mahāpātaka, some authorities hold no earthly expiation suffices short of death, the sin being worked out only through the narakas. (Manu 11.45–46)
- **The pariṣad's authority** — who *prescribes* the penance matters: a properly constituted assembly (ideally ten, or minimally three, Veda-knowing brāhmaṇas) determines the applicable prāyaścitta; this juridical dimension makes expiation a matter of dharma-court as much as of private conscience. (Manu 12.108–113)

## VI. Mythic prototypes of expiation

The gods themselves incur and expiate the mahāpātakas — the divine casebook that grounds the human system.

- **Indra's Brahmahatyā** — by slaying **Vṛtra / Viśvarūpa (Triśiras)**, sons of Tvaṣṭṛ and *brāhmaṇas*, Indra contracts the gravest sin; he **hides in a lotus-fibre** in Lake Mānasa, leaving Svarga kingless. He is purified by **distributing the Brahmahatyā into four vessels** — earth (which takes it as saline cracks), water (as foam), trees (as sap/gum), and women (as menstruation) — each granted a boon in exchange, and by the **Aśvamedha**. The paradigm of *dividing and displacing* an unbearable sin. (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6; Mahābhārata, Udyoga/Śānti Parva) See [indra.md](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/indra.md).
- **Śiva Kapālin** — for severing **Brahmā's fifth head**, Śiva incurs Brahmahatyā; the **Brahmā-kapāla** (skull) clings to his hand as *kāpālika* penance until he reaches **Kapālamocana** (at Vārāṇasī), where it falls — the mythic charter of the twelve-year skull-bearing mahāvrata. (Purāṇic; Śiva/Skanda Purāṇa)
- **Rāma's Aśvamedha** — after slaying **Rāvaṇa**, a brāhmaṇa by birth (grandson of the sage Pulastya), Rāma performs expiatory sacrifice; the horse-sacrifice recurs across the epics as the royal engine of great-sin expiation. (Rāmāyaṇa; Padma Purāṇa)
- **Tīrtha as expiation-engine** — sacred fords (Prayāga, Kāśī, Kurukṣetra, Gayā, Puṣkara) are extolled as places where even mahāpātakas dissolve by bathing/darśana — the geographical counterpart of the penitential vow. See [tirtha-and-tirtha-yatra.md](tirtha-and-tirtha-yatra.md).

## VII. The bhakti supersession — nāma over prāyaścitta

The great **Bhāgavata** intervention is the claim that **nāma-smaraṇa** (remembrance/utterance of God's name) and **bhakti** *supersede* the whole Dharmaśāstra expiation-machine — the doctrinal turn on which the **Ajāmila** story pivots.

- **Parīkṣit's question and Śuka's first answer** — asked how a soul may escape the narakas, **Śukadeva** first gives the orthodox reply: **prāyaścitta**, penances proportioned to the sin, as taught by Manu. (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.1.1–11) See [ajamila.md](../04-deep-dives/ajamila.md).
- **The critique of ritual atonement** — Śuka then judges prāyaścitta **incomplete**: like an **elephant that bathes and again throws dust over itself**, the penitent purified by *karma-kāṇḍa* sins anew, because atonement removes the *act* but not the *sinful propensity* (the impression in the heart). (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.1.9–18)
- **The superior expiation — devotion** — only **remembrance of Viṣṇu / bhakti** burns the very **root** of sin, as fire reduces fuel to ash or the sun dispels darkness. (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.1.15–19)
- **The Ajāmila proof** — the fallen brāhmaṇa **Ajāmila**, a lifelong sinner, cries "**Nārāyaṇa**!" at death (calling his infant son, *without* devotional intent); the **Viṣṇudūtas** wrest him from the **Yamadūtas'** noose, arguing that even a **semblance of the Name** (*nāmābhāsa*), uttered in ignorance, jest, or need, annuls sin more completely than any prāyaścitta. This is the scriptural charter of the Kali-yuga consolation that *nāma-saṅkīrtana* alone suffices. (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.1–3) See [ajamila.md](../04-deep-dives/ajamila.md).
- **The doctrinal stakes** — the Dharmaśāstra keeps prāyaścitta as *obligatory* and jurisdictionally enforced; the Bhāgavata/bhakti schools relativize it as a lower, provisional remedy for those not yet surrendered to God. Both positions are preserved, not reconciled — the corpus's standard treatment of a live disagreement. [disputed across traditions]

## VIII. The two clocks

- **Scriptural / traditional view** — prāyaścitta is *sanātana*, taught by Manu (the primal law-giver of this manvantara) and exemplified by the gods (Indra, Śiva) in cyclic mytho-time; the mahāpātakas and their remedies are eternal dharma, valid across yugas though *harder* to fulfil as Kali deepens (hence the bhakti shortcut).
- **Academic / historical view** — [scholarly] the énas/ṛṇa complex is **Vedic** (Ṛgvedic Varuṇa hymns, ~1500–1000 BCE); systematic prāyaścitta appears in the **Dharmasūtras** (Gautama, Baudhāyana, Āpastamba, ~600–300 BCE) and is elaborated in **Manusmṛti 11** (~200 BCE–200 CE) and **Yājñavalkya-smṛti** (~300–500 CE); the nāma-supersession of the Ajāmila episode is a **Bhāgavata / bhakti** development [scholarly] usually dated **c. 900–1000 CE**. Tradition reads one eternal institution; scholarship reads a layered evolution from Vedic guilt-before-Varuṇa to Purāṇic devotional grace.
