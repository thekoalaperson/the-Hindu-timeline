# Vastu Shastra — Sacred Architecture & the Vastu-Puruṣa-Maṇḍala

> Cross-cutting doctrine within [05-concepts-and-dharma](./README.md) of *The Hindu Timeline*. The canonical Hindu **science of building** (*vāstu-śāstra*, the **Sthāpatya-veda** *upaveda*) — the myth of the pinned cosmic being (*Vāstu-puruṣa*), the **gridded diagram** (*vāstu-puruṣa-maṇḍala*) onto which 45 deities are fixed, the directional (*dik*) principles of orientation, and the application to temple, house and town. **Vāstu-śāstra** ("the science of the dwelling-site," from *vāstu*, an inhabited plot or building, ← *vas*, "to dwell") is the Hindu discipline of sacred space: how to orient, proportion and lay out a structure so that it mirrors cosmic order (*ṛta*) and so the unseen powers of the directions are kept in harmony rather than offended. It is reckoned an **upaveda** — the **Sthāpatya-veda**, the "knowledge of building," attached to the Ṛgveda (see [Upavedas](../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md)) — and overlaps the broader **Śilpa-śāstras** (the crafts of sculpture, iconometry and town-planning). Its presiding myth and its master-diagram, the *vāstu-puruṣa-maṇḍala*, are the two things every later building rite returns to.

---

## 1. The Myth of the Vāstu-Puruṣa

- **The fallen cosmic being** — In the foundational myth a formless, ever-growing being is born (variously from a drop of Śiva's sweat, or unbidden from the cosmos) and swells until it threatens to blot out the worlds; it falls (or is felled) face-down to the earth. The gods rush upon it and **pin it down**, each holding a limb, fixing it permanently face-down (*adhomukha*) and head-to-the-northeast. (*Matsya Purāṇa*; *Bṛhat Saṃhitā* 52)
  - *Variant (Andhaka's blood — Matsya/late text):* the being arises from a drop of sweat that Śiva sheds while battling the asura **Andhaka**; the drop swells into a ravenous giant who, after devouring much, is overthrown and held down by the assembled devas. [late text]
  - *Variant (the obstructing growth):* in the simplest form it is an obstacle (*vāstu* = "the thing that blocks") born of the cosmic process, suppressed so that ordered, inhabitable space can exist at all.
- **The boon of indwelling worship** — Pinned and immortal, the being begs not to be left forgotten beneath the earth. **Brahmā** grants the boon: he shall be the **Vāstu-puruṣa**, lord of every building-site, and whoever raises a structure must honor him and the gods seated upon him; neglect of him brings *vāstu-doṣa* (architectural defect, misfortune). (*Matsya Purāṇa*; *Bṛhat Saṃhitā*)
- **Body as cosmogram** — His prone body *is* the plot. The myth converts a piece of ground into a living, divinely-occupied organism whose limbs must not be violated (hence rules against where to dig wells, cut doors, place hearths) — the theological charter for every rule that follows.

## 2. The Vāstu-Puruṣa-Maṇḍala — the Gridded Diagram

- **The maṇḍala** — The site is squared and subdivided into a grid of cells (*pada*), the pinned Vāstu-puruṣa drawn diagonally across it (head in the northeast, **Īśāna**; feet in the southwest, **Nairṛti**). Onto the grid are seated the **presiding deities** who hold him down, each governing the cell(s) over its limb. The square (not the circle) is the privileged Hindu cosmic figure — the symbol of the ordered, earth-bound, completed cosmos.
- **The 64-pada grid (8 × 8, *Maṇḍūka / Caṇḍita*)** — Sixty-four cells; the four central cells form the **Brahmasthāna**. The 8×8 form is prescribed especially for **temples** and shrines. (*Bṛhat Saṃhitā* 52; *Mānasāra*; *Mayamata*)
- **The 81-pada grid (9 × 9, *Parama-śāyika*)** — Eighty-one cells; the central **nine** cells (3×3) form the Brahmasthāna. The 9×9 form is prescribed especially for **dwellings** and secular construction. (*Mayamata*; *Mānasāra*; *Samarāṅgaṇa-sūtradhāra*)
  - *Variant (the 32 grids):* the *śāstras* enumerate a graded series of maṇḍalas from 1×1 up to 32×32 (1,024 cells) — *Sakala, Pecaka, Pīṭha, Mahāpīṭha … Indrakānta* — each suited to a different scale or purpose; the 64- and 81-pada are simply the two in standard use. [scholarly]
- **The Brahmasthāna — the sacred void** — The central zone belongs to **Brahmā** and is kept **open and unbuilt** — a courtyard, light-well, or (in a temple) the void directly above/around the *garbhagṛha*. It is the navel of the diagram, the still center through which cosmic energy is held to enter; building over it is a cardinal *vāstu-doṣa*.

## 3. The 45 Devatās on the Grid

- **The presiding deities** — Forty-five gods hold the Vāstu-puruṣa down: **32 "outer"** deities ringing the perimeter (often correlated with the 28 *nakṣatras* plus directional regents) and **13 "inner"** deities toward the center, with **Brahmā** enthroned at the core of the Brahmasthāna. They are invoked and worshipped in the *vāstu-pūjā* / *vāstu-śānti* rite before construction and at house-entry. (*Bṛhat Saṃhitā* 52; *Matsya Purāṇa*)
- **The eight Dik-pālas — guardians of the directions** — Anchoring the cardinal and intermediate points are the eight **Lokapālas / Dikpālas**, whose qualities set the "energetic" rules of orientation:
  - **Indra** — East (*pūrva*), the rising sun, sovereignty.
  - **Agni** — Southeast (*āgneya*), fire — the canonical zone of the **kitchen / hearth**.
  - **Yama** — South (*dakṣiṇa*), death, restraint.
  - **Nirṛti** — Southwest (*nairṛti*), dissolution — the **heaviest** quarter, given to the master bedroom / weight of the house.
  - **Varuṇa** — West (*paścima*), the waters.
  - **Vāyu** — Northwest (*vāyavya*), wind, movement.
  - **Kubera / Soma** — North (*uttara*), wealth.
  - **Īśāna (Śiva)** — Northeast (*īśānya*), the most **auspicious, light, sacred** quarter — given to the **water-source, the puja-room, and the deity's head**.

## 4. Principles of Orientation (Dik-Vāstu)

- **The northeast–southwest axis** — Because the Vāstu-puruṣa lies head (Īśāna, NE) to feet (Nairṛti, SW), the diagram is "lightest" in the northeast and "heaviest" in the southwest. The governing rule: keep the NE **open, low, light, watery, sacred**; keep the SW **closed, high, heavy, solid**. Slope of land and flow of water should run toward the NE/N/E. [scholarly]
- **Function by quarter** — Each room is assigned to the quarter whose deity suits it: hearth to fiery **Āgneya** (SE), sleep/mass to **Nairṛti** (SW), water and worship to **Īśāna** (NE), storage of grain/wealth to **Kubera** (N), and the central court left to Brahmā.
- **Cardinal alignment & the gnomon** — Buildings are oriented true to the cardinal directions, classically fixed by the **śaṅku** (gnomon) and the "fish" (*timi/matsya*) figure swept from its shadow at sunrise and sunset — the same instrument the *Śulba-sūtras* use to lay out a fire-altar square. The east-facing or north-facing entrance is generally favored. (*Bṛhat Saṃhitā*; *Mānasāra*)
- **Āya — auspicious measure** — Dimensions are not free: the building's perimeter, fixed in cubits (*hasta*) and the *aṅgula* module, must yield an auspicious remainder under the *āya-vyaya* (gain/loss) and other reckonings keyed to the owner's *nakṣatra* — a numerological proportioning that binds measure to omen. (*Mayamata*; *Mānasāra*)

## 5. Application — Temple, House, Town

- **Temple architecture** — The *vāstu-puruṣa-maṇḍala* (usually 8×8) is the generative ground-plan of the Hindu temple: the **garbhagṛha** ("womb-house," the dark sanctum) sits over the Brahmasthāna, the deity's image consecrated at the still center (the *mūrti* for which the whole edifice exists — see [Mantra, Japa & Mūrti-Pūjā](mantra-japa-and-murti-puja.md)); the **śikhara / vimāna** rises above it as a cosmic mountain (Meru). The maṇḍala fixes proportion, orientation, and the placement of subsidiary shrines, *maṇḍapas* and the *dhvaja*. The same śāstras govern the temples cataloged in [Sthala-Purāṇas & Major Temples](../06-sacred-geography/sthala-puranas-major-temples.md). (*Mayamata*; *Mānasāra*; *Agni Purāṇa*)
  - *Variant (Nāgara / Drāviḍa / Vesara):* the three regional temple "styles" — North-Indian *Nāgara* (curvilinear śikhara), South-Indian *Drāviḍa* (stepped pyramidal vimāna, towered gopuras), and the hybrid *Vesara* of the Deccan — all derive from the same maṇḍala but elaborate the elevation differently. [scholarly]
- **Domestic architecture** — The 9×9 grid governs the house: the open central courtyard (*brahmasthāna* → the Tamil/Kerala *naḍumiṟṟam*, the *aṅgaṇa*), the placement of door (*dvāra*), hearth, well, granary and bedroom by quarter, and the *vāstu-śānti* rite before occupancy. (*Mayamata*; *Samarāṅgaṇa-sūtradhāra*)
- **Town-planning (Nagara-vinyāsa)** — The śāstras prescribe whole **settlement plans** keyed to grids and named after their street-patterns — *Daṇḍaka, Sarvatobhadra, Nandyāvarta, Padmaka, Svastika, Prastara, Karmuka, Caturmukha* — fixing the king's palace, temples, markets, and the four-caste wards by direction. The grid-city is the maṇḍala writ large. (*Mānasāra*; *Mayamata*; *Bṛhat Saṃhitā*)

## 6. The Divine Architects & the Textual Tradition

- **Viśvakarmā and Maya** — The craft is mythically authored by two architect-figures: **Viśvakarmā**, all-maker and architect of the devas, and **Maya Dānava**, architect of the asuras (builder of the Pāṇḍavas' illusion-hall at Indraprastha) — the two patron-poles of the tradition, to whom the two great textual streams are retrospectively attributed. See [Viśvakarmā & Tvaṣṭṛ](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishvakarma-and-tvashtar.md). [folk]
- **Mayamata** — A major South-Indian (Drāviḍa) *vāstu/śilpa* treatise ascribed to **Maya**, comprehensive on site-selection, the maṇḍala, measure, temple and house construction, and iconography. (*Mayamata*) [scholarly]
- **Mānasāra** — The most encyclopedic of the surviving *śilpa-śāstras*, the standard reference on the 32 maṇḍalas, the *āya* reckonings, temple typology, and iconometry. (*Mānasāra*) [scholarly]
- **Samarāṅgaṇa-sūtradhāra** — The great North-Indian (Nāgara) treatise attributed to the **Paramāra king Bhoja of Dhārā** (11th c.), covering town-planning, palace and temple architecture, the maṇḍala, and even *yantra* (mechanical) devices. (*Samarāṅgaṇa-sūtradhāra*) [scholarly]
- **The Śilpa-śāstras** — The wider corpus of craft-manuals (*Aparājitapṛcchā*, *Viśvakarma-prakāśa*, *Kāśyapa-śilpa*, the *Śilparatna*, regional *vāstu* texts) governing sculpture, iconometry (*tālamāna*) and building together. [scholarly]
- **The Purāṇic vāstu chapters** — The **Matsya Purāṇa** (chs. on the Vāstu-puruṣa myth, the 45 devatās, and building rules), the **Agni Purāṇa** (temple architecture and image-making), and the **Garuḍa Purāṇa** (house-building and *vāstu* prescriptions) embed the science in the Purāṇic canon. (*Matsya*, *Agni*, *Garuḍa Purāṇas*)
- **Bṛhat Saṃhitā of Varāhamihira** — The 6th-century encyclopedist **Varāhamihira** gives, in his *Bṛhat Saṃhitā* (notably the *Vāstu-vidyā* and *Prāsāda-lakṣaṇa* chapters, esp. ch. 52–56), one of the earliest datable, systematic accounts of the Vāstu-puruṣa, the maṇḍala, the 45 deities, and temple proportion — a key anchor for dating the tradition. (*Bṛhat Saṃhitā*) [scholarly]

---

> **Two clocks:** the Vāstu-puruṣa myth and the boon of Brahmā belong to *scriptural* time (cross-yuga, sited in the cosmogony alongside Andhaka and the devas); the **datable textual tradition** is academic-clock — Varāhamihira's *Bṛhat Saṃhitā* (~6th c. CE), the *Mayamata*/*Mānasāra* (~mid-1st millennium CE, redacted later), and Bhoja's *Samarāṅgaṇa-sūtradhāra* (11th c. CE).
