# Mantra, Japa & Mūrti-Pūjā

> Cross-cutting doctrine within [05-concepts-and-dharma](./README.md) of *The Hindu Timeline*. The **theology of sacred sound** (mantra, *bīja*, the *praṇava* AUM, the Gāyatrī), the **discipline of repetition** (*japa* and the *mālā*), and **image-worship** (*mūrti*, *prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā*, the *upacāras*, *darśana*, *prasāda*) — the two great sensory technologies of Hindu practice: the ear and the eye. Back to the [master index](../README.md).
> Companion files: [The Three Mārgas](the-three-margas.md) (bhakti as the path this serves), [Yoga — Paths & Patañjali](yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md) (*japa* as *aṣṭāṅga* practice), [Festivals & Vratas](festivals-and-vratas.md) and [Regional Temple Festivals](regional-temple-festivals.md) (pūjā in calendar context), and the [Śruti corpus](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md) (where the mantras live).

Hindu practice rests on two complementary sacralizations of the senses: **sound** offered to and received by the ear (mantra, *japa*), and **form** offered to and received by the eye (*mūrti*, *darśana*). Both rest on a single theology: the formless absolute (*nirguṇa Brahman*) condescends into accessible *supports* — a vibration (*śabda*) or an image (*pratīka*) — through which the devotee meets the divine. This file is the *doctrinal-ritual* node cataloging how sound is held to work, how it is repeated, how an image is brought to life and served, and the recurring internal critiques that call image-worship a concession, a crutch, or an error.

## 1. The Theology of Sacred Sound (Mantra)

- **Mantra — the instrument of thought** - From *man* ("to think") + the instrumental suffix *-tra* ("instrument, tool"): literally "that which is an instrument of thought," or by traditional gloss *mananāt trāyate iti mantraḥ* — "that which protects (*trā*) the one who contemplates (*manana*) it." A sacred utterance — syllable, word, or verse — held to carry inherent power independent of, or beyond, its lexical meaning. The [Ṛgveda](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md) is itself a body of ~10,552 mantras across ten *maṇḍalas*; "mantra" first denotes simply a Vedic verse (as opposed to the prose *brāhmaṇa* portions).
  - *Variant (śabda-brahman):* in Mīmāṃsā and grammarian (Bhartṛhari) thought, sound itself is divine — *śabda-brahman*, the Word as the absolute. The Veda is *apauruṣeya* (authorless, eternal); its sounds are not human conventions but the eternal sonic fabric of reality, hence their efficacy. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Vedic vs Tantric):* **Vedic** mantras are metrical, meaning-bearing verses addressed to deities within *yajña*; **Tantric/Āgamic** mantras are often non-semantic *bīja* syllables whose power is purely sonic-vibrational and requires *dīkṣā* (initiation) to activate. The two streams overlap in later devotional practice. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (efficacy debate):* schools split on *why* mantras work — Mīmāṃsā ties efficacy to correct ritual performance (*adṛṣṭa*, unseen merit), Tantra to the deity *resident in* the sound, Vedānta to the mantra as a meditative *aid* whose real power is the realization it triggers.

- **Bīja-mantra — the seed syllable** - A monosyllabic "seed" (*bīja*) held to condense the entire essence (the *śakti*) of a deity or cosmic principle into one vibration; typically meaning-less in ordinary language but "fruitful" when sounded, like a seed containing the whole tree. Central to Tantra and *nyāsa* (ritual placement of sound on the body).
  - *Variant (the common bījas):* **Oṃ** (the praṇava, all-sound), **Hrīṃ** (Māyā / the Goddess, "Bhuvaneśvarī bīja"), **Śrīṃ** (Lakṣmī, abundance), **Klīṃ** (Kāma / Kṛṣṇa, attraction, the "kāma-bīja"), **Krīṃ** (Kālī), **Aiṃ** (Sarasvatī, speech/wisdom, the "vāgbhava-bīja"), **Duṃ** (Durgā), **Gaṃ** (Gaṇeśa), **Haṃ/Yaṃ/Raṃ/Vaṃ/Laṃ** (the five elements — ether/air/fire/water/earth — used in *bhūta-śuddhi*).
  - *Variant (derivation):* tradition derives each *bīja* from a deity's name (Durgā → Duṃ, Gaṇeśa → Gaṃ), though many are taught as revealed sounds without etymology.

- **Mahā-mantra & nāma-japa** - In bhakti streams the repeated **divine name** (*nāma*) is itself the supreme mantra — name and named are non-different (*nāma-nāmī-abheda*). The Gauḍīya **Hare Kṛṣṇa mahā-mantra** (Hare Kṛṣṇa / Hare Rāma, 16 names) and the *Rāma-nāma* and *Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya* (the Śaiva *pañcākṣara*, "five syllables") and *Oṃ Namo Nārāyaṇāya* (the Vaiṣṇava *aṣṭākṣara*, "eight syllables") are the great recited formulas. See [The Three Mārgas](the-three-margas.md) for *kīrtana*/*śravaṇa* within *navadhā bhakti*.

## 2. The Praṇava — AUM / OM

- **AUM as primordial sound** - The *praṇava* ("that which is sounded forth"), held to be the first vibration from which creation issues and the sonic body of *Brahman* itself — *the* mantra, source and seed of all others. The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad makes it the whole of reality: "*oṃ ity etad akṣaram idaṃ sarvam*" — "OM, this imperishable syllable, is all this" (Māṇḍūkya 1). The Gītā has Kṛṣṇa declare "*praṇavaḥ sarva-vedeṣu*" — "I am OM in all the Vedas" (Gītā 7.8), and "*oṃ ity ekākṣaraṃ brahma*" — OM, the one-syllabled Brahman (Gītā 8.13).
  - *Variant (the three+one mātrās):* AUM analyzed into **A** (waking, *jāgrat*, Virāj/Brahmā) + **U** (dream, *svapna*, Hiraṇyagarbha/Viṣṇu) + **M** (deep sleep, *suṣupti*, Īśvara/Śiva) + the silent **fourth** (*turīya*), the soundless ground after the syllable dies away — the actual locus of the absolute (Māṇḍūkya 8–12). [scholarly]
  - *Variant (cosmogonic):* in Purāṇic and Tantric framings the three letters map to the *trimūrti* (Brahmā-Viṣṇu-Śiva) and to creation-preservation-dissolution; the *anusvāra*/*bindu* (the dot) and *nāda* (the resonant tail) above the OM symbol denote the unmanifest. [late text]
  - *Variant (the written symbol):* the familiar ॐ glyph is a Devanāgarī ligature; the syllable predates and is independent of any one script — Tamil, Tibetan, and other traditions write it differently.

- **The Gāyatrī Mantra** - The most revered Vedic mantra, **Ṛgveda 3.62.10**, attributed to the seer **Viśvāmitra**, addressed to **Savitṛ** (the solar impeller); composed in the 24-syllable **gāyatrī** meter (three *pādas* of eight syllables), whence its name. Text (with the praṇava and the three *vyāhṛtis* prefixed in liturgical use):
  > *oṃ bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ / tat savitur vareṇyaṃ / bhargo devasya dhīmahi / dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt*
  — "OM. Earth, atmosphere, heaven. May we meditate on that excellent glory of the divine Savitṛ; may he impel our thoughts (*dhiyaḥ*)." It is a prayer not for boon but for the *quickening of the intellect*.
  - *Variant (the vyāhṛtis):* *bhūr bhuvaḥ svaḥ* — the three "utterances" naming the three worlds (earth / mid-space / heaven), prefixed in ritual but **not part of the original Ṛgvedic verse**; an expanded form adds *mahaḥ janaḥ tapaḥ satyam* for seven worlds. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (eligibility — disputed):* classically imparted only at the *upanayana* (sacred-thread *saṃskāra*, see [Varṇa, Āśrama & the Saṃskāras](varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md)) and traditionally restricted to "twice-born" (*dvija*) men, recited at *sandhyā* (dawn/midday/dusk). Reform movements (Vivekānanda, Arya Samaj, modern teachers) deliberately **universalized** it to all genders and castes. [disputed]
  - *Variant (Gāyatrī as Goddess):* personified as the goddess **Gāyatrī/Sāvitrī**, mother of the Vedas (*Veda-mātā*), consort of Brahmā — the mantra worshipped as a deity in her own right. [late text]
  - *Variant (deity-Gāyatrīs):* the *gāyatrī* meter spawned a genre — sect-specific 24-syllable invocations exist for Gaṇeśa, Śiva, Viṣṇu, Durgā, etc., each called a "Gāyatrī." [folk]

## 3. Japa — the Discipline of Repetition

- **Japa — recitative repetition** - The disciplined repetition of a mantra, the core devotional and meditative practice; the Gītā exalts it — "*yajñānāṃ japa-yajño 'smi*", "of sacrifices I am the sacrifice of repetition (japa)" (Gītā 10.25), naming *japa* the highest *yajña* because it requires no external apparatus and harms nothing.
  - *Variant (three grades of voice):* **vaikharī** (audible, spoken aloud), **upāṃśu** (whispered, lips moving but inaudible), and **mānasika / mānasa** (purely mental, soundless) — held in ascending order of merit, the mental being most powerful (often cited as 1 : 100 : 1000). [scholarly]
  - *Variant (likhita japa):* "written japa" — copying the mantra/name repeatedly by hand as a devotional discipline. [folk]
  - *Variant (ajapa-japa):* the "unrepeated repetition" — the spontaneous, breath-linked *haṃsa / so'ham* mantra said to recite itself with every inhalation-exhalation (~21,600 times/day), requiring no effort. [late text]

- **The mālā — the rosary** - The *japa-mālā*, a string of beads for counting repetitions; the standard count is **108** beads plus one larger **meru** (or *sumeru* / *bindu*) "guru bead" that is never crossed — on reaching it the practitioner reverses direction rather than stepping over the *guru*. Held in the right hand, told with the thumb, the index finger kept off the beads.
  - *Variant (why 108):* explanations proliferate — 27 *nakṣatras* × 4 *pādas*; 12 *rāśis* × 9 *grahas*; the 108 *Upaniṣads*; or numerological (1, 0, 8 = One, nothing, infinite). Open-ended; no single canonical reason. [folk]
  - *Variant (bead material by deity):* **rudrākṣa** seeds for Śiva/Śakti worship, **tulasī** wood for Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa worship, crystal (*sphaṭika*) for peace, sandalwood for general use — material matched to the *iṣṭa-devatā*. [folk]
  - *Variant (counts):* shorter wrist *mālās* of 27 (×4 = 108) or 54 beads; the *pūrṇa* count of a *puraścaraṇa* (intensive mantra observance) may run to hundreds of thousands or millions of repetitions.

- **Mantra-dīkṣā — initiation** - Many mantras (especially *bīja* and Tantric ones) are held to be inert unless received from a *guru* through formal **initiation** (*dīkṣā*, *abhiṣeka*); the *guru*-given mantra becomes the disciple's *iṣṭa-mantra*. Vedic mantras like Gāyatrī are transmitted at *saṃskāra*; *nāma*-mantras in many bhakti streams are explicitly **open to all without initiation** (a key egalitarian claim of the *sant* and Gauḍīya movements). [disputed]

## 4. Mūrti — the Image as Support

- **Mūrti / vigraha / arcā** - The consecrated image (literally "embodiment, form") of a deity, made to precise iconometric (*śilpa-śāstra* / *tālamāna*) and iconographic (*dhyāna-śloka*) specifications so that the form matches the deity's revealed appearance. Materials: stone (*śilā*), metal (the festival *utsava-mūrti* of bronze/*pañcaloha*), wood (as at [Jagannātha Purī](../06-sacred-geography/char-dham-and-chota-char-dham.md)), clay (impermanent festival images), or a *yantra*.
  - *Variant (mūrti is not the deity — pratīka):* the orthodox theological position is that the image is a **support** (*pratīka*, *pratimā*) or symbol, *not* the deity reduced to matter — a focusing-point for a formless or omnipresent God, "a telephone, not the person." Worship is of the deity *through* the form. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (arcāvatāra — the deity IS present):* Śrīvaiṣṇava theology counts the consecrated *arcā* (icon) as a genuine **descent** (*avatāra*) of Viṣṇu — the fifth mode of the Lord's presence (*para, vyūha, vibhava, antaryāmin, arcā*), fully and graciously self-limited into the image so the devotee may serve and see Him. Here the *mūrti* is no mere symbol. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (aniconic/natural forms):* not all worship is iconic — the Śiva **liṅga**, the Viṣṇu **śālagrāma** (fossil ammonite stone), the **bāṇa-liṅga** river-stones, the *yantra*, and natural objects (rivers, trees, the *tulasī* plant) are worshipped without anthropomorphic form. See [Artifacts, Symbols & Arts](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/README.md).
  - *Variant (svayambhū):* "self-manifest" images held to have appeared spontaneously (not human-carved) — deemed especially potent and sometimes requiring no *prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā*. [folk]

- **Prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā — the establishing of life** - The consecration rite (*prāṇa* "life-breath" + *pratiṣṭhā* "establishing") that transforms crafted matter into a living abode of the deity. Before it, the image is "merely stone or metal"; after it, the deity is held to be present and the *mūrti* must be served daily like a living person. Governed by the **Āgamas** and **Tantras** (Vaiṣṇava Pāñcarātra/Vaikhānasa, Śaiva Āgamas). Core movements: purification (*śuddhi*, *snapana*), *nyāsa* (placing the deity's *bīja*s on the image), **āvāhana** (invoking the deity to take residence), the breath-infusion proper, and **netra-unmīlana / cakṣur-unmīlana** — the "opening of the eyes," the climactic moment (often painting in the pupils) at which the deity is said to begin to *see*.
  - *Variant (avoiding the first gaze):* because the freshly-eye-opened deity's first glance is held to be overwhelmingly powerful, the artisan/priest often avoids meeting it directly — a mirror is shown, or the gaze is first directed at a sweet offering or a pot. [folk]
  - *Variant (āvāhana / visarjana for temporary images):* for festival clay images (Gaṇeśa, Durgā) the deity is invoked *for the duration* and then formally dismissed — **visarjana / udvāsana** — before immersion, so what is immersed is again "just clay." Permanent temple *mūrtis* are not dismissed. See [Festivals & Vratas](festivals-and-vratas.md). [scholarly]

## 5. Pūjā — the Service of the Image

- **Pūjā as hospitality** - Image-worship is structured as **welcoming an honored guest**: the deity is bathed, dressed, fed, garlanded, fanned, and entertained exactly as a royal visitor would be. This *atithi*-model is the logic behind the *upacāras* ("attendances, services").

- **Ṣoḍaśopacāra — the sixteen services** - The classic full pūjā of **sixteen** *upacāras* (offerings), each a hospitable act, performed with mantras. A standard sequence: (1) **āvāhana** (invocation), (2) **āsana** (offering a seat), (3) **pādya** (water for the feet), (4) **arghya** (water for the hands), (5) **ācamana** (water to sip), (6) **snāna / abhiṣeka** (bathing), (7) **vastra** (clothing), (8) **yajñopavīta** (sacred thread), (9) **gandha / candana** (sandal-paste, perfume), (10) **puṣpa** (flowers), (11) **dhūpa** (incense), (12) **dīpa** (lamp), (13) **naivedya** (food offering), (14) **tāmbūla** (betel), (15) **nīrājana / ārati** (waving of lamps), (16) **namaskāra / pradakṣiṇā / visarjana** (prostration, circumambulation, leave-taking). Counts and ordering vary by tradition and deity.
  - *Variant (pañcopacāra — the five):* the abbreviated daily worship of **five** core offerings keyed to the five senses — **gandha** (smell/touch), **puṣpa** (the named flower / hearing), **dhūpa** (smell), **dīpa** (sight), **naivedya** (taste). Other counts run to ten (*daśopacāra*), eighteen, thirty-two, or sixty-four (*catuḥṣaṣṭy-upacāra*) in elaborate temple service. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (mānasa-pūjā):* "mental worship" — the entire pūjā performed in imagination with no physical objects, esteemed as the highest form because it needs nothing external and cannot be defiled. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (nitya / naimittika / kāmya):* pūjā classed as **nitya** (daily obligatory), **naimittika** (occasional, festival-triggered), and **kāmya** (desire-motivated, for a specific boon).

- **Ārati / nīrājana** - The climactic waving of a lit (camphor) lamp before the deity in clockwise circles, accompanied by bells, conch, and song; the flame is then passed among devotees who pass their hands over it and to their eyes/head, receiving the deity's *tejas* (light/grace). The burning camphor — consumed leaving no residue — is itself a symbol of the ego dissolving in the divine. [folk]

- **Darśana — auspicious seeing** - The reciprocal *seeing* at the heart of image-worship: the devotee "takes *darśana*" of the deity and, crucially, **is seen by** the deity in return — a mutual gaze that is the central transaction of temple religion (hence the importance of the eyes, large and open, and of *netra-unmīlana*). To "have darśana" is the primary purpose of pilgrimage and temple-going. See [Sacred Geography](../06-sacred-geography/README.md).

- **Prasāda — graced return** - Food, water, flowers, or ash offered to the deity and returned to the devotee, now sanctified by the deity's "enjoyment" (*bhoga*) of it — the material vehicle of grace (*prasāda* = "grace, favor"). Eating *prasāda* internalizes the deity's blessing; refusing or wasting it is a grave discourtesy. **Tīrtha** (sanctified water), **vibhūti / bhasma** (sacred ash, Śaiva), **kumkuma** and **tilaka** (forehead marks) are kindred graced substances. [folk]

## 6. Aniconic Currents & Critiques of Image-Worship

A persistent internal counter-tradition treats image-worship as inferior, provisional, or wrong — usually grounding the critique in the *formless* (*nirguṇa*) absolute or in Vedic aniconism.

- **The Vedic aniconic baseline** - The earliest *śruti* religion is fire-and-word ritual (*yajña*), not image-worship; there is no temple or consecrated icon in the Saṃhitās. Image-worship is, historically, a **post-Vedic, Purāṇic-Āgamic** development — a fact used by reformers to brand it "un-Vedic." [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Mīmāṃsā opposition):* the orthodox Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā ritualists were staunch opponents of icon-worship in early-medieval India, holding the aniconic Vedic ritual programme alone as authoritative *dharma*. [scholarly]

- **The nirguṇa / saguṇa axis** - The deep doctrinal hinge: **nirguṇa Brahman** (the absolute without form or attributes) vs **saguṇa Brahman / Īśvara** (God with form and qualities). Advaita ranks *saguṇa* worship (including the *mūrti*) as a **provisional, lower** (*aparā*) means — valid and graciously effective for those not yet ripe for non-dual knowledge, but ultimately to be transcended. See [Ātman, Brahman & the Six Darśanas](atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md).
  - *Variant (nirguṇa bhakti — the Sants):* the medieval *Sant* / *Nirguṇī* poets — **Kabīr**, **Nānak**/Sikhism, **Ravidās**, **Dādū** — rejected the *mūrti* outright, worshipping a formless God through the **name** (*nāma*) and inner devotion, and ridiculing stone-worship ("if worshipping a stone could bring God, I'd worship a mountain"). [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Vīraśaiva / Liṅgāyat):* Basava's movement rejected temple icons and Brahminical pūjā in favor of the personal **iṣṭa-liṅga** worn on the body — a portable, individual aniconic focus. [scholarly]

- **The 19th-century reform critique (Arya Samaj)** - **Dayānanda Sarasvatī** (Arya Samaj, founded 1875) condemned *mūrti-pūjā* as **strictly forbidden** and un-Vedic — a late corruption that, he argued, fostered ignorance, priestly exploitation, and superstition; he called Hindus back to a *nirguṇa*, Veda-only theism. Brahmo Samaj (Rammohan Roy) similarly rejected idolatry. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (the defense):* defenders (and Vivekānanda, sympathetically) reply that the image is a *pratīka* / *kindergarten* of religion — a legitimate aid for the embodied mind, not the worship of stone; the cultured worshipper knows the deity is not the matter. The dispute is thus partly over *what worshippers actually believe* they are doing. [disputed]

- **Outside-tradition framings** - "Idol worship," the standard pejorative translation, imports an Abrahamic polemic; scholars prefer "icon/image worship," and many practitioners reject "idol" as a mistranslation of *mūrti* (a *form*, not a false god). [scholarly]
