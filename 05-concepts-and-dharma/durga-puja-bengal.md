# Durgā Pūjā — Bengal's Śāradīyā Goddess Festival & the Āgomoni Tradition

> Cosmic address: [The Hindu Timeline](../README.md) → [Concepts & Dharma](README.md) → **Durgā Pūjā (Bengal)**. The **Bengali / eastern-Indian** form of the pan-Hindu autumn Goddess festival — by participation the **largest single Hindu festival celebration in the world** — built on a double charter: the Goddess's **Mahiṣāsura victory** ([Devī Māhātmya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)) and **Rāma's *akāl-bodhan***, his "untimely" autumn awakening of Durgā before the war on Rāvaṇa ([Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/krittivasi-ramayan.md)).
>
> Parent: [Concepts & Dharma](README.md) · Peers: [Garbā & Dāṇḍiyā Rāsa](garba-and-dandiya-raas.md) (Gujarat's Navarātri body) · [Festivals & Vratas](festivals-and-vratas.md) (the pan-Hindu calendar) · [Regional Temple Festivals](regional-temple-festivals.md)

**Durgā Pūjā** (Bengali *Durgā Pujo*; also **Śāradīyā / Śāradotsava**, "the autumn festival," and **Akāl-bodhan**, "the untimely awakening") occupies the *Āśvina śukla* days **Ṣaṣṭhī through Vijayā Daśamī** — the same nine-night window as Sharad Navarātri, but with its own ritual grammar, iconography, and theology. Where Gujarat dances, **Bengal builds**: temporary shrine-pavilions (*paṇḍāl*) housing clay images (*pratimā*) of Durgā slaying Mahiṣāsura, flanked by her four children. Two clocks run throughout: the **charter myths** are Purāṇic and epic (Tretā-yuga *akāl-bodhan*; the Devī Māhātmya war), while the **attested festival** in its zamindari-to-public form is documented from roughly the **16th century CE onward** [scholarly].

---

## I. The two charters — Mahiṣāsura and the *akāl-bodhan* (kept distinct)

- **The Devī Māhātmya charter** — the pūjā's liturgical spine is the **Devī Māhātmya / Durgā Saptaśatī / Caṇḍī** (embedded in the [Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/markandeya-purana.md)): the gods' combined *tejas* births the many-armed Goddess, each god arms her, and she slays the buffalo-demon **Mahiṣāsura** — the scene every Bengali *pratimā* freezes at the kill. Full treatment: [Devī Māhātmya & the Great Goddess](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md). (Devī Māhātmya 2–4)
  - *Variant (spring as the "timely" season):* Bengali tradition holds the Goddess's *canonical* worship to be **vernal** — **Basantī Pūjā** in Chaitra, charter-linked to **King Suratha**, the dispossessed king of the Devī Māhātmya's frame-story who worships the Devī on a riverbank and regains his kingdom; autumn worship is the *exception* that became the rule. [regional] (Devī Māhātmya 1, 13; Bengali paddhati tradition)
- **The *akāl-bodhan* charter** — **Rāma**, counselled that Durgā/Caṇḍikā shields Rāvaṇa, **awakens the Goddess out of season** — in autumn, during *Dakṣiṇāyana*, the gods' "night" — worshipping her from **Ṣaṣṭhī to Daśamī** before crossing to Laṅkā; hence the festival's very days and its name *akāl-bodhan*, "untimely awakening." **Canonical home: [Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/krittivasi-ramayan.md)** (see its Akāl-bodhan section for the full episode, including the **108 blue lotuses** and **Rāma's offered eye**); summarized here, not duplicated. (Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ) [regional]
  - *Variant (Kālikā / Bṛhaddharma Purāṇa):* in the Purāṇic versions it is **Brahmā** (with the gods) who performs the untimely *bodhan* on Rāma's behalf, waking the sleeping Goddess in a **bel (bilva) tree**; Kṛttibās transfers the agency to Rāma himself. (Kālikā Purāṇa; Bṛhaddharma Purāṇa — see [Upapurāṇas overview](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/upapuranas-overview.md)) [late text]
  - *Variant ([Devī-Bhāgavata](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/devi-bhagavata-upapurana.md)):* the Śākta upapurāṇa likewise has Rāma worship the Devī in autumn at Nārada's urging to defeat Rāvaṇa — the Śākta canon claiming the Rāma story for the Goddess. [late text]

## II. The day-by-day arc — Mahālayā to Visarjan

- **Mahālayā — the hinge of the fortnights** — the new-moon day ending **Pitṛ-pakṣa** (the ancestors' fortnight, closed with mass **tarpaṇa** offerings on the Ganga's ghats — see [Śrāddha & Pitṛ rites](shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md)) and opening **Devī-pakṣa**, the Goddess's fortnight. At dawn Bengali households listen to ***Mahiṣāsura-mardinī*** — the radio *Chaṇḍī-pāṭh* (recitation of the Devī Māhātmya) made canonical by **Bīrendra Kṛṣṇa Bhadra**'s voice, broadcast on All India Radio **since the early 1930s** [scholarly] — the acoustic start of Pujo. On this day, at the image-workshops, the sculptor traditionally paints the Goddess's **eyes** last (***chokkhu-dān***, "gift of eyes"), the act that quickens the clay. [regional]
- **Ṣaṣṭhī — bodhan, āmantraṇ, adhivās** — the sixth lunar day: the formal ***bodhan*** ("awakening") of the Goddess — the ritual re-enactment of the *akāl-bodhan*, performed at the foot of a **bel tree or bel branch**; then *āmantraṇ* (invitation) and *adhivās* (sanctifying welcome-residence) install her for the coming days. The Goddess is now understood to have **arrived**. (Bengali pūjā-paddhati; Kṛttivāsī charter) [regional]
- **Saptamī — Nabapatrikā / Kolā-bou snān** — before dawn, the ***nabapatrikā*** ("nine plants") — a **banana plant** bundled with eight others: **colocasia (kachu), turmeric (haridrā), jayantī, bel (wood-apple), pomegranate (dāḍima), aśoka, mānaka (arum), and rice-paddy (dhānya)** — is **bathed in the Ganga**, wrapped in a **white sari with a red border**, and installed **beside Gaṇeśa** on the altar. Each plant is worshipped as a form of the Devī (Brahmāṇī in the banana, Kālikā in the kachu, Durgā in the turmeric, Cāmuṇḍā, Lakṣmī in the paddy, etc.) — the Goddess as **vegetation itself**. Then *prāṇa-pratiṣṭhā* enlivens the clay *pratimā* and the great pūjā proper begins. (Bengali paddhati; Kālikā-Purāṇa ritual stratum) [regional]
  - *Variant (the "Kolā-bou" / Gaṇeśa's wife):* popular usage calls the sari-clad plant **"Kolā-bou"** — the banana-bride — and folk speech makes her **Gaṇeśa's wife**; priests and paddhati insist she is **no bride but the Devī herself in plant form** (or Gaṇeśa's *mother* in a nine-plant body). Both readings circulate; cataloged, not merged. [folk]
  - *Variant (agrarian substrate):* scholars read the nabapatrikā as a pre-Purāṇic **harvest/fertility rite** — a vegetation-goddess worship absorbed into the Śākta frame, the autumn festival doubling as a harvest thanksgiving. [scholarly]
- **Mahāṣṭamī — puṣpāñjali and Kumārī Pūjā** — the eighth day, the festival's devotional peak: mass **puṣpāñjali** (flower-offerings with mantra, whole neighbourhoods reciting together), and in some houses and maths **Kumārī Pūjā** — the worship of a **living pre-pubescent girl as the Goddess herself**, seated, bathed, adorned and offered *upacāras* like the image. Charter: the Tantric-Śākta doctrine that the Devī dwells in all females, concentrated in the kumārī. (Devī Purāṇa / Tantric kumārī-pūjā stratum) [regional]
  - *Note (Belur Maṭh, 1901):* **Swami Vivekananda** performed Kumārī Pūjā at **Belur Maṭh's first Durgā Pūjā (1901)**, worshipping young girls as the Devī — since then the Ramakrishna Mission's Mahāṣṭamī kumārī-pūjā is the most famous public instance. [scholarly]
- **Sandhi Pūjā — the knife-edge of the festival** — performed in the **48-minute juncture (*sandhi*)** spanning the **last 24 minutes of Aṣṭamī and the first 24 of Navamī**: the Goddess is worshipped as **Cāmuṇḍā**, the terrible form, with **108 lamps** lit and **108 lotuses** offered. **Charter:** this juncture is the instant in the Devī Māhātmya war when the Devī, as Kālī/Cāmuṇḍā, **beheaded Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa** (whence the name Cāmuṇḍā) — the pūjā catches the Goddess at her moment of wrathful breakthrough. (Devī Māhātmya 7 — see [the Cāmuṇḍā episode](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)) [regional]
  - *Variant (Rāma's sandhi):* Bengali tradition also anchors Sandhi Pūjā in the *akāl-bodhan* — the **108 blue lotuses** Rāma offered (and the eye he was ready to give when one went missing) are the prototype of the 108-lotus offering. (Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ) [regional]
  - *Variant (balidān):* Sandhi Pūjā was classically the moment of **animal sacrifice (bali)** — a goat or buffalo; most modern pūjās substitute a **vegetal bali** (sugar-cane, ash-gourd, banana) or a symbolic cutting. Both practices persist and are attributed, not merged. [regional]/[folk]
- **Mahānavamī — homa, bhog, dhunuchi-nāch** — the ninth day: the concluding **fire-oblation (homa)**, the great cooked offering (***bhog***, typically *khichuṛi* distributed to all comers — the festival's open commensality), and at the evening *āratī* the ***dhunuchi-nāch*** — devotees dancing before the Goddess holding smoking **clay incense-censers (dhunuchi)** of burning coconut-husk and *dhuno* resin, to the drumming of the **ḍhāk**. [regional]/[folk]
- **Vijayā Daśamī — sindoor-khelā and visarjan** — the tenth day, "victory-tenth": after the mirror-farewell (*darpaṇ-visarjan*) the married women perform ***debī-baraṇ*** — bidding the Goddess farewell as a departing daughter with sweets, betel and **sindoor** — and then ***sindoor-khelā***, smearing vermilion on the Goddess and **on one another** in a red-drenched play of auspicious wifehood. Then the *pratimā* is carried in procession and **immersed (*visarjan / bhāsān*)** in the river; the clay dissolves back into the Ganga it came from, and people exchange **Vijayā greetings** — embraces (*kolākuli*), elders' blessings, sweets — with the cry ***"āsche bochor ābār hobe"* ("it will happen again next year")**. [regional]
  - *Note (inclusion):* sindoor-khelā traditionally belonged to **married women only**, excluding widows; recent decades have seen deliberate widow- and trans-inclusive editions — a live, documented shift. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (aparājitā):* on Daśamī the **aparājitā** creeper ("the unconquered") is worshipped for victory — the same Vijayā-daśamī charter as the pan-Indian Dussehra, catalogued in [Festivals & Vratas](festivals-and-vratas.md). [traditional]

## III. Āgomoni — the daughter-returns theology

- **Umā comes home** — Bengal's distinctive theology reads the festival not (primarily) as a warrior's victory but as a **homecoming**: **Durgā is Umā/Gaurī**, daughter of the mountain-king **Himālaya (Giri-rāj)** and queen **Menakā** ([Satī, Dakṣa & Pārvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md)), married away to the ash-smeared ascetic **Śiva** on Kailāsa. Each autumn she returns to her **natal home (bāper bāṛi)** for exactly the pūjā days — arriving at *bodhan*, leaving at *visarjan* — bringing her four children: **Gaṇeśa, Kārtikeya, Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī** (the four flanking figures of every Bengali *pratimā*). [regional]
- **Āgomoni & Vijayā songs** — the paired song-genres of arrival and parting: ***āgomoni*** songs voice **Menakā's longing** ("go, bring my Umā home") and her joy at the daughter's arrival; ***vijayā*** songs her grief as Umā must return to Kailāsa on Daśamī. The genre flowers in the **18th-century Śākta padāvalī** — notably **Rāmprasād Sen** and **Kamalākānta Bhaṭṭācārya** — which **humanizes the cosmic Goddess into a Bengali daughter**, sung in the run-up to Pujo. [regional]/[scholarly]
- **The two theologies superposed** — every *pratimā* holds both at once: the **demon-slaying Mahiṣāsura-mardinī** of the Devī Māhātmya *and* the **visiting daughter** of the āgomoni songs; the weeping at *visarjan* is simultaneously the send-off of a warrior-queen and of a married daughter going back to her husband's house. Deliberately double, never resolved. [regional]
- **Omens of coming and going** — the Bengali almanac (*pañjikā*) assigns the Goddess a **vehicle of arrival and departure** each year (elephant, horse, boat, palanquin), each foretelling the year's fortune (elephant = good harvest, horse = upheaval, etc.). [folk]/[regional] (pañjikā tradition)

## IV. Pratimā and paṇḍāl — the craft layer

- **Kumortuli** — north Kolkata's hereditary **potters' quarter**, the workshop-district where most *pratimās* are built: a straw armature lashed on a bamboo frame, layered with **Ganga clay**, painted, and dressed; images are exported worldwide to the Bengali diaspora. [regional]/[scholarly]
- **Punya-māṭi — the soils of the image** — the ritually prescribed clay-mixture for the Goddess includes, beside Ganga-bank clay, cow-dung and cow-urine, a handful of **soil from the doorstep of a courtesan's house (*beshyā-dvāra-mṛttikā*)**, ideally begged from her hand. Rationales differ by teller — that the visitor's virtue is left at her door, sanctifying the soil; or that the Goddess excludes no one — and the requirement, unevenly observed today, is carried in the pūjā-paddhati and oral practice rather than a single canonical text. [folk]/[oral tradition]/[disputed]
- **Ek-chālā to theme paṇḍāls** — the traditional format is the ***ek-chālā***: all five deities in one frame under a single painted backdrop (***chālchitra***), often ornamented with **ḍāker sāj** (silver-foil filigree); the 20th–21st-century **"theme pūjā"** turned paṇḍāl and image into avant-garde installation art, with celebrated designers and art-prizes — the festival as Kolkata's open-air biennale. [regional]/[scholarly]
- **The clay's round trip** — river clay → Goddess → *visarjan* → river again: the craft itself enacts the cyclic theology (form arises from and dissolves back into the formless), a point Bengali priests and modern commentators alike draw explicitly. [regional]

## V. From zamindari courtyard to sarbajanin street — the documented history [scholarly]

*(All items in this section are on the academic/historical clock.)*

- **Earliest claimed pūjās (16th c. CE)** — tradition credits **Kaṁsanārāyaṇ of Tāherpur** (Rajshahi, now Bangladesh, c. 1580) with the first grand Śāradīyā Durgā Pūjā in roughly its modern form, spending a fortune on it; rival claims exist (e.g. Bhabānanda Majumdār of Nadia), and none is securely documentable. [scholarly]/[disputed]
- **Sābarṇa Roy Choudhury pūjā (1610)** — the family pūjā at **Barisha (Behala, Kolkata)**, running continuously since 1610, is commonly counted the **oldest extant pūjā of the Kolkata region**. [scholarly]
- **Shobhabazar Rājbāṛi (1757)** — after Plassey, **Rājā Nabakṛṣṇa Deb** mounted a spectacular pūjā at his Shobhabazar palace with **Robert Clive** among the guests — the pūjā as colonial-era status theatre, template of the great **zamindari/bāroāri-babu pūjās** of 18th–19th-c. Calcutta. [scholarly]
- **Bāro-yāri (1790)** — at **Guptipara (Hooghly)**, **twelve friends (bāro yār)** barred from a household pūjā pooled subscriptions and held their own — the first recorded **community-subscription pūjā**, breaking the aristocratic monopoly. [scholarly]
- **Sarbajanin (1910)** — the first fully **public, all-comers pūjā (sārbajanīn, "of everyone")** is generally credited to **Bagbazar, Calcutta (Sanātan Dharmotsāhinī Sabhā, 1910)**; in the freedom-struggle decades the sarbajanin pūjā fused Goddess and nation (the **Bhārat Mātā** resonance) and became the festival's dominant form. [scholarly]
- **UNESCO (2021)** — **"Durga Puja in Kolkata"** was inscribed on UNESCO's **Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in December 2021** — cited for its fusion of religion, craft and public art; the peer inscription to Gujarat's 2023 *garbā* listing ([Garbā & Dāṇḍiyā](garba-and-dandiya-raas.md)). [scholarly]

## VI. Patterns

- **Two charters, one altar** — Mahiṣāsura-mardinī (Devī Māhātmya) and *akāl-bodhan* (Kṛttivāsī/Purāṇic) are braided but distinguishable: the *image* freezes the first, the *calendar and bodhan rite* enact the second. [scholarly]
- **Two clocks throughout** — Tretā-yuga charter and Svārociṣa-manvantara war-frame on the scriptural clock; c. 1580 → 1610 → 1757 → 1790 → 1910 → 2021 on the documented clock — never collapsed. [scholarly]
- **The Goddess domesticated, not diminished** — the āgomoni theology's daughter-Umā and the paddhati's Cāmuṇḍā of Sandhi Pūjā are the same Devī at two registers; Bengal keeps both, as this corpus keeps variants. [regional]
- **One festival, many regional bodies** — Bengal's paṇḍāl-pūjā stands beside Gujarat's [garbā](garba-and-dandiya-raas.md), Mysore's Dasarā and the North's Rāmlīlā as regional bodies of one Goddess-festival ([Festivals & Vratas](festivals-and-vratas.md)); its Śākta geography ties to [Kālīghāṭ and the Śakti Pīṭhas](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md) and [Kāmākhyā](../06-sacred-geography/kamakhya-nilachala-kamarupa.md). [scholarly]
