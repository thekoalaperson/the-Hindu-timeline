# The Pramāṇas — Means of Knowledge

> **Where this sits:** An epistemology file of the *concepts-and-dharma* layer — the prior question behind every doctrine in this knowledge base: *how do we validly know anything at all?* Every darshana in [atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md](./atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md) is, at root, defined by which pramāṇas it admits and how it ranks them.
> Parent: [../README.md](../README.md) · Sibling concepts: [.](.) · See also the school catalog in [../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md](../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md) and the yoga/jnana paths in [./yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md](./yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md).

**Pramāṇa** (*pra* + √*mā*, "to measure correctly") is the *instrument* of valid cognition (*pramā*) — the means by which a knower (*pramātṛ*) attains a true, non-contradicted, novel apprehension of an object (*prameya*). Indian epistemology is fundamentally a debate over the **inventory** of these instruments: a darshana is largely *constituted* by the subset it accepts. The classic spectrum runs from the materialist Cārvāka admitting only **one** (perception) up to Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā and Advaita Vedānta admitting **six**. Beyond the canonical six, Paurāṇic and minor lists name several more. Two related questions ride alongside: how a cognition is *validated* (*prāmāṇya-vāda* — intrinsic vs. extrinsic), and what counts as *error* (*khyāti-vāda*).

## 1. The Six Canonical Pramāṇas (in order of cumulative acceptance)

- **Pratyakṣa (perception)** — direct apprehension of a present object through a sense-faculty in contact with it (*indriyārtha-sannikarṣa*); the universally accepted, foundational pramāṇa on which the others lean. Accepted by **every** darshana, including Cārvāka.
  - *Subtypes (Nyāya):* **nirvikalpaka** (indeterminate, bare sensing prior to naming) and **savikalpaka** (determinate, judgmental — "this is a pot"); also **laukika** (ordinary) vs. **alaukika** (extraordinary — including yogic perception, *yogi-pratyakṣa*) (Nyāya Sūtra 1.1.4).
  - *Variant (Yoga / Patañjali):* admits **yogic perception** (*pratibhā* / *ṛtambhara-prajñā*) as a supersensory mode of pratyakṣa, by which the yogi directly cognizes subtle, hidden, and distant objects (Yoga Sūtra 1.48–49).
  - *Variant (Buddhist, Dignāga–Dharmakīrti):* pratyakṣa is *kalpanāpoḍha* — strictly free of all conceptual construction; only the bare particular (*svalakṣaṇa*) is its object, everything determinate being inference's work [scholarly].
  - *Variant (Advaita):* alongside ordinary perception, the witness-consciousness (*sākṣi-pratyakṣa*) directly knows internal states (pleasure, pain, ignorance) without sense-mediation.

- **Anumāna (inference)** — knowledge of an unperceived object via a perceived mark (*liṅga* / *hetu*) known to be invariably concomitant (*vyāpti*) with it: classically, inferring fire on a hill from smoke. Accepted by **all schools except Cārvāka**.
  - *Structure (Nyāya five-membered syllogism, pañcāvayava):* **pratijñā** (thesis: the hill has fire), **hetu** (reason: because smoke), **udāharaṇa** (general rule + example: wherever smoke, there fire, as in a kitchen), **upanaya** (application: this hill has such smoke), **nigamana** (conclusion: therefore it has fire) (Nyāya Sūtra 1.1.32–39).
  - *Variant (Buddhist / Dharmakīrti):* reduces the syllogism to essentials and grounds *vyāpti* in three relations — identity (*tādātmya*), causation (*tadutpatti*), and non-perception; emphasizes the "three marks of a valid reason" (*trairūpya*).
  - *Subtypes (Nyāya):* **pūrvavat** (cause→effect), **śeṣavat** (effect→cause), **sāmānyatodṛṣṭa** (inference by general correlation, not direct causal link).
  - *Why Cārvāka rejects it:* inference rests on *vyāpti* (universal concomitance), which can never itself be established by perception alone — so for the materialist it is unreliable [the canonical svabhāva-vāda objection].

- **Śabda (verbal testimony / word)** — knowledge from the statement of a trustworthy authority (*āpta*); for the orthodox schools, supremely the **Veda** (*śruti*), held authorless (*apauruṣeya*) and thus free of human defect. Accepted by **Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta, Jainism**; *not* by Cārvāka or Vaiśeṣika (which folds reliable testimony into inference).
  - *Subtypes (Nyāya):* **dṛṣṭārtha** (testimony about perceptible matters) and **adṛṣṭārtha** (about the imperceptible — heaven, dharma, the Self), where śabda becomes indispensable.
  - *Variant (Mīmāṃsā):* the Veda's authority is intrinsic and impersonal; its core injunctive force is **vidhi** (command), and *codanā* (Vedic injunction) is the sole means of knowing dharma (Mīmāṃsā Sūtra 1.1.2).
  - *Variant (Nyāya / theistic):* the Veda is valid because its author is a trustworthy person — namely **Īśvara** (God); authority is *pauruṣeya* but flawless.
  - *Variant (Vaiśeṣika):* does not list śabda separately — verbal knowledge is subsumed under **anumāna** (we infer the speaker's meaning), giving the school only two pramāṇas.

- **Upamāna (comparison / analogy)** — knowledge of the relation between a name and the thing it denotes, gained by likeness to something already known: told "a *gavaya* (wild ox) is like a cow," one later identifies the gavaya in the forest by that resemblance (Nyāya Sūtra 1.1.6). Accepted by **Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta**.
  - *Variant (Mīmāṃsā / Kumārila):* recasts upamāna as cognition of the *similarity itself* in the remembered cow when one sees the gavaya — a knowledge not reducible to perception, inference, or testimony.
  - *Variant (Nyāya):* the resulting knowledge is specifically the **name–object connection** (*saṃjñā-saṃjñi-sambandha*), distinguishing it from inference.
  - *Reduction by other schools:* Sāṃkhya, Buddhism, and Vaiśeṣika treat the analogical result as a species of inference or perception, hence do not list it separately.

- **Arthāpatti (postulation / presumption)** — positing an unstated fact as the *only* explanation that reconciles two otherwise contradictory but established truths: "Devadatta is fat, yet never eats by day" → one must postulate *he eats at night* (the only way to dissolve the contradiction). Accepted by **Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā, Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā, Advaita Vedānta**.
  - *Subtypes:* **dṛṣṭārthāpatti** (postulation to explain a *perceived* anomaly) and **śrutārthāpatti** (to explain a *heard/stated* anomaly).
  - *Variant (Nyāya):* denies arthāpatti as a distinct pramāṇa, reducing it to a form of inference (specifically, inference from the negative — *if not eating at night, then not fat*).
  - *Why Mīmāṃsā insists on it:* used to supply the implied force of Vedic words and to establish unseen potencies (*apūrva*) that link ritual act to its later fruit — central to Mīmāṃsaka ritual theory.

- **Anupalabdhi / Abhāva-pramāṇa (non-apprehension / negative proof)** — direct knowledge of the **non-existence** (*abhāva*) of an object via its non-perception under conditions where it *would* be perceived if present: "there is no pot on the floor," known immediately by not seeing one. Accepted by **only two**: **Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā** (Kumārila Bhaṭṭa) and **Advaita Vedānta**.
  - *Subtypes (four, per Kumārila):* non-perception of the **cause**, the **effect**, the **co-inherent**, and the **opposite/contradictory**.
  - *Variant (Prābhākara):* **rejects** anupalabdhi entirely — for Prabhākara there are no negative facts; "non-existence" is just the bare perception of the empty locus (the floor *as such*), so no separate pramāṇa is needed. This is the precise fault-line that makes Prābhākara a *five*-pramāṇa school and Bhāṭṭa a *six*-pramāṇa school.
  - *Variant (Nyāya):* knows absence (*abhāva*) but through a *special perception* (*anupalabdhi* as a mode of pratyakṣa via the "qualified-object" relation), not as a seventh-style independent pramāṇa.

## 2. How Each Darshana Counts — the Spectrum from 1 to 6

A school's identity is largely fixed by where it stops on this ladder; each higher count *adds* to the ones below.

- **Cārvāka / Lokāyata — 1:** *pratyakṣa* only. Perception is the sole valid means; inference, testimony, and the rest are rejected as unreliable, making the supersensible (soul, karma, afterlife, God) unknowable and therefore denied [materialist]. (See the heterodox schools in [atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md](./atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md).)
  - *Variant [disputed]:* some sources note "refined" Cārvākas (e.g., Purandara) accepted *worldly* inference while rejecting only inference about the transcendent — softening the strict one-pramāṇa stance [scholarly].
- **Vaiśeṣika — 2:** *pratyakṣa, anumāna*. Verbal testimony is folded into inference; comparison and the rest into perception/inference. (Kaṇāda's atomism.)
- **Buddhism (Dignāga–Dharmakīrti logic) — 2:** *pratyakṣa, anumāna*. Scripture (*āgama*) is admitted only as a *derived* form of inference (reliable testimony reduces to a special anumāna), not as an independent pramāṇa [scholarly].
- **Sāṃkhya — 3:** *pratyakṣa, anumāna, śabda* (Sāṃkhya Kārikā 4). The dualism of *puruṣa* and *prakṛti* is established largely by inference, with śruti as corroboration.
- **Yoga (Pātañjala) — 3:** *pratyakṣa, anumāna, āgama (=śabda)* (Yoga Sūtra 1.7) — though Yoga elevates a *purified* yogic perception above the ordinary kind.
- **Nyāya — 4:** *pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, śabda* (Nyāya Sūtra 1.1.3). The classical logicians' canonical list; arthāpatti is reduced to inference and anupalabdhi to a mode of perception.
- **Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā — 5:** the Nyāya four **+ arthāpatti**, but **minus** anupalabdhi (denied, as above).
- **Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā (Kumārila) — 6:** **all six** — adds *anupalabdhi* to make the full set. Mīmāṃsā's whole project (knowing dharma from texts) drives it toward the richest inventory.
- **Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara, Dharmarāja's *Vedānta Paribhāṣā*) — 6:** **all six**, inheriting the Bhāṭṭa list; śabda (the *mahāvākyas* of śruti) is given supremacy for knowing Brahman, since the non-dual Self cannot be reached by perception or inference (see the Mahāvākyas in [atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md](./atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md)).
- **Viśiṣṭādvaita (Rāmānuja) — 3:** *pratyakṣa, anumāna, śabda*; the other three are subsumed under these.
- **Dvaita (Madhva) — 3:** *pratyakṣa, anumāna, āgama (śabda)*; Madhva further distinguishes **kevala-pramāṇa** (knowledge as a valid cognition itself) from **anu-pramāṇa** (its instruments), a distinctive reframing [scholarly].
- **Jainism — 2 (in its own scheme):** *pratyakṣa* and *parokṣa* (direct vs. indirect knowledge), under which it sorts five kinds of indirect knowing — *smaraṇa* (memory), *pratyabhijñāna* (recognition), *tarka* (induction), *anumāna* (inference), *āgama* (testimony) — all governed by the relativist doctrines of *anekāntavāda* and *syādvāda* [distinct taxonomy, not a simple subset of the Hindu six].

## 3. Beyond the Six — Additional & Disputed Pramāṇas

Some Paurāṇic and minor lists extend the inventory; the orthodox schools generally **subsume** these under the canonical six rather than accepting them as independent.

- **Aitihya (tradition / hearsay)** — knowledge from uninterrupted tradition of unknown origin ("it is said that..."); often reduced to a weak form of śabda.
- **Sambhava (probability / inclusion)** — inference of a part from a whole or the probable from the established (e.g., a hundred includes ten); reduced to anumāna.
- **Abhāva (non-existence)** — listed separately in some Paurāṇic sets as the means of knowing absence; in the canonical six this is just *anupalabdhi* under another name.
- **Cheṣṭā / Ceṣṭā (gesture)** — knowledge conveyed by bodily gesture or sign-language.
- **Pratibhā (intuition / flash of insight)** — spontaneous supersensory cognition; in Yoga treated as a form of perfected perception rather than a separate pramāṇa.
- **Yukti (reasoning / coherence)** — reasoned combination of factors, named as a pramāṇa especially in the *Caraka Saṃhitā* (Āyurvedic epistemology), used for diagnosis and aetiology [discipline-specific].
- **Itihāsa (history)** — sometimes listed in extended sets as knowledge from historical narrative.
  - *Variant [late text / Paurāṇic]:* extended catalogs name as many as **ten** pramāṇas (attributed to Vedavyāsa) and even longer rosters (twelve), combining the six with aitihya, sambhava, abhāva, ceṣṭā, yukti, and others — but these are **not** standardized; the rosters vary by text and most darshanas treat them as derivative. [Open-ended: the additional-pramāṇa lists are not fixed across sources — counts of 8, 10, and 12 all appear.]

## 4. Validity & Error — the Companion Debates

Choosing an inventory of pramāṇas leaves two further questions, fiercely contested across the schools.

- **Prāmāṇya-vāda (the source of a cognition's validity):**
  - *Svataḥ-prāmāṇya (intrinsic validity):* a cognition is valid by its own arising; truth is the default, only error needs explaining — held by **Mīmāṃsā** and **Advaita Vedānta**.
  - *Parataḥ-prāmāṇya (extrinsic validity):* validity must be established by something external (corroboration, successful action); truth is *not* self-certifying — held by **Nyāya**.
  - *Variant (Buddhism):* validity is tested by *arthakriyā* (successful practical efficacy) — a cognition is valid if acting on it yields the expected result [scholarly].
- **Khyāti-vāda (theories of erroneous cognition — what happens when a pramāṇa fails):**
  - *Anyathā-khyāti (Nyāya):* in error, a real object is misapprehended *as* another real object (silver, real elsewhere, projected onto the perceived shell).
  - *Akhyāti (Prābhākara):* "error" is merely the *non-discrimination* of two real cognitions (a perception of "this" and a memory of "silver"), not a positive false content.
  - *Anirvacanīya-khyāti (Advaita):* the erroneously perceived object (the snake on the rope) is *indescribable* — neither real nor unreal — a temporary projection of *avidyā*, dissolved on right knowledge (see *māyā* in [atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md](./atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md)).
  - *Asat-khyāti (Mādhyamika Buddhist):* the content of error is simply non-existent (*asat*).
  - *Sat-khyāti / Yathārtha-khyāti (Viśiṣṭādvaita, Rāmānuja):* all cognition is of the real; even "error" grasps something genuinely present (*satkhyāti* via the *pañcīkaraṇa* mixing of elements) [scholarly].
