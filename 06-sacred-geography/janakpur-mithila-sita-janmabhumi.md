# Janakpur and Mithilā — Sītā's Birthplace

> Cosmic address: [The Hindu Timeline](../README.md) → [Sacred Geography](README.md) → **Janakpur / Mithilā**. The first-tier living *Rāmāyaṇa* pilgrimage city in the **Madhesh / Terai of southern Nepal**. At least since the early 18th century it has been identified as the capital of **Janaka's Videha-Mithilā** and the home of **Sītā (Jānakī, Maithilī)**. It is the *kṣetra* of the **Dhanuṣa-yajña**, where [Rāma](ayodhya.md) broke **Śiva's bow** (popularly called the **Pināka**) and won her hand. It is also the home of the great **Jānakī Mandir**, the heart of **Maithilī / Sītā devotion**, and the terrestrial counterweight to [Ayodhyā](ayodhya.md) in the *Sītā-Rāma* couple-cult.

## I. Identity, name & the "two clocks"

- **Mithilā = the city and the region; Janakpur(dhām) = the town** — In Vālmīki, **Mithilā** is Janaka's city (*purī*), and Janaka is "the Vaideha, lord of Mithilā" (*vaideho mithilādhipaḥ*). His people and country are the **Videhas** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 66.18–22; CE 1.65.18–22, 1.67.6). In the wider sense, which Burghart traces to Brahmanical literature, *Mithilā* is the whole country bounded by the Himalaya and the Ganges, the Kosi and the Gaṇḍakī (Burghart 1978: 257). **Janakpur** (Janakpurdhām) is the town in **Dhanusha district** of Nepal that tradition identifies with Janaka's capital. It is now the capital of **Madhesh Province** (formerly Province No. 2). [regional]
- **The Vedic Videha** — The *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* gives the oldest account of how the land came to be settled. **Videgha Māthava** carried the fire **Agni Vaiśvānara** in his mouth. The fire burned its way east from the Sarasvatī but did not cross the **Sadānīrā** river, so the land beyond was long thought unfit for brahmins. Brahmins later made it fit by their sacrifices, and the Sadānīrā remained "the boundary of the Kosalas and Videhas" (*Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 1.4.1.10–17). Historians read the story as a memory of Vedic culture moving eastward. [scholarly]
- **Videha = "the bodiless" line** — In Vālmīki, Janaka recites his line as **Nimi → Mithi → Janaka (the first) → Udāvasu → … → Hrasvaroman → himself and his younger brother Kuśadhvaja** (Bāla 71.3–13; CE 1.70.3–13). He gives no personal name for himself. "Janaka" works as a family title as well as a name: Sītā will "bring glory to the house of the Janakas" (*janakānāṃ kule*, Bāla 67.22; CE 1.66.22). — see the [Videha / Mithilā line](../03-lineages-vamsha/videha-mithila-line-janaka.md)
  - *Variant (Purāṇic etymology):* **Nimi** lost his body to a curse and chose to dwell in the blinking of creatures' eyes. Afraid of a kingless realm, the sages churned his body with fire-sticks, and a son was born. He was called **Janaka** from his birth (*janana*), **Vaideha** because his father was bodiless (*videha*), and **Mithi** from the churning (*mathana*) (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.5.8–23). The *Bhāgavata* adds that this **Mithila** built the city **Mithilā** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 9.13.11–13). Here Mithi and the first Janaka are one person; Vālmīki's list makes them father and son.
  - *Variant (the name Sīradhvaja):* the Purāṇas name Sītā's father **Sīradhvaja** ("plough-bannered"), son of Hrasvaroman. The *Bhāgavata* explains the name by Sītā's birth at the tip of his plough (*sīra*) (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.5.27–28; *Bhāgavata* 9.13.18). The name is Purāṇic; Vālmīki does not use it.
- **Scriptural clock (Tretā-yuga)** — within the *Rāmāyaṇa* frame Sītā's birth, the bow-sacrifice and the four-fold wedding belong to the **Tretā-yuga** of the current Vaivasvata Manvantara, the mythic register of every event below. (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*) — see the **two-clocks** distinction at [Bāla-Kāṇḍa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md).
- **Historical clock** — The living tīrtha is recent. Richard Burghart's study of the town's land documents shows the Rāmānandī and Daśanāmī ascetics settling there **at or shortly after the turn of the 18th century**. The earliest surviving land grant to name the "*janakpur mauja*" dates from **1740**. He found **no visible ruins at Janakpurdham earlier than the mid-19th century**, and called claims of "extensive ruins" there baseless (Burghart 1978: 266, 268 and n. 13). The present **Jānakī Mandir** dates only to **c. 1910**. The epic ground and the standing shrines belong to different clocks. [scholarly] — see § V below.
- **Cross-border kṣetra** — the *Sītā-janmabhūmi* claim is shared across the border. **Janakpur (Nepal)** holds the temple-centre, while **Sitamarhi (Bihar)** claims the ploughing-furrow birth-spot (locally **Punaura-dhām**). The ascetics' own tradition divides the two. Janakpur is the site of Janaka's palace, and **Sitamarhi**, about 32 miles south-west, is where he ploughed and Sītā came out of the furrow. Rāmānandī and Rāmānujī ascetics had hermitages there, and Burghart thought Sitamarhi might be as old as Janakpurdham or older (Burghart 1978: 262 and n. 6). [regional] [disputed — birth-spot localisation]

## II. Sītā's earth-birth — the furrow of Mithilā (Bāla-kāṇḍa 66)

- **Janaka's own words** — Janaka tells Viśvāmitra the story himself. "As I was ploughing the field, clearing it (*kṣetraṃ śodhayatā*), she rose up from my plough. I found her, and she is known by the name Sītā" ("furrow"). She grew up as his daughter and he set her aside as *vīrya-śulkā*, a bride to be won by strength (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 66.13–15; CE 1.65.14–15). Vālmīki does not say in this passage why the field was being ploughed. — see [Bāla-Kāṇḍa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md)
  - *Variant (sacrificial ground):* the Purāṇas say Janaka was ploughing the **ground for a sacrifice** (*yajana-bhuvam*, *yajñārtham*). The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* adds that the sacrifice was for a son (*putrārtham*) (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.5.28; *Bhāgavata* 9.13.18). Vālmīki's *Uttara-kāṇḍa* likewise places the birth "in a field seized by the plough-tip, on the altar (*vedyām*)" (CE 7.17.30).
  - *Variant (drought, popular telling):* a great drought struck Mithilā, so Janaka and his family priest held a sacrifice in a field south-west of the palace to win Indra's rain. Janaka turned the soil with a **golden plough** and found the infant in the furrow. This is the story as Burghart retells it at the opening of his study, where he credits it loosely to "the Ramayana" (Burghart 1978: 257). The drought is not in Vālmīki. [folk]
- **Sītā's own account** — In the forest, Sītā tells the sage's wife **Anasūyā** how she was found. "It is said" (*kila*) that she came up through the earth as Janaka ploughed. The childless king saw her covered all over in dust, took her on his lap and called her his own. A voice from the sky (*antarikṣe vāk*) then confirmed that she was rightfully his daughter. He gave her to his eldest queen to raise (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 118.27–33; CE 2.110.26–32).
- **Ayonijā — "not womb-born"** — Vālmīki calls Sītā *ayonijā* in Janaka's speech and again in her own account (Bāla 66.15, 66.26; CE 1.65.15, 1.65.27; Ayodhyā 118.37; CE 2.110.36). Her names **Sītā** ("furrow"), **Jānakī** ("Janaka's"), **Maithilī** ("of Mithilā") and **Vaidehī** ("of Videha") all tie her to this soil. Epithets such as **Bhūmijā** ("earth-born") are not found in the Critical Edition text.
  - *Variant (Lakṣmī incarnate):* Sītā is **Lakṣmī**, come down to partner Viṣṇu's Rāma-avatāra. This is the standard Vaiṣṇava reading, and it makes Mithilā the birthplace of the Goddess of fortune (*Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*; Vaiṣṇava theology). Vālmīki's Bāla-kāṇḍa only compares Sītā to **Śrī** and the couple to **Viṣṇu with Śrī** in similes (CE 1.76.17–18). The Yuddha-kāṇḍa does make the identification outright, in Brahmā's hymn: *sītā lakṣmīr bhavān viṣṇuḥ* ("Sītā is Lakṣmī, you are Viṣṇu") (Yuddha 117.28; CE 6.105.25).
  - *Variant (Vedavatī, Vālmīki's Uttara-kāṇḍa):* **Vedavatī**, a woman of the Kṛta age, is molested by Rāvaṇa. Before entering the fire she vows to be reborn, *ayonijā*, for his destruction. She "is born again among mortals in a field seized by the plough-tip", and in the Tretā men call her **Sītā** because she rose from the furrow (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Uttara 17; CE 7.17.25–31).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna):* Mārkaṇḍeya says only that Sītā was the daughter of **Janaka, king of Videha**, "whom **Tvaṣṭṛ** himself fashioned" to be Rāma's beloved queen. He tells no furrow-birth and no bow-trial (*Mahābhārata* 3.258.9, Critical Edition).
  - *Variant (Jaina, Rāvaṇa's daughter):* in **Guṇabhadra's *Uttarapurāṇa*** (9th c.) Sītā is born to **Rāvaṇa and Mandodarī**. Astrologers foretell that she will ruin him, so **Mārīca** buries her in a casket in the land of **Mithilā**, where Janaka finds and raises her (summarised in Pannalal Jain's introduction to Raviṣeṇa's *Padmapurāṇa*). The earlier **Saṅghadāsa's *Vasudevahiṇḍī*** (c. 5th c.) also makes her Rāvaṇa's daughter, later adopted by Janaka (Singaravelu 1982: 62 n. 3). [variant — non-Vālmīki]
  - *Variant (Jaina, Janaka's daughter):* the other main Jaina line, **Vimalasūri's *Paumacariya*** and Raviṣeṇa's *Padmacarita*, keeps Sītā as **Janaka's own daughter**. "Jaina tellings" therefore do not speak with one voice (Pannalal Jain, introduction to the *Padmapurāṇa*). [variant — non-Vālmīki]
  - *Variant (Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa):* in this late Śākta-leaning text, Rāvaṇa collects the blood of sages in a pot that held milk the sage **Gṛtsamada** had consecrated so that Lakṣmī would be born as his daughter. Mandodarī, neglected by Rāvaṇa, drinks it to kill herself and instead conceives. Afraid of scandal, she goes to **Kurukṣetra** and buries the embryo there. Later Janaka, ploughing that ground with a golden plough for a sacrifice at Kurukṣetra, finds the girl, and a voice from the sky tells him to raise her and name her Sītā (*Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa* 8.19–42). In this telling the furrow is at Kurukṣetra, not Mithilā. [variant — non-Vālmīki]
- **Janaka the philosopher-king** — tradition identifies the foster-father with the learned **Janaka of Videha** of the Upaniṣads. That Janaka held a great sacrifice with rich fees and became the patron and pupil of **Yājñavalkya** (*Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* 3.1.1; 4.1–4). The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* says of the whole Maithila line that its kings were "mostly devoted to knowledge of the self" (*ātma-vidyā*) (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.5.34). So Mithilā is also remembered as a **seat of Vedic learning and jñāna**. Vālmīki does not equate Sītā's father with the Upaniṣadic king, and historians date the latter's Videha to the late Vedic period. [scholarly] [tradition]

## III. The Dhanuṣa-yajña — the bow-breaking (Bāla-kāṇḍa 66–67)

- **Arrival under Viśvāmitra** — On the way, Rāma frees **Ahalyā** at Gautama's abandoned hermitage in a grove outside Mithilā (Bāla 48–49; CE 1.47.11–1.48.22). The party then reaches **Janaka's sacrificial enclosure**. Janaka welcomes Viśvāmitra together with his house-priest **Śatānanda**, Gautama's eldest son by Ahalyā. Viśvāmitra explains that the two princes have come to try the great bow (Bāla 50–51; CE 1.49.1–25, 1.50.2). Śatānanda then tells the long story of Viśvāmitra's life (Bāla 51–65). — see [Bāla-Kāṇḍa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md)
- **The Śiva-dhanus** — Next morning Viśvāmitra asks to see the bow (Bāla 66.5–6; CE 1.65.5–6), and Janaka tells its history. At the wrecking of **Dakṣa's sacrifice**, **Rudra** raised this bow against the gods for denying him a share. Once appeased, he gave it to them, and they left it with Janaka's ancestor **Devarāta**, "sixth from Nimi", as a **deposit held in trust** (*nyāsa*) (Bāla 66.8–13; CE 1.65.8–13). In this episode Vālmīki calls it Rudra's or Śiva's bow, never "Pināka". In the Critical Edition text *pināka* appears only as the name of a missile and in the epithet *pinākin* (CE 1.55.9, 3.23.27, 6.81.32). The name **Pināka** for the Mithilā bow is a later, popular usage.
  - *Variant (Paraśurāma's account, Bāla 75):* **Viśvakarman** made two bows. The gods gave one to **Tryambaka** (Śiva), with which he destroyed Tripura, and the other to **Viṣṇu**. When Śiva's bow was slackened and Śiva himself stunned by Viṣṇu's *huṃkāra*, the angry **Rudra** handed it to **Devarāta** in Videha (Bāla 75; CE 1.74.11–20). The kāṇḍa leaves its two provenances unreconciled. [scholarly]
- **The bride-price of valour and the siege** — Janaka vowed Sītā to whoever could string the bow, as a *vīrya-śulka* or "bride-price of valour". Kings came to woo her, and none could grasp the bow or even lift it. Angered at their rejection, they **besieged Mithilā**. After a full year Janaka's resources were exhausted. He won a four-fold army from the gods by austerity and routed the kings (Bāla 66.15–26; CE 1.65.15–25). Bāla 66–67 call this a standing trial of strength. They never use the word *svayaṃvara*.
- **Breaking the bow** — The bow is brought in an **eight-wheeled chest** that **five thousand** strong men can barely drag (Bāla 67.4; CE 1.66.4). Rāma opens the chest and takes the bow by the middle "as if in play". While many thousands of men watch, he strings it and draws it, and **it snaps in the middle**. The noise is like a thunderbolt and the earth shakes. Everyone falls senseless except Viśvāmitra, Janaka and the two princes (Bāla 67.13–19; CE 1.66.13–19). Janaka declares his vow fulfilled and sends envoys to Ayodhyā (Bāla 67.21–27; CE 1.66.21–27).
  - *Variant (internal contradiction, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa):* in her account to Anasūyā, Sītā says the bow was given to Janaka by **Varuṇa** at a great sacrifice, together with two inexhaustible quivers. She says Janaka called the kings to an assembly, resolved on a **svayaṃvara**, and that Rāma refused her until his father consented (Ayodhyā 118.38–39 and following; CE 2.110.37–52). Earlier, Rāma tells Lakṣmaṇa to fetch the **two bows Varuṇa gave Janaka** at his sacrifice, which are kept at their teacher's house (Ayodhyā 31.27–29; CE 2.28.12–14). The Bāla-kāṇḍa makes the bow **Śiva's** and stages no svayaṃvara. This is a noted textual discrepancy. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās, Rāmcaritmānas, Bāla-kāṇḍa):* the Avadhī telling expands the scene into a full **dhanuṣa-yajña / svayaṃvara**. Many kings fail, and Sītā first sees Rāma in the **flower-garden (Phulwārī)** while worshipping at the shrine of **Girijā** (Pārvatī). Lakṣmaṇa flares up when Janaka laments that the earth has no heroes left. After the bow breaks, **Paraśurāma bursts into the assembly at Janakpur**, and Lakṣmaṇa taunts him. In Vālmīki, Paraśurāma meets the wedding party **on the road home**, after it has left Mithilā (Bāla 74–76; CE 1.73.16–1.75). Tulsīdās's version is the most popular in North India and is staged every year in the Rāmlīlā.
  - *Variant (bow in three pieces, popular telling):* as Rāma strings the bow it breaks into **three pieces**. One rises to heaven, one sinks into the underworld, and one stays on the earth in Mithilā (the story as Burghart retells it, 1978: 258; the piece left on earth is the one shown at Dhanusā). [folk]
  - *Variant (Sītā's strength, folk):* as a child Sītā once moved the bow's case while sweeping, proving that she alone "matched" it. [folk]
- **Dhanuṣā-dhām** — At the village of **Dhanusā** ("the Bow"), **about twelve miles (c. 19 km) north-east of Janakpurdham**, a long, narrow seam of rock is worshipped as the piece of Śiva's bow that stayed on earth. Its discovery is credited to the ascetic **Sur Kisor**, whose songs (*Śrī Mithilā Bilās*, octave 29) praise "the Bow offered by Śiva, held in trust by Janak, and touched by the hand of Rām". The abbots of the Jānakī monastery have traditionally overseen the shrine. In **1792** King **Rana Bahadur Shah** closed an order appointing the Jānakī monastery's abbot with the words "Worship especially the Bow of Siva at Dhanusa" (Burghart 1978: 261, 272). It is a principal sub-tīrtha of the Janakpur circuit. (sthala-tradition) [regional]

## IV. The four-fold wedding — Vivāha Pañcamī (Bāla-kāṇḍa 70–73)

- **Daśaratha summoned** — Janaka's envoys reach Ayodhyā after three nights on the road (Bāla 68; CE 1.67). **Daśaratha** comes to Mithilā with **Vasiṣṭha**, Vāmadeva, Jābāli, Kāśyapa, Mārkaṇḍeya and Kātyāyana (Bāla 69; CE 1.68.4). Janaka sends for his younger brother **Kuśadhvaja**, who rules **Sāṅkāśyā** on the **Ikṣumatī** river (Bāla 70.2–6; CE 1.69.2–6). **Vasiṣṭha** recites the **Ikṣvāku** line (Bāla 70; CE 1.69), and Janaka recites the **Videha** line (Bāla 71; CE 1.70). — see [Ayodhyā](ayodhya.md)
- **The Sudhanvan war** — Within his genealogy Janaka also tells of an earlier war. **Sudhanvan**, king of Sāṅkāśyā, besieged Mithilā and demanded both the bow and Sītā. Janaka killed him in battle and installed Kuśadhvaja on Sāṅkāśyā's throne (Bāla 71.16–19; CE 1.70.16–19).
- **The offer and the wedding-star** — Janaka gives **Sītā to Rāma and Ūrmilā to Lakṣmaṇa**, saying it three times (Bāla 71.21–22; CE 1.70.21–22). He fixes the date by the stars, not by the calendar. "Today is **Maghā**; on the third day, under **Uttara-Phalgunī**, hold the wedding" (Bāla 71.24; CE 1.70.24). Uttara-Phalgunī is praised as the wedding-star "where Bhaga is lord" (CE 1.71.12).
- **Kuśadhvaja's daughters** — **Viśvāmitra**, with Vasiṣṭha's agreement, asks for Kuśadhvaja's two daughters as wives for **Bharata and Śatrughna**, and Janaka agrees (Bāla 72.1–11; CE 1.71.1–10). Daśaratha then performs the *godāna* rite for his sons (Bāla 72; CE 1.71.20–24).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata):* the *Bhāgavata* makes **Kuśadhvaja the son** of Sīradhvaja, not his brother (*Bhāgavata* 9.13.19). The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* agrees with Vālmīki that he was Sīradhvaja's brother and king of Sāṅkāśya (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.5.29).
- **The quadruple marriage** — On the wedding day **Yudhājit**, Bharata's maternal uncle, arrives (Bāla 73.1; CE 1.72.1). Janaka leads Sītā forward: "This is Sītā, my daughter, your partner in dharma; take her hand in yours" (Bāla 73.26–27; CE 1.72.17). Four marriages are solemnised as Vasiṣṭha directs: **Rāma–Sītā** and **Lakṣmaṇa–Ūrmilā** (Janaka's daughters), and **Bharata–Māṇḍavī** and **Śatrughna–Śrutakīrti** (Kuśadhvaja's daughters) (Bāla 73.26–35; CE 1.72.17–22). Janaka then gives a great bridal gift of cattle and wealth (CE 1.73.3).
- **Vivāha Pañcamī** — Vālmīki gives a wedding **star**, not a tithi. Maithilī calendrical tradition fixes the wedding on **Mārgaśīrṣa Śukla Pañcamī** and celebrates it as **Vivāha Pañcamī**, Janakpur's greatest festival. A re-enacted *barāt* (groom's procession) brings "Rāma" from Ayodhyā, and the wedding is staged at the **Vivāha Maṇḍap** (Maithilī tradition; Nepal Tourism Board). [regional]
- **The Maithilī wedding-paintings** — the wedding of Rāma and Sītā is the founding subject of **Mithilā / Madhubani painting**, the region's ritual folk art that traditionally covers wedding walls (*kohbar*). The *Sītā-Rāma vivāha* is its central image. (regional folk tradition) [folk]

## V. The Jānakī Mandir & the sacred topography of Janakpur

- **Jānakī Mandir (Nau Lakhā Mandir)** — the great white domed temple at the city-centre marks Sītā's home. It was built about **1910 CE (V.S. 1967)** by **Queen Vṛṣabhānu (Vrisha Bhanu) of Tikamgarh (Orchha)**, at a reputed cost of nine *lākh*, hence the name *Nau Lakhā* (temple history; Wikipedia, "Janaki Mandir"). [regional]
  - *Founding image, two datings:* temple tradition says a **golden image of Sītā was found on this spot in 1657 CE**, and credits the ascetic **Sūr Kiśor (Shurkishordas)** with the images. Burghart's documents date Sur Kisor's arrival and the founding of the Jānakī monastery to **the early 18th century**. They include a **1727** grant of *kuśa-birtā* land "in the name of the goddess Jānakī" by **Māṇik Sen of Makwanpur**, recorded in an 1811 confirmation (Burghart 1978: 266, 271). [disputed — 1657 date]

### The eighteenth-century "reappearance" of Janakpur

- **The ascetics' story** — the town's ascetics, pandits and bards say that ancient Janakpur vanished in the war of the Dvāpara age and lay lost under jungle until it "reappeared" in the Kali age (Burghart 1978: 258, 264). [oral tradition]
  - **Caturbhuj Giri**, a Daśanāmī ascetic, dreamed that Rāma told him the city lay beneath him. He dug up **four images** tangled in the roots of a **banyan** tree, which became the seat of the **Rām Candra monastery** (Burghart 1978: 259–260).
  - **Sur Kisor**, a Rāmānandī initiated at the **Galtā** monastery near Jaipur, worshipped Sītā as a young girl whose uncle he was. He left her image at **Lohargal** in Rajasthan and walked to Mithilā. There the same image rose from the ground under a **margosa (neem)** tree, which became the seat of the **Jānakī monastery** (Burghart 1978: 260).
- **What the documents show** — local Hindu chieftaincies already held the "jungle", as mounds with brick temples, **Durgā**, **Lakṣmī-Nārāyaṇa** and **Śiva** images, and Śiva temples at **Kalyāṇeśvar** and **Jaleśvar** dating from at least 1700 show. The earlier patrons worshipped tantric deities, not Rāma and Kṛṣṇa (Burghart 1978: 266–268, 276). The Sen kings of **Makwanpur**, and from 1762 the Shah kings of **Gorkha**, endowed the monasteries with land. Burghart ties the grants to their wars for the revenue-rich Terai (Burghart 1978: 269–274). The ascetics may have confirmed and spread an existing local tradition linking the place with Janaka, rather than discovering the site (Burghart 1978: 269). [scholarly]
- **Rām Mandir & the Rām Candra monastery** — the pagoda-roofed **Rām Mandir** is popularly said to have been built by the Gorkhā general **Amar Singh Thapa** (Wikipedia, "Janakpur"), but no documentary source is cited for this. The documented link is a land grant. In **1806**, in fulfilment of a vow to **Hanumān**, *Kājī* Amar Singh Thapa offered *kuśa-birtā* land to the Rām Candra monastery, confirmed by **King Girvan Yuddha Vikram Shah's copper-plate of 1807** (Burghart 1978: 271 n. 15, 272). The **Vivāha Maṇḍap** in the Jānakī Mandir complex marks the wedding-pavilion. (sthala-tradition) [regional] [disputed — builder]
- **The sacred ponds (sāgar/kuṇḍa)** — Janakpur is ringed with holy tanks, more than **200** by one count, where pilgrims bathe. **Gaṅgā Sāgar** and **Dhanuṣ Sāgar** near the centre are the chief ones (Wikipedia, "Janakpur"). Ascetic legends give several their meaning. **Ratna Sāgar**, the "Sea of Jewels", is where the Rāmānandī **Raghunāth Dās** saw a glittering jewel and recognised the site of Janaka's treasury. At **Sītā Kuṇḍ**, Sītā is said to have bathed before her Sanskrit lessons with **Śatānanda**; the ascetic **Rām Dās** settled there, and a *jinn* who haunted the pond agreed to move elsewhere. Burghart adds that dating the old trees on the banks of ponds such as Gaṅgā Sāgar might show some ponds to be older than 1700 (Burghart 1978: 261–263, 268–269). [oral tradition] [regional]
- **Parikramā circuits** — two circuits are walked today, and Burghart records an earlier one:
  - The one-day **inner circuit** of about 8 km round the city is walked on the full-moon night before **Holī**, many pilgrims prostrating along the whole route (Wikipedia, "Janakpur"). [regional]
  - The **Mithilā Madhya Parikramā** is a 15-day, c. 128 km circuit across the Nepal–Bihar border. The main annual walk runs from **Phālguna Amāvasyā to Holī (Phālguna Pūrṇimā)**; the *Mithilā Māhātmya* also allows it in Kārttika and Vaiśākha. It passes 15 stations, including **Kalyāṇeśvar (Kalna)**, **Girijā-sthān (Phulhar)**, **Jaleśvar**, **Dhanuṣā-dhām** and **Viśvāmitra's āśram at Bisaul**, before ending at the Jānakī Mandir. It does **not** include Sitamarhi or Punaura (Wikipedia, "Mithila Madhya Parikrama"). [regional]
  - Burghart records that in the early 19th century **Sītā Prasād of Sitamarhi** and **Sūr Dās of Pipra** "brought to light" a five-day circumambulation at the *pañca-kośa* distance. He does not link it to the modern 15-day walk. It ran at a ten-mile radius through four Śiva temples, **Kalyāṇeśvar, Jaleśvar, Kṣīreśvar and Mithileśvar**, which the ascetics took to be the south, west, north and east **gates of ancient Janakpur** (Burghart 1978: 277). [scholarly]
- **Rāmānandī devotional centre** — Janakpur is a stronghold of the **Rāmānandī** order and of **Sītā-upāsanā**, the *rasika* devotion that worships Sītā as supreme. This makes it the Goddess-side counterpart to Rāma-centred [Ayodhyā](ayodhya.md) and the exile-tīrtha [Chitrakūṭa](chitrakoot-kamadgiri.md). Burghart places the flowering of *rasik sādhanā* in the Ganges basin in the **17th–19th centuries**. The town's founding houses are both Daśanāmī (the Rām Candra monastery) and Rāmānandī (the Jānakī monastery). One oral account tells of an *akhāṛā* battle between the two orders over Janakpur, after which the *mauja* was divided into a **Rām paṭṭī** and a **Jānakī paṭṭī** (Burghart 1978: 265 n. 8, 274). (sectarian tradition) [scholarly]

## VI. Mithilā as a Śakti Pīṭha

- **The left shoulder of Satī** — In the *śakti-pīṭha* lists, **Mithilā** is counted among the seats where a part of **Satī's** body fell while Śiva wandered in grief. Here it is the **left shoulder** (*vāma-skandha*); the resident **Śakti is Umā** and her **Bhairava is Mahodara**: *mithilāyām umā devī vāmaskandho mahodaraḥ* (*Pīṭhanirṇaya*, attached to the *Tantra-cūḍāmaṇi*, v. 47; text as transcribed by greenmesg.org). The *Devī Bhāgavata*'s list of seats (7.30) does **not** name Mithilā. [late text] — see [51 Śakti Pīṭhas](51-shakti-peethas.md)
  - *Note [disputed]:* no single temple answers to "Mithilā". The candidates proposed are all in Bihar: **Uccaiṭh Vanadurgā** (Madhubani district), **Jayamaṅgalā** (Begusarai) and **Ugratārā** (Saharsa). The pīṭha-lists name the *region* more firmly than any shrine. (Jaleśvar near Janakpur is a **Śiva** temple, one of the parikramā "gates", and the list consulted does not name it as a pīṭha.) [disputed]
- **The Goddess doubled** — Mithilā thus carries **two distinct feminine sacralities**: the *Rāmāyaṇa* **Sītā-janmabhūmi** (Sītā as Lakṣmī, born of Earth) and the **Śākta pīṭha** (fragment of Satī/Pārvatī) — independent traditions the house style keeps separate rather than merging. [scholarly]

## VII. Standing in the network

- **A first-tier Rāmāyaṇa tīrtha** — though **not a Sapta-Purī**, Janakpur-Mithilā ranks with [Ayodhyā](ayodhya.md) and [Chitrakūṭa](chitrakoot-kamadgiri.md) as a core node of the living *Rāmāyaṇa* pilgrimage map — the unique **bride-side** centre of the epic. (pilgrimage tradition) [scholarly]
- **Heart of Maithilī identity** — Janakpur is a cultural capital of the **Maithilī** language-region. Folk wedding-song, Madhubani art and the *kanyādāna* self-image of Mithilā as **"the bride's father" to all Hindustan** all draw on the *Sītā-Rāma vivāha*. **Vidyāpati** (14th–15th c.), the founding poet of Maithilī lyric, wrote chiefly Rādhā–Kṛṣṇa and Śaiva songs, so the Sītā-Rāma cult in Maithilī devotion should not be dated "from Vidyāpati onward". Local tradition does place Vidyāpati, fleeing with Queen **Lakhimā Devī**, at **Banauli** near Janakpur (Burghart 1978: 267). (regional tradition) [regional]
- **Festival calendar** — **Vivāha Pañcamī** (the wedding), **Sītā Navamī / Jānakī Navamī** (Sītā's birth, *Vaiśākha Śukla Navamī*), **Rāma Navamī**, the **Phālguna parikramā** ending at Holī, and the regional **Chhaṭh** anchor the year. *Rāmlīlā* and *Rāmāyaṇa* recitation fill the calendar. [regional]
