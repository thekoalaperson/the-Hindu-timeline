# The folk Hanuman healing shrines — Mehandipur Balaji and Salasar Balaji

> Two of North India's most-thronged folk **Hanumān** *kṣetras*, both in **Rajasthan** and both calling the god by the affectionate child-name **Bālājī**: the *svayambhu* (self-manifest) rock of **Mehandīpur Bālājī** (Dausa–Karauli border), the great exorcism-court where **Bālājī** presides over **Pret-rāj** and **Bhairav (Kotwāl)** to judge and expel possessing spirits; and the bearded **Sālāsar Bālājī** (Churū district), whose black-stone image was *ploughed up at Āsotā in 1754 CE* and enshrined per **twin dreams** — one to the landowner, one to the saint **Mohandās**. Both are living Kali-Yuga cults of the **Tretā-born Chiranjīvī** monkey-god, worshipped for concrete relief (of ghosts, disease, barrenness, litigation) rather than for scriptural *līlā*.

Both shrines take **Hanumān** — **Rudra-avatāra**, son of **Vāyu**, one of the seven **Chiranjīvīs** (deathless ones), and a hero of the [Tretā-Yuga](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/hanuman.md) Rāmāyaṇa — and localise him as a Rajasthani **folk healer** worshipped under the diminutive **Bālājī** ("the child / dear one"). The **two clocks** stay apart throughout: in *scriptural* time the god is the eternal Chiranjīvī who still walks the earth wherever Rāma's name is sung (hence his presence in Kali Yuga is doctrinally native, not late); in *terrestrial / historical* time both cults are recent, documented **Kali-Yuga** foundations — Sālāsar precisely dated to **1754 CE**, Mehandīpur formalised over the last few centuries around a rock claimed as immemorial. (Mehandīpur Bālājī temple tradition; Sālāsar Bālājī tradition) [folk] [regional]

## I. Mehandīpur Bālājī — the svayambhu rock and its court of spirits

- **The site** — **Mehandīpur** lies on the **Dausa–Karauli** border of eastern Rajasthan, in a rocky notch of the **Arāvallī** ranges (locally the *ghāṭā* / "ghata mechi" ravine). The shrine is famed across the Hindī belt not for architecture but as an **exorcism centre** — the place to bring *sankaṭ* (affliction), *bhūt-pret bādhā* (possession by ghosts), *jādū-ṭonā* (black magic), madness, chronic illness and misfortune. Sufferers are called **saṅkaṭ-wāle**. (Mehandīpur Bālājī temple tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The svayambhu image** — the presiding **Bālājī** is a *svayambhu* (self-manifest) relief of **Hanumān** that emerged from the hill-rock itself; the deity is worshipped in **child (bāla) form**, the image bound into the living stone rather than a carved and installed *mūrti*. A stream of **water is said to flow perpetually from the chest / feet of the image**, held to be miraculous and never traced to a source. (temple tradition) [folk]
  - *Foundation legend (regional):* the first **Mahant** of the line is said to have received the deity in a **dream/vision** roughly a millennium ago and to have begun worship at the self-revealed rock; the temple's continuous **Mahant** custodianship and its present built form, however, belong to only the **last few centuries** — the corpus keeps the "immemorial rock" claim and the "recent institution" fact distinct. [folk] [disputed]

### The darbār — the three-deity tribunal

- **Bālājī (Hanumān) — the judge** — presiding lord of the *darbār* (court); the devotee's petitions are addressed to him, and it is his authority that binds the possessing spirits. He is the merciful **Kotwāl of Rāma**, whose mere presence tortures and expels the *pret*. (temple tradition) [folk]
- **Pret-rāj Sarkār — "king of the ghosts"** — the second throne of the court, a form associated with **Yama / the lord of departed spirits**, who **tries and sentences** the *pretas* and *bhūtas* dragged before the court. Possessing spirits are made to appear before Pret-rāj to confess and receive judgment. (temple tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **Bhairav (Kotwāl / Bhairav Bābā) — the constable-executioner** — the third throne, the **Kotwāl** ("police-chief") of the *darbār*, who **carries out the punishment** Pret-rāj decrees — the Rajasthani folk-Bhairava in his role as guardian-magistrate and enforcer, cognate with the [Bhairava](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vatuka-bhairava-origin.md) of the wider tradition and with folk-Bhairava cults such as [Khaṇḍobā / Mārtaṇḍa-Bhairava](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md). (temple tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The theology of the court** — the trio functions as a **spiritual judiciary**: Bālājī the presiding judge, Pret-rāj the sentencing magistrate, Bhairav the executing constable — an idiom of *governance and law* rather than *bhakti-līlā*, which is precisely what makes Mehandīpur read as a **folk tribunal for the afflicted**. (temple tradition) [folk]

### The ritual machinery — arzī, prasād and peśī

- **Arzī — the petition** — the devotee files an **arzī** (a formal "application" to the court): a prescribed offering (in the common form a portion of *lāḍḍū*/*peḍā* prasād, split — part offered to Bālājī, part to the spirits) laid before the deity with a stated request for relief. (temple tradition) [folk]
- **Two prasāds, two destinations** — Mehandīpur is distinctive in that its prasād is **not simply eaten and carried home**. One share is the **darkhwāst** offered to Bālājī and consumed on the spot; a second share — the **bhabhūt / bhog for the pretas** — is **thrown away behind the giver** for the spirits, and is emphatically **not brought back or looked at**. (temple observance) [folk] [regional]
- **Peśī — the "hearing"** — the visible core of the cult: the possessed are brought before the court for a **peśī** (a legal "appearance / hearing"), in which the *pret* speaking through the sufferer is compelled to **name itself, confess how it entered, and depart**. Bodily manifestations — thrashing, striking oneself, head-swinging, being "chained" invisibly before the throne — are read as the spirit resisting the court's authority. (temple tradition) [folk] [disputed]
- **Sawāmaṇī — the thanksgiving feast** — a fulfilled petitioner returns to hold a **sawāmaṇī**: an offering of prasād measured in the auspicious unit of **savā** (**1¼** — 1¼ maunds/kilos and its multiples), fed to the deity and to pilgrims as a communal thanksgiving. (temple tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The folk observances of departure** — a cluster of taboos surrounds the visit: pilgrims are told **not to eat or drink in the town**, **not to accept or bring home food from Mehandīpur**, **not to embrace or thank anyone**, and **not to look back** on leaving — lest an expelled spirit follow them home. These are living *folk* prescriptions, not canonical rules. (Mehandīpur observance) [folk] [oral tradition]

## II. Sālāsar Bālājī — the bearded Hanumān ploughed up at Āsotā (1754 CE)

- **The site** — **Sālāsar** is a small town in **Churū district**, in the Śekhāwāṭī tract of northern Rajasthan; its **Bālājī (Hanumān)** shrine is among the most-visited Hanumān temples in India, drawing immense crowds on the two **full-moon fairs** of **Chaitra Pūrṇimā** and **Āśvin (Śarad) Pūrṇimā** — the Hanumān-Jayantī seasons. (Sālāsar Bālājī tradition) [regional]
- **The bearded face — daaḍhī-mūñch wāle Bālājī** — Sālāsar's image is unique among Hanumān icons for its **beard and moustache (*daaḍhī-mūñch*)** — the god shown with full facial hair rather than the usual clean-shaven *vānara* face. The distinctive bearded form is bound up with the vision-account of the saint Mohandās (below) and is the shrine's signature. (Sālāsar tradition) [folk] [regional]

### The Āsotā discovery and the twin dreams

- **Ploughed from the field (1754 CE)** — tradition dates the finding to **Śrāvaṇa, Vikrama Saṃvat 1811 = 1754 CE**: a **Jāṭ cultivator** ploughing his field at **Āsotā** (in the Nāgaur country) struck a buried object; when his **wife cleaned the soil-caked stone**, it revealed a self-formed **image of Hanumān / Bālājī** — a *svayambhu* icon delivered up by the earth. (Sālāsar Bālājī tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The Āsotā landlord's dream** — the **Ṭhākur (landowner) of Āsotā**, learning of the find, had a **dream** in which the deity commanded that the image be **sent to Sālāsar** — the losing party in the discovery instructed by the god himself to relinquish it. (Sālāsar tradition) [folk]
- **Mohandās's simultaneous vision** — at Sālāsar the ascetic **Mohandās Mahārāj** — a **Ḍāḍhīca (Dādhīca) Brāhmaṇa** renunciant and lifelong Hanumān-*bhakta* settled at Sālāsar — received the **same night a vision** of Hanumān, who revealed the buried image at Āsotā **with its exact identifying marks**, so that when the icon arrived it matched the saint's dream precisely. The **twin dreams** — to the Āsotā Ṭhākur and to Mohandās — are the doubled sanction that authorises the transfer. (Sālāsar tradition) [folk] [regional]
  - *Variant (the bearded darśana):* one strand holds that Hanumān appeared to Mohandās **in a bearded human guise**, and that the deity is therefore worshipped at Sālāsar **with beard and moustache** in memory of that form of the *darśana* — the folk explanation for the icon's unique face. [folk] [disputed]
- **The oxen that chose the spot** — the image was carried from Āsotā to Sālāsar by **bullock-cart**; at the site where the **oxen halted and would go no further**, the shrine was raised — the god selecting his own ground, a motif shared with many Rajasthani folk foundations. (Sālāsar tradition) [folk]
- **Mohandās as founder-saint** — **Mohandās** installed and served the image and is venerated as the shrine's **founder-saint**; the temple's later custody passed through his lineage/successors. His *samādhi*/*dhūṇī* and relics of the founding are part of the Sālāsar complex, and the town's whole cult descends from his devotion. (Sālāsar tradition) [folk] [regional]

### The living cult

- **Nishān and the padayātrā** — devotees fulfil vows by carrying a **nishān** (a triangular flag on a long staff) **on foot** to Sālāsar, especially at the Pūrṇimā fairs — a mass **foot-pilgrimage (padayātrā)** from surrounding districts and from Delhi/Haryana, the flag planted at the shrine on arrival. (Sālāsar tradition) [regional]
- **Sawāmaṇī and the akhaṇḍ-jyot** — as at Mehandīpur, fulfilled petitioners host **sawāmaṇī** feasts (offerings in the **savā / 1¼** unit); an **unbroken lamp (akhaṇḍ jyoti / dhūṇī)** kept since the founding is part of the shrine's continuity-cult. (Sālāsar tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **A Śekhāwāṭī–Marwar Hanumān axis** — Sālāsar anchors a northern-Rajasthan Hanumān circuit and sits within the broader Rajasthani **folk-hero / rider-saint** devotional world of figures such as [Rāmdev Pīr of Runīchā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ramdev-pir.md) and [Tejājī / Vīr Tejā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/tejaji.md), and of the [village-guardian (grāmadevatā)](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/gramadevatas-village-deities.md) cults that structure vernacular Rajasthani worship. [regional] [scholarly]

## III. The two shrines compared — and the two clocks

- **Same god, two folk idioms** — both localise the **Chiranjīvī Hanumān** as **Bālājī** and both foreground **concrete relief** over metaphysics, but they diverge in register: **Mehandīpur** is an **exorcism-court** (Bālājī + Pret-rāj + Bhairav, *peśī*, thrown-away prasād, departure taboos), while **Sālāsar** is a **darśana-and-vow** shrine (bearded icon, nishān padayātrā, sawāmaṇī) without the tribunal machinery. (temple traditions) [folk]
- **Svayambhu / earth-given icons** — both rest on a *self-manifest* claim: Mehandīpur's relief **emerged from living rock**, Sālāsar's image was **ploughed from the earth** — the god giving himself rather than being sculpted, the recurring warrant of Indian folk *kṣetras* (cf. the *svayambhu*-formation logic of [Vaiṣṇo Devī's rock-piṇḍīs](vaishno-devi-trikuta-katra.md)). [folk] [regional]
- **Where they sit on the clock** — *scripturally*, the god is a **Tretā-Yuga** hero who never died and so is doctrinally **present in Kali Yuga** by his own nature; *historically*, both are **documented Kali-Yuga** foundations — **Sālāsar fixed to 1754 CE** by a named saint, Mehandīpur a **recent-centuries** institution around a rock of claimed immemorial age. Neither should be read back into the epic past: the **Rāmāyaṇa Hanumān** is the deity worshipped, but the **shrines are modern**. [scholarly] [regional]
- *Two-clocks note [scholarly]:* the pilgrim numbers, bus-routes, temple trusts, the 1754 discovery and the Mahant/Mohandās lineages are **terrestrial Kali-Yuga** facts; the god's deathlessness, his self-manifestation from rock and soil, and the spirit-court of Pret-rāj and Bhairav are told in **folk-sacred** time — the same myth-versus-terrain pairing the corpus keeps for every *sthala*.
