# Sacred geography beyond the subcontinent — Angkor, Champa, Java, Bali

> Cosmic address: [The Hindu Timeline](../README.md) → [Sacred Geography](README.md) → **Greater India**. The Purāṇic cosmos — [Meru and the sapta-dvīpa](meru-kailash-and-cosmic-mountains.md) — projected outward beyond *Bhārata-varṣa* onto the "Indianized" kingdoms of Southeast Asia and the migration diaspora: **Angkor** as a stone Meru dedicated to Viṣṇu, **Champa's** Śaiva towers, the Javanese **Trimūrti** of Prambanan, the living *tīrtha* island of **Bali**, the **Pañca Īśvaram** of Laṅkā and multi-faith **Kataragama**, and the Murugan hill of **Batu Caves**. For the literary counterpart (the Rāma-cycle as it traveled) see [Southeast Asian Rāmāyaṇas](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/southeast-asian-ramayanas.md); for living Balinese practice see [Agama Hindu Dharma](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/balinese-hinduism-agama-hindu-dharma.md).
>
> **Two clocks.** The *sthala*-legends of these sites reach into cyclic time — the Samudra Manthana, the Rāma-war, Skanda's campaign — but the **buildings themselves are documented Kali-Yuga history** ([scholarly] c. 4th–13th c. CE for the classical monuments, 19th–20th c. for the diaspora shrines). This file dates the **stone** and catalogs the **variant dedications**, never collapsing the archaeological clock into the scriptural one.

Beyond Purāṇic *Bhārata-varṣa* the same cosmographic grammar was built in brick, laterite, and volcanic tuff across a zone Sanskritists once called **"Greater India"** or, more neutrally, the **Indianized / Sanskritic states** of Southeast Asia. From roughly the 4th century CE, courts from the Mekong to Java adopted Sanskrit as a prestige liturgical language, the *devarāja* ("god-king") ideology, and the Purāṇic-Āgamic template of the **temple-mountain** — a shrine built as a scale-model of [Mount Meru](meru-kailash-and-cosmic-mountains.md), moated by the cosmic ocean, ringed by galleries standing for the *maryādā-parvata* wall-ranges. The transmission was maritime and mercantile, not conquest; the local dynasties Indianized themselves. Most of these polities later turned Buddhist or Muslim, leaving the Hindu layer as ruin and inscription — **except Bali**, where the tradition is unbroken and living.

## I. Angkor — Vishnuloka and the churning of the ocean (Cambodia)

- **Angkor Wat** — the largest religious monument on earth, built by the Khmer king **Sūryavarman II** ([scholarly] r. c. 1113 – c. 1150 CE) in Yaśodharapura, the Angkorian capital. Uniquely among the great Khmer state-temples — most of which are **Śaiva** — Angkor Wat was dedicated to **Viṣṇu**. Its consecration name and Sūryavarman's posthumous title point the same way: **Parama-viṣṇuloka**, "he who has gone to the supreme world of Viṣṇu," so the temple is quite literally a *Vishnuloka* in stone. (Khmer inscriptions; [scholarly])
- **The Meru model** — the layout is the [Purāṇic cosmos](meru-kailash-and-cosmic-mountains.md) diagrammed: the **central quincunx of five towers** = the five peaks of Meru; the successive **enclosure walls and galleries** = the ring-mountains hemming the world; the vast **moat** = the encircling cosmic ocean (*kṣīra-samudra* / the outer sea). Angkor Wat is anomalously **west-facing**, a orientation usually read as Viṣṇu-associated and/or funerary. [scholarly]
- **The Samudra Manthana relief** — the most celebrated of the temple's bas-reliefs, on the **east gallery ("Churning of the Sea of Milk")**: **92 asuras and 88 devas** (the counts vary in the reading) haul the serpent **Vāsuki**, wound around **Mount Mandara**, while **Viṣṇu** presides at the centre and, below, his tortoise **[Kūrma](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md)** avatāra bears the churning-pivot — the [Samudra Manthana](../04-deep-dives/samudra-manthana.md) rendered ~50 m long. (Depicting *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 8.6–8; *Mahābhārata*, Ādi-parva)
- **Epic galleries** — the other long reliefs stage the **[Rāmāyaṇa's](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/southeast-asian-ramayanas.md)** Battle of Laṅkā, the **Mahābhārata's** [Kurukṣetra](kurukshetra-dharmakshetra.md) war, the *Vaikuṇṭha* of Viṣṇu, and Yama's heavens-and-hells — a full Purāṇic-epic wall-cycle. (Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa*, Yuddha-kāṇḍa; *Mahābhārata*)
- **The wider Angkor field** — the temple sits inside a landscape of temple-mountains: the earlier **Bakong** and **Phnom Bakheng** (9th c., Śaiva state-liṅgas), **Banteay Srei** (967 CE, dedicated to Śiva as *Tribhuvanamaheśvara*), **Koh Ker**, **Beng Mealea**. The *devarāja* cult installed a royal **liṅga** as the axis of the realm. (Khmer epigraphy; [scholarly])
- **Buddhist supersession** — Sūryavarman's successor **Jayavarman VII** ([scholarly] r. c. 1181 – c. 1218) turned the state Mahāyāna Buddhist, building **Angkor Thom** and the **Bayon** (face-towers of Avalokiteśvara/the king). Angkor Wat itself was gradually converted to a **Theravāda Buddhist** shrine, which it remains — a Vaiṣṇava Meru still in worship, but Buddhist. [scholarly]

## II. Champa — Bhadreshvara and the Cham Shaiva towers (Vietnam)

- **My Son (Mỹ Sơn)** — the dynastic sanctuary of **Champa**, the Cham (Austronesian) Hindu kingdoms of central Vietnam, set in a valley in Quảng Nam. The Cham were predominantly **Śaiva**, worshipping Śiva as national protector-god, usually in the form of a **mukha-liṅga** (face-liṅga). My Son was in use from the 4th to the 13th–14th centuries CE. [scholarly]
- **Bhadreśvara** — the founding cult. King **Bhadravarman I** ([scholarly] late 4th c. CE) established at My Son a temple to Śiva under the compound name **Bhadreśvara** — *Bhadra-* (from the king's own name Bhadravarman) fused with *-īśvara* (Śiva) — the Cham signature of merging royal and divine identity in the temple's very name. The shrine was rebuilt across centuries; later kings renamed the liṅga (e.g. **Śambhu-bhadreśvara**). (Cham Sanskrit inscriptions; [scholarly])
- **The kalan towers** — Cham temples are brick tower-shrines (**kalan**), each a compact Meru with a single cella housing the liṅga or a deity image, roofed in receding tiers. My Son's groups are labelled A–H by French archaeologists; the masterpiece tower **A1** was **destroyed by US bombing in 1969**. [scholarly]
- **Po Nagar (Nha Trang)** — the great Cham **goddess** sanctuary at Kauṭhāra, dedicated to **Yang Pô Inâ Nagar**, "Lady Mother of the Realm," identified in Sanskrit inscriptions with **Bhagavatī / Umā** and depicted as **Mahiṣāsura-mardinī**. The temple remained a living cult and is today shared with Vietnamese (Buddhist/folk) worship of the goddess as **Thiên Y A Na**. [scholarly] [regional]
- **Afterlife** — Champa was absorbed by Đại Việt (final fall of the core 1471 CE); most surviving Cham communities are today **Muslim (Bani/Cham Bani) or a residual Hindu (Cham Balamon)** tradition in Ninh Thuận/Bình Thuận, keeping Śiva-liṅga rites at the Po Klong Garai and Po Rome towers. [regional]

## III. Prambanan — the Javanese Trimūrti (Indonesia)

- **Candi Prambanan (Loro/Rara Jonggrang)** — the largest Hindu temple in Indonesia, on the Central Java plain near Yogyakarta, built [scholarly] c. **mid-9th century CE** (traditionally begun c. 850, associated with **Rakai Pikatan** of the Sañjaya line / Medang-Mataram kingdom), contemporary with the [Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/southeast-asian-ramayanas.md). It was raised as a Hindu answer to the nearby Śailendra Buddhist **Borobudur** and **Sewu**. [scholarly]
- **The Trimūrti plan** — three principal shrines on the central terrace, each to one face of the [Trimūrti](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md): the tallest (**~47 m**) to **Śiva Mahādeva** at centre, **Brahmā** to the south, **Viṣṇu** to the north. Facing them across the court stand three smaller **vāhana** shrines for the mounts — **Nandi** (bull, for Śiva), **Haṃsa** (goose, for Brahmā), and **Garuḍa** (for Viṣṇu). (Site epigraphy; [scholarly])
- **The Śiva temple's cellas** — the central tower holds four chambers: **Śiva Mahādeva** (main), **Durgā Mahiṣāsura-mardinī** in the north cella — locally the "slender maiden" **Rara Jonggrang** of legend — **[Agastya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md)** (as Śiva the southern teacher, *Dakṣiṇāmūrti*-type) in the south, and **Gaṇeśa** in the west. [scholarly]
- **Narrative reliefs** — the inner balustrades of the **Śiva and Brahmā** temples carry the **Rāmāyaṇa** in continuous relief; the **Viṣṇu** temple carries the **Kṛṣṇāyana / Bhāgavata** Kṛṣṇa-cycle. The Rāmāyaṇa here follows the same regional stream as the Old Javanese Kakawin. (Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa*; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* / *Harivaṃśa*; [scholarly])
  - *Legend (folk):* the site's popular name derives from **Rara Jonggrang** — a princess who set the suitor-demon **Bandung Bondowoso** to build a thousand temples in one night; she foiled the last and was cursed into the stone Durgā image. [folk]

## IV. Bali — the living tirtha system (Besakih, Tanah Lot, Tirtha Empul)

Bali is the one place where Southeast Asian Hinduism never became a ruin: it is a living **[Agama Hindu Dharma](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/balinese-hinduism-agama-hindu-dharma.md)**, historically called **Agama Tīrtha**, "the religion of holy water" — a geography organized around consecrated springs, lake-temples, sea-temples, and the volcanic Meru of Gunung Agung.

- **Pura Besakih** — the **"Mother Temple"** on the southwest slope of **Gunung Agung**, Bali's highest volcano (~3,000 m), treated as the island's terrestrial **Meru** and the axis of Balinese sacred space. Besakih is a **complex of ~23 temples**, centred on **Pura Penataran Agung**, and is the greatest of the *Sad-Kahyangan* ("six sanctuaries of the world") that anchor the island. Its shrines are crowded with **meru towers** — the tiered, odd-numbered-roofed pagodas named directly for Mount Meru. [regional]
- **Tirtha Empul** — the **holy-spring temple** at Tampaksiring, founded [scholarly] **962 CE** (Warmadewa dynasty), where pilgrims perform *melukat* (purificatory bathing) in the spouted pools. Its *sthala*-legend has **Indra** pierce the earth to release an *amṛta* spring to revive his poisoned army in the war against the tyrant **Mayadanawa** — a local re-casting of the Indra–asura and amṛta motifs. [regional] [folk]
- **Tanah Lot** — the iconic **sea-temple** on a tidal rock off Bali's southwest coast, one of a chain of coastal shrines to the **sea-gods (Bhaṭāra Segara / Baruṇa = [Varuṇa])**, guarding the island against maritime demons. Its founding is ascribed to the 16th-c. Javanese-Balinese reformer-priest **Dang Hyang Nirartha** (Pedanda Sakti Wawu Rauh), who also seeded **Pura Uluwatu** and other clifftop sea-temples. [regional]
- **The water-mandala** — Balinese geography is a nested system: **Pura Ulun Danu Bratan / Batur** (lake-temples of the goddess **Dewi Danu**) crown the caldera lakes that feed the **subak** irrigation-temple network (a UNESCO cultural landscape), while every village keeps its **Kahyangan Tiga** (Pura Puseh–Desa–Dalem) triad. Holy water (*tīrtha*), not image-worship, is the operative sacrament. [scholarly] [regional]

## V. Sri Lanka — the Pancha Ishwarams and Kataragama

- **The Pañca Īśvaram** — the **five ancient Śiva temples (Īśvarams)** traditionally sited around the coast of Laṅkā to sanctify and protect the island, revered by Tamil Śaivas: **Naguleśvaram** (Keerimalai, north/Jaffna), **Tiruketīśvaram** (Mannar, northwest), **Munneśvaram** (Chilaw, west), **Tenaveśvaram / Tondeśvaram** (Devinuwara/Dondra, south), and **Koneśvaram** (Trincomalee, east). [regional]
  - **Koneśvaram** — the "**Temple of a Thousand Pillars**" on Swami Rock, Trincomalee, styled **Dakṣiṇa-Kailāsa** ("Kailāsa of the South"); its *sthala*-legend ties it to **Rāvaṇa** and his mother's Śiva-worship, and it is sung in the Tamil **Tēvāram** — making it, with Tiruketīśvaram, one of the **[Pāḍal Petṟa Sthalams](275-paadal-petra-sthalams.md)** that lie outside the mainland. (Tēvāram; *Dakṣiṇa Kailāsa Purāṇam*) [regional]
  - *Note:* the individual temples were repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt (Portuguese-era demolitions of the 16th–17th c.; modern reconstructions), so the "five" is a **conceptual protective set**, not five continuously standing structures. [scholarly]
- **Kataragama (Katirkāmam)** — the great **Murugan / Skanda** sanctuary of the deep south, home of **Kataragama deviyo**, and one of the most striking **multi-faith** pilgrimage sites in South Asia: worshipped simultaneously by **Hindus** (as Murugan), **Buddhists** (as a guardian deity of the island), **Muslims** (associated with **al-Khiḍr**), and the indigenous **Vedda**. The god's local myth centres on his marriage to the **Vedda maiden Vaḷḷi**, giving the shrine its second-consort character. Its **Esala** festival features **fire-walking** and **kāvaḍi**. Some Tamil traditions rank Kataragama alongside the [Aṟupaḍai Vīḍu](arupadai-veedu-murugan.md) of Murugan. [regional] [folk]
- **Ramayana overlay** — a modern devotional circuit maps the [Rāmāyaṇa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/southeast-asian-ramayanas.md) onto Sri Lankan sites (the **Sītā Amman** temple at Sītā Eliya, Rāvaṇa caves, Divurumpola as the *agni-parīkṣā* spot). This "Ramayana Trail" is largely a **20th–21st-c. pilgrimage-tourism construction**, not an ancient consecration. [disputed] [folk]

## VI. The indenture & migration diaspora — Batu Caves and beyond

The second wave of extra-subcontinental sacred geography is modern: temples founded by **19th–20th-c. Tamil, Telugu, and North-Indian labour migration** (indentured and free) across the British and colonial world. See [Indenture-era diaspora Hinduism](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/indenture-diaspora-hinduism.md).

- **Batu Caves (Malaysia)** — a limestone-cave temple complex north of Kuala Lumpur dedicated to **[Murugan](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md)**, developed by the trader **K. Thamboosamy Pillai** from [scholarly] **c. 1890**. A **272-step** climb past a colossal gilded **Murugan statue (42.7 m, dedicated 2006)** reaches the Temple Cave; it is the global focus of the **Thaipūsam** kāvaḍi pilgrimage, drawing over a million devotees. [regional]
- **Ganga Talao / Grand Bassin (Mauritius)** — a sacred **crater-lake** in the highlands, held to be linked to the [Gaṅgā](haridwar-rishikesh-ganga-dwara.md), the terminus of a mass **Mahāśivarātri** foot-pilgrimage by the island's indenture-descended Hindu majority; a giant **Śiva (Maṅgal Mahādev)** statue stands at its shore. [regional]
- **Other diaspora anchors** — the **Sri Mariamman** temples of **Singapore** (1827, the city's oldest Hindu temple) and **Bangkok**; the temple networks of **Trinidad, Guyana, Suriname, Fiji, and Durban (South Africa)**; and the Nallur–Jaffna Murugan complex feeding the same Śaiva-Tamil devotional world. These transplant *Bhārata's* [tīrtha](README.md) logic onto new soil rather than reproducing a Meru-model temple-mountain. [regional]
