# Kanipakam Varasiddhi Vinayaka — the self-manifest Ganesha in the well

> At **Kāṇipākam** (Chittoor district, **Andhra Pradesh**), on the banks of the **Bāhuda** stream, three disabled brothers digging a well strike a stone that **bleeds** — and are cured; the stone is a **svayambhū** (self-manifest) Gaṇeśa, worshipped ever since seated in the spring-fed **well-water of the sanctum**, held to be slowly **growing**, and famed across the Deccan as a **divine arbiter of oaths** before whom liars are undone. A Kali-Yuga *kṣetra* of Gaṇeśa as *Varasiddhi Vināyaka* ("boon-and-accomplishment-granting Vināyaka"), traditionally endowed by **Kulottuṅga Cōḻa I**.

**Names of the deity and place** — the god is **Śrī Varasiddhi Vināyaka Swāmy**, Gaṇeśa in his boon-granting (*vara*) and accomplishment-conferring (*siddhi*) aspect, the same epithet invoked in the household **Varasiddhi Vināyaka Vrata** of Bhādrapada Śukla Caturthī (Gaṇeśa Caturthī). The place-name **Kāṇipākam** is glossed in local tradition from Tamil–Telugu roots — **kāṇi** ("wetland / a measure of low, water-fed paddy land") + **pākam / parakam** ("the flowing-in, the swelling of water into it") — i.e. "the [land] into which the water flowed," the marshy paddy-field where the spring welled up over the buried image. The whole cycle is transmitted as the **Kāṇipākam sthala-purāṇa**, a regional Telugu oral-and-manuscript tradition rather than a fixed Sanskrit recension. (*Kāṇipākam Sthala-Purāṇa*; temple tradition) [regional] [folk]

## I. The two clocks — a timeless svayambhū, a datable temple

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — like the [Aṣṭavināyaka](ashtavinayaka.md) murtis and the Śaiva [jyotirliṅgas](12-jyotirlingas.md), the Kāṇipākam Gaṇeśa is claimed as **svayambhū** — self-arisen from the earth, never carved or artisan-consecrated — and therefore framed as **beginningless**, its manifestation belonging to no dated moment but to the standing present of the current **[Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)** of the 28th Mahāyuga. The sthala-purāṇa does not fix a yuga for the three brothers; the miracle is told as timeless *māhātmya*. [regional]
- **Academic / historical frame** — the **documented** shrine is a South Indian temple whose earliest fabric is credited to **Kulottuṅga Cōḻa I** (r. **c. 1070–1120 CE**), with later Vijayanagara-era enlargement (**14th c. CE** onward). So the corpus keeps the frames apart: a **beginningless self-manifest** god, worshipped in a **c. 11th-century** and later building, its cult attested and administered into the present. **[scholarly]** c. 1000–1120 CE.

## II. The self-manifest well — the three disabled brothers

- **The setting: drought and a dry field** — the core legend opens in a season of failed rains at Kāṇipākam. **Three brothers**, poor cultivators, each bear a bodily affliction — in the standard telling **one is blind, one deaf/mute, and one lame** (the trio of the *tridoṣa*-afflicted, "the three handicapped brothers"). To water their parched paddy they set to **deepening a well** in the low wetland (*kāṇi*). (*Kāṇipākam Sthala-Purāṇa*) [folk]
  - *Variant (which three afflictions):* tellings differ on the exact disabilities — most name **blind, deaf, and dumb (mute)**; others substitute **lame** for one — but all agree on **three brothers, each disabled, cured together** at the same instant. The corpus records the disagreement rather than fixing one roster. [regional] [folk]
- **The spade strikes the stone** — as they dig, an iron implement (spade / pickaxe / crowbar) rings against a hard object in the pit. Striking it again to break it out, they gash it — and **blood wells up from the stone**, reddening the water that has begun to fill the well. (*Kāṇipākam Sthala-Purāṇa*) [folk]
- **The bleeding stone and the triple cure** — the water, mingled with the blood of the wounded stone, **splashes over the three brothers, and all three are instantly made whole** — sight, hearing, and speech (or the use of limb) restored. The self-wounding stone is recognized as a **svayambhū Gaṇeśa**; the wound-mark is still shown on the image, its blood the sign of a living, self-manifest god rather than dead rock. (*Kāṇipākam Sthala-Purāṇa*) [regional] [folk]
- **The well that will not be emptied** — the villagers, witnessing the miracle, try to draw the image out or bale the well dry, but the **spring cannot be exhausted** — the more water is removed the more wells up, and the image will not be lifted from its place. Read as the god's own refusal to be moved, this fixes the murti **in situ, submerged in its own spring**, where it remains the object of worship. The self-filling well is the structural cousin of Kedāra's and Amarnāth's "un-made" natural liṅgas — a *kṣetra* where the divine sets its own terms of dwelling. [folk]

## III. The ever-growing svayambhū — the god who outgrows his armour

- **Seated in the water of the sanctum** — uniquely among major Gaṇeśa shrines, the Kāṇipākam murti is worshipped **standing in spring-water within the *garbhagṛha***; the sanctum floor is the mouth of the perennial well, and the water is held to be permanently self-renewing (a *jala-Gaṇapati* in effect). Water from the sanctum is distributed as **tīrtha**. (temple tradition) [regional]
- **The svayambhū is growing** — Kāṇipākam's signature claim is that the self-manifest image is **slowly increasing in size** — the living stone still growing as it "grew" out of the earth. The stock proof offered by the temple: a **silver *kavaca* (armour/covering)** donated by a devotee **decades ago no longer fits** the murti, the knees/belly having enlarged beyond it — cited as visible evidence of an ongoing, organic svayambhū rather than a static icon. [folk] [disputed]
  - *Variant (measure of the growth):* popular accounts give differing spans — that the image has visibly grown "over fifty years," or across generations of priestly memory; the growth is a **devotional / [folk]** claim, not a measured or [scholarly] one, and is cataloged here as tradition, not established fact. [disputed]
- **Signs read on the image** — devotees and priests read **perspiration, swelling, or changes on the murti** as the god's responses — especially in the oath-ordeal context below — reinforcing the sense of a **living, reactive** deity rather than an inert consecrated stone. [folk]

## IV. Kāṇipākam the oath-court — *satya-pramāṇam* before Vināyaka

- **The god as arbiter of truth** — Kāṇipākam's most distinctive living function is as a **shrine of oaths**: disputants who cannot settle a quarrel — over property, debt, theft, honour, or a broken promise — come to **swear before Varasiddhi Vināyaka**, on the belief that a **false oath taken here brings swift divine retribution** while the truthful are vindicated. The god is, in effect, a **court of last resort**. (temple tradition) [regional] [folk]
- **The rite of swearing** — the accused/oath-taker typically **bathes in the Bāhuda stream / the sacred tank**, then, wet-clothed, swears their innocence or the truth of their claim **before the deity in the sanctum**; the belief is that a perjurer will not survive the oath unscathed — variously that they **fall ill, vomit blood, or die within days**, or are otherwise visibly stricken — so that the mere **willingness to swear at Kāṇipākam** is itself treated as strong proof of honesty, the guilty commonly confessing rather than face the god. [folk]
- **Recognized beyond the temple** — the Kāṇipākam oath acquired a quasi-**judicial** standing in the region: local disputes were historically **remitted to the shrine for oath-settlement**, and the practice is popularly said to have been acknowledged even by courts as a means of resolving matters otherwise unprovable — a rare instance of a temple functioning as a socially-sanctioned truth-ordeal into modern times. [regional] [disputed]
- **A Gaṇapati parallel to the "witness" gods** — this oath-arbiter role places Kāṇipākam alongside the corpus's other **deity-as-witness / vindicator** legends — the walking [Sākṣī-Gopāl](sakshi-gopal-witness-krishna.md) who testifies for a wronged devotee, and the *bhakti* "mobile god" cluster — but here the god arbitrates by **punishing the liar** rather than travelling to testify: Vināyaka the remover of obstacles doubling as remover of falsehood. [scholarly]

## V. The Bāhuda stream and the wider kṣetra

- **The Bāhuda river of atonement** — the shrine stands on the **Bāhuda** (Bāhu-nadī / Bāhuda), a stream whose very name local tradition ties to a legend of **sin confessed and expiated** here — the water associated with the cleansing of guilt and the restoring of truth, in keeping with the site's oath-ordeal character. Pilgrims bathe in it before *darśana* and oath. (temple tradition) [regional] [folk]
- **The temple triad** — Kāṇipākam is not a single shrine but a small **cluster of three temples**: the **Varasiddhi Vināyaka** (the presiding Gaṇeśa in the well), the nearby **Śrī Maṇikaṇṭheśvara Swāmy** (Śiva) temple, and the **Śrī Varadarāja Swāmy** (Viṣṇu) temple — a Gaṇapatya–Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava grouping typical of a mature Deccan *kṣetra*. (temple tradition) [regional]
- **Varasiddhi Vināyaka Vrata** — the temple is the great public home of the **Varasiddhi Vināyaka** form invoked in the domestic **Bhādrapada Caturthī vrata**, so its cult overlaps the pan-Indian Gaṇeśa-Caturthī householder rite while grounding it in a specific self-manifest image. [regional]

## VI. History, festival, and the datable clock

- **Cōḻa foundation** — temple tradition and the district record credit the original stone shrine over the well to **Kulottuṅga Cōḻa I** (**c. 1070–1120 CE**), the Cōḻa–Cāḷukya emperor, consistent with the Cōḻa expansion into the Chittoor uplands. **[scholarly]** c. late 11th c. CE.
- **Vijayanagara enlargement** — the temple was **extended in the 14th century CE** and after, under **Vijayanagara** patronage — the phase that gave the complex much of its present scale, as with countless Rāyalasīma shrines. **[scholarly]** 14th c. CE onward.
- **The long Brahmotsavam** — Kāṇipākam's signature festival is a famously **long Vināyaka Brahmotsavam** beginning on **Gaṇeśa (Vinayaka) Caturthī** and running for about **twenty / twenty-one days** — among the longest Gaṇeśa festivals anywhere — with processional *vāhanas*, *rathotsava*, and immense pilgrim crowds; it is a major event of the Andhra festal calendar. (temple tradition) [regional]
- **A living svayambhū cult** — unlike a purely legendary node, Kāṇipākam pins together a **beginningless self-manifest god**, a **datable Cōḻa-and-Vijayanagara building**, and a **still-practised oath-ordeal** — a rare case where a *sthala-purāṇa* miracle, a historical temple, and a functioning social-judicial rite persist together into the documented present. [scholarly]
