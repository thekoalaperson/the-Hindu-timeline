# Tuljapur Tulja Bhavani — goddess of the Sāḍe-tīn Pīṭhas and Shivaji's sword

> The head-shrine of the Marathi country's Goddess: on the **Yamunācalā hill** of the **Bālā-ghāṭ** range at **Tuljapur** (Dhārāshiv / Osmanabad district, Marathwada, Maharashtra), where **Tvaritā** — "she who came in haste" — descended at the cry of the sage-widow **Anubhūti**, slew the demon **Kukura** in buffalo-form, and **stayed** as a standing power-seat. First of the **"three-and-a-half" (sāḍe-tīn) Śakti Pīṭhas** of Maharashtra and **kuladevī of the Bhosale house of Shivaji**, whose **Bhavānī sword** takes her name. This file is the *kṣetra* (place) treatment; the goddess's full myth and cult are the canonical home at **[Tulja Bhavani of Tuljapur](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/tulja-bhavani.md)**.
>

**Names of the kṣetra** — the town is **Tuljapur** ("the town of Tuḷjā"), the goddess's own name given to her seat; her hill is the **Yamunācalā** (var. *Yamunādri*), a spur of the **Bālā-ghāṭ** overlooking the **Marathwada** plateau. The presiding goddess is **Tuḷjā Bhavānī** (**Āī Bhavānī**, "Mother Bhavānī"), whose Sanskrit name **Tvaritā** — "she who hastens" — is folded by the *sthala* etymology into the place: *Tvaritā → Turajā → Tuḷjā*, the goddess who came **instantly** to a devotee's cry, and the town that bears her hurrying name (§II). The protected zone is the **Tuljā-kṣetra** on the sunken court of the Yamunācalā. (Tuljā-māhātmya, attrib. *Skanda Purāṇa*, Sahyādri-khaṇḍa; sthala-tradition) [late text] [regional]

---

## I. The kṣetra — the sunken seat on the Yamunācalā hill

- **A descending temple, not an ascending one** — unlike most hill-shrines one *climbs* to, Tuljapur is entered by **descending flights of steps** through successive gateways to the **sunken court (sabhā-maṇḍapa and garbhagṛha below grade)** where the goddess sits; the *kṣetra* is a hollow of śakti scooped into the Yamunācalā spur, not a summit temple. (temple tradition; Wikipedia, *Tulja Bhavani Temple*) [regional]
- **The Bālā-ghāṭ setting** — the hill belongs to the **Bālā-ghāṭ** escarpment of the **Marathwada** uplands, in **Dhārāshiv (Osmanabad) district** — the interior Deccan tableland between the Godāvarī and Bhīmā basins, well south of the Sahyādri crest, so the goddess's "Sahyādri-khaṇḍa" charter is a *regional* rather than a literal Konkan-ghat placement. [scholarly] [regional]
- **The two great gateways** — pilgrims pass the **Sardar Nimbalkar** and **Rājā Śāhāji / Parmār** gateways (named for the Maratha patrons who built them) into the temple court; the fabric is a layered **Yādava-to-Maratha** construction (§V), the gates and much of the maṇḍapa being **Maratha-period** additions to an older core. (temple tradition) [regional] [scholarly]

## II. The charter myth, localized on the ground

*The full narrative — Anubhūti, the demon Kukura, the Tvaritā etymology, the Mahiṣa and Mātaṅga slayings — is catalogued at the deity home, [Tulja Bhavani](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/tulja-bhavani.md) §3. Here it is told as it is fixed to the kṣetra's soil.*

- **Anubhūti's penance on the Mandākinī** — the widow of the ṛṣi **Kardama**, **Anubhūti**, undertakes fierce penance to the **[Devī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)** on the banks of a stream the *māhātmya* calls the **Mandākinī**, identified in temple tradition with the water at the foot of the Yamunācalā — so the tīrtha where she prayed is shown on the ground. (Tuljā-māhātmya, attrib. *Skanda Purāṇa* Sahyādri-khaṇḍa) [late text] [regional]
- **The demon Kukura and the Goddess who hastened** — the *daitya* **Kukura** (var. **Kūkur**) assails the ascetic; at her cry the Goddess arrives **tvarita** (in haste), fights the demon who takes **buffalo (mahiṣa) form**, and **beheads him** on the hill — re-enacting the **Mahiṣāsura-mardinī** act of the [Devī-Māhātmya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) on Deccan soil, and giving the seat its slain-buffalo iconography. (Tuljā-māhātmya) [regional]
- **The seat-fixing** — at Anubhūti's prayer the Goddess consents to **remain permanently** on the **Yamunācalā** — the move that turns a *māhātmya* rescue into a standing **pīṭha**; every later stratum (circuit-head, kuladevī, Maratha charter) rests on this "she stayed here" clause. (Tuljā-māhātmya; sthala-tradition) [regional]
- **The Mātaṅga and Mahiṣa localizations** — Tuljapur tradition additionally claims the slaying of the demon **Mātaṅga** (who oppressed gods and sages, the devas led by **Brahmā** appealing to her) *and* the great **Mahiṣāsura** killing itself for its hill — a *sthala* appropriation of the Devī-Māhātmya's battles. (sthala-tradition) [folk] [regional]
  - *Variant (Devī-Māhātmya, Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa):* in the canonical text the Mahiṣa-slaying is **not localized** to any Deccan site — it is narrated within the frame-story of the coming Sāvarṇi manvantara told to king Suratha; the placement on the Bālā-ghāṭ is the temple-tradition's own. (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 81–93) [scholarly]
- **Rāma at the seat** — a local recension makes **Rāma**, southbound in search of Sītā, receive the goddess's blessing here — earning her the epithet **Rāmavaradāyinī** ("she who gave the boon to Rāma") and folding Tuljapur into Rāmāyaṇa geography, as many Deccan kṣetras do. (sthala-tradition) [folk] [regional]

## III. The tīrthas, the movable image, and the sleep-cycles

- **The svayambhū Mahiṣāsura-mardinī mūrti** — the object of worship is a c. **three-foot granite / black-stone, eight-armed** image of the goddess **spearing the buffalo-demon**, weapons (trident, sword, bow, discus among them) in her hands and the severed **mahiṣa** at her feet — held **self-manifest (svayambhū)** by tradition. (temple tradition) [regional]
- **A rare *cala* (movable) goddess** — unusually among major svayambhū seats, Tuljā Bhavānī's image is **movable**: at fixed points of the year it is lifted from its **siṁhāsana (throne)**, the ritual precondition for the seat's famous sleep-cycles and its palanquin processions — a feature that distinguishes this kṣetra from the fixed *liṅga*- or stone-seats of most pīṭhas. (temple tradition) [regional]
- **The thrice-yearly nidrā (sleep)** — the goddess is held to **sleep three times a year** (~21 days total), the image removed from the throne and laid on a **silver bed (cāndī palang)**; the cycles are keyed to her battles and the two Navarātras — **Ghor nidrā** (Bhādrapada, ending as she wakes for the Śāradīya Navarātri), **Śram nidrā** (the "sleep of weariness" after Vijayādaśamī, her rest from the demon-war), and **Bhog nidrā** (Pauṣa, before the Śākambharī Navarātri). (temple tradition) [regional] [folk]
  - *Note:* the **names, order and exact dates** of the three nidrās vary between temple calendars and popular accounts; the fixed points are *three sleeps, ~21 days, tied to the two Navarātras and the post-victory rest.* [disputed] [folk]
- **The tīrthas of the kṣetra** — the *māhātmya* and living practice fix a set of sacred waters within the seat, bathed in before *darśana*: the **Gomukh tīrtha** (the cow-mouth spring), the **Kallola tīrtha** (whose waters are said to swell in "waves," *kallola*), and the **Mandākinī / Viṣṇu tīrtha** at the foot of the hill — a compact tīrtha-cluster that makes the descent into the court a bathing-pilgrimage as much as a shrine-visit. (Tuljā-māhātmya; temple tradition) [regional]
- **The Cintāmaṇi oracle** — a distinctive Tuljapur rite: the **Cintāmaṇi**, a pair of dice / stone counters before the goddess, is consulted as an **oracle** — pilgrims (and, in the bakhar tradition, Shivaji himself) put a question and read the goddess's *yes / no* from the throw, a living divinatory feature peculiar to this kṣetra. (temple / bakhar tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **Gondhaḷ and the goddess's bards** — Tuljā Bhavānī (with [Reṇukā of Māhūr](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/renuka-yellamma.md)) is the chief goddess of the Marathi **gondhaḷ** rite: hereditary performer-priests (**Gondhaḷī**, **Bhope**) sing her deeds by torch and *sambal*-drum at weddings and lineage vows — the kuladevī's living liturgy outside the Sanskrit frame, carried too in the [Dhangar oral epics](../90-literature-corpus/dhangar-oral-epics-biroba-mhaskoba-dhuloba.md) of the pastoral communities who claim her. [folk] [regional]

## IV. Head of the Sāḍe-tīn Śakti Pīṭha circuit

- **First of the "three-and-a-half" pīṭhas** — Marathi sacred geography counts **sāḍe-tīn (3½) Śakti Pīṭhas** as the Goddess's seats over the Marathi land, with **Tuljā Bhavānī of Tuljapur** most often named the **first / head seat**: the three full pīṭhas being **Tuljapur**, **[Mahālakṣmī / Ambābāī of Kolhapur](kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md)** and **[Reṇukā of Māhūr](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/renuka-yellamma.md)**, with **Saptaśṛṅgī of Vaṇī** (Nashik district) as the **ardha (half) pīṭha** — a living regional circuit **distinct from** the pan-Indian [51](51-shakti-peethas.md) and 18-Mahā lists (see [Temple Networks & Circuits](temple-networks-and-circuits.md)). (Marathi sthala-tradition; Wikipedia, *Three and a half Shakti Peethas*) [regional] [folk]
  - *Variant (assignments differ):* which seat is *ādi* (first) and which is the "half" **varies by telling** — most popular lists make Saptaśṛṅgī the half-seat and rank Tuljapur first, but some recensions order the four differently or rank Saptaśṛṅgī a full or original pīṭha. The variants are cataloged, not resolved. [disputed] [folk]
- **Not a Satī-limb pīṭha by the canonical roster** — Tuljapur's authority is a **māhātmya seat** (the Goddess *chose to stay* after the Anubhūti rescue), **not** a fallen body-part of [Satī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) in the classical **51 (var. 52) Śakti Pīṭha** enumeration (*Tantra Cūḍāmaṇi*), which does **not** list Tuljapur; its pīṭha-standing rests on the regional sāḍe-tīn reckoning and the Devī-Māhātmya-type charter, exactly as its circuit-partner [Kolhapur](kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md) belongs to the 18-Mahā list rather than the 51. [scholarly]
- **One Devī in four seats** — in Marathi devotion Bhavānī of Tuljapur, [Ambābāī of Kolhapur](kolhapur-mahalakshmi-karavira.md), [Reṇukā of Māhūr](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/renuka-yellamma.md) and Saptaśṛṅgī of Vaṇī are read as the **one Great Goddess** distributed across the land, while each keeps her own myth, calendar and clientele — the theology that binds the circuit. The paired *male* Deccan tutelaries — [Khaṇḍobā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md) and [Jyotibā of Kolhapur](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/jyotiba-kedarling-kolhapur.md) — form the complementary kuladaivat map. [regional]

## V. Shivaji, the kuladevī, and the Bhavānī sword

- **Kuladevī of the Bhosales and of the many** — Tuljā Bhavānī is the **clan-goddess (kuladevī) of the Bhosale house** of **Chhatrapati Shivaji (r. 1674–1680 CE)** [scholarly], and kuladevī to a vast range of **Maratha, Kunbi, Dhangar, Māḷī, Gondhaḷī** and border Kannada–Telugu communities; "**Jay Bhavānī**" became — with "Jay Śivājī" — the Maratha war-cry. (Maratha bakhar and povāḍā tradition) [regional] [folk]
- **Afzal Khan and the shrine (1659 CE)** — Maratha tradition holds that the Bijapur general **Afzal Khan**, marching on Shivaji in **1659**, **desecrated the Tuljapur shrine** (the image he broke a stand-in, the true mūrti hidden by its priests in the devotional telling), and that **Bhavānī assured Shivaji of victory** — fulfilled at **Pratapgad (10 Nov 1659)** where Afzal Khan was killed. The raid and the Pratapgad encounter are documented Maratha–Bijapur history; the goddess's assurances are hagiography. (Maratha bakhar tradition) [regional] [folk] — battle dating [scholarly]
- **The Bhavānī sword** — in the widely-told legend the goddess **herself appeared to Shivaji and gave him a divine sword**, the **"Bhavānī talvār,"** pledging him victory and the founding of *Hindavī svarājya*; stronger tellings have her **enter his sword-arm** at moments of crisis. Modern historians read the epiphany as legend and the historical kernel as a naming: an excellent **imported (often said European / Genoese-traded) blade** acquired for Shivaji and **named "Bhavānī"** after his kuladevī. (Maratha povāḍā and bakhar tradition; later hagiography) [folk] [oral tradition] — [scholarly] [disputed]
  - *The "Jagdambā" sword in London:* a famous Bhosale sword named **"Jagdambā"** (after the goddess as **Jagadambā**, "Mother of the world"), kept by the Kolhapur Chhatrapatis and popularly identified with the Bhavānī sword, was **presented to the Prince of Wales (later Edward VII) in 1875–76** and remains in the **British Royal Collection**; whether Jagdambā *is* the Bhavānī sword is itself contested, and its return is a recurring Maharashtrian demand. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The Rāmdās current** — the Bhavānī devotion of Shivaji's circle runs together with the Śakti-charged hymnody of **[Samarth Rāmdās](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/samarth-ramdas.md)**; tradition links guru, goddess and king in the *svarājya* piety, though Rāmdās's precise role is debated among historians. [regional] [disputed]

## VI. The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic clock** — the Anubhūti rescue, the Kukura / Mahiṣa and Mātaṅga slayings, the Rāma boon, and the Goddess's permanent enthronement on the Yamunācalā are **māhātmya events of the Purāṇic deep-past**, kept active as a standing seat through the present **[Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)** (which began **3102 BCE** on the scriptural clock). [late text] [regional]
- **Academic / historical clock** — the **Tuljā-māhātmya** survives as a **regional Purāṇic appendix attributed to the Skanda Purāṇa (Sahyādri-khaṇḍa)**, not the early Purāṇic core; the **standing temple** is generally assigned to the **Yādava era (c. 12th–13th c. CE)** with heavy **Maratha-period and modern** rebuilding; the goddess's pan-Maharashtrian apogee is **documented from the 17th c.** (Afzal Khan 1659, Shivaji's coronation 1674) and the **Jagdambā sword's 1875–76 passage to Britain** is [recorded history](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/recorded-history.md). The corpus keeps both frames without collapsing one into the other. [scholarly]
