# Kashmir tirthas — the Nilamata sacred landscape

> The sacred geography of the Kashmir valley (*Kaśmīra*, "of Kaśyapa") as charted by the **Nīlamata Purāṇa** and Kalhaṇa's **Rājataraṅgiṇī**: a drained lake-bed read as the body of the Goddess, laced with nāga-springs and *tīrthas*. In scriptural time the valley is **Satīsaras**, "the lake of Satī (Umā)," reclaimed at the head of the present Vaivasvata Manvantara; in academic time it is a glacial-lacustrine basin whose shrine-cult is documented from the **[scholarly] 6th–12th century CE** onward. This file gathers the *sites* (springs, hills, temples, peeth); the draining cosmogony's canonical home is [Kaśyapa](../04-deep-dives/kashyapa.md) and the charter-text's map is the [Nīlamata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/nilamata-purana.md).
>

**Kashmir** (Sanskrit *Kaśmīra*, from *Kaśyapa-mīra*/*Kaśyapa-mara*, "the lake — or land — of Kaśyapa") is treated across its own scripture as a divinized country: the land *is* Umā/Pārvatī, its great river *is* the Goddess flowing, and its innumerable springs *are* nāgas. The **two clocks** run side by side throughout: the **scriptural** frame places the lake's draining at the transition into the present Manvantara and the *Nīlamata*'s teaching-frame in early **[Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)** (Janamejaya's generation, just after the Bhārata war), while the **academic** frame dates the *Nīlamata* to **[scholarly] c. 6th–8th c. CE**, the Mārtāṇḍa temple to the 8th c., Śāradā's floruit to the 8th–12th c., and the living Kṣīr Bhavānī and Amarnāth cults to their documented centuries. Names, body-part assignments and legends vary between recensions and are cataloged, not merged, below.

## The making of the valley — Satīsaras drained

*Summary only; the canonical treatment of the draining myth (with variants) is [Kaśyapa](../04-deep-dives/kashyapa.md), and the charter-text map is the [Nīlamata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/nilamata-purana.md).*

- **Satīsaras, the lake through six manvantaras** — From the beginning of the *kalpa* the whole basin was the mountain-lake **Satīsaras**, "the lake of Satī (Umā)," which remained water through the **first six manvantaras**, becoming habitable land only in the present **[Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)**. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, cosmography) [regional]
- **Jalodbhava slain, the valley named for Kaśyapa** — The water-demon **Jalodbhava** ("water-born"), invincible while in his element, is destroyed when **Kaśyapa's** austerities bring the gods: the mountain-barrier at **Varāhamūla (Baramulla)** is breached, the waters run out, and **Viṣṇu** slays the demon on the drained ground. The land is thereafter **Kaśmīra, "of Kaśyapa,"** settled with **nāgas under Nīla** and brahmins. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; Rājataraṅgiṇī 1.25–27) [regional]
- **Kashmir is Pārvatī** — Both the *Nīlamata* and Kalhaṇa lay down the governing maxim that **the valley itself is the Goddess** — "Kaśmīrā is Pārvatī; its king a portion of Śiva" — which makes every spring, hill and river in it a limb of the divine landscape. (Rājataraṅgiṇī 1.42) [regional]

## The Vitastā — Pārvatī flowing (the Jhelum)

- **The river as the Goddess descended** — To purify the piśāca-defiled valley at Kaśyapa's entreaty, **Śiva** makes **Pārvatī flow forth as the river Vitastā (Jhelum)**, emerging through a channel his trident opens **one *vitasti* (a hand-span) deep — hence her name *Vitastā*.** Vexed by pollution she vanishes and must be coaxed back; other goddesses accompany her as the valley's tributaries. (Nīlamata Purāṇa) [regional] — the Vitastā among the seven rivers: [Sapta-Sindhu & the Indus rivers](sapta-sindhu-and-the-indus-rivers.md).
- **Verinag / the Nīlakuṇḍa — the source** — The Vitastā is held to rise at the deep blue spring **Verinag (Nīlanāga / Nīlakuṇḍa)** at the valley's head, the very seat of **Nīla-nāga** whose ordinances the *Nīlamata* records. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; living Kashmiri tradition) [regional]
- **The Vitastā's birthday (Vyeth Truvah)** — Worship of the river **on the day of her manifestation** — Bhādrapada bright 13th in living usage — is kept by **Kashmiri Pandits** as **Vyeth Truvah** (*Vyeth* being the Kashmiri name of the Vitastā). (Nīlamata Purāṇa; living Kashmiri Pandit practice) [regional] [folk]

## The nāga-springs — waters that are serpents

- **Springs as nāgas by the hundred** — The *Nīlamata* catalogs the valley's sacred waters as **named serpent-deities**, each spring or lake the seat of a nāga — the textual root of the living Kashmiri usage in which springs are still called **"nāg."** (Nīlamata Purāṇa, nāga-roster) [regional]
- **The great serpent-waters** — Among the roster's enduring identifications: **Ananta-nāga** (the springs of **Anantnag** town), **Mahāpadma-nāga** in the great lake **Mahāpadmasaras (Wular)**, **Takṣaka-nāga** at Jayavana (Zewan), and **Śeṣa's** high lake **Śeṣanāga (Sheshnag)** on the Amarnāth route. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; Kashmiri tradition) [regional] — the serpent-race: [Nāgas & Serpents](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md).

## Kṣīr Bhavānī — Rājñā Devī of Tulmul

The valley's foremost goddess-*tīrtha* and the tutelary shrine of the **Kashmiri Pandits**: the spring-temple of **Kṣīr Bhavānī** ("Bhavānī of milk") at **Tulmul** (Tulamula), north of Srinagar, where the goddess **Rājñā** ("the Queen"; Kashmiri *Ragnya* / *Ragnya Devi*) resides in a sacred spring. She is counted among the great heirs of the *Nīlamata*'s sacred map. [regional]

- **The color-omen spring** — The goddess is worshipped in a **hexagonal (ṣaṭkoṇa) spring** whose water is held to **change color** as an omen: milky-white, rose, red, green or blue in normal times, but turning **blackish or muddy-red before calamity** for Kashmir. The 1947 and later upheavals are locally read into darkenings of the spring. (living Kashmiri Pandit tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The milk and *kṣīr* offering** — Devotees offer **milk and *kheer* (rice-pudding, *kṣīra*)** at the spring — the offering that names the shrine **Kṣīr Bhavānī**; no blood-offering is made here, the goddess being received in her benign (*saumya*) milk-loving form. [regional] [folk]
- **The Lanka-transfer legend** — In the shrine's *sthala* legend the goddess **Rājñā was originally installed by Rāvaṇa in Laṅkā**, but — displeased by his conduct — asked **Hanumān** to carry her image and her spring-seat north to Kashmir, where she settled at Tulmul; the myth binds the Kashmiri goddess to the pan-Indian Rāmāyaṇa. (Kṣīr Bhavānī *sthala* tradition) [folk] [regional]
  - *Variant:* other tellings make **Hanumān** transport not an image but the presiding *nāga*-spring itself, or have the goddess reveal her buried Tulmul seat to a Pandit sage (Kṛṣṇa Paṇḍita / Govinda Joo Gadoo) in a dream in later centuries — the "rediscovery" strand rather than the Laṅkā-transfer strand. [folk] [regional]
- **Jyeṣṭhā Aṣṭamī — the great mela** — The shrine's principal festival is **Jyeṣṭhā Aṣṭamī** (the eighth of the bright half of Jyeṣṭha, May–June), the goddess's *jayantī*, when Pandits from across the valley and the diaspora gather at the spring; it is the central annual pilgrimage of Kashmiri Pandit identity. (living Kashmiri Pandit practice) [regional] [folk]
- **Tutelary of Kashmiri Pandit identity** — With the **1990 exodus** of the Pandits, Kṣīr Bhavānī became the diaspora's chief pilgrimage-anchor and emblem of return; replica shrines (e.g. at Jammu and beyond) carry the cult, and the Jyeṣṭhā Aṣṭamī gathering at Tulmul is a marker of continued Pandit presence in the valley. [scholarly] [regional]
- *Two-clocks note [scholarly]:* Rājñā/Ragnya is a **regional** goddess of the *Nīlamata*'s tīrtha-heritage; her present temple structures and the recorded color-omen and mela belong to **documented Kali-Yuga history** (the marble temple over the spring is early-20th-century, associated with Mahārāja Pratāp Singh), while her Laṅkā-transfer and spring-manifestation are told in *sthala*/scriptural time. The frames are kept apart.

## Harmukh and Gaṅgabal — the northern Kailāsa

- **Harmukh, the abode of Śiva** — The peak **Harmukh (Haramukuṭa, "Hara's crown/diadem," ~5,142 m)** in the north of the valley is revered as an abode of **Śiva**, a Kashmiri counterpart to **Kailāsa**; folk tradition forbids its summit to climbers as the god's own seat. [regional] [folk] — cf. the cosmic-mountain complex: [Meru, Kailāsa & the Cosmic Mountains](meru-kailash-and-cosmic-mountains.md).
- **Gaṅgabal — the high lake and the ash-immersion** — Below Harmukh lies the alpine lake **Gaṅgabal (Uttaraganga / Haramukh-Gaṅgā)**, revered as a Kashmiri **Gaṅgā**. Kashmiri Pandits made pilgrimage here to **immerse the ashes and bones of their dead**, the northern equivalent of the Gaṅgā-immersion at Haridwar/Prayāga; the **Harmukh-Gaṅga yātrā** (often around the Bhādrapada full moon) is the valley's great death-rite pilgrimage. (living Kashmiri Pandit tradition) [regional] [folk]

## Mārtāṇḍa — the Sun temple of Kashmir

- **The great Sūrya temple at Mattan** — The ruined **Mārtāṇḍa** temple (*Mārtāṇḍa*, "the sun," an epithet of **Sūrya**) on the Mattan karewa near Anantnag is Kashmir's most celebrated ancient shrine, dedicated to the **Sun-god**; it commands the valley from a plateau, its colonnaded court and trefoil-arched sanctum among the finest of early Kashmiri stone architecture. [scholarly] — the Sun-god's household: [Sūrya & his family](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/surya-and-his-family.md); cf. the eastern Sun-temple: [Konark](konark-sun-temple-arka-kshetra.md).
- **Attributed to Lalitāditya** — By Kalhaṇa's chronicle the temple was built (or its cult monumentalized) by **Lalitāditya Muktāpīḍa** of the **Kārkoṭa** dynasty, **[scholarly] c. 725–756 CE** (8th century) — placing it firmly in **documented Kali-Yuga history**, not myth. (Rājataraṅgiṇī 4; art-historical dating) [scholarly]
  - *Note:* Kashmiri tradition also links the site to older Sun-worship (a *Mārtāṇḍa-tīrtha* and spring predating the surviving temple), so Lalitāditya's building monumentalized an existing solar cult rather than founding it. [regional] [scholarly]
- **The Bhaviṣya / karewa tradition** — Local legend derives the name-place from the sage **Kaśyapa's** solar rites and the birth of **Mārtāṇḍa** (the "dead-egg" son of Aditi whom Sūrya's story explains); the temple's later demolition (attributed to Sikandar Shah, early 15th c.) belongs to documented history. [regional] [scholarly]

## Śāradā Pīṭha — goddess of learning on the Kishangangā

- **The seat of Sarasvatī/Śāradā** — On the **Kishangangā (Neelum/Kṛṣṇagaṅgā)** in the far west of greater Kashmir stands the ruined **Śāradā Pīṭha**, seat of the goddess **Śāradā** (a form of **[Sarasvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/saraswati.md)**), from which Kashmir earned the epithet **Śāradā-deśa** and its script the name **Śāradā**. [regional] — the tīrtha's *Nīlamata* pedigree: [Nīlamata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/nilamata-purana.md).
- **One of the Śakti Pīṭhas** — In the 18 Mahā-śakti-pīṭha reckoning (and the wider 51-list), **Śāradā Pīṭha** is counted a seat of the Goddess as **Sarasvatī/Śāradā**; the site now lies in ruins in **Pakistan-administered Kashmir (Neelum valley)**. (Śakti-pīṭha tradition) [cross-border] [disputed] — full list: [The 51 Śakti Pīṭhas](51-shakti-peethas.md).
- **A pan-Indian seat of learning** — Śāradā Pīṭha was a renowned **university and manuscript-library** of the early medieval period, drawing scholars from across India; the tradition that a *sarvajña-pīṭha* ("throne of the all-knowing") there was ascended from the south is attached to **[Ādi Śaṅkara](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/adi-shankara.md)** in his hagiographies. **[scholarly] c. 8th–12th c. CE** was its floruit. [scholarly] [regional]

## Śaṅkarācārya hill — Śiva over Srinagar

- **The Jyeṣṭheśvara temple on the hill** — The hill overlooking Srinagar and the Dal lake, now called **Śaṅkarācārya hill**, bears an ancient **Śiva** temple (**Jyeṣṭheśvara**), the older name of the hill being **Gopādri / Gopa-parvata**. [regional] — the Śiva myth-cycle: [Śiva myth-cycle](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md).
- **The Śaṅkara association** — The hill is named for **[Ādi Śaṅkara](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/adi-shankara.md)**, who tradition holds meditated there during his Kashmir visit and there received (or composed) the **Saundaryalaharī** / had his vision of Śakti — the renaming binding the site to the Advaita ācārya. The stone temple's fabric is variously dated; Kalhaṇa credits a Gopāditya with an early Jyeṣṭheśvara foundation, later repaired. (Rājataraṅgiṇī; hagiographic tradition) [regional] [disputed]

## The living Pandit calendar — Nīlamata rites that survive

- **Herath (Śivarātri)** — Śiva's night in Phālguna is kept by Kashmiri Pandits as **Herath** (< *Hara-rātri*), on the **trayodaśī** (a day before the pan-Indian caturdaśī), with the **vaṭuk** rite — water-vessels installed and worshipped as **Vaṭuka Bhairava**. (Nīlamata Purāṇa festival-ordinances; living practice) [regional] [folk] — the deity: [Vaṭuka Bhairava](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vatuka-bhairava-origin.md).
- **The Himalayan feasts** — The *Nīlamata*'s ordinance-calendar preserves purely valley rites with no pan-Indian counterpart: the **first-snowfall festival** (*nava-hima-pāta*) and the winter spirit-offerings (Pandit **Khetsimāvas**, food left for the yakṣa on the Pauṣa new moon) — the covenant with the valley's serpent- and spirit-populations that the *Nīlamata* frames as the terms of settlement. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; living practice) [regional] [folk]
- **Continuity into Kashmir Śaivism** — The valley the *Nīlamata* charters is the same soil that produced the **Kashmir Śaiva** (Trika/Pratyabhijñā) corpus of the **[scholarly] 9th–11th c. CE**; the nāga-springs, Vitastā rites and Herath remain live threads of Pandit practice. [scholarly] [regional] — see [Kashmir Śaiva texts](../90-literature-corpus/kashmir-shaiva-texts.md).
