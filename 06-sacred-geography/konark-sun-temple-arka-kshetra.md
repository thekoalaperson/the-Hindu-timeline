# Konārk Sun Temple — Arka Kṣetra

> The premier **Sūrya (Sun) temple** of India and a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** on the Odisha coast — the **Arka Kṣetra / Konārka**, where the Sun-god's shrine was conceived as a colossal stone **chariot (ratha)** drawn by **seven horses** on **twelve pairs of wheels**, built c. **1241–1258 CE** under the Eastern Gaṅga king **Narasiṃhadeva I**. Third leg of the Odisha triad beside [Jagannāth Purī](jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md) and [Liṅgarāja, Bhubaneswar](lingaraja-bhubaneswar-ekamra-kshetra.md).
>

**Arka Kṣetra** — "the field of **Arka** (the Sun)," also **Konārka** ("**Koṇa-Arka**," the Sun of the corner/angle) — is one of the few great Indian shrines dedicated not to Viṣṇu, Śiva or the Goddess but to **Sūrya** himself. Its glorification belongs to the solar-cult (*saura*) stratum of the Purāṇas: the **Brahma Purāṇa** (Konārka / Arka-kṣetra section), the **Sāmba Purāṇa**, and the **Bhaviṣya Purāṇa**, all of which carry the **Sāmba leprosy legend** that founds Sun-worship in India. As with neighbouring [Purī](jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md) and [Ekāmra](lingaraja-bhubaneswar-ekamra-kshetra.md), what follows separates the **legend-layer** (*māhātmya* / "scriptural clock") from the **historical layer** ("academic clock") wherever they diverge. For the genre, see [Sthala-Purāṇas of Major Temples](sthala-puranas-major-temples.md).

## I. The Sāmba Legend — Founding of the Solar Cult

The charter-myth of Arka Kṣetra is the **Sāmba (Śāmba) story**, the foundation narrative of organised Sun-worship in India, told in the **Sāmba Purāṇa** and **Bhaviṣya Purāṇa** and localised to Konārka in the **Brahma Purāṇa**.

- **Sāmba's pride and the curse** — **Sāmba**, son of **Kṛṣṇa** by **Jāmbavatī**, is exceptionally handsome and vain. Provoked variously by **Nārada**, he is cursed (by Kṛṣṇa, or by his own arrogance) and afflicted with **leprosy (kuṣṭha / white leprosy)**. (Sāmba Purāṇa; Bhaviṣya Purāṇa) [legend]
  - *Variant (the provocation) [late text]:* Nārada contrives for Sāmba to be seen amid Kṛṣṇa's wives, who are smitten; Kṛṣṇa, deceived into thinking Sāmba seduced them, curses him with disease — a frame echoing the Mahābhārata-era Yādava household.
- **The penance to Sūrya** — counselled that only **Sūrya**, lord of health and the dispeller of darkness and disease, can cure him, Sāmba undertakes a severe **tapas** to the Sun, traditionally for **twelve years**, on the banks of the **Candrabhāgā** river at **Mitravana** ("the grove of Mitra/the Sun"). (Sāmba Purāṇa) [legend]
- **The self-revealed image and the cure** — Sūrya is pleased; Sāmba bathes in the Candrabhāgā and recovers a **divine image of Sūrya** that the celestial architect **Viśvakarmā** had fashioned, found floating in the river. He installs it and is **cured of leprosy** — establishing the spot as **Arka Kṣetra**, the place where the Sun heals. (Sāmba Purāṇa; Brahma Purāṇa) [legend]
- **Importing the Maga priests** — finding no Brahmin willing to serve a "graven" Sun-image, Sāmba brings the **Maga (Bhojaka / Śākadvīpīya) priests** — sun-worshipping priests from **Śākadvīpa** (read by scholars as **Iranian / Zoroastrian magi**) — to officiate. This episode is the Purāṇic memory of the **Indo-Iranian solar cult's** entry into India. (Sāmba Purāṇa; Bhaviṣya Purāṇa) [scholarly]

## II. Arka Kṣetra in the Sacred Geography of the Sun

- **The triad of Sūrya-kṣetras** — Purāṇic geography names a set of great Sun-shrines linked to the Sāmba/Candrabhāgā cult; **Konārka (Arka Kṣetra)** in Odisha, **Mūlasthāna (Multan)** in the northwest, and **Mitravana / Kālapriya** are recurrently invoked as nodes of pan-Indian Sun-worship. Konārka becomes the eastern, sea-facing apex. (Sāmba Purāṇa; Bhaviṣya Purāṇa) [regional]
- **The Candrabhāgā tīrtha** — the now-silted river (and its sea-mouth) just north of the temple is the bathing-*tīrtha* of the legend; the annual **Candrabhāgā / Māgha Saptamī (Ratha Saptamī)** sunrise bath, when pilgrims face the rising Sun, re-enacts Sāmba's cure. (sthala-purāṇa / temple tradition) [regional]
- **The three-deity coast** — in living Odia tradition Konārk completes a sacred triangle with [Jagannātha at Purī](jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md) (Viṣṇu) and [Liṅgarāja at Bhubaneswar](lingaraja-bhubaneswar-ekamra-kshetra.md) (Śiva) — Sun, Viṣṇu and Śiva anchoring the Odisha *kṣetra*-landscape. [regional]
- **Sūrya among the Navagrahas** — as the chief **graha**, the Sun worshipped here connects to the wider parihāra geography of the [Navagraha Temples](navagraha-temples.md); Konārk's ruined **Navagraha slab** (the nine planets carved on a single architrave) is itself a famous icon. [scholarly]

## III. The Stone Chariot — Architecture as Cosmology

- **The temple as Sūrya's ratha** — the whole structure is conceived as the **war-chariot of the Sun-god**, hauled across the sky: a monumental **ratha** mounted on **24 great carved wheels (twelve pairs)** and drawn by **seven straining horses** (of which one or two survive). It is the supreme expression of the **chariot-temple** idea in Indian art. (architectural tradition) [scholarly]
- **Twelve wheels = twelve months** — the **twelve pairs of wheels** are widely read as the **twelve months** (or the *rāśis*/zodiac signs); each wheel, with its eight major and eight minor spokes, doubles as a **sundial** that tells the time from the shadow of its axle-pin. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (the count) [disputed]:* some read the wheels as **24 hours** or the *pakṣas* (fortnights) of the year rather than twelve months; the "month" reading is the most common but not the only one.
- **Seven horses = seven days / seven rays** — the **seven horses** are glossed as the **seven days of the week**, or as the **seven colours/rays of sunlight**, or the seven Vedic metres (*chandas*) on which the Sun's chariot of Vedic cosmology runs (cf. the steeds of the *Ṛgvedic* Sūrya). [scholarly]
- **The three Sūryas of the day** — the temple held three images of the Sun on its three projections (south, west, north) — the **morning (Bāla / Udaya), midday (Madhyāhna) and evening (Astā)** Sun — so that the deity could be greeted at each phase of his daily course. [regional]
- **The Naṭa-mandira and erotic friezes** — a detached **dance-hall (Naṭa-mandira)** fronts the complex; the walls carry the celebrated **maithuna (erotic) friezes**, read variously as Tantric symbolism, auspicious *maṅgala* imagery, or worldly life surrendered at the Sun's feet. European mariners named the dark, looming tower the **"Black Pagoda,"** a navigation landmark against Purī's whitewashed **"White Pagoda."** [scholarly][historical]

## IV. Two Clocks — Legend vs History

- **Scriptural clock** — the **Sāmba cure**, the **Candrabhāgā** *tīrtha*, Viśvakarmā's self-revealed image, and the Maga priests sit in **mythic māhātmya time**, undatable on any calendar; Konārka is "the place the Sun has always healed." [legend]
- **Academic clock** — the surviving monument is a **mid-13th-century CE** Kaliṅga (Nāgara) masterpiece, built/commissioned by the **Eastern Gaṅga** king **Narasiṃhadeva I** (r. c. 1238–1264 CE), conventionally dated **c. 1241–1258 CE**, plausibly to commemorate military victory; the **Sun-cult itself is far older**, with the Maga-priest stratum reflecting an **Indo-Iranian (magian) solar tradition** absorbed centuries earlier. (academic / historical) [scholarly]
- **Collapse of the śikhara** — the towering main **deul (śikhara)**, reputedly ~70 m, **collapsed** (over the 16th–19th centuries; causes debated — structural failure, foundation on sand, deliberate slighting, or removal of a load-bearing element); worship had lapsed and the deity-image is said to have been **shifted to Purī's Jagannātha temple** for safety. Today the well-preserved **jagamohana (porch)**, deliberately filled with sand and sealed by the British in **1903** to prevent its fall, dominates the ruin. [historical][disputed]
- **The "lodestone" legend** — a persistent **folk tradition** holds that a great **magnetic lodestone** capping the temple kept an iron image of the Sun suspended in mid-air, and that its removal (by Portuguese sailors, whose compasses it deranged) caused the tower's fall — almost certainly a **legend**, not history. [folk][disputed]

## V. Konārk and the Daśāvatāra

- **Sūrya, the Vedic solar pivot** — Konārk's deity belongs to the **Āditya / Sūrya** theology rather than to the [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) of Viṣṇu; yet, in the syncretic Odia setting beside Jagannātha-Viṣṇu, the Sun is read as a manifestation of the supreme. Konārk's friezes themselves carry avatāra and Vaiṣṇava imagery, and Sūrya is genealogically the ancestor of the **Solar Dynasty (Sūryavaṃśa)** of [Rāma](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md). [scholarly]

## Sources

- **Sāmba Purāṇa** — the principal Sun-cult text: Sāmba's leprosy, his twelve-year tapas on the Candrabhāgā, the cure by Sūrya, the floating Viśvakarmā image, and the import of the **Maga (Śākadvīpīya) priests** — the charter-myth of Arka Kṣetra.
- **Bhaviṣya Purāṇa** (Brāhma-parvan, solar/Sāmba sections) — the Sāmba legend and the establishment of Sun-worship and the Maga priesthood in India.
- **Brahma Purāṇa** (Konārka / Arka-kṣetra section) — localisation of the Sāmba/Candrabhāgā cure to **Konārka**, and the sanctity of the Arka Kṣetra and its *tīrtha*.
- **Sthala-purāṇa & temple tradition** — Candrabhāgā / Ratha-Saptamī sunrise bath; the three daily Sūryas; the "Black Pagoda"; the lodestone folk-legend — living and local layer.
- **Academic / historical** — Eastern Gaṅga patronage under **Narasiṃhadeva I** (c. 1238–1264 CE), c. 1241–1258 CE construction; Kaliṅga architecture; the collapse of the *deul* and 1903 sand-sealing; Indo-Iranian (magian) origins of the *saura* cult — see also [Jagannāth Purī](jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md) and [Liṅgarāja, Bhubaneswar](lingaraja-bhubaneswar-ekamra-kshetra.md).
