# Panjurli and Guliga — the boar-daiva and the fierce watcher of Tulu Bhūta-Kola

> Parent: [08 · Beings & Bestiary](README.md) → the **daivas / bhūtas of Tuḷu Nāḍu**: deified spirits worshipped through the all-night **Bhūta-Kōla** possession-ritual of coastal Karnataka.
> Cosmic address: **outside the Sanskritic yuga-clock**. These are Dravidian folk-deities whose worship is held **[scholarly] pre-Vedic** in substratum; their origin-paddanas graft onto the Śaiva household (Śiva–Pārvatī on Kailāsa) but belong to an oral, [regional] stratum, not the Purāṇic chronology. Academic frame: paddanas recorded **[scholarly] 19th–20th c.**; systematic study from Peter J. Claus's fieldwork (**c. 1970s**); global fame after the film *Kantara* (**2022 CE**).
> Kindred nodes: [Tuḷu paddanas & the Bhūta cult](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md) · [Kalkuḍa–Kallurṭi](../04-deep-dives/kalkuda-kallurti.md) · [Theyyam deities](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/theyyam-deities-and-thottam-myths.md).

The **daivas** (or *bhūtas*) of **Tuḷu Nāḍu** — the Tuḷu-speaking coast of Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa and Uḍupi — are not the gods of the Sanskrit pantheon but a parallel order of **deified spirits**: some deified heroes and heroines, some animal- or nature-powers, some fierce guardian-forces. They are propitiated not in temples of image-worship but through the **Bhūta-Kōla** (also *daiva-nēma*, *daiva-kōla*), an all-night rite in which a costumed dancer-medium is possessed by the daiva, becomes it, dances its wrath, and delivers **oracle and judgement** (*nuḍi*) to the assembled village. The two most widely invoked of all are **Panjurli**, the compassionate **boar-daiva** raised in Śiva's own household, and **Guliga**, the wrath-born devourer who is his fierce complement — the "cool" justice and the "hot" enforcement of the same order. This node is their class-level home, together with the minor daivas **Bobbariya** and **Kordabbu**, whose stories show how far across community and creed the daiva-pantheon reaches.

---

## The ritual frame — Bhūta-Kōla and the paddana

- **The rite** — *Bhūta-Kōla / daiva-nēma* is an **overnight spirit-possession performance** held in the village or family shrine (*sāna*, *garaḍi*, *bhūtasthāna*), in which the deity is invited into a human medium to dance, arbitrate disputes, bless land and cattle, and pronounce binding oracle. It is Hindu folk practice of the Tuḷuva, held to reach back to **[scholarly] pre-Vedic, Dravidian** layers of coastal religion. (*Buta Kola* liturgy; Peter J. Claus.) **[folk] [oral tradition] [regional]**
- **The paddana** — the **paddana** is the sung oral epic/ballad that narrates a daiva's origin, deeds and prerogatives; it is the "breath" to the Kōla's "body," recited or sung (often by women at transplanting, or by the ritual specialists) to summon and legitimate the possession. More than **ten distinct paddanas** are attached to Panjurli alone across the Tuḷuva region. (*Oral Epics in India*, 1989; Claus, paddana studies.) **[oral tradition]**
- **The performers** — the impersonators are drawn from specific hereditary communities — **Nalike, Parava, Pambada, Paṇāra** and allied groups — who train in the dance, the make-up, and the paddana; the patron is typically a landholding family or a whole village. (Ethnographic sources.) **[regional]**
- **The costume-body** — the medium wears the towering **aṇi** (a great arched back-halo of tender coconut fronds), the **gaggara** (heavy bronze anklets whose sound drives the trance), and elaborate **muga-varṇa** (face-painting in turmeric-yellow, vermilion and black), so that the man is visually erased and the daiva made present. (Kōla practice.) **[regional]**
- **The two clocks** — the *scriptural* frame does not place these events on the yuga-spine at all; the paddanas set the origins vaguely "in the time of Śiva" or in a heroic past. The *academic* frame treats the cult as an old Dravidian substratum **[scholarly]**, with its texts recorded and studied only from the **19th–20th c.** onward, and worldwide attention arriving with **Kantara (2022 CE)**, whose climax stages a Guliga-Kōla. **[scholarly]**

## Panjurli — the boar raised in Śiva's household

*Tuḷu **panji** = "pig / boar"; Panjurli is the boar-daiva, guardian of fields and giver of justice.*

- **Origin I — the orphaned piglet adopted by Pārvatī** — In the widespread paddana, a wild boar strays into the celestial gardens of **Śiva and Pārvatī** and is killed, leaving an orphan piglet. **Pārvatī**, moved to pity, raises the boar-child in the household of Kailāsa; but grown wild and mischievous it ravages the divine garden, and **Śiva**, angered, casts it down — transforming it into a **guardian-spirit sent to Earth to protect crops and people and to enforce justice**. (Panjurli paddana.) **[folk] [oral tradition]**
  - *Variant II (Panjurli paddana):* the boar-being is **created by Śiva himself** and then **cursed by Pārvatī** for destroying her **kadaḷi-vana** (plantain / banana grove); the curse is softened into a commission — he is **ordained to be born on Earth as a divine spirit** to raise people "from evil to good." Here the wrath is Pārvatī's, not Śiva's. [variant] **[folk]**
- **His office** — descended, Panjurli becomes the **rakṣaka** of cultivated land: he guards the **fields against wild boar and beast** (the danger he was himself born of), protects cattle and household, and above all **adjudicates** — swearing oaths, settling boundary and property disputes, punishing the false. He is fundamentally a **benevolent, forgiving, justice-giving** daiva. (Panjurli paddanas; Claus.) **[regional]**
- **The boar-face** — in the Kōla he is danced with a fierce boar-tusked visage; iconographically he is the most recognisable of the daivas, and the emblematic figure of the tradition to outsiders since *Kantara*. **[regional]**
- **Syncretisation with Varāha / Śiva** — because both are boars, popular exegesis identifies Panjurli with **Varāha**, Viṣṇu's boar-avatāra ([Varāha–Hiraṇyākṣa](../04-deep-dives/varaha-hiranyaksha.md); [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md)); yet by his Kailāsa origin he is equally reckoned an emanation of **Śiva**, and is titled **Śiva-Śambhūta** ("sprung from Śiva"). The two identifications co-exist and are **not harmonised**. [disputed] **[folk]**
- **An open-ended clan of Panjurlis** — Panjurli is less one spirit than a whole **category**: dozens of localised forms are named — e.g. **Kuppe Panjurli, Varte Panjurli, Uraṇḍra Panjurli, Aṇṇappa Panjurli** and many more — each with its own shrine, precinct and paddana. Manuscript and field lists never close the set. (Bhūta rosters of Tuḷunāḍu.) **[regional]**

## Guliga — the wrath-born watcher

*Guliga (Guḷiga, Gulige, Guḷigan) — the fierce devouring daiva, keeper of oaths and boundaries; Panjurli's dark counterpart.*

- **Origin — born of stone-and-ash, the insatiable one** — in the paddana Guliga is a **goblin-being born from a stone** that **Pārvatī** finds in a heap of ash (in some tellings, ash **Śiva** flings into water); newborn, he is possessed of a **bottomless hunger** and would swallow whatever he is set near — in the fiercest tellings **devouring the very earth**. To master him he is **handed to Viṣṇu**, who by trick or ordinance curbs his appetite and fixes him as a **guardian**, the **Kṣetra-pāla** ("protector of the precinct"). (Guliga paddana.) **[folk] [oral tradition]**
- **The hot to Panjurli's cool** — where Panjurli forgives and adjudicates, Guliga is **destructive, black-bodied, wild-eyed, blood-taking** — the daiva of **rage and retribution**. In practice the two are a **complementary pair**: Panjurli passes the judgement, Guliga carries out the sentence; Panjurli is worshipped by day-cool offerings, Guliga demands the fierce, sometimes blood, oblation. Together they hold the land. (Kōla practice; Claus.) **[regional]**
- **Oath- and boundary-keeper** — Tuḷuva households invoke Guliga as guardian of **land, cattle and crop** and as the terror that enforces a **sworn word** — false oath before Guliga is believed to bring swift ruin. He is stationed at thresholds and field-edges as *kṣetra-pāla*. **[regional]**
- **Fierce forms** — Guliga appears in many local guises, including the wrathful **Guliga-Bhairava** — assimilating him to Śaiva **Bhairava**, the terror-form guardian (cf. [Vaṭuka-Bhairava](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vatuka-bhairava-origin.md)). [variant] **[folk]**
- **The paired Kōla** — the **Panjurli–Guliga double-nēma** is the signature performance of the cult and the sequence dramatised in *Kantara*, where the Guliga possession forms the climactic act — the just boar-daiva and the fierce watcher embodied on one ritual night. **[regional]**

### Name-collision — three "Guligas/Gulikas" to keep apart

- **Guliga the daiva** — the Tuḷu bhūta above. **[regional]**
- **Gulika the nāga** — a serpent counted among the **Aṣṭanāga** in some Purāṇic/āgamic lists (see [Nāgas & Serpents](nagas-and-serpents.md)); a **different being**, Sanskritic and pan-Indian, not the coastal daiva. [disambiguation]
- **Gulika / Māndi the upagraha** — in **jyotiṣa**, Gulika is a malefic **shadow sub-planet**, reckoned a "son of Śani/Saturn" (see [Navagraha](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/navagraha.md)). Folk etymology sometimes links the daiva's fierceness to this astrological Gulika, but the identification is **[disputed]** and secondary.

## Bobbariya and Kordabbu — the reach of the daiva-pantheon

The daivas are drawn from **every layer of coastal society**, not only its Hindu majority — which is exactly why the tradition maps a whole social order.

- **Bobbariya (Bobbarya)** — a **sea-spirit and daiva of trade and the boats**, invoked by fishermen and merchants against **storm and shipwreck**. His paddana makes him a **Byāri (Māppila) Muslim merchant-mariner** who dies at sea and is deified into the Tuḷu pantheon; his emblem is the **bobbariyana kambo**, a tall mast-like post planted at his shrine. That a **Muslim trader** is worshipped as a Hindu daiva is the tradition's clearest instance of **coastal syncretism** — the bhūta-rosters explicitly note that "spirits of Muslim origin like Bobbariya and Ali-bhūta are revered here." (Bobbariya paddana; Tuḷunāḍu bhūta rosters.) **[folk] [regional]**
- **Kordabbu (Koragajja / Kordabbu)** — a daiva of the **Koraga**, a tribal, historically most-marginalised community of the coast. His paddana makes him a **Koraga hero endowed with supernatural power** — able to make a **dry stick sprout green**, to raise water, to work wonders — who at death is deified. As **Koragajja** ("Koraga grandfather") he is one of the most sought daivas for the recovery of **lost things and unfulfilled vows**. His cult gives the lowest-ranked community its **own tutelary god within the same ritual grammar** as the landholders' Panjurli. (Kordabbu / Koragajja paddana.) **[folk] [oral tradition] [regional]**
- **The social map** — read together, the roster reads as a **society made divine**: a boar (wild nature tamed to guard the fields), a devouring goblin (raw force bound to guard the boundary), a Muslim trader (the sea and commerce), a Koraga wonder-worker (the tribal margin), alongside the great **hero-cults of Siri and Koṭi-Cennaya** — each community's dead heroes and powers seated as daivas and answered to at the Kōla. (Claus; *Oral Epics in India*.) **[scholarly] [regional]**
