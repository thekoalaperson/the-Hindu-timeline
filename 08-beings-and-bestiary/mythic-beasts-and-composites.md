# Mythic Beasts and Composite Creatures

> Part of [08 · Beings & Bestiary](README.md) → the **named composite & hybrid beasts** of the bestiary: full entries for **Śarabha** (Śiva's eight-legged lion-bird), **Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa** (the two-headed eagle of the Vaiṣṇava counter-myth), **Navaguñjara** (the nine-animal theophany of the Oḍiā Mahābhārata), and **Saramā with the Sārameyas** (Indra's divine bitch and Yama's death-hounds), plus pointer entries for the composites whose canonical homes sit in section 09 (**Makara**, **Yāḷi/Vyāla**).
> See also [master index](../README.md), the bird-beast kin in [Airāvata & celestial animals](airavata-and-celestial-animals.md), the prey-race of [Nāgas & serpents](nagas-and-serpents.md), and the death-path context in [Pitṛs & ancestors](pitrs-and-ancestors.md).

This node is the canonical home for the bestiary's **named composite creatures**. **Śarabha** is the colossal *aṣṭapāda* (eight-legged) part-lion/part-bird form Śiva assumes to overpower Viṣṇu's man-lion **Narasiṃha** — a beast that became a literal flashpoint of **Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava sectarian polemic** — and **Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa** is the two-headed eagle the Vaiṣṇava counter-tradition raises against it, a bird that migrated from sectarian myth into **Karnataka's royal heraldry and state emblem**. **Navaguñjara** is the nine-animal composite in which Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa appears to Arjuna in **Sāraḷā Dāsa's Oḍiā Mahābhārata** — a regional Viśvarūpa-lesson with no Sanskrit-epic basis. [folk/regional] **Saramā** is the Ṛgvedic **devaśunī** (divine bitch), Indra's messenger who tracks the stolen cosmic cows across the Rasā to the **Paṇis' Vala cave**, and the mother of the two four-eyed hounds who guard **Yama's path of the dead**. Two clocks run throughout: scripture reads these as cosmic agents and divine forms; comparative scholarship reads Saramā's hounds as an inherited Indo-European death-dog mytheme (cf. Greek Kerberos, the Norse Garmr) and the bird-beast duels as datable sectarian polemic. [scholarly]

## Śarabha — the Aṣṭapāda Lion-Bird of Śiva

- **Form** — A gigantic composite beast **mightier than the lion and the elephant**, eight-legged (*aṣṭapāda*), part-lion and part-bird, with wings, talons and a great mane; in some descriptions a thousand arms, matted hair, and the crescent moon. The Śaiva texts make it a manifestation of **Śiva himself** (the *Śarabheśvara-mūrti*). *(Source: Śarabha Upaniṣad; Liṅga Purāṇa 2.48; Śiva Purāṇa.)*
- **Occasion — to subdue Narasiṃha** — After **Viṣṇu's Narasiṃha** (the man-lion) disembowels **Hiraṇyakaśipu**, his fury does not subside and the worlds tremble. Śiva assumes the **Śarabha** form to pacify (or, in stronger tellings, to overpower and bind) Narasiṃha, restoring cosmic order. *(Source: Liṅga Purāṇa 2.48; Śiva Purāṇa.)*
  - *Variant (pacification — Liṅga/Śiva Purāṇa, Śaiva):* Śarabha seizes the raging Narasiṃha until his rage cools; Narasiṃha then bows to Śiva, and his flayed lion-skin and hide become Śiva's garment/ornament. The Śaiva reading is a **subordination of Viṣṇu's avatāra to Śiva**. [disputed]
  - *Variant (Vaiṣṇava counter — Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa):* Vaiṣṇava traditions reverse the outcome — Narasiṃha takes the form of the two-headed eagle **Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa** (or the bird **Sharabha** is itself defeated), and it is Viṣṇu's form that prevails over Śiva's beast. The episode is openly a **sectarian flashpoint**, each camp claiming the victory. [disputed] *(Source: Vaiṣṇava Purāṇic & āgamic counter-traditions; Vidarbha/temple lore.)*
- **Associated goddess — Pratyaṅgirā / Śūlinī** — The ferocious goddess **Pratyaṅgirā** (also **Śūlinī Durgā / Atharvaṇa Bhadrakālī**) is bound to the Śarabha cult: in several tellings she emerges from, or fights alongside, Śarabha to quell Narasiṃha, and is invoked in counter-sorcery (*pratyaṅgirā* = "she who repels back"). She is iconographically lion-faced, paired with the Śarabheśvara. *(Source: Kālikā Purāṇa; Tantric/āgamic Pratyaṅgirā traditions.)*
  - *Variant (regional iconography):* South-Indian temples (e.g. **Tirubhuvanam Śarabheśvara**, Tamil Nadu) depict a winged, bird-faced Śarabha flanked by the goddesses **Śūlinī** and **Pratyaṅgirā** clutching the subdued Narasiṃha. [regional]
- **Scriptural-vs-historical clock** — The Śarabha myth is **late and sectarian**: it has no Vedic basis (the Ṛgvedic *śarabha* is merely a wild beast/deer-like animal), and surfaces only in the Purāṇic-Upaniṣadic strata to assert Śaiva supremacy after Narasiṃha's cult was established. The *Śarabha Upaniṣad* is a minor (Atharvan-affiliated) text, not an early Upaniṣad. [scholarly] *(Source: Śarabha Upaniṣad; comparative dating of Purāṇic Śaivism.)*

## Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa — the Two-Headed Eagle of Narasiṃha

- **Form** — A colossal **two-headed bird of prey** (eagle) of immeasurable strength, conventionally shown with wings spread and with **elephants or lions gripped in its beaks and talons** — an emblem of physical force beyond any single-headed creature. *(Source: Vaiṣṇava āgamic & South-Indian temple traditions; Karnataka temple iconography.)* [regional]
- **Occasion — the Vaiṣṇava counter to Śarabha** — In the Vaiṣṇava telling of the Śarabha episode (above), when Śiva's Śarabha descends on the raging **Narasiṃha**, Viṣṇu's man-lion does not submit but assumes the still-mightier **Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa** form; the two composite beasts battle (in some tellings for **eighteen days**) and it is the two-headed bird that prevails — the exact inverse of the Śaiva claim. The pair of myths is an open **sectarian flashpoint**, each camp scripting its own god's victory. [disputed] *(Source: Vaiṣṇava Purāṇic & āgamic counter-traditions; temple sthala-lore.)*
  - *Variant (Śaiva inverse):* Śaiva tellings keep the frame but reverse the ending — Śarabha (or a second, fiercer Śiva-form) subdues even the Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa, and both combatants finally lay their terrible forms aside. [disputed] *(Source: Śaiva āgamic counter-tellings.)*
- **Iconography — the chain of destruction** — The celebrated Hoysaḷa relief at the **Chennakeśava temple, Belūr** carves the bird atop a "food-chain of forms": a deer devoured by a python, the python seized by an elephant, the elephant by a lion, the lion by Śarabha — and Śarabha itself in the talons of **Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa**, the visual argument that the two-headed bird ends the escalation. [regional] *(Source: Belūr Chennakeśava temple sculpture; Karnataka temple-art surveys.)*
- **Heraldry — from myth to state emblem** — The bird's documented career is largely **artistic and dynastic**: a ceiling sculpture at the **Rāmeśvara temple, Keḷadi** (Shivamogga district); coin-types of **Vijayanagara** (notably under Acyutadevarāya, 16th c.); the dynastic crest of the **Keḷadi Nāyakas** and the **Mysore Woḍeyars**; and today the official **state emblem of Karnataka**. [regional] *(Source: Vijayanagara numismatics; Mysore/Keḷadi dynastic heraldry; Government of Karnataka emblem.)*
- **Scriptural-vs-historical clock** — Like Śarabha, the Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa has **no Vedic or early Purāṇic basis**: it surfaces as a late sectarian counter-image in the Śarabha–Narasiṃha polemic, and its firmly datable life is in **Karnataka temple art, coinage and heraldry from the Vijayanagara period onward**. [scholarly] *(Source: comparative dating of the Śarabha/Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa polemic; Karnataka art history.)*

## Navaguñjara — the Nine-Animal Form of the Oḍiā Mahābhārata

- **Form** — A composite of **nine animals** (*nava* + *guñjara*): conventionally the **head of a rooster**; the **neck of a peacock**; the **hump of a bull**; the **waist of a lion**; a **serpent for a tail**; three legs — of an **elephant**, a **tiger**, and a **horse or deer** — and for a fourth limb a **raised human arm holding a lotus** (or a discus/wheel). The exact member-list varies slightly across tellings and paintings. [folk/regional] *(Source: Sāraḷā Dāsa, Oḍiā Mahābhārata; Odishan paṭṭacitra convention.)*
- **The Arjuna theophany** — While **Arjuna** performs penance on a hill (Maṇibhadra), the impossible beast appears before him. He raises the Gāṇḍīva to shoot — then reasons that **no natural creature could exist in such a form**, recognizes the apparition as a form of **Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa**, lowers the bow and folds his hands; the deity blesses him. The episode functions as a regional **Viśvarūpa-lesson**: divinity exceeds every category nature offers, and the wise response to the incomprehensible is surrender, not the arrow. [folk/regional] *(Source: Sāraḷā Dāsa's 15th-century Oḍiā Mahābhārata.)*
- **Textual status** — The Navaguñjara is **absent from Vyāsa's Sanskrit Mahābhārata**; it is an innovation of **Sāraḷā Dāsa's 15th-century Oḍiā Mahābhārata** and belongs to the Odishan regional tradition. [folk/regional] [scholarly] *(Source: Sāraḷā Mahābhārata scholarship.)*
- **Cultural afterlife** — The Navaguñjara-Arjuna scene is carved on the **northern side of the Jagannātha temple, Puri**, and is a stock motif of Odisha's **paṭṭacitra** painting and **Gañjapā** playing-card traditions. [regional] *(Source: Puri Jagannātha temple iconography; Odishan folk-art surveys.)*

## Pointer entries — composites whose canonical homes lie elsewhere

- **Makara** — the aquatic composite (crocodile-fish, often with elephant snout and foliate tail): **vāhana of Gaṅgā and Varuṇa**, banner-emblem of **Kāmadeva** (*makara-dhvaja*), source of Viṣṇu's *makara-kuṇḍala* earrings, the zodiacal **Makara rāśi** (Capricorn), and the *makara-toraṇa* arch of temple doorways. Canonical homes: [temple iconographic motifs](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/temple-iconographic-motifs.md) (the makara-toraṇa and threshold role) and [vāhanas & celestial vehicles](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/vahanas-and-celestial-vehicles.md) (the mount role). *(Source: Wikipedia, "Makara (Hindu mythology)"; see linked files.)*
- **Yāḷi / Vyāla (Śārdūla)** — the leonine **leogryph guardian-beast** rearing on the pillars and balustrades of southern temples, in elephant-, horse-, and dog-headed sub-types. Canonical home: [temple iconographic motifs](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/temple-iconographic-motifs.md). *(Source: Wikipedia, "Yali (mythology)"; see linked file.)*
- **Timiṅgila** — the abyssal "**swallower of whales**" (*timi* = whale; the *timiṅgila* swallows the timi, and the *timiṅgila-gila* swallows even that): a graded series of ever-vaster sea-monsters invoked in epic descriptions of the ocean's terrors (the Sundara Kāṇḍa crossing; epic ocean-catalogues). The mother of aquatic broods, **Timi**, appears among the variant Kaśyapa-wives in [the Prajāpatis](the-prajapatis.md). *(Source: Rāmāyaṇa, Sundara Kāṇḍa; Mahābhārata ocean-descriptions.)*

## Saramā and the Sārameyas — Indra's Bitch and the Hounds of Yama

### Saramā — the Devaśunī of the Vala myth

- **Saramā** — The **devaśunī** ("bitch of the gods"), the divine female dog who serves as **Indra's messenger and tracker** in the great **Vala / Paṇi** cattle-myth. When the **Paṇis** (a niggardly demon-clan) steal and hide the cosmic cows (the dawns / waters / light) in the **Vala cave**, Indra sends Saramā to find them. *(Source: Ṛgveda 3.31; 1.62; 10.108.)*
- **Crossing the Rasā** — Saramā crosses the cosmic boundary-river **Rasā** — a feat the Paṇis marvel at, since it is otherwise impassable — and tracks the lowing cattle to the Paṇis' stronghold at the edge of the world. *(Source: Ṛgveda 10.108.)*
- **The Saramā–Paṇi saṃvāda** — Ṛgveda **10.108** is a **dialogue hymn** (*saṃvāda-sūkta*) between Saramā and the Paṇis: the Paṇis try to bribe her to defect and share their hidden wealth and cattle; **Saramā refuses**, declaring she comes as Indra's envoy and that Indra and the **Aṅgirases** will break their cave. Her refusal seals the Paṇis' doom. *(Source: Ṛgveda 10.108; Bṛhaddevatā; Sāyaṇa's commentary.)*
  - *Variant (Bṛhaddevatā elaboration):* the Bṛhaddevatā and Sāyaṇa narrativize the bare hymn — Saramā first **drinks the Paṇis' milk** (or is offered it) and so is briefly suspected of disloyalty, but reports the cave's location to Indra, who with the Aṅgirases splits Vala and releases the cows/dawns. [late text] *(Source: Bṛhaddevatā; Sāyaṇa on ṚV 10.108.)*
  - *Variant (allegory — scholarly):* the "cows" are read as **dawns, rays of light, or the waters**, the Vala cave as the night/rock that imprisons them, and Saramā as **Uṣas-light or intuition (the "tracking" mind)** that finds the hidden illumination — a naturalistic/ritual reading of the myth. [scholarly] *(Source: comparative Vedic studies; Sāyaṇa.)*
- **Saramā's offspring** — She is mother of the **Sārameyas** ("sons of Saramā"), the two hounds — by some accounts begotten of a divine union, and matriarch of the dog-race generally; in later epithet the dogs of the world are her brood. *(Source: Ṛgveda 10.14; 7.55; Bṛhaddevatā.)*

### The Sārameyas — Śyāma & Śabala, the Four-Eyed Hounds of Yama

- **The two hounds** — Yama, lord of the dead, owns **two dogs**, the **Sārameyas**, named **Śyāma** ("dark/black") and **Śabala** ("brindled/spotted"; also given as **Śarvara**). They are **four-eyed** (*caturakṣa*), broad-nosed, brindled, and they **guard the path** the dead must travel to reach Yama's realm. *(Source: Ṛgveda 10.14.10–12.)*
- **Guardians / heralds of the dead** — In the funeral hymn ṚV 10.14, the deceased is bidden to **"run past" Yama's two dogs**, the brindled four-eyed sons of Saramā, who **watch the road** and patrol among men as Yama's messengers — and is prayed to be granted safe passage and well-being. *(Source: Ṛgveda 10.14.10–12; see [Pitṛs & ancestors](pitrs-and-ancestors.md) for the death-path.)*
  - *Variant (name — Śabala / Śarvara):* the second hound's name appears as both **Śabala** and **Śarvara**; the latter is philologically linked to Greek **Kerberos (Cerberus)**, supporting a shared Indo-European death-dog. [scholarly] *(Source: comparative Indo-European mythology; Bloomfield, Macdonell.)*
  - *Variant (four eyes):* the "four eyes" are read literally (a monstrous trait) or as **two pairs of eye-spots/markings** above the eyes (a real feature of some dogs), the latter a naturalistic gloss. [disputed]
- **Comparative frame** — Saramā's hounds belong to a wider Indo-European complex of **dogs that guard the threshold of death** — Greek **Kerberos**, Norse **Garmr** at the gate of Hel — making the Sārameyas the Vedic witness to an inherited mytheme rather than a purely Indic invention. [scholarly] *(Source: comparative Indo-European mythology.)*
- **Scriptural-vs-historical clock** — Scripturally the dogs are timeless guardians of Yama's road; historically they are an **early Ṛgvedic (oldest stratum) figure** that fades in the later epic-Purāṇic period, where Yama's iconography shifts toward the buffalo-mount and the noose, and the dogs survive mainly in *śrāddha* allusions and the dog-honouring of festivals like **Kukur Tihar**. [scholarly]
