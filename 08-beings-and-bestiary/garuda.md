# Garuḍa — King of Birds, Mount of Viṣṇu

[Beings & Bestiary](./README.md) · [Master Index](../README.md) — Vainateya, the golden-winged sun-bird; son of Vinatā and Kaśyapa, devourer of serpents, bearer of Viṣṇu, and the emblem of the Garuḍa-dhvaja. This file gives the full biography; for his role purely as a vehicle see [vāhanas](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/vahanas-and-celestial-vehicles.md), for his prey-kin see [nāgas and serpents](./nagas-and-serpents.md), and for his vulture nephews see the [Rāmāyaṇa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/) cycle.

Verse numbers below follow the Pune critical edition (CE) of the Mahābhārata and Baroda CE of the Rāmāyaṇa as given on GRETIL; Ganguli's English section numbers differ.

## Names, Epithets, and the Vedic Antecedent

- **Garuḍa** - "King of Birds" (pakṣirāja), the great golden bird (suvarṇa-kāya). Born of Vinatā, hence the metronymic **Vainateya** (Mahābhārata 1.28.14, 1.29.9, and throughout the Ādi Parva).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata vulgate, star passage after CE 1.26.3, \*343):* the sages, seeing him fly off with the huge bough, named him **Garuḍa** because he had taken up a heavy burden (*guruṃ bhāraṃ samāsādya*). Excluded from the CE text; Ganguli's translation, made from the vulgate, includes it. [interpolation]
  - Modern lexical glosses derive the name from *gṛ* "to swallow" (devourer). [scholarly] The gloss "wings of speech," repeated in popular reference works, is not an epic etymology; its source is **locus not verified**.
- **Suparṇa** - "He of Beautiful Wings," the name all creatures gave him when he let a single feather fall before Indra's vajra (see amṛta-quest below): *surūpaṃ patram ālakṣya suparṇo 'yaṃ bhavatv iti* (Mahābhārata 1.29.21). The word itself is older: the Ṛgveda calls the heavenly bird *suparṇa* (Ṛgveda 1.164.46, 10.149.3).
- **Tārkṣya** - In the Ṛgveda, **Tārkṣya Ariṣṭanemi** is a distinct divine steed or bird invoked for safe passage (Ṛgveda 1.89.6, 10.178.1). In the epic and later texts the name is applied to Garuḍa (Mahābhārata 1.26.18; Amarakośa 1.1.70). The Bhāgavata names Vinatā's husband **Tārkṣya** (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.6.2, 6.6.21) and calls Garuḍa *tārkṣya-putra*, "son of Tārkṣya" (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.17.7); the BBT (Prabhupāda) translation glosses *tārkṣyāya* as "unto Kaśyapa" [translator's gloss]. Read that way, Tārkṣya is a patronymic.
- **Garutmān** - "the winged one." The Ṛgveda names the heavenly *suparṇa garutmān* among the names of the one Being: "they call him Indra, Mitra, Varuṇa, Agni, and he is the heavenly, fair-winged Garutmān" (Ṛgveda 1.164.46; cf. 10.149.3). The epic uses Garutmān as a plain name of Garuḍa (Mahābhārata 1.28.1, 1.29.16), and the Vālakhilyas' sacrifice asks for a being who is *kāmavīryaḥ kāmagamaḥ*, "of strength at will, going where he wills" (Mahābhārata 1.27.13).
- **Śyena ("falcon")** - Vedic antecedent: the Ṛgveda's falcon (*śyena*) seizes **Soma** and carries it down from heaven, (Ṛgveda 4.26.4–7, 4.27.1–5); in 4.27.1 the speaker says "a hundred iron forts guarded me" before he flew out as a falcon (*śatam mā pura āyasīr arakṣann adha śyeno javasā nir adīyam*). This is the mytheme later attached to Garuḍa's theft of amṛta. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Vedic philology):* Garuḍa is best read as a Purāṇic-epic synthesis of three Vedic bird-figures, **Garutmān, Śyena, and Tārkṣya Ariṣṭanemi**, fused into one personality only in the Mahābhārata. [scholarly]
- **The Vedic version of the wager** - *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 3.6.2.2–8 tells the plot in Vedic form. **Kadrū** (the earth) and **Suparṇī** (Vāc, speech) wager their own selves on who can see farther. Suparṇī sees a white horse at a post on the far shore of the ocean. Kadrū sees its tail blowing in the wind. Kadrū wins and sends Suparṇī to fetch Soma from heaven for the gods, and the **Gāyatrī** metre brings it down. The Brāhmaṇa calls this the story of Suparṇī and Kādrava (Eggeling's translation). [scholarly]
- **Other epithets** - The Amarakośa gives nine names together: *garutmān garuḍas tārkṣyo vainateyaḥ khageśvaraḥ nāgāntako viṣṇurathaḥ suparṇaḥ pannagāśanaḥ* (Amarakośa 1.1.70–71). Others found elsewhere include Pakṣirāja (Mahābhārata 1.28.1), Sarpārāti (enemy of serpents), Amṛtāharaṇa (stealer of nectar), Gaganeśvara (lord of the sky), and **Kāśyapi**, son of Kaśyapa, which Mahābhārata 5.99.10 also uses as a name in his bird-line.

## Birth from Vinatā's Egg

- **Parentage** - Son of the prajāpati **Kaśyapa** by **Vinatā**, daughter of Dakṣa; his maternal aunt and rival is **Kadrū**, mother of the nāgas. Kaśyapa, pleased, offered his two wives boons. Kadrū chose a thousand equally mighty nāga sons. Vinatā chose two sons who would surpass Kadrū's in strength, energy and valour. Kaśyapa granted her "one and a half" sons (*adhyardhaṃ putram*), pointing ahead to the half-formed Aruṇa (Mahābhārata 1.14.5–10). See [the prajāpatis](./the-prajapatis.md).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 1.59.39):* in the Ādi Parva's general genealogy Vinatā has **six** sons: Tārkṣya, Ariṣṭanemi, Garuḍa, Aruṇa, Āruṇi and Vāruṇi. The same genealogy later names only "the two famous sons of Vinatā, Garuḍa and Aruṇa" (1.60.67), as do Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.21.18 and Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa 3.13.32.
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.6.21–22):* Vinatā, here called **Suparṇā**, is a wife of **Tārkṣya** (see Names above). She bears Garuḍa, "the mount of the Lord of sacrifice," and **Anūru**, "the thighless," that is, Aruṇa, the Sun's charioteer.
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 6.34):* Vinatā's children are Garuḍa, Aruṇa, and a daughter, **Saudāmanī**, "famed in the sky," i.e. lightning.
- **Origin in the Vālakhilyas' tapas** - When Kaśyapa held a sacrifice for sons, Indra mocked the thumb-sized **Vālakhilya** sages, who were struggling to carry a single leaf-stalk. In anger they offered oblations for a second Indra, a hundred times stronger, to frighten the king of the gods. Kaśyapa made peace: the being would be "Indra of the birds," and Indra would remain Indra. Vinatā then conceived. Of her sons Aruṇa became the Sun's forerunner and Garuḍa was consecrated **Indra of the birds** (Mahābhārata 1.27.5–35; Bṛhaspati gives Indra this explanation at 1.26.35–36).
- **The two eggs** - Kadrū laid a thousand eggs and Vinatā two. Servants kept them in warm, moist vessels for **five hundred years**, after which Kadrū's serpent sons hatched while Vinatā's twins did not (Mahābhārata 1.14.12–14).
- **Aruṇa, the broken egg** - Impatient and ashamed, Vinatā broke one egg. The son inside had only the upper half of his body formed (*pūrvārdhakāyasaṃpannam*). In anger he cursed his mother to be **the slave of the rival she was competing with, for five hundred years**. The son in the second egg would free her, if she waited **another five hundred years** for him to hatch. He then rose into the sky and is seen at dawn (Mahābhārata 1.14.15–21), as the forerunner of the Sun (*ādityasya puraḥsaraḥ*, 1.27.34). See [the thirty-three devas](./the-thirty-three-devas.md) for Sūrya's retinue.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata vulgate, CE Appendix I, passage 14 after 1.20.15):* Garuḍa carries Aruṇa east on his back. There Brahmā sets him in front of Sūrya as **charioteer** to screen the Sun's world-burning anger at Rāhu. Ganguli includes this; the CE omits it. [interpolation]
- **Garuḍa hatches** - The wager and Vinatā's enslavement (below) came first. Then, "in the meantime, when his time came," Garuḍa split the egg without his mother's help. He was blazing like a heap of fire, and the creatures ran for shelter to Agni, thinking him Agni swelling to burn them. Agni told them it was Garuḍa, his equal in brilliance. The gods and seers praised him as lord of birds and he drew in his radiance (Mahābhārata 1.20.4–15). On hatching he flew off at once in search of food (1.14.22–23).

## The Wager of Kadrū vs. Vinatā and Vinatā's Enslavement

- **The bet on Uccaiḥśravas** - The sisters disputed the colour of **Uccaiḥśravas**, the divine horse risen from the churning of the ocean. Vinatā said pure white; Kadrū said it had a black tail. The loser would become the winner's slave (*dāsībhāva*), and they agreed to look the next day (Mahābhārata 1.18.1–5). See [nāgas and serpents](./nagas-and-serpents.md) for the nāga side of this episode, and the Śatapatha form of the wager above.
- **Kadrū's deceit** - Kadrū ordered her thousand sons to become jet-black hairs and enter the horse's tail. Those who refused she cursed to **burn in Janamejaya's snake-sacrifice**, a curse that Brahmā and the gods let stand because the serpents had grown too many. This links forward to the Sarpa-satra and the rescue by **Āstīka** (Mahābhārata 1.18.6–10). Brahmā then gave Kaśyapa the lore that removes poison (*viṣahaṇī vidyā*, 1.18.11).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata vulgate, CE Appendix I, passage 13 at 1.20.1):* after talking it over, the serpents decide to obey their mother and hang in the tail as black hairs, so that she will not burn them. The CE text only says the tail was seen covered in black hairs (1.20.2).
- **Enslavement** - The sisters flew across the ocean, saw many black hairs on the tail, and Vinatā lost and became Kadrū's slave (Mahābhārata 1.20.1–3). Garuḍa, born afterwards, was made to serve the nāgas: at Kadrū's order Vinatā carried her and Garuḍa carried the serpents, flying so near the sun that they fainted (1.21.3–6). He carried them to a pleasant island and asked his mother why he must obey them (1.23.1–9). This is the root of his lifelong hatred.

## The Amṛta-Quest

- **The price of freedom** - When Garuḍa asked what would free them from slavery, the serpents set the price: "bring the amṛta by force" (*āharāmṛtam ojasā*; Mahābhārata 1.23.10–12).
- **The Niṣādas and the brāhmaṇa** - Vinatā told him to eat the thousands of Niṣādas in a remote place by the sea, but never to harm a brāhmaṇa, who would burn his throat like an ember (Mahābhārata 1.24.1–6). He gulped down the Niṣādas. A brāhmaṇa he swallowed burned his throat, and Garuḍa let him go together with his Niṣāda wife (1.24.10–1.25.5).
- **The elephant and the tortoise** - Still hungry, Garuḍa met his father **Kaśyapa**. Kaśyapa told him of the quarrelling brothers **Vibhāvasu** and **Supratīka**, who had cursed each other over dividing their inheritance and become a giant **tortoise** and **elephant**, still fighting in a lake. Garuḍa seized one in each talon (Mahābhārata 1.25.7–26).
- **The Vālakhilyas' bough** - He landed on a great branch, a hundred yojanas long, of a **rohiṇa** tree, which broke under him. Seeing the **Vālakhilya** sages hanging head-down from it, he caught the branch in his beak so as not to kill them and flew about with no place to set it down (Mahābhārata 1.25.31–1.26.4). On Mount **Gandhamādana**, Kaśyapa calmed the sages, who let go of the branch and left for the Himālaya. Garuḍa dropped it on a remote mountain and ate the elephant and tortoise on its peak (1.26.5–26).
- **Battle with the devas** - Terrible omens appeared in heaven. Bṛhaspati told Indra that a bird born of the Vālakhilyas' tapas was coming for the Soma, and the gods ringed the amṛta in armour (Mahābhārata 1.26.27–47). Garuḍa killed **Bhauvana**, the guardian of the Soma (1.28.3–4). He blinded the gods with a dust-storm raised by his wings until Vāyu blew it away (1.28.5–9), then scattered them with wings, breast, beak and talons. The **Sādhyas** and **Gandharvas** fled east, the **Vasus** and **Rudras** south, the **Ādityas** west, and the **Aśvins** north (1.28.14–17). He also fought and struck down the warriors Aśvakranda, Reṇuka, Krathana, Tapana, Ulūka, Śvasana, Nimeṣa, Praruja and Praliha (1.28.18–22).
- **The threefold guard** - A ring of **fire** surrounded the amṛta. Garuḍa made himself **ninety times ninety mouths** (*navatyā navatīr mukhānām*, i.e. 8,100), drank up rivers, and put the fire out with them. Then he took a smaller, golden form to enter (Mahābhārata 1.28.24–1.29.1). He slipped between the spokes of a **sharp-edged iron wheel** spinning round the amṛta (1.29.2–4). Beneath it lay two **serpent guardians** whose glance burned to ash. He blinded their eyes with dust and tore them apart in the middle (1.29.5–9). He took the amṛta, smashed the machine, and flew off **without drinking it** (1.29.10–11).
- **The boon of immortality without amṛta** - **Viṣṇu** met him in the sky, pleased by his lack of greed in not tasting the amṛta, and offered a boon. Garuḍa asked to stand **above Viṣṇu**, and to be **ageless and immortal even without amṛta** (*ajaraś cāmaraś ca syām amṛtena vināpy aham*). He in turn offered Viṣṇu a boon. Viṣṇu (called Kṛṣṇa here) chose him as his **mount** and set him on his **banner**: "you will stand above me" (Mahābhārata 1.29.12–16).
- **Indra's vajra and the feather** - Next, **Indra** struck him with the **vajra**. Garuḍa laughed and said he would honour the sage from whose bone the vajra was made, the vajra itself, and Indra, by shedding one feather "whose end you will never find," for the blow did not hurt him. All creatures, seeing the beautiful feather, named him **Suparṇa** (Mahābhārata 1.29.17–21). The sage is unnamed here; elsewhere the epic names him **Dadhīca** (Mahābhārata 3.98). See [vāhanas](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/vahanas-and-celestial-vehicles.md).
- **The pact with Indra** - Indra asked for friendship and to know Garuḍa's strength. Garuḍa said he could carry the whole earth, with Indra on it, on a single feather (Mahābhārata 1.29.22–1.30.5). Indra asked for the Soma back. Garuḍa agreed that Indra could snatch it from wherever he set it down, and **Garuḍa asked as his boon that the mighty serpents become his food** (*bhaveyur bhujagāḥ śakra mama bhakṣyāḥ*; 1.30.6–13).
- **The trick that frees Vinatā** - Garuḍa set the amṛta on **kuśa (darbha) grass** in front of the nāgas, declared his mother free, and told them to bathe before drinking. While they bathed, **Indra carried the nectar back to heaven**. The nāgas licked the grass where it had lain, and their tongues split in two, hence the **forked tongue of serpents**. The grass, touched by amṛta, became purifying (Mahābhārata 1.30.14–20). The same tale is retold in Kathāsaritsāgara 4.2.196–200.

## Mount of Viṣṇu and the Garuḍa-dhvaja

- **Viṣṇuratha** - From the amṛta episode onward Garuḍa is the swift vehicle that bears **Viṣṇu / Nārāyaṇa** (and Kṛṣṇa) across the worlds (Mahābhārata 1.29.16; Amarakośa 1.1.71 *viṣṇuratha*). In the Gajendra episode, Hari comes "carried by Garuḍa, who is made of the Vedic metres" (*chandomayena garuḍena*; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.31–32). In the Gītā Kṛṣṇa says "among birds I am Vainateya" (Bhagavad Gītā 10.30). Images often show him holding up Viṣṇu's feet with his hands. [iconography]
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 12.11.19):* in the Bhāgavata's allegory of the Lord's emblems, Garuḍa, "called Suparṇa," is the **threefold Veda** that carries the Puruṣa of sacrifice (*tri-vṛd vedaḥ suparṇākhyo yajñaṃ vahati pūruṣam*).
- **Garuḍa-dhvaja** - Viṣṇu's banner bears the figure of Garuḍa (Mahābhārata 1.29.16; Garuḍa calls himself *dhvajavāsin*, "dweller on the banner," at 5.103.28). "Garuḍadhvaja" is itself an epithet of Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa. The **Heliodorus pillar** at Besnagar (Vidiśā) calls itself in its inscription the *garuḍadhvaja* of **Vāsudeva**, god of gods. It was set up by Heliodoros son of Dion of Taxila, a self-styled *bhāgavata* and envoy of the Indo-Greek king Antialkidas to King Kāśīputra Bhāgabhadra. It is commonly dated to the late 2nd century BCE (c. 113–110 BCE) and is among the earliest dated physical evidence of the Vāsudeva/Garuḍa cult. [scholarly] See [artifacts & symbols](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/).
- **Iconography** - Anthropomorphic (a man's torso with beak, wings and talons) or fully zoomorphic (a great eagle or kite); golden-bodied, often four-armed bearing a pot of amṛta or a parasol, frequently shown trampling or holding nāgas. [iconography]

## Eternal Enmity with the Nāgas

- **Sarpārāti** - Through the boon he asked of Indra (Mahābhārata 1.30.12) and his grievance over the wager, Garuḍa preys on serpents forever. He is invoked against snakes and poison (see Garuḍa-vidyā below). See [nāgas and serpents](./nagas-and-serpents.md).
- **Sumukha and the humbling of Garuḍa** (Mahābhārata 5.96–103, told by Kaṇva to Duryodhana) - Indra's charioteer **Mātali**, looking for a husband for his daughter **Guṇakeśī**, chooses the nāga **Sumukha** of Airāvata's line, grandson of Āryaka. Sumukha's father **Cikura** had been eaten by Garuḍa, and Garuḍa had announced he would eat Sumukha within the month (5.101.23–24, 5.102.12–16). At Viṣṇu's urging, Indra grants Sumukha a long life, but not amṛta (5.102.21–28). Garuḍa storms in and boasts that he, who carries Viṣṇu, is stronger than all of them (5.103.1–17). Viṣṇu lays his **right arm** on Garuḍa's shoulder. Garuḍa collapses under a weight he feels as the whole earth, sheds his feathers, and begs forgiveness, which Viṣṇu grants (5.103.18–30).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata vulgate, star passage after CE 5.103.30, \*449):* Viṣṇu flicks Sumukha with his big toe onto Garuḍa's breast, and from then on Garuḍa lives with that serpent. [interpolation]
- *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.17.1–12), the monthly tribute:* by an earlier arrangement, the serpent clans each placed an offering (*bali*) under a tree for Garuḍa every month, at each *parvan*, to protect themselves. Kāliya, proud of his poison, ate Garuḍa's share. Garuḍa beat him with his left wing, and Kāliya fled to a pool of the Yamunā. Garuḍa could not enter that pool because of the sage **Saubhari**'s curse, laid after Garuḍa took a fish there against Saubhari's protest. This is why Kāliya lived there until Kṛṣṇa drove him out.
- *Variant (Kathāsaritsāgara 4.2.201–207; Harṣa's Nāgānanda), one serpent a day:* after the amṛta trick Garuḍa kept swooping on the nāgas until **Vāsuki** made a compact to send him **one nāga each day** to a spot on the seashore, so that he would not ravage Pātāla. This is the frame of the **Jīmūtavāhana** story, in which the vidyādhara prince offers himself in place of the nāga Śaṅkhacūḍa and turns Garuḍa from killing. A Vetāla tale retells it (Kathāsaritsāgara 12.23.100–104). [late text]

## Garuḍa's Other Epic Adventures

- **Gālava's friend and carrier** (Mahābhārata 5.104–117, told by Nārada) - When the young sage **Gālava** despaired of paying Viśvāmitra's fee of eight hundred moon-white horses with one black ear (5.104.26), Garuḍa, "his friend," offered to carry him anywhere (5.105.16–19). The episode includes the description of the four quarters (5.106–109).
  - **Śāṇḍilī** - On Mount **Ṛṣabha** they were fed by the ascetic brāhmaṇī **Śāṇḍilī**. Garuḍa woke to find himself featherless, "a lump of flesh with a face and feet." He confessed he had thought of carrying this perfected woman off to where Brahmā, Śiva and Viṣṇu dwell. She restored his wings, telling him never to slight a woman (5.111.1–18).

## Offspring and the Suparṇa / Bird-Race

- **Wife** - A wife named **Unnati** (or Vināyakā) is given in modern reference works (e.g. World History Encyclopedia), but **locus not verified**. Searches of Mahābhārata 1 and 5, Bhāgavata, Viṣṇu, Matsya (adhyāyas 1–176) and Harivaṃśa found no such name. [variant rosters]
- **The Suparṇas** - Garuḍa's line, the **Suparṇas / Sauparṇeyas**, live in a world of their own, serpent-eaters devoted to Viṣṇu. Nārada tells Mātali that the race was spread by **six sons of Garuḍa**: **Sumukha, Sunāman, Sunetra, Suvarcas, Surūpa and Subala** (Mahābhārata 5.99.1–8). He then names nearly fifty more, called "Garuḍa's offspring" (*garuḍātmajāḥ*), explicitly "only by way of sample" (*pradeśamātreṇa*). The names include Suvarṇacūḍa, Nāgāśin, Kāśyapi, Vainateya, Viṣṇudhanvan and Gurubhāra (5.99.9–15). This bird-son **Sumukha** is a different figure from the nāga Sumukha above. [open-ended roster]
- **Aruṇa's line — the vulture-brothers** - Garuḍa's elder, half-formed brother **Aruṇa**, by his wife **Śyenī**, fathered **Sampāti** and **Jaṭāyu**, the noble vultures of the Rāmāyaṇa, which makes Garuḍa their **paternal uncle** (Mahābhārata 1.60.67; Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa 3.13.32–33, where Jaṭāyu calls himself *śyenīputra*, Sampāti being the elder). Matsya Purāṇa 6.35–36 adds that Sampāti's sons were Babhru and Śīghraga, and Jaṭāyu's included Karṇikāra and Śatagāmin. See the [Rāmāyaṇa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/) cycle.
  - *Variant (some Purāṇic/folk tellings):* Sampāti and Jaṭāyu are made **sons of Garuḍa himself** rather than nephews. **Locus not verified**: the Mahābhārata, Vālmīki, Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.21 and Matsya Purāṇa 6 all make them Aruṇa's sons. [folk] [disputed]
- **Rāmāyaṇa cameo** - When Indrajit's serpent-arrows bind Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa, a great wind heralds Garuḍa. The nāgas who had become the arrows flee at the sight of him. He strokes the brothers' faces, their wounds close, and their strength is doubled. Asked who he is, he says only that he is **Garutmān**, Rāma's friend, "your own life-breath walking outside you." He warns them of rākṣasa deceit and flies off (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa 6.40.33–59 CE; 6.50 in the vulgate).
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās, Rāmcaritmānas, Uttarakāṇḍa):* after cutting the nāgapāśa, Garuḍa is troubled that the Lord could be bound (*bandhana kāṭi gayo uragādā, upajā hṛdayã̄ pracaṇḍa biṣādā*). His doubt leads him, by way of Śiva, to the crow-sage **Kākabhuśuṇḍi**, whose telling of the Rāma-story to Garuḍa frames the close of the Mānas. Exact dohā numbers not verified. [late text] [regional]

## Garuḍa-vidyā, the Garuḍopaniṣad, and the Garuḍa Purāṇa

- **Garuḍa-vidyā (anti-venom mantra-science)** - A body of mantra and ritual invoking Garuḍa to neutralise **snake venom and poison** of every kind. In the Garuḍa Purāṇa, Dhanvantari teaches the *gāruḍa* lore, said to have been taught by Garuḍa to Kaśyapa. The practitioner who completes its *nyāsa* "becomes Garuḍa" (*naro vai garuḍāyate*), and spirits, nāgas and fevers flee at the sight of him (Garuḍa Purāṇa 1.197.1, 1.197.53–55). The epic already ties poison-lore to this family: Brahmā gives Kaśyapa the *viṣahaṇī vidyā* (Mahābhārata 1.18.11). The tradition continues in the Tantric Garuḍa-tantras. (Atharvaveda-affiliated traditions; āgamic Garuḍa-tantras.) [scholarly]
- **Garuḍopaniṣad** - A minor **Vaiṣṇava Upaniṣad** attached to the **Atharvaveda**, made up of charms (Garuḍa-vidyā) said to prevent and cure snakebite and all poison; among the 108 Upaniṣads of the Muktikā list. [late text]
- **Garuḍa Purāṇa** - One of the eighteen **Mahāpurāṇas**, classed as *sāttvika* (Vaiṣṇava) in the Padma Purāṇa's three-way grouping (Uttarakhaṇḍa 236.18–21 as usually cited; the Sanskrit was not checked against a printed edition). Its own Brahmakāṇḍa sorts the Purāṇas the same way and ranks the Bhāgavata and the Gāruḍa as *sattvottama*, "highest in sattva" (Garuḍa Purāṇa 3.1.52). Its frame: Hari taught it to Garuḍa, Garuḍa to Kaśyapa, and the Sūta heard it through Vyāsa (Garuḍa Purāṇa 1.1.11, 1.3.8). It is famed especially for its second part, the **Dharmakāṇḍa / Pretakalpa** (*pretakhaṇḍa*), set as a dialogue between Kṛṣṇa and Garuḍa (*śrīkṛṣṇagaruḍasaṃvāda*). That part covers death, the afterlife journey, funeral rites and rebirth, hence its recitation after a death. A third part, the Brahmakāṇḍa, follows (Garuḍa Purāṇa 2, colophons; 3.1). See [literature corpus](../90-literature-corpus/).

## Garuḍa Beyond the Hindu Frame

- **Buddhist garuḍas** - A whole race of golden winged beings (Pāli *garuḷa* / *supaṇṇa*) in perpetual war with the nāgas; one of the classes attending the Buddha (Pāli Canon; Jātakas). [cross-tradition]
- **Jain Garuḍa** - In Śvetāmbara lists Garuḍa is the yakṣa (attendant spirit) of the sixteenth Tīrthaṅkara **Śāntinātha**, paired with the yakṣī Nirvāṇī; Digambara pairings differ (Jain iconographic lists; primary locus not verified). [cross-tradition]
- **Southeast Asia** - National/royal emblem in **Indonesia (Garuda Pancasila)** and **Thailand (Khrut)**, descending directly from the Indic Viṣṇu-Garuḍa cult.
