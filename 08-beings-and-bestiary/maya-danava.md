# Maya Dānava — The Demon-Architect of the Asuras

> Part of [08 · Beings & Bestiary](README.md) → the **asura craftsman-magician**: builder of the floating cities, master of *māyā* (illusion-architecture), and the demonic counter-pole to the deva-artificer Viśvakarman.
> Cosmic address: a **Dānava** (son of Danu, [asura roster §VI](asura-wars-and-roster.md)) who recurs across yugas — a near-*chiranjivi* engineer whose works span the Śaiva [Tripura war](../04-deep-dives/tripura-dahana.md), the [Rāmāyaṇa Laṅkā world](rakshasas.md) (through his daughter Mandodarī), the Mahābhārata, and the [Siddhāntic astronomy](../00-time-and-cosmology/siddhantic-astronomy-and-astronomers.md) tradition.

**Maya** (मय; *Mayāsura*, *Maya Dānava*) is the supreme **architect and illusionist of the asuras** — the demonic mirror of the gods' craftsman **Viśvakarman**. Where Viśvakarman raises the cities of the devas, Maya raises the fortresses, halls, weapons and flying machines of the anti-gods; where Viśvakarman embodies divine *śilpa* (craft), Maya embodies *māyā* (the architecture of illusion). He is unusual among asuras in being **rarely a war-leader and never definitively slain**: across the Tripura war, the Rāmāyaṇa, and the Mahābhārata he survives as the recurring **engineer-figure**, an effectively *chiranjivi*-like persistence that makes him a cross-yuga constant rather than a one-yuga villain. His name is the very word for "illusion-magic," and the Purāṇas and the *Sūrya Siddhānta* fold him into the dual identity of demon-craftsman **and** receiver of revealed astronomy.

## Parentage & Family (variants)

- **Dānava birth** — Maya is reckoned a **Dānava**, son of **Danu** by **Kaśyapa Prajāpati**, and thus brother to the Vedic asuras of Danu's line — notably **Namuci** (the foam-slain foe of Indra) — placing him in the [asura genealogy](asura-wars-and-roster.md) among Vṛtra, Śambara and Vipracitti. *(Source: Mahābhārata Ādi Parva; Purāṇic Dānava-lists.)*
  - *Variant (parentage):* some lists make him simply "an asura/Dānava chief" without fixing the maternal line; the Danu-descent is the standard but not universal attribution. [disputed] *(Source: Purāṇic recensions.)*
- **Father of Mandodarī** — Maya's daughter **Mandodarī**, born (in the common tradition) of the **apsarā Hemā**, becomes the **chief queen of Rāvaṇa** of Laṅkā — making the asura-architect the **father-in-law of the great rākṣasa king** and a grandfather-line root of Indrajit/Meghanāda. This single tie threads Maya directly into the [Rākṣasa world](rakshasas.md) and the Rāmāyaṇa. *(Source: Rāmāyaṇa; Uttara Kāṇḍa tradition.)*
  - *Variant (mother of Mandodarī):* Mandodarī's mother is variously the apsarā **Hemā** or a frog-maiden **Madhurā** in regional tellings. [regional] *(Source: Rāmāyaṇa recensions; folk Mandodarī legends.)*
- **Father of Māyāvatī** — A daughter **Māyāvatī** (also linked to **Ratī**'s rebirth) appears as wife of **Pradyumna** (Kṛṣṇa's son, the reborn Kāmadeva) in the Śambara cycle — a further generational tie binding Maya's line into the Dvāpara Kṛṣṇa-story. [disputed] *(Source: Bhāgavata/Harivaṃśa Pradyumna cycle.)*
- **Sons** — Maya is given asura sons, most often named **Dundubhi** and **Māyāvī** (or Māyāvin), rākṣasa-asuras slain in the Vānara saga of the Rāmāyaṇa (Vālin's foes). [regional] *(Source: Rāmāyaṇa Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa tradition.)*

## The Master Illusionist-Builder

- **Counterpart to Viśvakarman** — Maya is repeatedly framed as the **asura-side architect** against the deva-side **Viśvakarman**: the two are the cosmos's twin craftsmen, and the rivalry is explicit in tellings where Maya's *māyā*-built halls out-dazzle the gods' work. He is master of *vāstu* (architecture), *yantra* (machines), gem-craft, and the building of *vimāna*-like flying cities. *(Source: Mahābhārata Sabhā Parva; Purāṇic craft-traditions.)*
- **Builder of Tripura's three cities** — In the **Tripura-dāhana** war, Maya is the artificer who constructs the **three flying fortresses** — one of **gold**, one of **silver**, one of **iron**, set in heaven, air and earth — for the asura brothers **Tārakākṣa, Vidyunmālī and Kamalākṣa** (sons of Tāraka). Their mobility and the once-in-a-thousand-years alignment-clause make them all but indestructible, until **Śiva as Tripurāntaka** burns all three with a single arrow. See the full account at [Tripura-dāhana](../04-deep-dives/tripura-dahana.md). **[Satya/Treta, primordial deva-time].** *(Source: Śiva Purāṇa Rudra-saṃhitā; Matsya Purāṇa 129–140; Mahābhārata Karṇa Parva 8.24.)*
  - *Variant (metal/loka assignment):* texts differ on which metal occupies which world; gold-in-heaven and iron-on-earth are most common, the silver–air pairing is unstable. [scholarly] *(Source: Tripura source-texts; see [Tripura-dāhana](../04-deep-dives/tripura-dahana.md).)*

## The Khāṇḍava Rescue & the Mayasabhā

The Mahābhārata gives Maya his most developed episode — the one that ties the asura-architect to the *Bhārata* war itself.

- **Spared at the Khāṇḍava fire** — During the **burning of the Khāṇḍava forest** (Ādi Parva), where **Agni** consumes the woodland with **Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa** guarding the perimeter so no creature escapes, the Dānava **Maya** flees the flames and runs to Arjuna for refuge. Arjuna shelters him; Kṛṣṇa (and Agni) consent to spare him. **[Dvāpara].** *(Source: Mahābhārata Ādi Parva, Khāṇḍava-daha.)*
- **The debt of gratitude** — In return for his life Maya offers to build a **wonder for the Pāṇḍavas**; Kṛṣṇa directs him to raise a great assembly-hall for **Yudhiṣṭhira** at the new capital **Indraprastha**. *(Source: Mahābhārata Sabhā Parva.)*
- **The Mayasabhā (the illusion-palace)** — Maya constructs the **Mayasabhā**, the celebrated **hall of illusions**: crystal floors that look like water and pools that look like solid floor, gem-pillars, walls that deceive the eye — gathering rare materials (the club of Yauvanāśva, the conch Devadatta, gems) from a former asura treasury near **Bindusaras / Mount Maināka**. It is the most magnificent court of its age. *(Source: Mahābhārata Sabhā Parva.)*
  - *Variant (materials):* the hall's marvels and the inventory Maya fetches vary by recension; the optical-illusion floor is the constant, load-bearing detail. *(Source: Sabhā Parva recensions.)*
- **Seeding Duryodhana's humiliation** — At Yudhiṣṭhira's *Rājasūya*, **Duryodhana** tours the Mayasabhā, **mistakes the crystal floor for water and lifts his robe, then falls into a real pool taken for floor** — and is laughed at (in many tellings by **Draupadī**, "the blind son of a blind father"). His burning shame here is a proximate spark of the dice-game and ultimately the **Kurukṣetra war** — making Maya's illusion-craft a hinge of the epic's tragedy. *(Source: Mahābhārata Sabhā Parva.)*
  - *Variant (the mockery):* whether **Draupadī**, the Pāṇḍavas, or the servants laugh — and the exact taunt — differs across recensions and regional retellings; some critical readings note the most famous "blind son" line is absent from the oldest strata. [disputed] *(Source: Sabhā Parva; critical-edition scholarship.)*

## Cross-Yuga Persistence — the Recurring Engineer

- **The chiranjivi-like architect** — Maya is never given a death-by-avatāra the way the war-leader asuras are (he survives Tripura, the Khāṇḍava fire, and is absent from any slaying-roster). The epic and Purāṇic strata together present him as a **single recurring craftsman spanning Satya → Treta → Dvāpara** — the asura world's permanent engineer, effectively *chiranjivi*-like even where no text formally lists him among the immortals. **[cross-yuga].** *(Source: Purāṇic & epic strata; [asura roster §VI](asura-wars-and-roster.md).)*
- **Maya-built marvels elsewhere** — Tradition scatters further works to him: subterranean asura-palaces, magical weapons and *yantras*, and (in some tellings) a hand in the architecture of **Laṅkā** or its halls through the Mandodarī connection. [folk/regional] *(Source: Purāṇic and folk traditions.)*

## Maya & the Sūrya Siddhānta

- **Recipient of revealed astronomy** — The astronomical classic the **Sūrya Siddhānta** frames its own teaching as **revealed by Sūrya (the Sun) to the asura Maya**: the Sun, unable to descend himself, dispatches an **emissary (a *puruṣa* born of the Sun)** to **Maya at the end of the Satya/Kṛta Yuga (or its junction)**, who imparts the doctrine of planetary mean motions, the Earth's sphericity, eclipse computation, and the **4,320,000-year Mahāyuga**. Maya is thus cast as the **human-tradition's conduit** for the science — the asura-architect doubling as astronomer. See [Siddhāntic astronomy §2](../00-time-and-cosmology/siddhantic-astronomy-and-astronomers.md). *(Source: Sūrya Siddhānta, opening frame.)*
  - *Variant (author vs. recipient):* tradition variously credits Maya as **author/compiler** of the text or merely as its **divinely-instructed recipient**; the extant recension is a mid-1st-millennium-CE redaction that has only retained the Maya-frame as a sacred attribution. [scholarly — the "two clocks": the *narrative* sets it at a yuga-junction; the *historical* text is centuries CE.] *(Source: Sūrya Siddhānta; histories of Indian astronomy.)*
  - *Variant (which Maya):* whether the astronomer-Maya is identical to the Tripura/Mahābhārata architect is a **harmonization** of shared name; the texts assume one figure, scholarship treats the identification as traditional. [disputed] *(Source: Purāṇic-Siddhāntic tradition.)*

## Maya ↔ Episode ↔ Yuga (quick map)

| Episode | Role of Maya | Yuga (scriptural clock) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tripura's three cities | Builder of the gold/silver/iron *pura* | Satya/Treta | Śiva P.; Matsya P.; MBh Karṇa |
| Sūrya Siddhānta | Recipient of solar astronomy | Satya/Kṛta junction | Sūrya Siddhānta |
| Mandodarī | Father of Rāvaṇa's queen | Treta | Rāmāyaṇa |
| Khāṇḍava-daha | Spared by Arjuna & Kṛṣṇa | Dvāpara | MBh Ādi Parva |
| Mayasabhā | Builder of the illusion-hall | Dvāpara | MBh Sabhā Parva |

> **Two clocks:** the *narrative* places Maya across Satya–Treta–Dvāpara as one recurring being; the *historical* Sūrya Siddhānta is a CE-era astronomical text that merely retains his name as a sacred frame. Parentages, the mother of Mandodarī, and the author-vs-recipient question vary by recension — flagged inline.
