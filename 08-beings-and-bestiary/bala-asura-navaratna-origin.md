# Bala (Vala) the Asura — the Self-Sacrificed Demon & the Origin of the Nine Gems

> Part of [08 · Beings & Bestiary](README.md) → an **asura of the primordial deva–asura wars** whose willing death is the **charter myth of Indian gemology** (*ratna-śāstra*).
> Cosmic address: an undated episode of the recurring **deva–asura conflict** ([roster](asura-wars-and-roster.md)) — narrative time is primordial/cross-yuga; the *texts* that carry it are historical (Gupta-to-medieval) [scholarly].

**Bala** (बल; also **Vala**, *Balāsura*) is the great asura who — unlike every other demon in the bestiary — dies not in battle but **as a consenting sacrificial victim** of the gods. His sanctified body, dismembered by the sacrifice, does not decay: each member becomes the **seed (*bīja*) of a precious stone**, and where the fleeing gods drop those seeds, the earth's **gem-mines** arise — blood becoming ruby, teeth pearl, eyes sapphire, bile emerald. The tale is the opening frame of the **ratna-parīkṣā** ("examination of gems") literature: the **Garuḍa Purāṇa**'s gemology chapters (Pūrva-khaṇḍa, chs. ~68–70 and following), the **Agni Purāṇa**'s parallel gem section (ch. 246), and — in its fullest form — the independent lapidary treatise of **Buddhabhaṭṭa**, the *Ratnaparīkṣā*. Through it, every jewel worn as a planetary remedy (the **navaratna** of the [nine grahas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/navagraha.md)) is, in origin, a relic of a demon's perfect act of giving.

---

## I. Who is Bala? — identity and the Vedic name-echo

- **Bala the asura-king** — In the lapidary frame-story Bala is a **mighty asura (daitya) lord**, so powerful that he **overcomes Indra and the gods in war**; the devas cannot slay him by force. His defining trait is not violence but an **unbreakable fidelity to his word** — the very virtue the gods exploit. *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa, Pūrva-khaṇḍa gem chapters; Buddhabhaṭṭa's Ratnaparīkṣā, opening verses.)*
  - *Variant (name-form):* the texts and their editions oscillate between **Bala** ("strength") and **Vala**; b/v alternation is routine in Sanskrit manuscripts, and translations of the same chapter use both. [scholarly] *(Source: editions of the Garuḍa Purāṇa; L. Finot's edition of the Ratnaparīkṣā.)*
  - *Variant (the Ṛgvedic Vala):* the **Vedic Vala** is a different figure — the **cave/rock-enclosure demon** (or the personified cave itself) split open by [Indra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/indra.md), Bṛhaspati and the Aṅgirases to free the stolen cows/dawns (Ṛgveda 3.31; 10.108; see [Saramā and the Paṇis](mythic-beasts-and-composites.md) and [Aṅgiras](../04-deep-dives/angiras.md)). Whether the Purāṇic gem-asura deliberately reuses that ancient name, or is an unrelated Bala, is a **harmonization question**: the lapidary texts themselves do not connect the two, and scholarship treats the identity as a name-echo rather than a continuous biography. [disputed] [scholarly] *(Source: Ṛgveda Vala hymns vs. Garuḍa Purāṇa/Ratnaparīkṣā frame; comparative discussions of the navaratna myth.)*
  - *Variant (Indra's epithet):* later tradition knows Indra as **Balavṛtrahan**, "slayer of Bala and Vṛtra," pairing a demon *Bala* with Vṛtra as Indra's two archetypal victims — a pairing sometimes read back into the gem-myth's asura. [disputed] *(Source: epic-Purāṇic epithets of Indra.)*
- **Place in the asura roster** — Bala belongs to the generic **daitya/asura war-cycle** rather than to a fixed genealogy: the gem texts introduce him simply as a great asura of the deva–asura wars, without the careful Kaśyapa–Diti/Danu pedigree given to Hiraṇyakaśipu or Maya. See the [asura roster & wars](asura-wars-and-roster.md) for the genealogical frame he floats free of. *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa gem chapters — frame narrative.)*

## II. The self-sacrifice — the myth, event by event

- **Bala's ascendancy** — In the war of gods and demons, **Bala grows invincible**; the devas, unable to defeat him, are reduced to stratagem. **[primordial deva–asura war-time; cross-yuga narrative frame].** *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa, Pūrva-khaṇḍa ch. ~68; Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā.)*
- **The gods' request — a victim for the sacrifice** — The devas, preparing (or feigning) a great **yajña**, come to Bala and ask him to serve as the **paśu, the sacrificial victim**, knowing he never refuses a petitioner. **Bala consents** — the asura, bound by truthfulness and generosity, gives his own body to his enemies' rite. *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa, Pūrva-khaṇḍa gem chapters; Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā.)*
  - *Variant (trick vs. free gift):* tellings differ in tone — some frame it as the gods' **deception** of a magnanimous foe (the request itself is the weapon), others as Bala's **conscious self-offering** that wins him imperishable merit; the texts let both readings stand, and the gem-charter needs only the consent. [disputed] *(Source: recensions and retellings of the ratna-parīkṣā frame.)*
- **Death and transfiguration** — Bala is **bound and immolated/dismembered** in the sacrifice. Because he died as a **sanctified victim in a divine rite**, his body does not become carrion: his **members turn into the seeds of gems** (*ratna-bīja*), radiant and imperishable. *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa, Pūrva-khaṇḍa ch. ~68; Agni Purāṇa 246; Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā.)*
- **The scattering — how the mines were sown** — The **gods, nāgas, yakṣas and other beings rush to carry off** the shining seeds through the sky in their vimānas; startled (in the common telling, while fleeing or jostling), they **drop them** — and wherever a seed falls, in **oceans, rivers, mountains and forests**, a **gem-mine (ākara)** arises there. The world's gem-deposits are thus the fallout-map of a single sacrifice. *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa, Pūrva-khaṇḍa gem chapters; Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā.)*
  - *Variant (who carries the seeds):* the carrier-lists differ — devas alone, or devas with **nāgas** ([serpent-lords](nagas-and-serpents.md)) and celestial beings; the emerald episode below singles out **Garuḍa** as one such carrier. *(Source: Ratnaparīkṣā tradition; Garuḍa Purāṇa.)*

## III. The member-to-gem concordance

- **The canonical mapping** — The lapidary texts distribute Bala's body across the gem-kingdom; the list as carried in this corpus's [Garuḍa Purāṇa profile](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/garuda-purana.md):
  **blood → ruby** (padmarāga) · **teeth → pearl** (muktā) · **bile → emerald** (marakata) · **eyes → sapphire** (indranīla) · **flesh → coral** (vidruma) · **skin → topaz** (puṣparāga) · **marrow → chrysoberyl** · **nails → cat's-eye** (vaidūrya) · **fat → rock-crystal** (sphaṭika). *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa, Pūrva-khaṇḍa chs. ~68–70; Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā; Agni Purāṇa 246.)*
  - *Variant (bones → diamond):* Buddhabhaṭṭa — and the Garuḍa Purāṇa's own diamond chapter — derive **diamond (vajra) from Bala's bones**, a tenth correspondence the nine-item summary omits; the pun is exact, since *vajra* names both Indra's **thunderbolt** and the **diamond**, the two hardest things in the cosmos. *(Source: Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā; Garuḍa Purāṇa vajra chapter.)*
  - *Variant (unstable assignments):* the **soft-tissue attributions wander** between texts and manuscripts — skin, flesh, fat, marrow, semen and even the asura's **battle-cry or luster** are variously matched to topaz, coral, crystal, bhīṣma-stone and lesser gems; only the "hard" pairs (blood–ruby, teeth–pearl, eyes–sapphire, bile–emerald, bones–diamond) are stable across the tradition. [scholarly] *(Source: comparison of Garuḍa Purāṇa, Agni Purāṇa 246, and Buddhabhaṭṭa recensions.)*
- **Why the mapping convinces** — the correspondences are **iconic**: red blood for the ruby, white enamel for the pearl, dark iris for the blue sapphire, bile's sheen for emerald-green, bone for adamant. The myth encodes a **color-and-substance taxonomy** of gems as much as a story. [scholarly] *(Source: studies of the Indian lapidary tradition.)*

## IV. Gem by gem — the seeds, their carriers, and the mines

- **Pearl (teeth)** — Bala's teeth fall into the **waters**, seeding the pearl; the lapidaries then enumerate the famous **eight sources of pearls** — from **oysters, conch-shells, elephants (frontal pearls), boars, fish, serpents (nāga-maṇi), bamboo, and the clouds** — of which only the oyster-pearl is common; the other seven are wonder-pearls of kings. *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa, pearl chapter (Pūrva-khaṇḍa ch. ~69); Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā.)*
  - *Variant:* the cloud-pearl (falling from the sky, seized by the gods before it lands) is flagged even within the texts as all-but-unobtainable — a limit-case of the taxonomy. *(Source: Ratnaparīkṣā tradition.)*
- **Ruby (blood)** — Bala's **blood** is carried through the sky and falls on the island of **Laṅkā/Siṃhala (Ceylon)** — in the lapidary telling into the river called **Rāvaṇagaṅgā** — whence the **ruby-gravels of Ceylon**, the classical world's ruby source. [regional charter — the myth maps real Sri Lankan gem-beds.] *(Source: Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā (ruby section); Garuḍa Purāṇa ruby chapter.)*
- **Emerald (bile)** — Bala's **bile** is seized by the **serpent-king Vāsuki** (in the standard telling), from whom **[Garuḍa](garuda.md)** — the gods' eagle and the Purāṇa's own eponym — snatches it; dropped (or disgorged) by Garuḍa, it falls in a remote **mountain land beyond the frontier**, seeding the emerald mines. The enmity of eagle and serpent thus writes itself into the emerald's pedigree. *(Source: Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā (emerald section); Garuḍa Purāṇa emerald chapter.)*
- **Diamond (bones)** — the bones scatter widest, and the lapidaries list **eight diamond-bearing tracts** of the classical map (named variously — e.g. Himālaya, Saurāṣṭra, Kaliṅga, Kosala, Veṇvātaṭa, Sūrpāraka, Pauṇḍra, Mataṅga in Buddhabhaṭṭa's list), each yielding stones of a characteristic tint and virtue. [scholarly — the geography reflects historical Indian diamond trade.] *(Source: Buddhabhaṭṭa, Ratnaparīkṣā (diamond section); cf. Garuḍa Purāṇa vajra chapter.)*
- **Sapphire (eyes)** — the eyes fall in **Siṃhala** as well, making Ceylon doubly blessed — ruby from the blood, blue sapphire from the eyes — matching its real status as the subcontinent's gem-island. *(Source: Ratnaparīkṣā tradition; Garuḍa Purāṇa sapphire chapter.)*

## V. Ratna-parīkṣā — the śāstra the myth charters

- **From myth to manual** — After the origin-story, the Garuḍa Purāṇa's gem chapters turn technical: **testing authenticity** (immersion in water, abrasion, fire-resistance, luster against light), **grading** by color, clarity, weight and cut, the **flaws (doṣa)** that make a stone dangerous and the **virtues (guṇa)** that make it protective. The Bala myth is the **charter** that explains *why* gems have power at all: they are relics of a sanctified body. *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa, Pūrva-khaṇḍa gem chapters; Agni Purāṇa 246.)*
- **The moral physics of jewels** — a **pure gem** worn by a king wards off calamity (poison, serpents, disease, the wrath of planets); a **flawed gem**, even unknowingly worn, invites ruin — so the śāstra insists the king retain **expert gem-examiners** (*ratna-parīkṣaka*). *(Source: Garuḍa Purāṇa gem chapters; Ratnaparīkṣā tradition.)*
- **The navaratna and the nine grahas** — the mature **navaratna** set — ruby (Sūrya), pearl (Candra), coral (Maṅgala), emerald (Budha), yellow sapphire (Bṛhaspati), diamond (Śukra), blue sapphire (Śani), hessonite (Rāhu), cat's-eye (Ketu) — welds Bala's relics to the **nine planets** as astrological remedies; see the full correspondence table in [Navagraha](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/navagraha.md) and the remedial frame in [Jyotiṣa](../00-time-and-cosmology/jyotisha-and-the-zodiac.md). *(Source: jyotiṣa/ratna tradition.)*
  - *Variant (dating the weld):* the **planet-to-gem assignment is a later systematization** than the Bala origin-myth — the classical lapidaries grade and locate gems but do not yet prescribe the nine-planet amulet; the navaratna-as-graha-remedy belongs to the medieval astrological synthesis. [scholarly] [late text] *(Source: histories of Indian gemology and jyotiṣa.)*

## VI. Resonances — the asura's body in the wider mythology

- **Dadhīci's bones** — the closest structural twin: the ṛṣi **[Dadhīci](../04-deep-dives/dadhichi.md)** gives up his body so his **bones become the vajra** that slays Vṛtra; Bala gives up his so his **bones become vajra-the-diamond**. Sage and asura mirror each other as willing victims whose skeletons arm and adorn the world. *(Source: comparative — Mahābhārata/Purāṇic Dadhīci cycle vs. the ratna charter.)* [scholarly]
- **The dismembered Puruṣa** — the template of a **primordial being's division into the world's goods** goes back to the **Puruṣa-sūkta** (Ṛgveda 10.90), where the cosmic Man's sacrifice yields the orders of society and cosmos; the Bala myth applies the same sacrificial anatomy to the mineral kingdom. [scholarly] *(Source: Ṛgveda 10.90; comparative studies of sacrifice-cosmogony.)*
- **The other gem-origin** — the tradition's *second* great jewel-charter is the **[Samudra Manthana](../04-deep-dives/samudra-manthana.md)**, which churns the **Kaustubha** and the ocean's ratnas into being; individual named jewels (Kaustubha, Syamantaka) have their own histories in [Divine objects & treasures](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/divine-objects-and-treasures.md). The two charters coexist without harmonization: the ocean yields the **singular divine jewels**, Bala's body the **mineral species and their mines**. *(Source: Bhāgavata/Viṣṇu Purāṇa churning narratives vs. Garuḍa Purāṇa ratna chapters.)*

## VII. Two clocks — where this sits in time

- **Scriptural clock** — the sacrifice of Bala belongs to the **undated primordial deva–asura war-time**: before the mines existed, in the same mythic stratum as the churning of the ocean — effectively **cross-yuga charter time**, not pinned to a yuga of the current Mahāyuga. *(Source: frame narrative of the Garuḍa Purāṇa gem chapters.)*
- **Academic clock [scholarly]** — **Buddhabhaṭṭa's *Ratnaparīkṣā*** is dated broadly to the **mid-first millennium CE** (Gupta-era horizon; edited and translated by **Louis Finot**, *Les lapidaires indiens*, 1896); the **Garuḍa Purāṇa's gemology chapters** are generally placed **c. 800–1000 CE** within a Purāṇa whose compilation spans roughly the 1st millennium CE; the **Agni Purāṇa's** parallel (ch. 246) belongs to that encyclopedia's **c. 800–1100 CE** redaction. Scholars read the three as one flowing lapidary tradition — the Purāṇas condensing material of the Buddhabhaṭṭa type. *(Source: histories of Sanskrit gemological literature; see the [Garuḍa Purāṇa profile](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/garuda-purana.md) and [Agni Purāṇa profile](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/agni-purana.md).)*
- **Never collapse the clocks** — the *story* is primordial; the *texts* are medieval. The myth's function is charter, not chronicle: it licenses a real historical craft (gem-testing for kings, documented in the ratna-śāstra branch of the [auxiliary sciences](../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md)) by rooting it in sacrificial prehistory.
