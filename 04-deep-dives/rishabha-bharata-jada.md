# Ṛṣabhadeva, Bharata & the Deer — Renunciation across Births

> Cosmic address: [Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md) → an early-dynasty episode of the [Priyavrata line](../03-lineages-vamsha/README.md), narrated in the [Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/smriti-puranas.md). Parent: [04-deep-dives](./) · See also the rival [Śakuntalā–Bharata](./shakuntala-bharata.md) eponym claim and the [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md).

A single soul traced across an emperor, a deer, and a silent sage — the Bhāgavata's most sustained parable on how **attachment at the moment of death determines the next birth**. The narrative arc runs through **Bhāgavata Purāṇa 5.3–5.14**: the avatāra-father Ṛṣabhadeva (5.3–6), his son Emperor Bharata (5.7–8), and the deer→Jaḍa Bharata sequence with the Rahūgaṇa teachings (5.8–14). The same Ṛṣabha is, in **Jain tradition**, the first Tīrthaṅkara (Ādinātha) — both readings are catalogued below.

---

## I. Ṛṣabhadeva — the Avatāra (Bhāgavata 5.3–6)

- **Birth from Merudevī (BhP 5.3)** - King Nābhi (son of Āgnīdhra, of the Priyavrata line) and his wife Merudevī perform a sacrifice longing for a son; Viṣṇu himself appears and agrees to descend as their child. He is born to Merudevī and named **Ṛṣabha** ("bull"), counted as an **avatāra of Nārāyaṇa** (BhP 5.3; also Viṣṇu Purāṇa 2.1).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata numbering):* Ṛṣabha is enumerated among the līlā-avatāras of the Lord; some Vaiṣṇava lists place him as the **8th avatāra** in the Bhāgavata's enumeration of incarnations. [scholarly]
- **Marks of greatness (BhP 5.4)** - Ṛṣabha bears all auspicious bodily marks; Indra, envious, withholds rain from his realm Ajanābha, so Ṛṣabha by yoga-power rains upon it. He marries **Jayantī** (given by Indra) and fathers **one hundred sons**, the eldest being **Bharata** — after whom the land becomes **Bhārata-varṣa / Ajanābha → Bhārata** (BhP 5.4; 5.7).
  - *Variant (eponym, this strand):* the Bhāgavata explicitly derives **Bhārata-varṣa from this Bharata, son of Ṛṣabha** — competing with the Dushyanta-line claim (see §V).
  - *Sub-sons:* nine became kings of the nine divisions; eighty-one became brāhmaṇas; nine — **Kavi, Havi, Antarīkṣa, Prabuddha, Pippalāyana, Āvirhotra, Drumila, Camasa, Karabhājana** — became the **Nava-Yogendras**, paramahaṃsa preachers of Bhāgavata-dharma (BhP 5.4; their teachings later given in 11.2–5).
- **The Ṛṣabha-gītā — instructions to his sons (BhP 5.5)** - Before renouncing, Ṛṣabha delivers a celebrated discourse: the human body is **not meant for sense gratification** but for **tapas** and self-realization; attachment (saṅga) binds, association with saintly persons (sādhu-saṅga) liberates. A core renunciation teaching of the Purāṇa.
- **Renunciation and the avadhūta path (BhP 5.5–6)** - Ṛṣabha crowns Bharata, abandons his kingdom, and wanders as a naked **avadhūta**, behaving like a madman, mute, deaf — outwardly inert to escape social entanglement (the same posture his descendant Jaḍa Bharata will later adopt).
- **Death by forest fire (BhP 5.6)** - Wandering through Karṇāṭaka/Koṅka/Veṅka and the Kuṭakācala region, his body — emaciated by austerity — is consumed by a wildfire kindled by friction of bamboos; he quits the body absorbed in the Self.
  - *Note:* the Bhāgavata adds that the regional king **Arhat**, hearing of Ṛṣabha's path in the coming **Kali-yuga**, will misconstrue it and found an irreligious (non-Vedic) creed — a passage read by some as a polemical Bhāgavata gloss on **Jainism**. [scholarly] [disputed]

## II. Ṛṣabha as First Tīrthaṅkara — the Jain Account (Ādinātha)

The Jain tradition independently venerates **Ṛṣabhanātha / Ṛṣabhadeva**, also **Ādinātha** ("first lord"), **Ādīśvara**, **Yugādideva**, **Nābheya**, as the **1st of 24 Tīrthaṅkaras** of the present descending half-cycle (avasarpiṇī). (Kalpa-sūtra; Ādi-purāṇa of Jinasena; Hemacandra's Triṣaṣṭi-śalākā-puruṣa-carita.)

- **Parents and birth (Jain)** - Born to King **Nābhi** and Queen **Marudevī** at **Ayodhyā (Vinītā / Ikṣvāku-bhūmi)** — the same parental names as the Bhāgavata (Nābhi, Merudevī≈Marudevī), a striking convergence. (Jain Ādi-purāṇa.)
- **Founder of civilization** - In an age when humans lived on wish-fulfilling kalpa-trees that began to fail, Ṛṣabha taught the **six livelihoods (ṣaṭ-karma)** — agriculture (asi/masi/kṛṣi traditions), writing, arithmetic, commerce, crafts, and the arts; established marriage, law, and the kingdom. Credited with founding ordered society. (Jain tradition.)
- **The hundred sons; Bharata and Bāhubali** - Fathers **100 sons** (by Sumaṅgalā and Sunandā) and daughters **Brāhmī** (script) and **Sundarī** (mathematics). Eldest **Bharata** becomes the first **cakravartin** (universal emperor); second son **Bāhubali** famously renounces after a duel with Bharata and attains kevala-jñāna (the colossal Gommaṭeśvara statue at Śravaṇabeḷagoḷa depicts him). (Jain Ādi-purāṇa; Hemacandra.)
  - *Variant (eponym, Jain):* India is named **Bhārata after this Bharata, son of Ṛṣabha** — the Jain assertion mirrors the Bhāgavata's, reinforcing the Ṛṣabha-line claim over the Dushyanta-line.
- **Renunciation and omniscience** - Witnessing the sudden death of the dancer **Nīlāñjanā** (sent by Indra), Ṛṣabha perceives impermanence, renounces, and undertakes extreme austerity — including a **year-long fast** broken only when Prince **Śreyāṃsa** offers sugarcane juice (commemorated as **Akṣaya-tṛtīyā**). He attains **kevala-jñāna** (omniscience) at **Purimatāla** and preaches the fourfold order. (Jain tradition.)
- **Nirvāṇa at Aṣṭāpada (Mount Kailāsa)** - Attains **mokṣa/nirvāṇa** on **Mount Aṣṭāpada**, identified with **Kailāsa**. His emblem is the **bull (vṛṣabha)**. (Kalpa-sūtra.)
- *Cross-tradition note:* whether the Bhāgavata's Ṛṣabha and the Jain Ādinātha are the **same figure refracted by two traditions** is debated; the shared parents, the bull association, the 100 sons, the eldest son Bharata, and the radical nude-ascetic mode strongly link them. [scholarly]

## III. Emperor Bharata (Bhāgavata 5.7–8a)

- **A model reign (BhP 5.7)** - Crowned by Ṛṣabha, **Bharata** rules the earth (renamed **Bhārata-varṣa** after him) righteously, marries **Pañcajanī** (daughter of Viśvarūpa), fathers five sons, and performs many sacrifices, all the while remaining inwardly detached and devoted to Vāsudeva.
- **Renunciation at Pulaha-āśrama (BhP 5.7)** - After ruling for an immense span, Bharata divides the empire among his sons and retires to the hermitage of **Pulaha** at **Hari-kṣetra / Śālagrāma** on the banks of the river **Gaṇḍakī**, living on roots, flowers, and water, absorbed in worship and meditation on the Lord.

---

## IV. The Deer, and the Birth as Jaḍa Bharata (Bhāgavata 5.8)

- **The orphaned fawn (BhP 5.8)** - At the Gaṇḍakī, a pregnant doe drinking water is startled by a **lion's roar**; leaping the river in terror she miscarries and dies. The newborn **fawn** is carried off by the current; Bharata rescues it, brings it to his āśrama, and rears it.
- **Attachment overtakes austerity (BhP 5.8)** - The ascetic's heart fastens upon the deer — feeding it, protecting it from wolves, fretting when it strays, carrying it on his shoulder. His meditation and devotional practice **slacken**; the very renunciate who gave up an empire is bound by a single animal.
- **Death with the deer in mind (BhP 5.8)** - At the end of his life, lying down to die, Bharata's last thought is of the deer beside him. **"As one thinks at death, so is one's next birth"** — and so the great king is **reborn as a deer**.
- **The conscious deer (BhP 5.8)** - By the grace of his prior devotion he is born **remembering his past life** (jāti-smara) in the body of a deer in the Kālañjara mountains. Lamenting his fall, the deer-Bharata abandons the herd, returns to Pulaha-āśrama and Śālagrāma, lives austerely on dry leaves, and there quits the deer-body — this time wholly detached.

## V. Jaḍa Bharata — the Silent Sage (Bhāgavata 5.9–14)

- **Birth in a brāhmaṇa family (BhP 5.9)** - The soul is reborn as the son of a pious brāhmaṇa of the **Āṅgirasa** line, by his second wife. Determined never again to be entangled, he deliberately acts **deaf, dumb, and dull** — earning the mocking name **Jaḍa Bharata** ("inert / idiot Bharata"). He performs no duties, eats whatever comes, endures abuse, and conceals his realized state.
- **Exploited as a laborer (BhP 5.9)** - After his father dies, half-brothers treat him as a fool and set him to menial farm work; he does it indifferently. Hired to guard a field, he is even seized by followers of a **dasyu-chief (a band of Bhīlla/Śūdra robbers)** to be **offered as a human sacrifice to Bhadra Kālī** — but the goddess herself, enraged at the attempt to kill a saint, springs from her image, slays the would-be sacrificers, and frees him (BhP 5.9).
- **The palanquin and King Rahūgaṇa (BhP 5.10)** - Conscripted by the **chief of King Rahūgaṇa of Sindhu-Sauvīra** to help carry the royal **palanquin**, Jaḍa Bharata walks erratically — stepping carefully to avoid treading on ants — jolting the ride. The king rebukes him sarcastically; Jaḍa Bharata finally **breaks his silence** with a piercing reply on the body, the self, and false ego.
- **The Bharata-gītā / instructions to Rahūgaṇa (BhP 5.11–12)** - In a sustained discourse he teaches: the mind is the seat of bondage and liberation; designations like "king" and "carrier," "fat" and "thin" pertain only to the body, not the **ātman**; only **bhakti** and the dust of devotees' feet (mahat-saṅga) cut the knot of material existence. Rahūgaṇa descends and bows in humility.
- **The Forest of Material Existence — the great allegory (BhP 5.13)** - Jaḍa Bharata expands the teaching into the famous **bhavāṭavī (forest of saṃsāra)** allegory: the conditioned soul as a lost merchant wandering a fearful forest beset by thieves (senses), jackals (wife/family), mosquitoes and gnats (gossip and envy), will-o'-the-wisps and a whirlwind (illusion) — a complete map of bondage. (BhP 5.13)
- **Detailed decoding of the allegory (BhP 5.14)** - Śuka-deva, at Parīkṣit's request, then unpacks each forest image point by point — the eleven robbers (ten senses + mind), the python of sloth, the burden of family, the mirage of sense-pleasure — concluding the Jaḍa Bharata cycle. (BhP 5.14)
- **Outcome** - Jaḍa Bharata, having delivered the king and never again falling to attachment, completes his life liberated — the parable's resolution of the chain begun by one fawn.

## VI. The Two "Bharata" Eponym Claims [disputed]

Hindu tradition transmits **two distinct kings named Bharata**, each credited with giving **Bhārata-varṣa / Bhārata** its name:

- **This strand — Bharata, son of Ṛṣabhadeva** - Asserted by the **Bhāgavata Purāṇa (5.7)** and **Viṣṇu Purāṇa (2.1)**, and emphatically by **Jain tradition** (Ādi-purāṇa): the land is named for Ṛṣabha's eldest son, the first cakravartin. This is the **cosmographic / Purāṇic** derivation (the varṣa-naming of Jambūdvīpa).
- **The rival strand — Bharata, son of Duṣyanta and Śakuntalā** - Asserted in the **Mahābhārata (Ādi-parva)** and popularized by Kālidāsa's *Abhijñāna-śākuntalam*; this is the **epic/dynastic** derivation, the ancestor of the Kurus and Pāṇḍavas. See [Śakuntalā & Bharata](./shakuntala-bharata.md).
- *Reconciliation attempts (scholarly):* the two are usually treated as **separate persons in separate epochs** (the Ṛṣabha-Bharata far earlier in the dynasty than the Pauravā Duṣyanta-Bharata); the name "Bharata" recurs, and which one "the country is named for" depends on whether one privileges Purāṇic cosmography or epic genealogy. The Timeline **catalogs both** rather than choosing. [scholarly]
