# The Bhagavad Gītā

> Where this sits: a 700-verse philosophical dialogue embedded in the **Bhīṣma Parva** of the [Mahābhārata](../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md), spoken on the first day of the Kurukṣetra war during the [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) of our 28th Mahāyuga, just before the onset of [Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md). Spoken by [Kṛṣṇa](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md), the 8th avatāra of [Viṣṇu](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md).
>
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](./) · Up to: [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md)

The Bhagavad Gītā ("Song of the Lord") is a dialogue between the warrior-prince **Arjuna** and his charioteer **Kṛṣṇa**, delivered in the no-man's-land between the two armies at Kurukṣetra. Though only ~700 verses, it became the de facto summation of Hindu soteriology — one of the three canonical "starting points" (*prasthāna-trayī*) of Vedānta alongside the [Upaniṣads](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md) and the Brahma-sūtras. It reconciles the three great paths — **karma** (action), **bhakti** (devotion), **jñāna** (knowledge) — into one integral discipline.

## Textual placement & frame

- **Location in the Mahābhārata** - Occupies the Bhīṣma Parva (6th book), chapters 23–40 of the Bhīṣma Parva proper (in the critical edition's continuous numbering ≈ 6.25–6.42). The 18 chapters (*adhyāyas*) of the Gītā echo the 18 *parvas* of the epic and the 18 days of the war. (Mahābhārata, Bhīṣma Parva)
- **The narrative envelope** - The whole battlefield scene is narrated retrospectively by **Sañjaya**, charioteer-bard of the blind king **Dhṛtarāṣṭra**, granted divine sight (*divya-cakṣus*) by [Vyāsa](../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md) to relay the war. The Gītā opens with Dhṛtarāṣṭra's single question (1.1) and closes with Sañjaya's awe-struck epilogue (18.74–78). (Mahābhārata)
- **Verse count** - The standard accepted text is **700 verses**; some manuscripts and the Kashmiri recension count **701** or up to **745** verses ("Kashmiri/Abhinavagupta recension"). 700 is the historic standard. [scholarly]
- **Composition date** - Most academics place the text's composition between the **2nd century BCE and 2nd century CE** (estimates range c. 5th c. BCE – 1st c. CE), later than the Gītā's *in-narrative* Dvāpara-yuga setting (war traditionally placed c. 3102 BCE, just before Kali Yuga began). Distinguish scriptural internal time from text-critical dating. [scholarly]
- **Speakers tally** - Kṛṣṇa speaks the vast majority of verses; Arjuna ~85; Sañjaya ~40; Dhṛtarāṣṭra one (1.1).

## Context: Arjuna's despondency (the *viṣāda*)

- **The conch-blast prelude** - Both armies sound their conches; Kṛṣṇa's is *Pāñcajanya*, Arjuna's *Devadatta*, Bhīma's *Pauṇḍra*. Arjuna asks Kṛṣṇa to drive his chariot into the gap between the armies to survey the foe. (Gītā 1.14–25)
- **Arjuna's collapse** - Seeing his own kinsmen, teachers (Droṇa), and grandsire (Bhīṣma) arrayed to be killed, Arjuna's limbs fail, his bow Gāṇḍīva slips from his hand, his skin burns; he refuses to fight, fearing the sin of slaying family and the ruin of clan-dharma (*kula-dharma*). (Gītā 1.28–47)
- **The crisis defined** - Arjuna sinks into the chariot, casting away his bow and arrows — the human predicament of duty vs. attachment that the entire Gītā answers. This *arjuna-viṣāda* gives Chapter 1 its name. (Gītā 1.47, 2.1–9)
- **Surrender as student** - Arjuna formally takes refuge: "I am your disciple (*śiṣyas te'ham*); instruct me" (2.7) — converting a battlefield refusal into a teaching occasion.

## The 18 chapters — mapped to the three yoga triads

The text is traditionally divided into three *ṣaṭkas* (sextets): **Karma-ṣaṭka** (1–6), **Bhakti-ṣaṭka** (7–12), **Jñāna-ṣaṭka** (13–18). Vaiṣṇava commentators read these as resolving into *tat tvam asi*: 1–6 explore *tvam* (the self/jīva), 7–12 *tat* (the Lord/devotion), 13–18 *asi* (their union).

### Karma-ṣaṭka — chapters 1–6 (the self, action, and discipline)

- **Ch. 1 — Arjuna-viṣāda Yoga** ("Arjuna's Despair") - Survey of armies; Arjuna's breakdown and refusal to fight. The problem is posed. (Gītā 1)
- **Ch. 2 — Sāṅkhya Yoga** ("The Yoga of Knowledge") - The doctrinal seed-chapter: the imperishable **ātman** that "is not slain when the body is slain" (2.20); *svadharma* of the warrior; introduction of **niṣkāma-karma** (action without attachment to fruit, 2.47 — *karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana*); the *sthitaprajña* (person of steady wisdom, 2.54–72). (Gītā 2)
- **Ch. 3 — Karma Yoga** ("The Yoga of Action") - Why action cannot be escaped; action as **yajña** (sacrifice); acting for *loka-saṅgraha* (world-welfare); doing one's own duty imperfectly is better than another's done well (echoed 3.35). (Gītā 3)
- **Ch. 4 — Jñāna-Karma-Sannyāsa Yoga** ("Renunciation of Action in Knowledge") - Kṛṣṇa declares the doctrine eternal, taught first to Vivasvān (the sun); the famous **avatāra verse** "*yadā yadā hi dharmasya glānir bhavati*…" (4.7–8) — God incarnates whenever dharma declines; action burned up in the fire of knowledge. (Gītā 4)
- **Ch. 5 — Karma-Sannyāsa Yoga** ("Renunciation of Action") - Reconciles renunciation (*sannyāsa*) and selfless action (*karma-yoga*) as leading to the same goal; the sage sees the self as actionless even while acting. (Gītā 5)
- **Ch. 6 — Dhyāna / Ātma-saṃyama Yoga** ("The Yoga of Meditation") - Practical meditation: posture, moderation in food/sleep, steadying the restless mind; the fate of the *yoga-bhraṣṭa* (one who falls from yoga, reborn in favorable conditions). (Gītā 6)

### Bhakti-ṣaṭka — chapters 7–12 (the Lord and devotion)

- **Ch. 7 — Jñāna-Vijñāna Yoga** ("Knowledge and Realization") - Kṛṣṇa's two natures (lower/material *prakṛti* and higher/conscious); four types of devotees; rare is the one who knows Vāsudeva is all (*vāsudevaḥ sarvam iti*, 7.19). (Gītā 7)
- **Ch. 8 — Akṣara-Brahma Yoga** ("The Imperishable Absolute") - The last thought at death determines the next birth (8.6); the path of light (*devayāna*) vs. smoke (*pitṛyāna*); cosmic time — Brahmā's day and night reckoned in thousands of yugas (8.17, harmonizing with the [kalpa cosmology](../00-time-and-cosmology/brahma-lifetime.md)). (Gītā 8)
- **Ch. 9 — Rāja-Vidyā Rāja-Guhya Yoga** ("The Royal Knowledge, Royal Secret") - The supreme secret: the Lord pervades all yet is not contained in them; even the lowly-born who turn to Him in devotion are uplifted; "offer me a leaf, a flower, a fruit, water" (9.26). (Gītā 9)
- **Ch. 10 — Vibhūti Yoga** ("Divine Glories") - Kṛṣṇa enumerates his manifestations: "Among the Ādityas I am Viṣṇu… among rivers the Ganges… of letters I am 'A'" (10.21–42) — God as the essence of the best in every category. (Gītā 10)
- **Ch. 11 — Viśvarūpa-Darśana Yoga** ("The Vision of the Cosmic Form") - The theophany; see below. (Gītā 11)
- **Ch. 12 — Bhakti Yoga** ("The Yoga of Devotion") - Devotion to the personal Lord declared easier than meditation on the formless Absolute; the qualities of the beloved devotee (*bhakta*) — friendly, compassionate, free of egoism, content. (Gītā 12)

### Jñāna-ṣaṭka — chapters 13–18 (metaphysics and liberation)

- **Ch. 13 — Kṣetra-Kṣetrajña-Vibhāga Yoga** ("The Field and Its Knower") - The body as "field" (*kṣetra*) and the conscious self as "knower of the field" (*kṣetrajña*); Sāṅkhya analysis of *prakṛti* and *puruṣa*. (Gītā 13)
- **Ch. 14 — Guṇatraya-Vibhāga Yoga** ("The Three Guṇas") - The three strands of *prakṛti* — **sattva** (clarity), **rajas** (passion/activity), **tamas** (inertia/darkness) — bind the embodied self; the *guṇātīta* (one who transcends the guṇas). (Gītā 14)
- **Ch. 15 — Puruṣottama Yoga** ("The Supreme Person") - The inverted cosmic *aśvattha* (peepal) tree to be cut with the axe of detachment; the three *puruṣas* (perishable, imperishable, and the Supreme — Puruṣottama). (Gītā 15)
- **Ch. 16 — Daivāsura-Sampad-Vibhāga Yoga** ("Divine and Demonic Natures") - Catalog of *daivī* (divine) vs. *āsurī* (demonic) qualities; the three gates to hell — lust, anger, greed (16.21); scripture as authority for conduct. (Gītā 16)
- **Ch. 17 — Śraddhā-traya-Vibhāga Yoga** ("The Three Kinds of Faith") - Faith, food, sacrifice, austerity, and charity each classified by guṇa; the formula *oṃ tat sat*. (Gītā 17)
- **Ch. 18 — Mokṣa-Sannyāsa Yoga** ("Liberation through Renunciation") - The longest chapter; the grand synthesis: *tyāga* (relinquishment of fruits) vs. *sannyāsa*; the fourfold *varṇa* duties by guṇa; the supreme counsel "abandon all dharmas and take refuge in Me alone" (*sarva-dharmān parityajya mām ekaṃ śaraṇaṃ vraja*, 18.66); Arjuna's doubts dispelled — "I will do your word" (18.73). (Gītā 18)

## The Viśvarūpa — the cosmic theophany (Chapter 11)

- **The request and the granted eye** - Arjuna asks to see Kṛṣṇa's divine form; mortal eyes cannot perceive it, so Kṛṣṇa grants him a *divya-cakṣus* (divine eye). (Gītā 11.3–8)
- **The vision** - Arjuna beholds the **Viśvarūpa** (Universal Form): infinite faces, eyes, arms, and bellies; all gods, sages, and beings contained within; "if a thousand suns rose at once, that might match its splendor" (11.12). (Gītā 11.9–14)
- **Time the destroyer** - The most-quoted line: "**kālo'smi loka-kṣaya-kṛt pravṛddho**" — "I am Time (Kāla), the mighty world-destroyer, here to annihilate the worlds" (11.32). Kṛṣṇa shows the Kaurava warriors already rushing into his blazing mouths to their deaths — Arjuna is merely the *nimitta* (instrument). (Gītā 11.32–34)
- **Arjuna's terror and apology** - Overwhelmed, Arjuna praises and begs forgiveness for past familiarities ("calling you 'Kṛṣṇa, friend' in jest"); pleads for the gentle four-armed and then the human form to return. (Gītā 11.35–46)
- **Restoration** - Kṛṣṇa resumes his *saumya* (gentle) human shape, noting this terrible form is granted to almost none, not by Veda-study, sacrifice, or austerity — only by undivided devotion (11.52–54). (Gītā 11)

## Key concepts

- **Niṣkāma karma** ("desireless action") - Act for the act's sake, surrendering claim to results; the central ethical innovation, freeing action from binding *karma*. (Gītā 2.47–51)
- **Svadharma** ("one's own duty") - "Better one's own duty though imperfect than another's well-performed; better to die in one's own dharma" (3.35, repeated 18.47) — a warrior must fight; abdication is itself adharma. (Gītā 2.31–37, 3.35)
- **The three guṇas** - Sattva, rajas, tamas as the operative threads of *prakṛti*; all action, faith, food, and personality types are analyzed through them (Ch. 14, 17, 18). (Gītā 14)
- **The imperishable ātman** - The self is unborn, eternal, uncuttable, unburnable; death is a mere changing of garments (2.22). (Gītā 2.12–30)
- **Yoga as equanimity & skill** - "*Samatvaṃ yoga ucyate*" — yoga is evenness of mind (2.48); "*yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam*" — yoga is skill in action (2.50). (Gītā 2)
- **Avatāra doctrine** - God descends age after age to protect the good, destroy evildoers, and re-establish dharma (4.7–8) — the scriptural charter for the [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md). (Gītā 4)
- **Sthitaprajña** - The portrait of the liberated sage of steady wisdom, undisturbed by pleasure or pain (2.55–72). (Gītā 2)
- **Prapatti / śaraṇāgati** - Total surrender as the final teaching (18.66) — the seed of later Vaiṣṇava devotional theology. (Gītā 18)

## Major commentaries & interpretive variants

The Gītā's terse verses support radically different systematic readings; each ācārya read it as confirming his own school.

- **Śaṅkara — Advaita (non-dualism)** [c. 8th c. CE] - Earliest surviving complete bhāṣya. Reads the Gītā as teaching that liberation comes through **jñāna** (knowledge of the identity of ātman and Brahman); *karma-yoga* is preparatory, purifying the mind for knowledge but not itself the final means. The world is ultimately *māyā*. (Śaṅkara, *Gītā-bhāṣya*)
  - *Variant (Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, c. 16th c.):* later Advaitin *Gūḍhārtha-dīpikā* harmonizes jñāna and bhakti within Advaita.
- **Rāmānuja — Viśiṣṭādvaita (qualified non-dualism)** [c. 11th–12th c. CE] - Reads the climax as **bhakti** to the personal Lord Nārāyaṇa; selves are real, distinct, and dependent "modes" of God; surrender (*prapatti*, 18.66) and loving devotion are the supreme means. Rejects Śaṅkara's *māyā*-monism. (Rāmānuja, *Gītā-bhāṣya*)
- **Madhva — Dvaita (dualism)** [c. 13th c. CE] - Reads the Gītā as teaching an absolute, eternal distinction between God (Viṣṇu), souls, and matter; devotion to Viṣṇu through a proper hierarchy (*tāratamya*) of souls; liberation is service, never identity, with God. (Madhva, *Gītā-bhāṣya* & *Gītā-tātparya*)
- **Jñāneśvarī / Bhāvārtha-dīpikā (Jñāneśvar / Dnyāneshwar)** [1290 CE] - The oldest surviving literary work in Marathi; an Advaita-flavored verse commentary (9,000+ *ovis*) foundational to the **Vārkarī** bhakti tradition of Maharashtra; emphasizes *akṛtrima bhakti* (guileless devotion) and selfless action offered as worship. [regional tradition] (Jñāneśvar, *Jñāneśvarī*)
- **Abhinavagupta — Kashmir Śaiva reading** [c. 10th–11th c. CE] - The *Gītārtha-saṅgraha* interprets the Gītā through nondual Kashmir Śaivism; preserves the longer (Kashmiri) recension with extra verses. [scholarly]
- **Bāl Gaṅgādhar Tilak — karma-yoga / activist reading** [*Gītā-Rahasya*, written in prison 1910–11] - Argues the Gītā's primary message is **karma-yoga** (energetic, selfless action in the world), not renunciation or contemplative withdrawal; reads Kurukṣetra as a *dharma-kṣetra* legitimizing just struggle — a charter for nationalist activism. [modern]
- **M. K. Gandhi — allegorical ahiṃsā reading** [*Anāsaktiyoga*, Yeravda jail 1929; Eng. tr. Mahadev Desai 1946] - His "spiritual dictionary"; reads the battle **allegorically** as the inner war between good and evil within every person, and extracts **anāsakti** (non-attachment) supporting non-violence — explicitly *not* an endorsement of literal warfare. [modern]
  - *Variant (Aurobindo, *Essays on the Gita*, 1916–20):* synthesizes karma, bhakti, jñāna into an integral yoga of self-transcendence and divine action in the world. [modern]
  - *Variant (Vinoba Bhave, *Talks on the Gita*):* a Sarvodaya/spiritual-activist reading in the Gandhian lineage. [modern]
- **Vaiṣṇava devotional (Gauḍīya / ISKCON — Bhaktivedanta's *Gītā As It Is*, 1968):* reads Kṛṣṇa as the Supreme Personality of Godhead and the goal as pure *bhakti* (Kṛṣṇa-prema); among the most widely circulated modern editions. [modern]

## Reception & afterlife

- **Prasthāna-trayī status** - Together with the Upaniṣads and Brahma-sūtras, the Gītā is one of the three foundational texts every Vedānta school must comment upon to establish its system. (tradition)
- **The Gītā-māhātmya** - Many editions append a "Glories of the Gītā" appendix (from the Padma Purāṇa / Varāha Purāṇa) praising recitation of the text. [late text] (Purāṇic appendices)
- **Modern global influence** - Translated into English by Charles Wilkins (1785, the first), Edwin Arnold (*The Song Celestial*, 1885), and influential on Thoreau, Emerson, and 20th-c. independence-era thinkers. [scholarly]
