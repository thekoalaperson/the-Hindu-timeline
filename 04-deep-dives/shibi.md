# Śibi — the King & the Dove

> Cosmic address: a *Manvantara-frame exemplary tale* of the present **[7th — Vaivasvata — Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)**, set among the kings of the **[Chandravaṃśa (Lunar dynasty)](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)** — Śibi being a descendant of Anu son of Yayāti, and son of Uśīnara. It is the tradition's pre-eminent parable of **dāna (gift), śaraṇāgati (the duty to protect a refugee), and ahiṃsā weighed against the natural order**, narrated in the *Mahābhārata* and re-told as a Buddhist *Jātaka* and a Jain *kathā*.
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · See also: [Yayāti](yayati.md) · [Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) · [Chandravaṃśa lineage](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) · [karma / dharma](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md)

The episode of **King Śibi and the dove** is the most celebrated illustration in Hindu (and pan-Indic) literature of the principle that a king must **protect whoever takes refuge with him, at any cost to himself**. The gods **Indra** (king-tester par excellence) and **Agni** disguise themselves as a **hawk** and a **dove**; the hunted dove falls into Śibi's lap; the hawk demands its lawful prey; and the king resolves the impossible conflict — between the dove's claim to sanctuary and the hawk's claim to food — by offering his own flesh, equal to the dove's weight, on a balance-scale. When the bird forever outweighs the flesh, he offers his whole body. The two gods then reveal themselves and bless him. The same hero generates a parallel cycle of stories: in the **Buddhist Sibi Jātaka** he gives away his **eyes** to a blind beggar (Sakka in disguise). The variant policy of this knowledge base catalogs **all** of these below.

## Where Śibi sits — lineage and frame

- **A Lunar-dynasty (Chandravaṃśa) king** - Śibi (also **Śivi, Cibi, Sivi, Auśīnara**) is reckoned son of **Uśīnara** (some texts: by his queen Mādhavī), of the **Anu** branch of the Lunar line descending from [Yayāti](yayati.md); his city is variously **Uśīnara / the Śibi (Sivi) country** (*Mahābhārata, genealogical references; Purāṇic lists*). The historical **Śibi / Sivi tribe** of the northwest (Punjab) traced descent from him. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (some retellings):* He is loosely called a **Cōḷa (Chola) king** or a king "of the south" in several popular South-Indian and devotional retellings, harmonizing the legend with regional Cōḷa pride. The epic/Purāṇic placement is northwestern (Uśīnara). [folk] [disputed]
- **His son by the boon** - The gods' blessing promises Śibi a son, **Kapotaroma / Kapataroman** ("dove-haired," named for the episode), continuing the line (*Mahābhārata, Vana Parva*).
- **Frame within the epic** - The tale is told to the **Pāṇḍavas during their forest exile** as one of the inspirational legends of ancient kings recounted at the tīrthas — placing it in the **mythic past relative to the Mahābhārata war**, deep within the current [28th Mahāyuga's Dvāpara→Kali transition](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md), narrated within the [Vana / Araṇya Parva](mahabharata/03-vana-parva.md).

## The Mahābhārata account — the dove and the hawk (Vana Parva)

The canonical Hindu version appears in the **Vana (Araṇya) Parva** of the *Mahābhārata* — in the *Tīrtha-yātrā* (pilgrimage) sub-parva, narrated to Yudhiṣṭhira (the section is given as Vana Parva ch. ~130–131 in many editions; cited as *Section CXCVI* in Ganguli's numbering of the related Aṣṭāvakra/legend block).

- **The disguise** - **Agni** takes the form of a **dove (kapota)** and **Indra** the form of a **hawk/falcon (śyena)**. The hawk pursues the dove across the sky (*Mahābhārata, Vana Parva*).
  - *Variant (role assignment):* The dominant reading makes **Indra the hawk** and **Agni the dove**. A minority of retellings swap them or make the hawk **Yama** or **Sūrya**; the wisdomlib English summary even glosses the pursuer as a hawk while elsewhere noting solar associations. The Indra-hawk / Agni-dove pairing is standard. [disputed]
- **The refuge** - The exhausted dove falls into **Śibi's lap** (or at his feet, in his sacrificial enclosure), trembling, and begs protection. By the **kṣatriya dharma of śaraṇāgati**, a suppliant who seeks refuge must never be abandoned (*Mahābhārata, Vana Parva*).
- **The hawk's argument** - The hawk arrives and makes a **dharma-argument of its own**: this dove is its **divinely-ordained food**; to deprive a starving creature of its natural prey is itself *adharma* and will cause the hawk (and its own dependents) to die. "By saving one life you destroy another" — the tale's central ethical knot (*Mahābhārata, Vana Parva*).
- **Śibi's offer** - Śibi proposes a substitute: he will give the hawk **any other food** — a bull, boar, deer, anything. The hawk refuses all but **the dove**, then accepts a counter-offer: **flesh from Śibi's own body, cut from his right thigh, equal in weight to the dove** (*Mahābhārata, Vana Parva*).
- **The balance that never balances** - A **scale (tulā)** is brought; the dove on one pan, the king's sliced flesh on the other. Śibi cuts flesh from his thigh, then more from his limbs and body — **yet the dove always weighs more**. Finally he **mounts the scale himself**, offering his whole body (*Mahābhārata, Vana Parva*).
- **The revelation and boon** - At that supreme moment **Indra and Agni resume their true forms**. Śibi's wounds are healed and his body made whole and resplendent; the gods declare his fame imperishable, and promise him a glorious son, **Kapotaroma** (*Mahābhārata, Vana Parva, Section CLXLVI summary*).
- **The point of the test** - The whole was a **devic test (parīkṣā)** of whether the king's vow to protect refugees was absolute. It demonstrates that **dharma upheld without limit overrides even death** — and that the apparent conflict of two dharmas (protect the suppliant / feed the hungry) is resolved by the king interposing **his own body** as the price. [scripture]

## Variant: the eye-donation cycle (Buddhist Sibi Jātaka)

A wholly different test attaches to the same hero in the **Buddhist *Sibi Jātaka*** (Pāli *Jātaka* no. 499) and in **Āryaśūra's *Jātakamālā*** (story 2), where the king is a **previous birth of the Bodhisattva (the future Buddha)**.

- **The blind beggar** - **Sakka (Indra)**, disguised as a **blind, aged brāhmaṇa**, approaches King Sivi — who is famed for refusing no gift — and begs for **an eye**, so that the king may keep one for himself (*Sibi Jātaka, no. 499; Jātakamālā 2*). [late text]
- **He gives both** - Sivi, holding that a gift half-given is no gift, has his **physician remove both his eyes** and bestows them on the beggar, becoming blind himself — the supreme *dāna-pāramī* (perfection of giving) (*Sibi Jātaka*). [scripture / Buddhist]
- **The restoration** - By the **Act of Truth (saccakiriyā)** — Sivi's declaration that his gift was given with a perfectly joyful, ungrudging heart — his eyes are miraculously **restored** (as "celestial / truth-born" eyes surpassing the originals); Sakka reveals himself and praises him (*Sibi Jātaka*).
  - *Variant (Jātakamālā wording):* Āryaśūra emphasizes the king's **unwavering serenity** under the surgeon's knife and frames the restoration as the fruit of his unshakable resolve toward Buddhahood. [late text]
  - *Variant (identification):* Chinese Buddhist tradition sometimes splits the two cycles between **two kings** — the eye-giver appearing as "Kuai-mu Wang" (the king of joyful/pleasant eyes) — rather than identifying the flesh-giver and eye-giver as one Śibi. [scholarly]

## Variant: the flesh-for-dove Jātaka (Buddhist parallel to the epic)

The dove-and-hawk **flesh-offering** also has a Buddhist form, distinct from the eye-Jātaka, in which the Bodhisattva-king ransoms a **pigeon/dove** from a **hawk** with his own flesh.

- **The Jātaka pigeon-ransom** - In this cycle (told of King Sivi and depicted widely in Buddhist art, e.g. **Gandhāran reliefs** and the **Ajaṇṭā / Central-Asian cave paintings**), a dove fleeing a hawk seeks the king, who cuts his flesh to match its weight on a scale; as in the epic the bird is supernaturally heavy and the king finally gives his whole body, after which the disguised gods restore him (*Buddhist Jātaka tradition; Jātaka art*). [Buddhist]
- **Cross-tradition kinship** - This is the **direct counterpart of the Mahābhārata episode** — same actors (dove, hawk, scale, self-sacrifice), differing only in religious framing (the king as Bodhisattva vs. as exemplary Hindu monarch). Scholars treat the flesh-and-scales motif as a **shared pan-Indic legend** that the Mahābhārata, the Jātakas, and Jain texts each adapted. [scholarly]

## Variant: Jain tellings

- **Jain exemplar of compassion** - Jain *kathā* literature likewise preserves Śibi as a paragon of **ahiṃsā and dāna**, retelling the dove-and-hawk (or a pigeon-ransom) as proof that the truly compassionate sovereign will give his own body before allowing a refugee to be harmed. The emphasis falls on **non-violence and the protection of all living beings** (*Jain narrative tradition*). [late text] [scholarly]

## Themes and significance

- **The conflict of two dharmas** - The episode's enduring power is that **both** claimants are *right*: the suppliant dove is owed protection, the hungry hawk is owed its food. Classical dharma-śāstra discussion cites Śibi for the principle that when duties collide irreconcilably, the **king bears the cost in his own person** rather than failing either party. [scripture]
- **Śaraṇāgati — the absolute refuge-bond** - Śibi is the archetypal proof that *abhaya-dāna* (the gift of fearlessness/protection) is the highest gift a kṣatriya can give. This same ethic animates [Rāma's pledge to Vibhīṣaṇa](ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md) and the bhakti doctrine of surrender. (Contrast the devotion-tales [Ambarīṣa & Durvāsā](ambarisha-durvasa.md) and [Gajendra Mokṣa](gajendra-moksha.md), where the *Lord* protects the refugee; here the *king* is himself the protector.)
- **A standard "test of the righteous king"** - Śibi joins [Hariścandra](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) and others as a king whom the gods (especially **Indra**) test to destruction to display that dharma is unbreakable — a recurring narrative type used across the epics and Purāṇas. [scholarly]
- **The truth-act and the heavier-than-the-world bird** - The dove's supernatural, ever-increasing weight dramatizes that **the worth of a single protected life outweighs the king's whole body** — a measure of value inverted from the worldly. [scripture]
- **One of the great *dāna* exemplars** - In later devotional lists Śibi is invoked alongside **Karṇa, Bali, and Dadhīci** as the supreme givers; Karṇa in the *Mahābhārata* explicitly measures his own generosity against Śibi's. [scripture]
