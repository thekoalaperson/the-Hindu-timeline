# The Lakṣmī Purāṇa — Śriyā the Caṇḍāluṇī and the Goddess's Boycott of Purī

> Cosmic address: a *Kali-Yuga vrata-charter tale* set in the ever-present Purī of **Jagannātha** — timeless in the manner of temple legend, recurring every year on the **Thursdays (Gurubāra) of Mārgaśira**. Historically [scholarly] it is an **Odia poem of the early 16th century** by **Balarāma Dāsa** of the Pañcasakhā circle — a "Purāṇa" by title and function, not a Sanskrit purāṇa.
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Place: [Jagannātha Purī](../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md) · Deity: [Lakṣmī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/lakshmi.md) · Festival frame: [Festivals & Vratas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md)

The **Lakṣmī Purāṇa** (*Lakṣmī Purāṇa* / *Mahālakṣmī Purāṇa*) of **Balarāma Dāsa** is the most consequential short poem of Odia household religion: on a Mārgaśira Thursday, **Lakṣmī** leaves the Purī temple to inspect who keeps her rites, finds the high-caste houses negligent and the home of **Śriyā**, a **caṇḍāluṇī** (woman of the "untouchable" caṇḍāla community), spotless and devout — and accepts her worship. For this "pollution" **Balabhadra** has her expelled from the temple; Lakṣmī withdraws with all wealth and food, and the two divine brothers, Jagannātha and Balabhadra, starve as beggars until they unknowingly **eat food cooked by Lakṣmī in an outcaste woman's house**. Her price for returning is a standing charter: in Jagannātha's Purī, **food carries no caste**. The poem is at once the founding text of caste-critique bhakti in Odisha, a manifesto for the dignity of women's household labor, and the recited liturgy of the **Māṇabasā Gurubāra** vrata that Odia women keep to this day. [regional]

## The text, the author, the school

- **Balarāma Dāsa and the Pañcasakhā** — The poem is ascribed to **Balarāma Dāsa**, eldest of the **Pañcasakhā** ("five friends") — Balarāma Dāsa, Jagannātha Dāsa, Acyutānanda Dāsa, Yaśovanta Dāsa, Ananta Dāsa — the 15th–16th-century Odia devotional poets who centered **Utkala Vaiṣṇavism** on [Jagannātha of Purī](../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md) and wrote scripture in Odia for households the Sanskrit canon did not reach. Balarāma Dāsa is also the author of the Odia **Jagamohana (Daṇḍi) Rāmāyaṇa**. (Odia literary tradition) [regional]
- **Dating [scholarly]** — Balarāma Dāsa is conventionally placed **c. late 15th – mid 16th century** (traditional dates vary and are not securely documented); the *Lakṣmī Purāṇa* is generally assigned to the **early 16th century**. The Pañcasakhā overlap with **Caitanya's** years in Purī (1510–1533), though the relation of their school to Caitanya's is locally debated — parallel rather than derivative (see [Nimbārka, Vallabha & Caitanya](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/nimbarka-vallabha-chaitanya.md) and the [Purī file's Pañcasakhā entry](../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md)). [scholarly] [disputed]
- **"Purāṇa" by function** — Despite the title, this is a short vernacular narrative poem, not one of the eighteen Sanskrit purāṇas: it borrows the purāṇic name to claim scriptural authority for a **women's vrata liturgy**, exactly as the [Satyanārāyaṇa Vrata Kathā](satyanarayana-vrata-katha.md) rides the Skanda Purāṇa's name. Its natural literary kin are the eastern vernacular deity-charter poems catalogued at [regional and oral epics](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md). [regional] [late text]
- **A recited scripture** — The poem is not merely *about* a vrata; it **is** the vrata's core text: Odia women read or hear the *Lakṣmī Purāṇa* aloud during the Mārgaśira Thursday worship, so the story of Lakṣmī's boycott is re-performed annually in the households it charters. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa; living Odia practice) [regional]

## The Gurubāra morning — Lakṣmī inspects Purī

- **Lakṣmī goes out to see who honors her** — In the temple (Śrīmandira) of Purī, on a **Thursday of Mārgaśira** — the day and month sacred to her — Lakṣmī takes leave of **Jagannātha** and goes out into the town to see for herself who keeps her worship. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa, opening) [regional]
- **The high-caste houses fail the inspection** — Roaming the lanes at dawn, the goddess finds the **brahmin and high-caste households unswept and their women still asleep**, the morning rites of Lakṣmī neglected — prosperity's own precondition (the clean, ordered, worshipful house) ignored by precisely those who claim ritual precedence. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
- **Śriyā's house passes it** — In the **caṇḍāla quarter**, the outcaste woman **Śriyā** has risen before dawn, bathed, cleaned and smeared her courtyard, drawn the **jhoṭi/chitā** rice-paste designs, and set out Lakṣmī's worship with single-minded devotion. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
- **The goddess accepts an untouchable's worship** — Lakṣmī, pleased, **enters Śriyā's home and receives her pūjā**, blessing her with prosperity; retellings of the poem specify boons of wealth and offspring for the childless devotee. The scene is the text's theological pivot: the goddess of śrī judges houses by **devotion and discipline, not birth**. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
  - *Variant (emphasis):* tellings differ on whether Lakṣmī goes out specifically *because* it is her vrata-day or on a general inspection of the world; the Gurubāra frame is standard in the recited versions. [oral tradition]

## Expulsion — Balabhadra's caste verdict

- **Balabhadra learns of the visit** — **Balabhadra** (the elder brother, Balarāma; see the [caturdhā-mūrti of Purī](../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md)) learns that Lakṣmī has entered a caṇḍāla's house. Guardian of orthodox propriety in the poem, he declares her **polluted** and tells Jagannātha that a wife who has broken caste cannot re-enter the temple. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
- **Jagannātha yields to his elder brother** — Jagannātha, deferring to Balabhadra, **expels Lakṣmī from the Śrīmandira**. The poem is explicit that the Lord of the universe here acts against his own consort at the bidding of caste scruple — the wrong the rest of the narrative exists to punish. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
- **Lakṣmī's parting vow** — Leaving, Lakṣmī vows that the brothers will learn what a world without her means: they will find **neither food nor water** until they have eaten **from her hand — in an outcaste's house**. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
  - *Variant (the taunts):* many retellings expand the expulsion scene with taunts against Lakṣmī's pedigree — the ocean-born goddess of no gotra (cf. her churning-birth at [Samudra Manthana](samudra-manthana.md)) — answered by her own sharp rebuttals; the details vary by edition and performance. [oral tradition] [disputed]

## The boycott — a temple stripped of Śrī

- **Everything follows her out** — When Lakṣmī withdraws, **all wealth, food, and abundance leave with her**: the temple stores empty, the kitchen fails, servants and comforts vanish. The brothers wake to a bare Śrīmandira — the poem's object lesson that śrī is not an ornament of the god's house but its substance. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
- **Lakṣmī's new house** — Lakṣmī establishes herself in a palace raised for her away from the temple — the poem has it built at her command (retellings name **Viśvakarmā** as the builder) — and there she lives **as if a woman of the caṇḍāla quarter**, setting the stage for the brothers' test. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional] [oral tradition]
- **The brothers turn beggars** — Starving, Jagannātha and Balabhadra disguise themselves as **brahmin mendicants** and go begging through Purī. Lakṣmī's power runs ahead of them: at every door the cooked food is spoiled or gone, every hearth fails, and even **drinking water dries up** at their approach. The Lords of the world learn hunger and thirst door by door. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]

## The meal in the outcaste's house — and the settlement

- **"There is food at one house — but the mistress is a caṇḍāluṇī"** — At last the famished brothers are told of a household where food is freely given; the warning attached is that its mistress is an outcaste woman. **Balabhadra balks**; **Jagannātha argues necessity** — for the starving, accepting food is no sin; hunger does not check the giver's caste (the classic *āpad-dharma* reasoning turned to the poem's egalitarian purpose). They eat. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
- **The recognition** — The meal is unmistakable: only one cook in the three worlds makes such food. The brothers realize they have eaten **rice cooked by Lakṣmī herself, served in an "untouchable's" house** — the exact act for which she was expelled, now committed, under compulsion of hunger, by her judges. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
- **Lakṣmī's conditions for returning** — Lakṣmī reveals herself and sets terms before she will re-enter the Śrīmandira: chief among them, that in Jagannātha's temple **no caste distinction shall attach to food** — all may eat together of the Lord's rice without pollution. Jagannātha concedes; Lakṣmī returns and abundance returns with her. (Lakṣmī Purāṇa) [regional]
  - *Variant (the women's clause):* recited versions and retellings also press a second moral — that a wife and her household labor are not to be slighted, and that the woman of the house is Lakṣmī's own seat; how explicitly this is framed as a "condition" varies by edition. [oral tradition]
- **Charter of the Mahāprasād** — The settlement is Purī's living practice: the temple's **Mahāprasād**, cooked in the Ānanda Bājāra kitchen, **dissolves caste once offered — anyone may share it from the same vessel** (see [Jagannātha Purī §VI, Mahāprasād](../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md)). The Lakṣmī Purāṇa supplies the story Odia tradition tells to explain *why* the Lord's food outranks the caste order. [regional]

## The vrata it charters — Māṇabasā Gurubāra (Gurubāra Oṣā)

- **The observance** — On the **Thursdays of Mārgaśira** (Nov–Dec, the new-rice harvest month), Odia women keep the **Māṇabasā Gurubāra** vrata (Odia *oṣā*, "vow-observance"): the house is cleaned and freshly smeared, **jhoṭi/chitā** rice-paste murals — including Lakṣmī's footprints walking *inward* — are drawn, and a **māṇa** (the wooden grain-measure) is filled with **newly harvested paddy** and installed and worshipped as Lakṣmī herself ("māṇa-basā" = the seating of the measure). (Odia vrata practice; Lakṣmī Purāṇa as liturgy) [regional] [folk]
- **The poem as the rite's kathā** — Reading or reciting the **Lakṣmī Purāṇa** is part of the observance, exactly as the kathā is the non-negotiable core of the [Satyanārāyaṇa vrata](satyanarayana-vrata-katha.md): the vrata annually re-tells the goddess's verdict that discipline and devotion — not caste — bring her into a house. (living practice) [regional]
- **Kin observances** — In the corpus's festival layer this stands beside eastern India's autumn Lakṣmī cult, the [Kojāgarī/Śarad Pūrṇimā Lakṣmī Pūjā](../05-concepts-and-dharma/sharad-purnima-kojagari-lakshmi-puja.md), and the broader vrata catalog at [Festivals & Vratas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md). [regional]

## Caste-critique bhakti — the poem's place

- **A goddess against the varṇa order** — The poem's target is the purity-pollution machinery of [varṇa-jāti orthodoxy](../05-concepts-and-dharma/varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md): the deity herself crosses the caste line, is punished for it by the guardians of propriety, and wins — forcing the gods to eat "polluted" food and to abolish caste at their own table. In Indian bhakti literature it belongs with the tradition of saints who confronted untouchability — [Chokhāmeḷā](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/chokhamela.md) at Paṇḍharpur, [Ravidās](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/ravidas.md) in the north (see [north-Indian sants](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/north-bhakti-sants.md)) — but is unusual in making the **goddess**, not the saint, the rebel. [regional] [scholarly]
- **A women's text** — The poem dignifies precisely the labor assigned to women — sweeping, smearing, cooking, the ordering of the house — as **Lakṣmī's own criterion of worth**, and stages a wife successfully disciplining her divine husband and brother-in-law. Modern critics (notably **Satya P. Mohanty**, who reads the poem as radical, egalitarian pedagogy) have called it a **proto-feminist** text of early Odia literature — a modern characterization, not the poem's own vocabulary. [scholarly]
- **Vernacular scripture from below** — With the Pañcasakhā generally (Jagannātha Dāsa's Odia *Bhāgavata* above all), the *Lakṣmī Purāṇa* exemplifies the eastern bhakti move of writing scripture in the spoken tongue for audiences outside brahminical Sanskrit culture — compare [Śaṅkaradeva's Assamese Vaiṣṇavism](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/sankaradeva-and-assamese-vaishnavism.md) next door. [regional] [scholarly]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural/cyclic frame** — The tale is set in the standing sacred present of **Kali-Yuga Purī**, where Jagannātha, Balabhadra and Lakṣmī are permanently installed; it carries no vaṁśa anchor and no dated king. Its time signature is **calendrical and recurring**: the Thursdays of Mārgaśira, every year, when the māṇa of new paddy is seated and the poem is read again.
- **Academic/historical frame [scholarly]** — An **Odia poem of the early 16th century** (Balarāma Dāsa, Pañcasakhā school; the author's exact dates are traditional and uncertain), contemporary with Caitanya's Purī period and with the Sūryavaṁśī Gajapati kingdom's Jagannātha-centered state cult. The **Māṇabasā Gurubāra** vrata and the poem's recitation remain living Odia practice; print editions and modern translations (and readings such as Mohanty's) have carried it into current debates on caste and gender. [scholarly]
