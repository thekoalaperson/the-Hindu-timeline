# Bhaja Govindam (Moha Mudgara)

> *Up to [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · master [index](../README.md). The "Worship Govinda" hymn — Ādi Śaṅkara's short, fierce *vairāgya* (renunciation) song, also titled **Moha-mudgara** ("the Hammer that Smashes Delusion"); a devotional companion to the [Bhagavad Gītā](./bhagavad-gita.md) and counted among the wider [lesser-Gītās / Gītā-saṅgraha catalog](./lesser-gitas-catalog.md).*

The **Bhaja Govindam** (also **Bhajagovindam**, **Mohamudgara**, **Dvādaśa-mañjarikā Stotra**) is a brief Sanskrit devotional poem attributed to **Ādi Śaṅkarācārya**, the founder of the systematic Advaita Vedānta school. In a handful of melodic, hard-hitting verses it turns the abstract non-dualism of Śaṅkara's commentaries into a popular **call to renunciation, devotion to Govinda (Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa), and remembrance of death** — warning that grammar, wealth, lust, and worldly attachment are useless at the hour one dies. Its refrain *bhaja govindaṃ bhaja govindaṃ govindaṃ bhaja mūḍha-mate* ("Worship Govinda, worship Govinda, worship Govinda, O fool!") is among the most-recited verses in all Hindu devotional life. [scholarly]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / hagiographic time** - In its own legend the hymn is composed *in situ* on the streets of **Kāśī (Vārāṇasī)** by Śaṅkara during his earthly mission; the frame-story belongs to the saint's traditional biography (the *Śaṅkara-vijaya* literature) and carries no yuga-claim beyond "within our present age." [hagiographic]
- **Manuscript / academic time** - Text-critically the poem is a **post-Vedic, historical-period stotra**: if by Śaṅkara himself it dates to the traditional 8th c. CE (some scholars place Śaṅkara 7th–early 8th c.); many academics regard at least the supplementary verses, and possibly the whole, as a later *stotra* in Śaṅkara's name. Either way it sits squarely in **recorded history**, not mythic time. Keep the saintly ascription distinct from the manuscript record. [scholarly] [disputed authorship]

## The frame-story — Śaṅkara and the old grammarian

- **The aged scholar reciting grammar** — Walking the lanes of **Kāśī (Vārāṇasī)** with his disciples, **Ādi Śaṅkara** comes upon an **old paṇḍita (Brahmin grammarian)** laboriously memorizing the rules of Pāṇinian grammar — drilling the conjugation **"ḍukṛñ-karaṇe"** (a grammatical aphorism for the verbal root *kṛ*, "to do/make") — as death visibly approaches him. (traditional frame of the *Mohamudgara*) [folk]
- **The rebuke** — Moved to pity, Śaṅkara breaks into song: *bhaja govindaṃ … nahi nahi rakṣati ḍukṛñ-karaṇe* — "Worship Govinda, worship Govinda, O deluded one; when the appointed hour of death arrives, your **rules of grammar will not, will not, save you.**" Mere dry learning cannot rescue a soul at the end; only devotion to God can. (Bhaja Govindam, opening verse) [folk]
- **Why "Mohamudgara"** — From this scene the poem earns its alternate title **Moha-mudgara**, "the **hammer (*mudgara*) that shatters delusion (*moha*)**" — the wake-up blow struck against attachment and intellectual vanity. (traditional)
  - *Variant (the grammarian's identity):* tradition does not name the old man; he stands as an everyman type of the scholar who mistakes erudition for liberation. Some retellings make him Śaṅkara's prompt for an impromptu street-teaching to the crowd that gathers. [folk]

## Structure — the Dvādaśa-mañjarikā and the disciples' verses

- **The core "Dvādaśa-mañjarikā" (twelve-blossom bunch)** — The hymn's nucleus is a cluster of verses styled a **mañjarī ("blossom-cluster, bouquet")**; the central body is the **Dvādaśa-mañjarikā Stotra**, the "**twelve-blossom**" hymn — twelve verses (beyond the refrain-verse) by Śaṅkara himself. (Bhaja Govindam / Dvādaśa-mañjarikā) [scholarly]
- **The Chaturdaśa-mañjarikā — the disciples' added verses** — Tradition holds that, inspired by the master, **fourteen of Śaṅkara's disciples each contributed one verse**, expanding the set; these added stanzas are the **Caturdaśa-mañjarikā ("fourteen-blossom") Stotra**. The composite poem thus runs to roughly **31 verses** (1 refrain-verse + 12 of Śaṅkara + 14 of the disciples + a few concluding/phala-śruti verses), though counts vary by edition. (traditional ascription) [folk] [scholarly]
  - *Variant (named disciples):* the popular tradition (followed by Swāmī Cinmayānanda's widely-used edition) assigns the fourteen verses to disciples including **Padmapāda, Toṭaka(cārya), Hastāmalaka, Sureśvara (Maṇḍana Miśra)**, and others; the exact roster and verse-attributions differ across sources and are **devotional, not text-critical.** [folk] [disputed]
  - *Variant (verse total):* manuscripts and printed editions give the whole as **anywhere from ~12 to ~31 (or 33) verses**, depending on whether only the Dvādaśa-mañjarikā, or the full Caturdaśa-mañjarikā plus closing verses, is counted. Treat exact totals as edition-dependent. [disputed]

## Content and themes

- **Worship Govinda, not grammar** — The governing thesis: at death, ritual erudition, syntax, and learning are powerless; **devotion (bhakti) to Govinda** is the one refuge. Knowledge that does not turn the heart to God is vanity. (verse 1) [scholarly]
- **The futility of wealth and lust** — Verses warn against craving riches (*mā kuru dhana-jana-yauvana-garvam*, "take no pride in wealth, retinue, or youth") and against infatuation with the body and sensual beauty — all of which **time sweeps away in a moment**. (Dvādaśa-mañjarikā) 
- **The body's decay and the cycle of birth** — Repeated meditations on **old age, sickness, grey hair, the toothless mouth**, and the relentless wheel of *punar-api jananaṃ punar-api maraṇam* — "**again birth, again death, again lying in the mother's womb**" — the *saṃsāra* one must yearn to escape. (Dvādaśa-mañjarikā, a much-loved verse)
- **The impermanence of family and relationship** — Stanzas observe that **wife, children, and kin cling only while one prospers**; love rooted in self-interest evaporates with one's fortunes — therefore do not over-attach to household ties. (Dvādaśa-mañjarikā)
- **Vairāgya (dispassion) and satsaṅga** — The path it urges: **keeping the company of the holy (*satsaṅga*)**, which leads to non-attachment (*nissaṅgatva*), thence to freedom from delusion, thence to the **immovable Reality** and liberation-in-life (*jīvan-mukti*). (Dvādaśa-mañjarikā) [scholarly]
- **Control of breath, senses, and mind; the inner witness** — Later (disciples') verses fold in distinctly **Advaita/Yoga** counsel: *prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra*, discrimination (*viveka*), and meditation on the **Self that is the witness** — the practical bridge from devotion to non-dual realization. (Caturdaśa-mañjarikā)
- **Surrender and the name of God as the boat across saṃsāra** — The closing thrust returns to grace: take refuge in **Murāri (Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa)**, for the divine **Name** ferries the devotee across the ocean of worldly existence when nothing else can. (concluding verses) [scholarly]
- **Devotion *and* knowledge — not opposed** — Though framed as a *bhakti* hymn, the poem is read in Advaita circles as teaching that **bhakti is the preparatory discipline** that purifies the mind for *jñāna*; Śaṅkara the supreme non-dualist here speaks the language of loving devotion deliberately, for the ordinary seeker. [scholarly]

## Recitation tradition and reception

- **A staple of daily and congregational devotion** — Bhaja Govindam is **memorized, sung, and chanted across India** — in homes, temples, and *satsaṅg* gatherings — prized for its simple, urgent message and its singable metre; it is among the first Sanskrit hymns many devotees learn. [folk]
- **Swāmī Cinmayānanda's famous exposition** — In the 20th century **Swāmī Cinmayānanda** (founder of the Chinmaya Mission) delivered and published a celebrated commentary/discourse series on Bhaja Govindam that made it a centerpiece of modern Vedānta teaching and popularized the disciple-verse tradition. [scholarly] [modern]
- **Musical settings** — The hymn has been set to music repeatedly; the rendition by **M. S. Subbulakshmi** (with the discourses of the Kāñcī tradition) is among the best-known recordings, fixing the melody in the popular ear. [regional] [modern]
- **Place among the stotras** — It belongs to the large corpus of **devotional stotras attributed to Śaṅkara** (alongside the *Nirvāṇa-ṣaṭka, Saundarya-laharī*, *Kanakadhārā-stotra*, etc.); within the broad "Gītā/stotra" devotional anthologies it is sometimes grouped with the popular [lesser Gītās](./lesser-gitas-catalog.md) and the [Bhagavad Gītā](./bhagavad-gita.md) it so often paraphrases. [scholarly]

## Sources

- *Bhaja Govindam / Mohamudgara*, attributed to **Ādi Śaṅkarācārya** — the primary text (refrain-verse + Dvādaśa-mañjarikā + Caturdaśa-mañjarikā). Sanskrit text and translations widely available (e.g. sanskritdocuments.org; Chinmaya Mission editions).
- Swāmī Cinmayānanda, *Bhaja Govindam* (Central Chinmaya Mission Trust) — the standard modern commentary; source for the frame-story of the Kāśī grammarian, the *Mohamudgara* title, and the disciple-verse (Caturdaśa-mañjarikā) tradition. [scholarly]
- Traditional *Śaṅkara-vijaya* hagiographies (Mādhava's *Śaṅkara-dig-vijaya* et al.) — for the Kāśī episode and Śaṅkara's biography. [hagiographic]
- *Note on authorship/verse-count:* the ascription to Śaṅkara, the assignment of fourteen verses to named disciples, and the total verse-count (~12 / ~31 / ~33) are **devotional traditions**; academic opinion on Śaṅkara's date (trad. 8th c. CE) and on whether all verses are his is divided. Borderline points flagged [disputed] above. [scholarly] [open question]
