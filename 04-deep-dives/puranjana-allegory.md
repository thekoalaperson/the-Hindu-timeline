# The Purañjana Allegory — The Soul as King of the City of Nine Gates

> *Cosmic address: a philosophical parable (rūpaka) spoken in the primordial Svāyambhuva era, narrated by the sage **Nārada** to the ritualist King **Prācīnabarhi** within Bhāgavata Purāṇa Skandha 4 — a teaching on the embodied soul that is timeless rather than dated. A deep-dive episode-file.*
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](./) · Up to [README](../README.md)

The **Purañjana-upākhyāna** ("the tale of Purañjana," Bhāgavata Purāṇa **4.25–29**) is the Bhāgavata's single most sustained and self-conscious **allegory (rūpaka / ātma-vidyā-rūpaka)**: a story whose every character, place and turn is a deliberately coded stand-in for a feature of the embodied soul's (jīva's) bondage in the body. It is told by [Nārada](narada.md) to **Prācīnabarhi** — a king so absorbed in fruitive Vedic sacrifice (karma-kāṇḍa) that he has forgotten the Self — precisely to wean him from ritual obsession by means of a parable. Purañjana means "he who fashions a body for himself" or "he who enjoys within the city (puru)"; the whole narrative decodes the **deha (body) as a city** and the **ātman as its deluded king**. Nārada supplies the decoding himself in 4.28–29, making this one of the few Purāṇic stories with an internal, authoritative key. Below the parable is unpacked element by element.

---

## The narrative frame

- **Why the parable is told** — King **Prācīnabarhi**, son of the Barhiṣad Pracetās' line, is so engrossed in performing continuous karma-kāṇḍa sacrifices (the ground strewn with kuśa-grass blades, *barhis*) that he is bound by fruitive desire; Nārada arrives to redirect him from ritual to self-knowledge (Bhāgavata 4.25.1–8). The same pedagogical move — story as medicine for a ritualist king — frames much of the surrounding Skandha (cf. [Pṛthu](prithu-prithvi.md), the Pracetās).
- **Allegory as method** — Nārada deliberately chooses a *paroksa* (indirect, veiled) teaching: he tells a "story of a king named Purañjana" and only afterward reveals "that king was none other than *you* — and *I*" (the soul), because veiled teaching pierces a mind hardened against direct instruction [scholarly].

## Dramatis personae — the coded cast (Nārada's own key, 4.28–29)

- **Purañjana — the embodied soul (jīva)** — a king who, seeking a place to enjoy, wanders the earth looking for a suitable city; he stands for the **conditioned living entity** that desires a body in which to taste sense-objects (Bhāgavata 4.25.10; key at 4.28.54).
- **The Unknown Friend / Avijñāta-suhṛt — the Supersoul (Paramātman)** — Purañjana is accompanied everywhere by an **unknown companion** whom he never recognizes and never inquires about; Nārada decodes this as **Paramātman / Viṣṇu in the heart**, the eternal well-wisher the soul forgets (Bhāgavata 4.25.10; key at 4.28.54). The whole tragedy turns on this *non-recognition*.
- **The City of Nine Gates / Navadvāra-pura — the body** — Purañjana at last finds, in the foothills of the Himālayas, a splendid city with **nine gates**, surrounded by gardens, towers and canals; this is the **human body** with its nine apertures (two eyes, two ears, two nostrils, mouth, genitals, anus) (Bhāgavata 4.25.13–22; key 4.29.2). The seven ramparts = the elements/sheaths; the gardens = sense-pleasures.
  - *On the count of gates:* the Bhāgavata fixes the body's openings at **nine** (navadvāra), matching the **Bhagavad-gītā 5.13** ("navadvāre pure dehī") and the Śvetāśvatara/Kaṭha imagery of the body-city; the soul "dwells at ease in the city of nine gates" [scholarly].
- **Queen Purañjanī — the intelligence (buddhi)** — at the seventh gate Purañjana meets a beautiful woman attended by ten servants and guarded by a serpent; he marries her. Nārada decodes her as **buddhi (intelligence/discrimination)**, by whose agency the soul comes to think "I am this body, this is mine" (Bhāgavata 4.25.20–42; key 4.29.3–5). The soul's enslavement *to its own intelligence* is the parable's psychological core.
- **The five-hooded serpent — the vital air (prāṇa)** — the snake guarding the queen, with five hoods and one that stays awake while she sleeps, is the **prāṇa** (life-breath) with its five-fold function (prāṇa, apāna, vyāna, udāna, samāna), keeping watch over the body (Bhāgavata 4.25.21; key 4.29.7).
- **The eleven-strong retinue — the ten senses + the mind** — the queen's attendants number **ten servants** (each with hundreds of wives) plus their **commander**; these are the **ten indriyas** (five jñānendriyas of perception, five karmendriyas of action) led by the **mind (manas)** as their general — the eleven "soldiers" through whom the soul enjoys (Bhāgavata 4.25.37–41; key 4.29.6, 4.29.18). The "wives" of the servants are the sense-objects.
- **The two-faced / many-functioned city features** — the city's gates are paired and named for the functions of the apertures (the two eyes named **Khadyotā and Āvirmukhī** opening to the town of **Vibhrājita = form**; the ears **Nalinī and Nālinī** to **Saurabha = sound/scent**; the mouth-gate to taste and speech, etc.), each "gate" leading the king out to a corresponding field of sense-enjoyment (Bhāgavata 4.25.49–57). The catalogue of named gates is the parable's most intricate decoding.

## The soul's entanglement and downfall

- **A hundred years of sense-enjoyment** — Purañjana lives with Purañjanī in the nine-gated city, ruled entirely by her moods, hunting and indulging the senses for **a hundred years**, utterly forgetful of his unknown friend and of his own nature (Bhāgavata 4.25.62–4.26). The "hunting expedition" in which he slaughters animals is the soul's violent pursuit of pleasure.
- **Caṇḍavega and the Gandharvas — Time and the days/nights** — a **Gandharva king named Caṇḍavega** ("violently swift"), with **360 male Gandharva soldiers and 360 female** companions, repeatedly assaults and plunders the city; Nārada decodes Caṇḍavega as **the year / Time (kāla)** and his 720 followers as the **360 days and 360 nights** that steadily erode the body (Bhāgavata 4.27.1–11; key 4.29.20–23). A serpent named **Prajāra** ("wearer-out") who also depletes the city is the **passage of the year** wearing out lifespan.
- **Kālakanyā — the Daughter of Time, Old Age (jarā)** — **Kālakanyā** ("daughter of Time"), rejected as a bride by all because whoever weds her is doomed, is **old age / decrepitude (jarā)**; unable to find a husband, she is taken (by arrangement of **Yavana-rāja = Death/Yama**, with his brothers **Prajvāra = fever** and the soldiers = diseases) to attack and overrun Purañjana's city (Bhāgavata 4.27.19–30; 4.28). Old age clutches the body that nothing else will claim.
- **The siege and ruin of the city** — the combined armies of Time, fever and disease overwhelm the nine-gated city; the serpent (prāṇa) tries to flee, the city is sacked, and Purañjana, broken by attachment to his queen, children and home, is dragged toward death (Bhāgavata 4.28.1–16).
- **The anxious death — bound by attachment** — at the moment of death Purañjana, instead of remembering his eternal friend, is consumed by **thoughts of his beloved Purañjanī**; "as a man thinks at death, so he becomes" — and dying with his consciousness fixed on a *woman*, he is **reborn as a woman** (Bhāgavata 4.28.17–23; cf. Bhagavad-gītā 8.6). This is the parable's pivot: the last thought determines the next body.

## Rebirth and liberation

- **Rebirth as Vaidarbhī — the soul transmigrates** — Purañjana is reborn as **Vaidarbhī**, the daughter of King Vidarbha, and is married to a wise, dispassionate king named **Malayadhvaja** (Bhāgavata 4.28.24–34). The soul carries its attachments across the death-gate into a new sex and station — a vivid teaching that gender and identity are bodily, not of the ātman [scholarly].
- **Widowhood and the brāhmaṇa-friend's reminder** — when Malayadhvaja dies and Vaidarbhī grieves at his pyre, prepared to enter the fire, a **brāhmaṇa friend** appears and reveals: *"You are neither Vaidarbhī nor was he your husband; you are my eternal companion — the soul who once was Purañjana; we are two friends (the jīva and Paramātman) who together entered the city to enjoy"* (Bhāgavata 4.28.51–65). The forgotten **Unknown Friend** at last speaks, restoring self-knowledge.
- **Liberation through knowledge (ātma-jñāna)** — by recognizing the eternal friend in the heart and discriminating the Self from the body-city, the soul is freed from the cycle of enjoyment, transmigration and death; Nārada closes by stating the parable's purpose — that Prācīnabarhi (and every hearer) should likewise recognize the Paramātman and renounce the bondage of fruitive desire (Bhāgavata 4.29.1–27, 4.29.79–85).
- **The explicit key (4.28.54–4.29.27)** — uniquely, Nārada then itemizes the code in plain terms: *"By the word 'Purañjana' I mean the living entity… the unknown friend is the Supersoul… the city of nine gates is the body… the woman is the intelligence,"* and so on — making the Purañjana tale the Bhāgavata's clearest worked example of **rūpaka (extended metaphor)** as a vehicle for **ātma-vidyā** (self-knowledge).

## Method, parallels and afterlife

- **The "two clocks" within the tale** — the parable folds **scriptural time into psychological time**: Caṇḍavega's 720 soldiers are the literal 360 days and nights of a year, yet they function as *kāla* the devourer; the "hundred years" of enjoyment is both a lifespan and the soul's whole career of bondage. The story is set in primordial (Svāyambhuva) narrative time but its referent — the embodied soul — is **timeless**, belonging to no single yuga [scholarly].
- **The body-as-city topos** — the "city of nine gates" is a shared Upaniṣadic-Gītā image: **Bhagavad-gītā 5.13** (*navadvāre pure dehī*), **Śvetāśvatara** and **Kaṭha** body-city verses; the Purañjana tale is the most fully *dramatized* expansion of this topos in the corpus, comparable in pedagogy to the Self-vs-Death dialogue of [Naciketas](nachiketa-katha-upanishad.md).
- **Allegorical kin in the Bhāgavata** — it stands among the Bhāgavata's teaching-parables and is catalogued with them in the [Bhāgavata tales catalog](bhagavata-tales-catalog.md); thematically it pairs with the **Piṅgalā** and **Avadhūta** dispassion-teachings of Skandha 11 as the corpus's premier vairāgya allegories.
- **Anthology fame** — the Purañjana-ākhyāna is a perennial favourite of commentators (Śrīdhara Svāmī, Vīrarāghava), of bhakti-Vedānta expositors, and of modern anthologies of Indian allegory, frequently excerpted as *the* classical Sanskrit illustration of sustained philosophical allegory — the Hindu counterpart often compared by scholars to *The Pilgrim's Progress* for its one-to-one moral cartography [scholarly].

## Sources

**Primary text**
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa / Śrīmad Bhāgavatam**, **Skandha 4, chapters 25–29** — the Purañjana-upākhyāna narrated by Nārada to King Prācīnabarhi:
  - **4.25** — Nārada introduces the parable; Purañjana seeks a city, finds the City of Nine Gates, meets and marries Queen Purañjanī (intelligence); the serpent-guard (prāṇa) and the named gates (the apertures).
  - **4.26–27** — a hundred years of sense-enjoyment and hunting; the assault of Caṇḍavega and the 720 Gandharvas (Time / days and nights); the serpent Prajāra; the rejected bride Kālakanyā (old age) and Yavana-rāja (Death) with Prajvāra (fever).
  - **4.28** — the siege and sack of the city; Purañjana's anxious death fixed on his queen; rebirth as Vaidarbhī; marriage to Malayadhvaja; widowhood and the brāhmaṇa-friend's revelation.
  - **4.29** — Nārada's explicit decoding of every figure (jīva, Paramātman, body, buddhi, prāṇa, the eleven senses+mind, Time, old age, death); the lesson for Prācīnabarhi; liberation through self-knowledge.
