# Purūravas & Urvaśī — the Mortal King & the Apsarā

> Where this sits: the founding love-story of the **Candravaṃśa (Lunar dynasty)**, oldest in the whole Hindu mythic corpus — its core is a Ṛgvedic dialogue hymn (X.95), older than the epics. Parent: [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · Lineage context: [../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) · Cosmic slot: very early in the 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga — Purāṇic tradition sets Purūravas in the **Tretā Yuga**, at the very head of the Lunar line (long before [Yayāti](yayati.md), [Nahuṣa](nahusha.md), Bharata, Kṛṣṇa).

Purūravas, son of **Budha** (Mercury, son of the Moon) and **Ilā/Sudyumna** (the gender-shifting child of Manu), is the first mortal king of the Candravaṃśa, reigning from **Pratiṣṭhāna** (Prayāga, on the Gaṅgā–Yamunā confluence). His love for the apsarā **Urvaśī** — won under fragile conditions, lost when a gandharva-trick breaks them, then resolved through a fire-ritual that makes him a gandharva — is the **single oldest narrated romance in Indian literature**, surviving as the enigmatic Ṛgveda X.95 *saṃvāda* (dialogue) hymn and steadily prosified and moralised over a millennium into the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, the Purāṇas, and finally Kālidāsa's stage romance *Vikramorvaśīya*. The variant record is unusually wide: the same episode is, by turns, a solar allegory (Dawn fleeing the Sun), a ritual etiology (origin of the three sacred fires), a moral parable, and a courtly comedy with a happy ending.

---

## I. Lineage & cosmic placement

- **Born of Budha and Ilā** - Purūravas is the son of Budha (son of Candra/Soma) and Ilā, Manu Vaivasvata's gender-shifting child — making the Lunar line *matrilineal at its hinge* through Manu, grafted onto the Moon's line. (very early Tretā; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.1, IX.14; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.1, 6)
  - *Variant (Ilā/Sudyumna ambiguity):* texts differ on whether **Ilā** (female) or **Sudyumna** (her male form) is the parent connecting to Budha — see [../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md). (*Bhāgavata* IX.1)
- **First king of the Candravaṃśa** - founds and rules from Pratiṣṭhāna (Prayāga); performs penance to Brahmā and gains sovereignty; renowned as righteous, generous, and a great sacrificer. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6; *Bhāgavata* IX.14)
- **Dynastic position** - the spine runs: Atri → Candra (Soma) → Budha (m. Ilā) → **Purūravas** → Āyu → Nahuṣa → Yayāti → {Yadu … Pūru}. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6; *Bhāgavata* IX.14; *Mahābhārata* Ādi Parva)

## II. The oldest layer — Ṛgveda X.95 (the dialogue hymn)

- **The saṃvāda hymn** - RV X.95 is an 18-verse dialogue (with no narrative frame) in which Purūravas pleads with the vanishing Urvaśī and she rebuffs him. The legend is present only in seeds — the prose story is reconstructed from it by later texts. (*Ṛgveda* X.95)
- **Purūravas's plea** - he begs his "wife" to "stay, cruel in mind," to talk with him; without an exchange of words "we shall not win joy even in the most distant home." (RV X.95.1)
- **Urvaśī's refusal** - she answers that pursuit is useless: "What am I to do with this speech of thine? I have passed away like the first of the dawns." She likens herself to the **wind**, ungraspable: "Like the wind, hard to catch." (RV X.95.2)
- **The wolf imagery & his despair** - she warns of his rashness, evoking a desolate, half-mad state; commentators read her rebuke that he should not give himself to grief, that "wolves" (or evil) should not devour the unwary one who runs out into the night. (RV X.95.13–15)
- **"Four autumns"** - she recalls she dwelt among mortals for a season, eating a drop of ghṛta (ghee) once a day, then "satisfied, departed thence" — the kernel of the later "ghee / once-a-day" condition. (RV X.95.16; cf. the prose conditions in §III)
- **The unborn son** - she tells him she carries his child and that "what mortal woman gives birth shall come to thee" — read by tradition as **Āyu**, their son; she bids him go home, he cannot win her. (RV X.95.5, 10)
- **Her final consolation** - she tells him the gods grant that he will go to heaven (svarga) and rejoice there among the gods — the seed of the later gandharva-apotheosis. (RV X.95.18)
  - *Variant (solar/naturalistic reading) [scholarly]:* Max Müller and the comparative-mythology school read Purūravas as the **Sun** and Urvaśī as the **Dawn (Uṣas)** who must flee at his approach — a nature-allegory. Later Indologists (e.g. those collected by Wendy Doniger) treat this as one layer among several rather than the "original" meaning.
  - *Variant (etymology):* "Urvaśī" glossed as "widely pervading / wide-extending" (*uru* + *aś*); "Purūravas" as "crying much / roaring greatly" (*puru* + *rava*) — fitting his loud lamenting in the prose versions. [scholarly]

## III. The Brāhmaṇa narrative — Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa XI.5.1 (the conditions, the rams, the fire)

This is the first full prose telling and the source of nearly every later detail; it is framed as a ritual etiology for the **three sacred fires** and the agnihotra.

- **The three conditions** - when Urvaśī weds Purūravas she sets terms: *(1)* "Thrice a day shalt thou embrace me"; *(2)* "do not lie with me against my will"; *(3)* **"let me not see thee naked, for such is the way to behave to us women."** (*Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* XI.5.1.1)
- **The two rams (lambs)** - she keeps two pet **rams** (lambs) tied to her couch; their protection is bound up with her staying. (*ŚB* XI.5.1.1–2)
- **The gandharvas' plot** - the **Gandharvas**, missing Urvaśī among them, scheme to recover her. By night they steal one ram, then the other; Urvaśī cries out that she is being robbed "as if there were no hero and no man" near her. (*ŚB* XI.5.1.2–3)
- **The fatal sight** - Purūravas, stung, springs up to pursue the thieves **without dressing**, thinking it would take too long to put on his garment; at that instant the Gandharvas produce a flash of **lightning**, and Urvaśī sees him **naked** "as by daylight." The condition is broken — she vanishes. (*ŚB* XI.5.1.3–4)
- **His wandering & the swan-lake** - "He wandered about, lamenting, all over Kurukṣetra." At a lotus-pond called **Anyataḥplakṣā** he finds apsarās swimming as **aquatic birds (swans)**; Urvaśī recognises him and reveals herself. (*ŚB* XI.5.1.4–5)
- **The reunion postponed** - he begs her to return; she refuses but, when he threatens to die, relents partially: **"Come back the last night of the year hence"** — for one night, and "a son will have been born to thee." (*ŚB* XI.5.1.5–6)
- **The night with the apsarās** - he returns after the year; a golden-palace appears; Urvaśī comes to him for the night, then tells him the Gandharvas will grant him a **boon** the next morning, and he should ask **to become one of them.** (*ŚB* XI.5.1.7–9)
- **The fire-ritual (apotheosis)** - the Gandharvas give him a **fire-pan** (or fire) and tell him this is the means to gandharva-hood. He carries it home with his boy, leaves it in the forest, and on returning finds the fire gone and an **aśvattha (pippala) tree** standing where the fire had been, and a **śamī tree** where the boy was. He returns to the Gandharvas, who instruct him to make **araṇi (fire-churning sticks)** — an upper stick of **aśvattha wood that has grown out of (in/around) a śamī tree** — and by churning to **kindle the fire afresh.** (*ŚB* XI.5.1.10–15)
  - *Detail:* the wood "of the aśvattha grown in the śamī" is itself an etiology — that combination becomes the prescribed araṇi for generating the ritual fire.
- **He becomes a Gandharva** - in another age men lacked the three fires, so he first **establishes the three sacred fires**, churns the fire, offers oblations, and **ascends to the world of the Gandharvas**, becoming one of them. (*ŚB* XI.5.1.16–17)
  - *Note:* in this oldest prose telling the resolution is **ritual**, not romantic — Purūravas wins not Urvaśī but the *status* that lets him join her realm. The "happy reunion" is a later (esp. Kālidāsan) development.

## IV. The Purāṇic recension — Viṣṇu & Bhāgavata Purāṇas

- **Why she descended** - Urvaśī is on earth because of a **curse**: in the Purāṇic frame she is sent to dwell among mortals (the curse of **Mitra**, in some tellings Mitra–Varuṇa, after she spurns/wrongs them), and so weds Purūravas. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6; *Bhāgavata* IX.14)
  - *Cross-myth:* the same Mitra–Varuṇa episode (their seed spilled at the sight of Urvaśī into a pot) is the birth-story of **Vasiṣṭha** (and Agastya) — Urvaśī is the catalyst. (*Ṛgveda* VII.33; *Bhāgavata* VI.18)
- **The conditions, Purāṇic form** - she will stay only while *(1)* he protects her **two rams**, *(2)* she is fed only **ghee/ghṛta**, and *(3)* she **never sees him naked** except in love-making. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6; *Bhāgavata* IX.14)
- **Duration of the union** - the Viṣṇu Purāṇa gives an extraordinary span: they live together (in the Himālayas/at Pratiṣṭhāna) for **sixty-one thousand years**. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6)
  - *Variant:* other tellings give modest spans or leave it unspecified; the 61,000-year figure is the Viṣṇu Purāṇa's hyperbolic register, not the epic/Brāhmaṇa norm. [disputed]
- **The break & the gandharvas** - the Gandharvas (chafing at her absence from heaven) steal the rams at night; Purūravas leaps up naked to pursue; a lightning-flash (Indra's doing in some tellings) reveals him; Urvaśī departs. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6; *Bhāgavata* IX.14)
- **The fire & reunion** - grieving, he wins the Gandharvas' favour; they give him a **fire-vessel**; by the śamī/aśvattha araṇi he kindles fire, establishes the three fires, performs sacrifice, and attains the **gandharva-world**, rejoining Urvaśī. (*Bhāgavata* IX.14; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6)
- **The six sons** - the Viṣṇu Purāṇa names **six** sons of Purūravas and Urvaśī, born of the annual reunions: **Āyu** (eldest, the dynastic heir), **Dhīmat (Amāvasu in some lists)**, **Amāvasu**, **Viśvāvasu**, **Śatāyu (Śrutāyu)**, and **Śrutāyu (Dṛḍhāyu)**. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.6)
  - *Variant (counts & names):* lists vary between five and eight sons and on the exact names (Āyu/Amāvasu are the constants); the **Bhāgavata** and **Mahābhārata** foreground **Āyu** as the heir through whom the line continues to Nahuṣa. [disputed]
- **The theft of fire / institution of sacrifice** - the Bhāgavata adds that Purūravas, finding the world had only one fire, **divided/established the three sacrificial fires**, becoming the first to do so — a Lunar-dynasty cultural-hero motif. (*Bhāgavata* IX.14)

## V. The epic & later references

- **Mahābhārata** - Purūravas appears in genealogical and didactic passages (Ādi Parva genealogy; Ādi/Sāntiparva references) as the Lunar founder who consorted with Urvaśī; the epic treats it as ancestral history rather than retelling the romance in full. (*Mahābhārata* Ādi Parva; cf. genealogies)
  - *Variant (his fall) [late text/folk]:* some Purāṇic and epic notices say Purūravas, grown arrogant and greedy, **robbed the brahmins of their wealth/gold** and was cursed (by sages/Sanatkumāra) to perish — a darker coda absent from the Brāhmaṇa and from Kālidāsa.
- **Padma / Matsya / other Purāṇas** - repeat the marriage, the conditions, the loss, and the gandharva-fire, with minor reorderings; treated as a fixed dynastic episode. (*Matsya Purāṇa*; *Padma Purāṇa*) [late text]

## VI. Kālidāsa's *Vikramorvaśīya* — the courtly romance (and happy ending)

Kālidāsa's five-act *nāṭaka* (Gupta era, c. 4th–5th c. CE) is the most famous version and **departs sharply** from scripture: the conditions, rams, and fire-ritual are dropped, and the lovers are united for life. [late text/scholarly]

- **Act I — The rescue** - the demon **Keśin** abducts Urvaśī (and the apsarā Citralekhā); King Purūravas (titled **Vikrama**) rescues her in his aerial chariot; love at first sight, but the apsarās are recalled to Indra's heaven. (*Vikramorvaśīya*, Act I)
- **Act II — The lovesick king & the birch-leaf** - Urvaśī visits the pining king invisibly and sends a love-message written on a **birch-bark leaf**; comic by-play with the king's vidūṣaka (jester) who loses the leaf. (Act II)
- **Act II–III — The curse on stage** - performing the role of **Lakṣmī** in a celestial drama in Indra's court, Urvaśī, asked whom she loves, says **"Purūravas"** instead of **"Puruṣottama (Viṣṇu)"**; the playwright-sage **Bharata** curses her to fall from heaven. Indra softens it: she may live on earth with Purūravas **until he sees the face of the son she bears him.** (Act II–III)
- **Act III — Marriage & jealousy** - the lovers wed; Urvaśī, jealous, strays into the **forbidden grove of Kumāra (Kārtikeya)** where no woman may enter, and is **transformed into a vine/creeper.** (Act III)
- **Act IV — The mad-scene & the gem** - the distraught king wanders the forest "raving," addressing animals, clouds, and rivers in search of her (the play's celebrated *vilāpa*); a **Saṅgamanīya ("union") gem** restores her from the vine and reunites them. (Act IV)
- **Act V — The son & Indra's reprieve** - their son **Āyus**, secretly raised by the sage **Cyavana** (because the curse-term would separate them once the king saw the boy), is brought to court; the moment Purūravas sees Āyus, the curse should activate and Urvaśī must return to heaven — but **Indra**, needing Purūravas as ally in the deva–asura wars, **revokes the term and grants that Urvaśī may remain with him for the rest of his mortal life.** Happy ending. (Act V)
  - *Variant (Kālidāsa vs. scripture):* the play **inverts the older arc** — scripture ends in separation (resolved only by Purūravas's own apotheosis into a gandharva), while Kālidāsa ends in lifelong union by Indra's grace. The conditions/rams/fire-ritual are entirely omitted; Cyavana, the Kumāra grove, the curse-by-Bharata, and the union-gem are Kālidāsan additions. [scholarly]
  - *Cross-link:* the foster-father **Cyavana** is the sage of [Cyavana & Sukanyā](cyavana-sukanya.md).

## VII. The variant map (at a glance)

- **Why Urvaśī is on earth** - *(a)* of her own desire / a temporary sojourn (Ṛgveda, Śatapatha); *(b)* cursed by **Mitra/Mitra–Varuṇa** (Viṣṇu, Bhāgavata); *(c)* cursed by the sage **Bharata** for the slip of the tongue (Kālidāsa). [disputed]
- **The breaking condition** - "see him naked" (Brāhmaṇa, Purāṇas) vs. "see their child's face" / entering the forbidden grove (Kālidāsa).
- **The resolution** - separation + Purūravas becomes a **gandharva** by the fire-ritual (Śatapatha, Purāṇas) vs. **lifelong union** by Indra's reprieve (Kālidāsa).
- **The son** - **Āyu/Āyus** is constant across all versions as the heir continuing the Lunar line (→ Nahuṣa → [Yayāti](yayati.md)). (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa*; *Bhāgavata*; *Vikramorvaśīya*)
- **The fire** - the araṇi of **aśvattha-grown-on-śamī** and the institution of the **three sacred fires** is the Śatapatha's enduring ritual gift, carried by the Purāṇas and dropped by Kālidāsa.

## Sources

- **Ṛgveda X.95** — the Purūravas–Urvaśī *saṃvāda* (dialogue) hymn; oldest layer of the legend (Griffith and Eggeling-era translations; sacred-texts.com).
- **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa XI.5.1** (Eggeling, SBE vol. 44) — the full prose narrative: the three conditions, the rams, the lightning, the swan-lake at Kurukṣetra, the gandharva-boon, the araṇi/śamī–aśvattha fire-churning, and Purūravas's apotheosis into a gandharva; framed as the etiology of the three sacred fires.
- **Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.1, IV.6** — Lunar-dynasty genealogy; the Mitra curse, the 61,000-year union, the six sons, the gandharva-fire reunion.
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa IX.1, IX.14** — Ilā/Sudyumna hinge; the conditions, the loss, the establishment of the three fires, Āyu as heir.
- **Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva** — genealogical/didactic notices placing Purūravas at the head of the Candravaṃśa; darker "robbed the brahmins" coda in some recensions.
- **Kālidāsa, *Vikramorvaśīya*** (c. 4th–5th c. CE) — five-act dramatic adaptation: Keśin's abduction, the birch-leaf, Bharata's curse, the Kumāra-grove vine, the Saṅgamanīya gem, Cyavana fostering Āyus, Indra's lifelong reprieve.
- **Comparative scholarship** — Max Müller (solar/Dawn allegory); Wendy Doniger O'Flaherty, *Hindu Myths* / studies of RV X.95 (layered readings); standard Indological notes on the etymologies of Urvaśī and Purūravas.
