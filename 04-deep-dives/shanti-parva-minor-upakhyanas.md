# Śānti-Parva minor tale cycle — Jāpaka, Cirakārī, Kuṇḍadhara, Ṛṣabha–Tanu, Pūjanī

> Where this sits: a grouped deep-dive on **five didactic insets of the Śānti Parva** (Book 12 of the [Mahābhārata](mahabharata/12-shanti-parva.md)) — small *itihāsas* and *upākhyānas* that Bhīṣma recites from his bed of arrows to Yudhiṣṭhira in the closing **[Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md)** of the current 28th Mahāyuga, on the eve of Kali. Each teaches one point of *āpaddharma* or *mokṣadharma*: the power of japa, the wisdom of slow deliberation, the superiority of dharma over wealth, the leanness of hope, and the deathlessness of enmity.
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Master index: [../README.md](../README.md) · Companion: [The Mokṣadharma Dialogues](mokshadharma-dialogues.md) · Roster home: [Catalog of Minor Upākhyānas](minor-upakhyanas-catalog.md)

These five tales are drawn from the two didactic blocks that fill the back half of the Śānti Parva — the **Āpaddharma** ("dharma in extremity") and the **Mokṣadharma** ("dharma of liberation") — where the framing is always the same nested one: **Bhīṣma** recalls a *purātana itihāsa* (an ancient story), inside which a sage or a bird or a cloud-spirit instructs a king, to answer one of Yudhiṣṭhira's questions. None is large enough for a first-tier node; each was carried in the corpus only as a gap-line or a single cross-reference, and this file promotes all five together. **Chapter numbers below follow the Ganguli/Calcutta vulgate**, which the Critical Edition (BORI) renumbers and in places abridges. [scholarly]

**Two clocks.** The tales are *narrated* as events of the deep past of our 28th Mahāyuga (Ikṣvāku's Solar dawn, Gautama's Tretā, Brahmadatta's Dvāpara Pāñcāla), but the *composition* of the Āpaddharma–Mokṣadharma stratum is dated by scholars to **c. 200 BCE – 400 CE**, among the youngest doctrinal layers of the epic. [scholarly]

## Jāpaka — the Gāyatrī-reciter and King Ikṣvāku (XII.189–193)

- **The question** - Yudhiṣṭhira asks whether **japa** (silent, repeated recitation) alone — without sacrifice, gift, or works — can lead to the highest end. Bhīṣma answers with the *Jāpaka-upākhyāna*, a long defence of recitation as a complete path equal to Sāṃkhya knowledge and Yoga discipline. (MBh XII.189–193, Ganguli; the *Jāpakopākhyāna*)
- **The reciter** - A Brahmin devotes his whole life to the japa of the **Sāvitrī / Gāyatrī** (the *sāvitrī-mantra*), observing every rule of purity and restraint for the full prescribed term; the goddess **Sāvitrī** appears to him, pleased, and he asks only for the region attainable by japa. (MBh XII.189–192, Ganguli)
- **Death deferred, the deities dispute** - When **Yama / Mṛtyu (Death)** and the messengers come, the regents of the quarters, Yama, and other gods **fall into dispute** over where so much accumulated merit should carry him — no single heaven is adjudged sufficient — and he is offered a succession of celestial rewards, all of which he declines in favour of **emancipation (mokṣa)**. (MBh XII.192–193, Ganguli)
- **The contest of generosity with Ikṣvāku** - The Solar-dynasty king **Ikṣvāku** (son of Vaivasvata Manu, founder of the Ikṣvāku/Sūryavaṃśa line) meets the reciter; each tries to **give away the fruit of his own merit to the other** — the Brahmin offering the accumulated store of his japa, the king the merit of his righteous rule — and neither will accept, each holding the other's deeds worthier. Their mutual refusal becomes an exemplum of selfless *dāna* and of the incommensurable value of japa-merit. (MBh XII.192–193, Ganguli) [scholarly]
- **The point** - The reciter attains **union with Brahman**; the tale's thesis is that **japa is a self-sufficient discipline** — properly performed it yields the same liberation as knowledge or works, and its merit is so weighty it embarrasses the very ledgers of heaven. A charter text for the later *japa-yoga* and mantra-recitation traditions. (MBh XII.193, Ganguli)

## Cirakārī — the son who deliberated too slowly to kill his mother (XII.258)

- **Gautama's terrible order** - The Saptarṣi **[Gautama](gautama-ahalya.md)**, discovering the adultery of his wife **[Ahalyā](characters/ahalya.md)** (with Indra in disguise), in a spasm of rage commands his son **Cirakārī** to *slay his mother*, and then leaves the hermitage. This is the same Gautama–Ahalyā matter whose Rāmāyaṇa arc (curse and redemption by Rāma) is treated in [Ahalyā](characters/ahalya.md); the Śānti-Parva knows Cirakārī as their son. (MBh XII.258, Ganguli; the *Cirakārin-upākhyāna*)
- **The name as fate** - **Cirakārī** means "the slow-actor / one who takes a long time to do anything." Named for his lifelong habit of never acting in haste — deliberating exhaustively before the smallest deed — he here **sits down to deliberate** the monstrous command instead of obeying it at once. (MBh XII.258, Ganguli)
- **The weighing of conflicting dharmas** - His interior debate is the tale's whole substance: the duty of **obedience to a father** (whose word is scripture to a son) against the duty to **protect a mother** (glorified above father and teacher, the ground of one's very body), the enormity of **mātṛ-vadha (matricide)** — one of the great sins — set against the sin of **disobeying a father's charge**. He reasons through each in turn, resolving to honour both by acting *slowly*. (MBh XII.258, Ganguli) [scholarly — a set-piece on the hierarchy and collision of duties]
- **Slowness redeems** - Meanwhile **Gautama's rage cools**; repenting his cruel order and grieving the wife he had condemned, he returns — to find Ahalyā alive and Cirakārī still deliberating. Overjoyed, Gautama **blesses his son's tardiness**, declaring that in grave matters delay is wisdom: *"whoever acts slowly in deeds of moment is never destroyed."* The proverb-name becomes praise. (MBh XII.258, Ganguli)
- **Note (roster)** - The [Catalog of Minor Upākhyānas](minor-upakhyanas-catalog.md) itself flags Cirakārī among the "neighbouring tales not yet stubbed"; this is his first full entry. (internal)

## Kuṇḍadhara — the cloud-spirit who granted dharma, not wealth (XII.263)

- **The devotee and his patron** - A poor, virtuous Brahmin worships the cloud-being **Kuṇḍadhara** — reckoned foremost among the **clouds (megha)** and himself a devotee of Nārāyaṇa — hoping the pleased deity will grant him **wealth**, that he might live at ease and give in charity. (MBh XII.263, Ganguli; the *Kuṇḍadhara-upākhyāna*)
- **The higher gift** - Kuṇḍadhara, though gratified, judges that **riches breed pride, negligence and sin**, and that the truest boon he can bestow is **attachment to righteousness (dharma) itself**. He therefore grants the Brahmin not gold but **steadfast devotion to virtue and detachment**, leaving him outwardly as poor as before. (MBh XII.263, Ganguli)
- **The disappointment and its resolution** - The Brahmin, seeing others prosper while he remains destitute, is at first **wounded and resentful**; but the gift works in him, and through the *dharma* Kuṇḍadhara implanted he arrives at **peace, equanimity, and finally the friendship of the deity and the way beyond rebirth** — discovering that poverty-with-righteousness far outweighs wealth-with-sin. (MBh XII.263, Ganguli)
- **The point** - A **true benefactor gives dharma, not fortune**; the story inverts the ordinary logic of boon-granting to argue that spiritual endowment is the only durable gift and material prosperity a spiritual liability. (MBh XII.263, Ganguli) [scholarly] · cf. [Pāpa, Puṇya & Prāyaścitta](../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md)

## Ṛṣabha and the gaunt sage Tanu — on hope, the hardest thing to slay (XII.125–126)

- **The meeting** - The royal sage **Ṛṣabha**, wandering to a sacred hermitage, encounters an ascetic named **Tanu** — so wasted by austerity that he is *tanu*, "lean/thin," reduced almost to a shadow — and asks the cause of a great sighing being he has witnessed there. (MBh XII.125–126, Ganguli; the *Tanu / Ṛṣabha-gītā*) [note: a **royal sage Ṛṣabha of this dialogue**, distinct from the Bhāgavata avatāra-king Ṛṣabhadeva]
- **The teaching on hope (āśā)** - Tanu explains that his emaciation is the fruit of **disappointed hope**: a person had once come to him with an expectation the sage could not fulfil, and the pain of that thwarted hope has wasted him. He then delivers the tale's aphorism — that **nothing is thinner, leaner, harder to sever than hope (āśā)**; hope is more slender than the sage's own shrunken frame, and the disappointment of another's hope is the heaviest of injuries. (MBh XII.125–126, Ganguli) [scholarly]
- **The turn** - True happiness belongs only to **him who slays hope entirely** — who neither entertains desire nor becomes the object of another's expectation. Ṛṣabha departs instructed that **freedom from hope/desire is the ground of contentment**, a compressed *nivṛtti* teaching set within the Āpaddharma. (MBh XII.126, Ganguli)
- **Thematic kin** - Pairs with the parva's other renunciation-of-desire insets (Piṅgalā the courtesan, the *Pitā–Putra saṃvāda* of Medhāvin) catalogued in [The Mokṣadharma Dialogues](mokshadharma-dialogues.md). (internal)

## Pūjanī — the bird who blinded Brahmadatta's prince (XII.137)

- **The bird in the palace** - **Pūjanī**, a female bird gifted with knowledge of past, present and future, dwells in the inner apartments of **King Brahmadatta of Kāmpilya** (the Pāñcāla king; see the disambiguation and pedigree in [The Seven Brahmins Reborn](pitrikalpa-seven-brahmins-brahmadatta.md)). She lays a chick at the same hour the queen bears the **prince**, and the two young — bird and boy — are raised as playmates. (MBh XII.137, Ganguli; the *Pūjanī-upākhyāna*)
- **The two fruits** - Each day Pūjanī flies to the ocean-shore and returns with **two nourishing (ambrosial) fruits**, one for her own chick and one for the prince, whom the fruits make strong — a daily kindness binding the two households. (MBh XII.137, Ganguli)
- **The killing and the vengeance** - One day the infant prince, in **childish cruelty**, seizes and crushes Pūjanī's chick. Returning to find her young dead, Pūjanī laments, then in revenge **tears out the prince's eyes with her talons**, blinding him, and prepares to fly away. (MBh XII.137, Ganguli)
- **The debate on enmity** - Brahmadatta urges her to stay, arguing the injuries are now *mutual and cancelled* — his son slew her chick, she blinded his son, so neither owes the other and no fear need remain. **Pūjanī refuses**, delivering the tale's discourse: that **enmity once kindled never truly dies**, that the injured and the injurer can never again dwell together in confidence, that reconciliation offered by the stronger is a snare, and that the wise part company rather than trust a former foe. She flies off, choosing distance over a poisoned peace. (MBh XII.137, Ganguli) [scholarly]
- **The point** - A **nīti / āpaddharma** parable — set among the epic's fable-style animal insets, kin to the [Hitopadeśa](../90-literature-corpus/hitopadesha.md) and Pañcatantra tradition — on the **irreversibility of enmity** and the futility of trusting one who has wronged you or whom you have wronged. (MBh XII.137, Ganguli)

## Reading the cycle together

- **One frame, five lessons** - All five are Bhīṣma's answers on the arrows-bed: **japa suffices** (Jāpaka), **deliberate slowly in grave matters** (Cirakārī), **dharma outranks wealth** (Kuṇḍadhara), **kill hope to be happy** (Ṛṣabha–Tanu), and **enmity cannot be un-made** (Pūjanī). Two (Jāpaka, Kuṇḍadhara, Ṛṣabha) are *mokṣadharma*, renunciation-toward-liberation; two (Cirakārī, Pūjanī) are *āpaddharma/nīti*, worldly ethics under strain. (MBh XII, Śānti Parva)
- **Recension caution** - Chapter numbers, and even which insets a manuscript carries, vary between the **Ganguli/vulgate** and the **Critical Edition**; treat the figures above as vulgate references. The corpus catalogs, and does not harmonize, such variation. [scholarly]
