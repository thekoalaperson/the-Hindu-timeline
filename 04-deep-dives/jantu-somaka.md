# Jantu — King Somaka sacrifices his only son for a hundred

> Where this sits: a *Mahābhārata* tīrtha-inset narrated by the sage **Lomaśa** to the exiled Pāṇḍavas — the tale of **Somaka**, eponymous ancestor of the Pāñcāla **Somakas**, who immolates his single frail son **Jantu** so that his hundred queens may each bear a son, then in death refuses heaven without his priest.
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Frame: [Vana Parva](mahabharata/03-vana-parva.md) · Twin tale: [Suvarṇaṣṭhīvin — Sṛñjaya's golden son](suvarnashthivin-srinjaya-narada-parvata.md)

This is a short, savage *upākhyāna* of the **Āraṇyaka-parvan's Tīrtha-yātrā** (pilgrimage) block: two chapters that pose a single ethical knot — a father who kills the one child he has in order to be granted a hundred, and a king who will not enjoy his heaven while the man who did the deed for him burns. It is told **not** as a horror but as a *tīrtha-māhātmya* charm, spoken by Lomaśa at a holy ford on the Sarasvatī associated with Somaka's fire-rite. The corpus has carried only the **roster line** for this tale (Vana 121–135) in the [Vana Parva map](mahabharata/03-vana-parva.md); this is its first full telling.

---

## A note on names — which Somaka, which Jantu

- **Somaka the Pāñcāla ancestor** — this Somaka is the **eponym of the Somakas**, the royal clan of Pāñcāla; the Ādi Parva genealogy makes him father of a hundred sons (Jantu eldest) and forebear of **Pṛṣata → Drupada**, so the Pāñcālas of the great war are the "Somakas." He belongs to the **Lunar dynasty (Candravaṃśa)**, the Puru–Ajamīḍha branch. Not to be confused with the *soma* plant, with Soma the moon-god, or with Somadatta of the Bāhlīkas. (See [Solar & Lunar Dynasties](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).)
- **Jantu** — the name simply means **"a living creature / a born thing"** (*jantu*), a pointed, almost anonymous name for a child who exists only to be born, killed, and reborn. This Jantu is unrelated to any other minor bearer of the word in the epics. [scholarly]
- **The priest** — named only by his office in most of the text: Somaka's **ṛtvij / purohita** (officiating chaplain). The narrative hinge is that his name matters less than his *deed and its fruit*.

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — Somaka reigns in the **deep past of the current 28th Mahāyuga**; by the Ādi Parva pedigree he stands only a few generations above Drupada (Somaka → … → Pṛṣata → Drupada), placing his reign in the **late Dvāpara Yuga**, shortly before the Kurukṣetra generation. The tale itself is *narrated* in Dvāpara, during the Pāṇḍavas' twelve-year forest exile (Kali began 3102 BCE, after that war).
- **Academic / historical frame** — the episode belongs to the **Tīrtha-yātrā stratum of the Āraṇyakaparvan**, part of the epic's pilgrimage-catechism layer; the constituted Critical Edition text is BORI/Poona (Āraṇyakaparvan, 1942). Text-critical dating of this didactic tīrtha material: **[scholarly] c. 200 BCE – 300 CE**, later than the epic's martial core. The story is a **narrative charter for a tīrtha and for the doctrine of shared karmic fruit**, not datable history.

## The tale — Vana Parva CXXVII–CXXVIII (CE 3.127–128)

### The setting

- **Lomaśa at the ford** — as Lomaśa conducts Yudhiṣṭhira and his brothers along the pilgrimage circuit, he points out a sacred spot and tells the story of **Somaka** to explain its sanctity and to console the grieving king with an exemplum of a ruler's devotion (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva CXXVII).
- **A hundred wives, no son** — Somaka, a righteous king, had **a hundred wives of equal rank** but for a long time **not one son by any of them**, though he strove greatly. In old age at last **a single son, Jantu, was born** — to one queen among the hundred (*Vana* CXXVII).

### The overloved child

- **The hundred mothers** — Jantu was reared by **all hundred queens at once**, doted upon past all measure; each mother's whole world was the one boy (*Vana* CXXVII).
- **The ant-bite** — one day **an ant stung Jantu on the hip**, and the child's cry brought **all hundred wives springing up together, wailing**, raising an uproar that filled the palace. Somaka, hearing the tumult from his council, learned it was only an ant-bite — and was struck by the **misery of having but one son among a hundred women**, each of whom would be desolate if that single life failed (*Vana* CXXVII) — the inset's signature scene.
- **Somaka's lament** — he judged **one son a curse, not a blessing**: better to have none than to hang the hope of a hundred queens on a single frail thread. He resolved to seek a means to **many sons**, whatever its cost (*Vana* CXXVII).

### The dreadful remedy

- **The priest's prescription** — Somaka's **ṛtvij** told him of a **sacrifice (yajña)** by which he might beget a hundred sons: he must **offer Jantu himself as the victim** in the fire; when the queens **inhale the smoke of the burning fat (medas/vapā)** of their sacrificed son, each of the hundred will conceive, and **Jantu will be reborn** to his own mother as the **eldest**, bearing a **golden mark** on his back (*Vana* CXXVII).
- **The king consents** — Somaka, though it "wrings the heart," accepts, saying he places himself wholly in the priest's hands: "Do what must be done." The priest takes the deed and its consequence upon himself as officiant (*Vana* CXXVII).
- **The mothers resist** — as the rite is prepared the **hundred queens seize Jantu and drag him away**, weeping, crying out; but the priest **wrests the boy from them**, slays him, and **offers his fat into the fire** according to the ordinance. The queens, forced to breathe the rising smoke, **swoon and fall** in a heap of grief (*Vana* CXXVII–CXXVIII) — the horror at the center of the tale.

### The hundred sons — and the golden mark

- **The promise fulfilled** — in due course **every one of the hundred queens conceives**, and in time each bears a son. **Jantu is reborn as the eldest**, to his original mother (unnamed in the text, called only "his former mother"), distinguished by the **golden mark on his back** by which the priest had foretold him (*Vana* CXXVIII).
- **The price deferred** — the sacrifice "worked," but its sin is not cancelled; it is only **not yet paid**. The tale now turns from the miracle to its reckoning.

### The reckoning — Somaka shares his priest's hell

- **The priest dies first** — after some years the **officiating priest dies** and, for the killing of Jantu, **descends to hell** to suffer for the deed (*Vana* CXXVIII).
- **Somaka comes to the other world** — when in the fullness of time **Somaka too dies**, he arrives before **Yama / Dharma** and learns that his old chaplain is **being tormented in hell on his account** — for a rite the priest had performed at the king's bidding and for the king's sake (*Vana* CXXVIII).
- **The king refuses heaven** — Somaka declares he **will not enter heaven while his priest burns**. He argues the sin was **his own** — done for him, at his command — and demands to **go to hell and share the priest's suffering**, or else to **take the whole punishment on himself** so the priest may be freed (*Vana* CXXVIII) — the ethical core of the tale.
- **Dharma's objection** — the god of death answers with the ordinary law: **each soul reaps its own act**; "the fruit of a deed is enjoyed by the doer alone," and one cannot simply assume another's karma (*Vana* CXXVIII).
- **Somaka's insistence** — Somaka will not accept a solitary heaven; he insists on **equal partnership in demerit as he had in the benefit**, refusing to be parted from the man who acted for him. His fidelity overrides the plea of self-interest, the tale's moral triumph (*Vana* CXXVIII).
- **The shared term, then heaven together** — Yama grants it: Somaka is allowed to **dwell in hell for the measured term** needed to exhaust the priest's demerit; when the sin's fruit is spent, **both king and priest ascend to the higher worlds together** — Somaka's own vast merit undiminished, now crowned by his refusal to be saved alone (*Vana* CXXVIII).

## Reading the tale

- **A charter for shared karmic fruit** — against the flat rule that "each reaps his own," the story stages a **loophole of loyalty**: a beneficiary may *voluntarily* shoulder the demerit of one who sinned on his behalf, and by that willingness redeem him. It sits beside the epic's other meditations on the transfer and sharing of karmic fruit (see [Puruṣārthas — karma, saṃsāra, mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md); [Pāpa, Puṇya & Prāyaścitta](../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md)). [scholarly]
- **The king who will not be saved alone** — Somaka's stance prefigures **Yudhiṣṭhira's** own refusal, at the very end of the epic, to enter heaven without his dog and his kin, and his choice to visit hell for their sake (see [Svargārohaṇa Parva](mahabharata/18-svargarohana-parva.md)). The Somaka inset, told to Yudhiṣṭhira in exile, quietly rehearses the lesson he will live out. [scholarly]
- **The moral cost never cancelled** — unlike a straightforward boon-tale, the narrative insists the **sacrifice of Jantu was a sin that had to be burned off in hell**, even though it "succeeded." The miracle and the guilt are held together, not merged — the text refuses to let a pious end launder a terrible means.
- **The uñcha-of-fatherhood inversion** — where the *dāna* tales exalt giving to others (see [King Śveta & the golden mongoose](dana-upakhyanas-shveta-and-the-mongoose.md)), this tale probes the **darker arithmetic of dynastic anxiety**: a father trading the one for the hundred, and then paying for it. [scholarly]
- **Variant emphases (recensional):** the story is stable across recensions in outline; chapter numbering shifts (Ganguli's Vana **CXXVII–CXXVIII** = Critical Edition **3.127–128**). the mother is never named (referred to only as "his former mother"), and Ganguli places the golden mark on Jantu's **back**; some retellings leave the mark's location unspecified. [scholarly]
