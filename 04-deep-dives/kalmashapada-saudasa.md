# Kalmāṣapāda (Saudāsa)

> *Where this sits: a deep-dive into **Kalmāṣapāda** — the Ikṣvāku ([Sūryavaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)) king **Saudāsa / Mitrasaha**, cursed into man-eating rākṣasa-hood as collateral damage of the [Viśvāmitra–Vasiṣṭha feud](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md), and the cautionary mirror-image of the dharma-kings of the Solar line.*
> *Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Cosmic address: Śveta-Varāha Kalpa › 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara › 28th Mahāyuga, the deep dynastic past of the Solar dynasty (manvantara-past), many generations above Rāma. See also: [Uttaṅka](uttanka.md) (his queen Madayantī's earrings), [Ādi Parva](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md) (the Vasiṣṭha upākhyāna that houses the tale).*

**Kalmāṣapāda** ("spotted-foot," from the discoloration his cannibal-curse left on his legs), also called **Saudāsa** ("son of Sudāsa"), **Mitrasaha**, and in some lists **Aśmaka's father / Sarvakarman's grandfather**, is the great Solar-dynasty *cautionary* king: a righteous Ikṣvāku monarch destroyed not by his own hubris but by being caught between the two greatest ṛṣis of the age. His arc is the dark inverse of [Māndhātṛ's](mandhata.md) flawless empire — a good king reduced to a flesh-eating ogre, rendered impotent by a brahmin widow's curse, and finally redeemed only to father his heir by *niyoga* through the very sage whose son he had devoured. The episode is told most fully in the **Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva** (Vasiṣṭha upākhyāna) and recapitulated in the **Viṣṇu Purāṇa**, **Bhāgavata Purāṇa IX.9**, and **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa**.

---

## Who Saudāsa is

- **An Ikṣvāku king of Ayodhyā** - Saudāsa is a descendant of the Solar line (son of King **Sudāsa**, hence the patronymic), and the hereditary purohita of his house is **Vasiṣṭha** himself — the same brahmarṣi whose family stands at the center of the curse (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, Vasiṣṭha upākhyāna; Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4*). [scripture]
  - *Variant on the names:* the texts use **Saudāsa**, **Mitrasaha**, and **Kalmāṣapāda** for one and the same king — **Saudāsa** the patronymic, **Mitrasaha** a personal/throne name, **Kalmāṣapāda** the epithet acquired only after the curse darkens his feet. Some Purāṇic lists also gloss him **Amitrasaha** or place him as son of **Sudāsa son of Sarvakarman**. [scholarly]
- **His place in the lineage** - He sits on the Ayodhyā branch of the [Sūryavaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md); through his niyoga-born son **Aśmaka** the line continues (Aśmaka → Mūlaka → ... eventually toward the segment that, in the standard Purāṇic genealogy, runs down toward Daśaratha and Rāma). The whole point of the tale's resolution is that **the Ayodhyā line does not die with him**. [scholarly]

## The curse-origin: the Śakti–Vasiṣṭha feud

- **The narrow path** - Out hunting, King Saudāsa meets **Śakti (Śaktri)**, eldest of [Vasiṣṭha's hundred sons](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md), on a path too narrow for both. The king arrogantly orders the sage to give way; Śakti refuses (the brahmin's right of way). Struck with a whip (or insulted) by the king, the enraged Śakti **curses Saudāsa to become a man-eating rākṣasa** (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, Vasiṣṭha upākhyāna*). [scripture]
- **Viśvāmitra exploits the moment** - **Viśvāmitra**, locked in his long rivalry with Vasiṣṭha, is secretly present and seizes the chance: he **dispatches a rākṣasa named Kiṃkara (or simply a rākṣasa) to possess the cursed king**, so that the curse will fall not merely on Saudāsa but, through him, on Vasiṣṭha's own sons (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva*). [scripture]
- **The first victim is Śakti himself** - Possessed and ravenous, Kalmāṣapāda's **first meal is Śakti** — the very sage who cursed him — and then, one by one, Vasiṣṭha's remaining ninety-nine sons. Viśvāmitra's stratagem thus engineers the extinction of Vasiṣṭha's entire line of sons through the cursed king (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva*). This is the hinge that ties Kalmāṣapāda into the larger [Viśvāmitra–Vasiṣṭha cycle](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md). [scripture]
  - *Variant (the curse's trigger):* tellings differ on whether the king **strikes Śakti with a whip**, splashes him, or merely refuses to yield and insults him; all agree the brahmin's curse of cannibalism follows the affront. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Viśvāmitra's role):* in the leaner Purāṇic summaries (Viṣṇu, Bhāgavata) the king becomes a rākṣasa **directly by Śakti's curse**, and the Viśvāmitra-sent-rākṣasa amplification is the **Mahābhārata's** distinctive layer. The timeline keeps both. [scholarly]

## The killing of the brāhmaṇa couple and the wife's counter-curse

- **The cooked-flesh outrage** - In the standard Purāṇic core: while in his cannibal state (or, in another framing, after a wicked or rākṣasa-instigated cook serves him human flesh), King Mitrasaha-Kalmāṣapāda gives a **forest-dwelling brāhmaṇa couple** as alms — but the food turns out to be **human flesh**. The enraged brāhmaṇa **curses the king** (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4; Bhāgavata Purāṇa IX.9.18–24; Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa*). [scripture]
- **The hunting-ground killing (Mahābhārata core)** - In the Ādi Parva's telling, the now-rākṣasa Kalmāṣapāda comes upon a **brāhmaṇa and his wife making love** in the forest and **seizes and devours the husband** despite the wife's desperate plea that he wait until the union is finished and she has conceived (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva*). [scripture]
- **The wife's counter-curse** - The bereaved brāhmaṇī, having entered fire (or in her grief), **curses the king**: that **whenever he approaches a woman in love / for procreation, he shall die that instant** — rendering him unable ever to father a child by ordinary union (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva; Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4*). This is the curse that makes the later *niyoga* birth of his heir necessary. [scripture]
  - *Variant (who curses, and to what effect):* the **Mahābhārata** has the **wife of the devoured brāhmaṇa** curse him to **die if he ever unites with his queen**; several **Purāṇic** versions have the **brāhmaṇa husband** (fed human flesh) curse him to **rākṣasa-hood for twelve years** *and* a separate curse of childlessness. The motif of **death-on-intercourse** is the load-bearing one for the dynasty's continuation. [scholarly]
  - *The "Mitrasaha" water-curse variant (Bhāgavata/Mārkaṇḍeya):* the king, about to retaliate by cursing the brāhmaṇa in turn, takes consecrated water in his palm; his queen **Madayantī** stays his hand, warning that to curse a brahmin would be sin. Unwilling to waste the curse-charged water and unwilling to harm the sage, he **pours it on his own feet**, which are **scorched black** — and from this his feet become *kalmāṣa* (spotted/dark), giving him the name **Kalmāṣapāda**. [scripture]

## Queen Madayantī

- **The faithful queen** - **Madayantī** (Madayanti, also given as **Damayantī** in some recensions) is Saudāsa's chief queen, who shares his long ordeal of the curse and whose patience and counsel run through the tale (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva; Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4*). [scripture]
- **Her counsel restrains the king** - In the Bhāgavata/Mārkaṇḍeya strand it is Madayantī who **stops the king from cursing the brāhmaṇa**, redirecting the curse-water onto his own feet — she is thus the proximate cause of the name "Kalmāṣapāda" and a model of the wife who guards her husband from sin. [scripture]
- **Her earrings and the Uttaṅka link** - In the **Āśvamedhika Parva** retelling of the [Uttaṅka](uttanka.md) earring-quest (*Mahābhārata 14.52–58*), it is **Madayantī's celestial earrings** that the sage Uttaṅka is sent to fetch; he must first get the leave of her husband **Saudāsa, then in his man-eating state**, who agrees to let Uttaṅka go on his errand before being eaten. The two deep-dives interlock here. [scripture]
- **Mother of the heir** - It is **on Madayantī** that the line is finally continued — but, because of the death-on-intercourse curse, **not by the king directly** (see below). [scripture]

## The release by Vasiṣṭha

- **Vasiṣṭha does not retaliate** - Bereaved of all hundred sons by the cursed king, **Vasiṣṭha refuses vengeance** — in the wider cycle he even attempts suicide repeatedly and is refused by the rivers, then is consoled by the survival of his grandson **Parāśara** (future father of Vyāsa). His response to Kalmāṣapāda is mercy, not destruction (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva*). [scripture]
- **The encounter and the rākṣasa expelled** - Vasiṣṭha comes upon the cursed king (in some tellings about to devour another brāhmaṇa). The sage **sprinkles consecrated water** charged with mantra upon him, **driving out the rākṣasa-possession** and freeing Saudāsa from his twelve-year (or curse-bound) cannibal state, restoring his mind and his kingship (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva; Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4*). [scripture]
  - *Variant (duration & manner):* the **Mahābhārata** frames the release as Vasiṣṭha lifting the rākṣasa-possession after **twelve years**; the **Purāṇas** more often have the king's cannibal spell end with the term of the brāhmaṇa's curse, after which Vasiṣṭha (as family purohita) restores him. [scholarly]
- **The remaining obstacle** - But the **second curse — death if he unites with a woman — still stands**, so the freed and restored king cannot father an heir on Madayantī. The release from cannibalism is therefore only half the resolution. [scripture]

## The niyoga birth of Aśmaka

- **Niyoga by Vasiṣṭha** - Because Saudāsa cannot approach Madayantī without dying, and the Ayodhyā line would otherwise end, **Vasiṣṭha himself begets a son on Queen Madayantī by *niyoga*** (the levirate practice of raising seed for a man unable to do so) — at the king's own request, the purohita continuing the royal line he had every reason to let lapse (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva; Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4; Bhāgavata Purāṇa IX.9*). [scripture]
- **The seven-year gestation → Aśmaka** - Madayantī carries the child for an extraordinarily long term (a tradition of **seven years**); when she does not deliver, she **strikes her womb with a stone (aśman)** to bring forth the child, who is therefore named **Aśmaka** ("the stone-born") (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4; Bhāgavata Purāṇa IX.9*). [scripture]
  - *Variant (folk etymology):* the name **Aśmaka** is universally glossed from *aśman* ("stone") and tied to the delivery-by-stone, though the precise detail (queen striking the womb vs. a stone used to induce birth) varies. [scholarly]
- **The line continues** - From **Aśmaka** descends **Mūlaka** (called **Nārīkavaca**, "armoured-by-women," in a separate Paraśurāma-related legend) and onward the Ikṣvāku succession of Ayodhyā — so the cannibal-king's house, against all odds, **does not die out**, the very point of the redemption-arc (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4; Bhāgavata Purāṇa IX.9*). Links to the [Solar & Lunar lineages](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md). [scholarly]

## Themes & significance

- **The cautionary mirror of the dharma-king** - Where [Māndhātṛ](mandhata.md) is the flawless cakravartin and Hariścandra the truth-tested king, **Kalmāṣapāda is the *good king ruined from outside*** — a study in how even a righteous Ikṣvāku can be turned into a monster by a single act of arrogance toward a brahmin and by being the pawn of a sage's feud. The lesson is the supremacy of the brahmin and the catastrophe of *brahma-droha* (offense against brahmins). [scholarly]
- **Collateral damage of the great feud** - He is the instrument, not the author, of the slaughter of Vasiṣṭha's sons — the [Viśvāmitra–Vasiṣṭha rivalry](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) reaching down to devour an innocent dynasty. The Mahābhārata uses him precisely to show the feud's body-count. [scripture]
- **Niyoga and dynastic continuity** - The resolution makes Kalmāṣapāda a charter-case for **niyoga**: the sage who lost a hundred sons to the king nonetheless **fathers the king's heir**, so that *dharma* (the survival of a royal line) overrides personal enmity — a pointed precedent that the Mahābhārata's own frame (Vyāsa fathering Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Pāṇḍu, Vidura by niyoga in the [Ādi Parva](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md)) deliberately echoes. [scholarly]
- **The two clocks** - Kalmāṣapāda belongs to **scriptural time** — the deep manvantara-past of the Solar dynasty, placed by the Purāṇas many generations above Rāma — with **no historical-clock date**; he survives in tradition as a genealogical and moral landmark, not a datable reign. [scholarly]

## Sources

- **Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, Vasiṣṭha upākhyāna** — the fullest narrative: Saudāsa's clash with Śakti on the path; Śakti's curse of cannibalism; Viśvāmitra's sending of a rākṣasa to possess the king; the devouring of Śakti and Vasiṣṭha's hundred sons; the killing of the brāhmaṇa in the forest and his wife's counter-curse of death-on-intercourse; Vasiṣṭha's release of the king from possession after twelve years; and the niyoga birth of Aśmaka on Madayantī. [scripture]
- **Mahābhārata, Āśvamedhika Parva 14.52–58 ([Uttaṅka](uttanka.md)opākhyāna)** — Saudāsa/Kalmāṣapāda in his man-eating state grants the sage Uttaṅka leave to fetch **Queen Madayantī's celestial earrings**. [scripture]
- **Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4** — Solar-dynasty genealogy: Saudāsa/Mitrasaha, the human-flesh-alms outrage, the brāhmaṇa's curse, Vasiṣṭha's restoration, and the niyoga birth of Aśmaka (the stone-born), continuing the Ayodhyā line. [scripture]
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa IX.9 (SB 9.9.18–39)** — the Mitrasaha/Kalmāṣapāda episode: the cook's human-flesh dish, the brāhmaṇa's curse, Madayantī staying the king's counter-curse, the water poured on his own feet (the "spotted-foot" etymology), and Aśmaka's birth via Vasiṣṭha. [scripture]
- **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa** — parallel telling of the cursed king, the queen's restraint, and the kalmāṣa-foot naming. [scripture]

- Wisdom Library, "Kalmashapada" / "Saudasa" / "Mitrasaha" — Purāṇic source-citations for the curse, Madayantī, and Aśmaka — https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/kalmashapada
