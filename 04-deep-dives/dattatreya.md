# Dattātreya — the Triple-Deity Avatar & the 24 Gurus

> **Where this sits:** A deep-dive into one episode-cycle of the cosmic timeline. Dattātreya is born in the present (7th, [Vaivasvata](../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)) Manvantara of the current Kalpa, in the line of Brahmā's mind-born sons. He is counted among the **[avatāras of Viṣṇu](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md)** (in the longer Bhāgavata lists, not the standard ten) and simultaneously claimed by Śaiva/Nāth and Śākta-adjacent streams. ⬆ Parent index: [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · Master index: [README](../README.md)

Dattātreya (*Datta* "the given" + *Ātreya* "son of Atri") is the rare deity in whom **[Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md)** are fused into a single body — three heads, six arms — born when the [Trimūrti](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md), having gone to test the chastity of the sage Atri's wife Anasūyā, were reduced by her power to crying infants and reborn as her son(s). He is the archetypal **avadhūta** (the "shaken-off" liberated wanderer beyond caste, āśrama, and ritual), the patron of yogins, and the teacher of the celebrated **24 gurus drawn from nature** (Bhāgavata 11.7–9).

---

## Birth, parentage, and the chastity-test

- **Atri and Anasūyā's austerity (the boon)** - Atri, a [mind-born son of Brahmā](../03-lineages-vamsha/README.md) and one of the Saptarṣi, performs severe tapas with his wife Anasūyā (daughter of Kardama and Devahūti) seeking a son equal to the Supreme. (Bhāgavata 4.1; Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa)
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata 4.1.15–33):* Atri prays to "the Lord of the universe"; **all three** of Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva appear together, each claiming to be the one invoked, and grant that portions of all three will be born to him — **Soma (Candra/Moon)** as the partial incarnation of Brahmā, **Dattātreya** as the partial incarnation of Viṣṇu, and **Durvāsā** as the partial incarnation of Śiva. Here Dattātreya is specifically the **Viṣṇu-portion**.
  - *Variant (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, Anasūyā cycle):* The legend foregrounds **Anasūyā's incomparable chastity (pātivratya)** as the engine of the birth.

- **The Trimūrti come to test Anasūyā** - Provoked (in most tellings by Nārada's praise of Anasūyā as the most chaste woman alive, which stings the three goddesses Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī, and Pārvatī), the goddesses dispatch their husbands to break her vow. (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa; later sthala-māhātmyas)

- **The impossible demand** - Disguised as wandering mendicants arriving while Atri is away, the three demand that Anasūyā serve them food **naked (nagna)** — knowing a chaste wife cannot refuse a guest yet cannot comply without breaking her vow. (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa)

- **The infants miracle** - Anasūyā sprinkles water (or by the mere power of her vow / *satya-saṃkalpa*) and turns the three gods into **crying babies**; in that infant state she serves them naked without sin, then suckles and cradles them. (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa)
  - *Variant:* She declares, "If my chastity is true, let these become my children," and the transformation follows instantly.

- **The goddesses' plea and the boon fulfilled** - Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī, and Pārvatī, finding their husbands missing, come to Anasūyā and beg their return; she restores them. Pleased, the Trimūrti grant that they will be **born as her sons**. (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa)

- **The three (or one) sons** -
  - *Variant (most common, three sons):* **Candra/Soma** (Brahmā-portion), **Dattātreya** (Viṣṇu-portion), and **Durvāsā** (Śiva-portion) are born. Soma rises as the Moon, Durvāsā departs to wander as the wrathful ascetic ([see Ambarīṣa–Durvāsā](./ambarisha-durvasa.md)), and **Dattātreya remains** as the combined teacher. (Bhāgavata 4.1; Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa)
  - *Variant (unified single avatāra):* In Datta-devotional tradition the three deities fuse into **one child, Dattātreya**, possessing **three heads and six arms** — the literal Trimūrti-in-one. (Datta Sampradāya hagiography; iconographic standard)
  - *Variant [late text]:* A telling makes Dattātreya, Durvāsā, and Candra born to an "unwed" mother Anasūyā by direct divine fiat, without Atri's biological fatherhood — emphasizing pure divine descent.

- **Iconography of the fused deity** - Dattātreya is depicted with **three heads** (Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Śiva) and **six arms** bearing their emblems (rosary & water-pot, conch & discus, trident & ḍamaru). He is attended by **four dogs** — read as the four [Vedas](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md) (or the four yugas / four guṇa-states) — and a **cow** (Kāmadhenu / Mother Earth / Vedic sanctity), standing before an **audumbara (cluster-fig) tree**. (Datta Sampradāya; Britannica; iconographic texts)

## Dattātreya as combined Brahmā–Viṣṇu–Śiva

- **The non-sectarian synthesis** - Dattātreya is theologically unique: he dissolves the [Trimūrti's](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md) functional split (creation/preservation/destruction) into a single liberated form, making him a deity acceptable across Vaiṣṇava, Śaiva, and yogic lines. (pan-Purāṇic; scholarly)
- **Viṣṇu-avatāra status** - In the Bhāgavata's expanded avatāra enumeration (1.3 and 2.7), Dattātreya is named among the līlā-avatāras of Viṣṇu — the one who taught **brahmavidyā** (knowledge of Brahman) to Yadu, Alarka, Prahlāda, and others. (Bhāgavata 1.3.11; 2.7.4)
- **Teacher of asuras and kings** - He is recorded as guru to **Kārtavīrya Arjuna** (the thousand-armed Haihaya king, to whom he grants boons), to **Alarka**, and to **Prahlāda** — and as imparter of the **Tripura-rahasya** and tantric/mantra lore. (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa; Tripurā-rahasya [late text])

## The Avadhūta ideal

- **Definition** - The **avadhūta** is the renunciate who has "shaken off" (ava-dhū) all bonds — caste, āśrama, ritual obligation, social shame, even clothing — living in spontaneous bliss as a jīvanmukta. Dattātreya is the paradigm avadhūta. (Bhāgavata 11.7–9; [see moksha](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md))
- **Avadhūta Gītā** - The terse non-dual (Advaita) poem **Avadhūta Gītā** ("Song of the Free Soul") is traditionally attributed to Dattātreya (as spoken to his disciple Kārtika/Svāmī Kārttikeya). It teaches radical identity of ātman and Brahman and the worthlessness of external observance for the realized. (Avadhūta Gītā; [atman-brahman](../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md))
  - *Variant [scholarly]:* The extant Avadhūta Gītā is dated by academics to roughly the 9th–10th c. CE or later — far post-Vedic — and its Dattātreya attribution is devotional rather than historical.
- **Jīvanmukti without works** - The avadhūta needs no temple, fire, or rule; his very wandering body is the teaching. This anti-ritual, pro-direct-realization stance later flows into Nāth and Sant currents. (scholarly)

## The teaching of the 24 Gurus (Bhāgavata 11.7–9)

> Framing: In the **Uddhava Gītā** section, Kṛṣṇa retells to Uddhava how the **avadhūta-brāhmaṇa** (Dattātreya) once answered King **Yadu** — founder of the Yādava line ([Chandravaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)) — who, astonished at the ascetic's serene bliss amid the forest, asked the source of his wisdom. The avadhūta replied that he had taken **24 gurus** from the natural world, learning one lesson from each. (Bhāgavata 11.7.32 – 11.9)

- **1. Earth (pṛthivī)** - Patience and forbearance: it endures being trampled, dug, and burdened yet steadfastly nourishes. From mountain and tree: live wholly for the good of others. (Bhāgavata 11.7.37–39)
- **2. Air / Wind (vāyu)** - Move among objects untainted, like air passing over fragrant and foul things alike without taking on their qualities; the yogin engages the world unattached. (11.7.40–43)
- **3. Sky / Ether (ākāśa)** - The Self is all-pervading, boundless, and undivided — clouds and bodies pass through it yet never touch or stain it. (11.7.44–45)
- **4. Water (āp)** - Be pure, sweet, and purifying by nature; cleanse and refresh all who come, holding no discrimination. (11.7.46–47)
- **5. Fire (agni)** - Blaze with austerity, consume what is offered without storing, remain pure though it touches the impure; latent in all fuel as the Self is latent in all forms. (11.7.48–52)
- **6. Moon (candra)** - The waxing and waning are of the phases, not the moon itself; so birth, growth, decay, and death belong to the body, never to the changeless ātman. (11.7.53)
- **7. Sun (sūrya)** - Draws up water only to return it as rain; the wise take from the senses only to give back, untouched by enjoyment. As one sun is reflected in many vessels yet is one, the Self appears many but is one. (11.7.54–55)
- **8. Pigeon / Dove (kapota)** - **A cautionary guru of attachment:** a doting dove, bound by love to mate and chicks, flings itself into the fowler's net to die with them — excessive family attachment is the gateway to ruin. (11.7.56–74)
- **9. Python (ajagara)** - Contentment: eat whatever comes of its own accord, much or little or nothing, lying still without effort — accept fate's provision without anxious striving. (11.8.2–4)
- **10. Ocean / Sea (samudra)** - Remain deep, full, and unmoved whether rivers pour in or droughts withhold; the sage's mind neither overflows in gain nor dries in loss. (11.8.5–6)
- **11. Moth (pataṅga)** - Lust through the **eye**: the moth, deluded by the flame's beauty, plunges to its death — sense-infatuation destroys the foolish. (11.8.7–9)
- **12. Honeybee / Bee (madhukara, bhramara)** - Take only the essence, like the bee sipping nectar from many flowers without harming them; gather wisdom from many scriptures and teachers, taking the gist. (11.8.10–12)
- **13. Honey-gatherer / Beekeeper (madhu-hā)** - The counter-lesson: hoarders lose all — the bees toil to amass honey only for the gatherer to seize it; do not greedily accumulate. (11.8.13–15)
- **14. Elephant (gaja / kuñjara)** - Lust through **touch**: the wild bull-elephant, lured by a captive she-elephant (a decoy over a pit), is trapped and enslaved — desire for the opposite sex is bondage. (11.8.16–17)
- **15. Deer (hariṇa / mṛga)** - Lust through **sound / hearing**: the deer, entranced by the hunter's music, is snared — heed not seductive, frivolous songs. (11.8.18; cf. example of Ṛṣyaśṛṅga)
- **16. Fish (mīna)** - Lust through **taste**: the fish, hooked by greed for the bait, perishes — the unconquered tongue drags the rest of the senses to ruin; taste is hardest to conquer. (11.8.19–21)
- **17. Piṅgalā the courtesan** - **Renunciation born of disappointment:** the prostitute Piṅgalā, after a night of futile waiting for a paying lover, awakens to disgust with sense-craving, abandons hope (āśā), and in giving up desire finds peace and sleeps blissfully — **hope is the greatest misery; hopelessness (renouncing desire) is the greatest happiness.** (11.8.22–44)
- **18. Kuraṛa bird / Osprey (kuraṛa)** - A bird carrying a piece of flesh is attacked by stronger birds; it drops the flesh and is at once safe — **abandon possessions** and find security; clinging to objects invites attack. (11.9.1–2)
- **19. Child (bālaka / arbhaka)** - The little child, free of honor and dishonor, anxiety and ego, plays absorbed in its own bliss — the sage lives carefree, indifferent to worldly opinion. (11.9.3–5)
- **20. Maiden / Girl with bangles (kumārī)** - To feed guests privately, a young woman removes her noisy bangles until only **one** remains on each wrist, so the clatter won't reveal her poverty — **where many dwell there is quarrel, even between two there is talk; therefore dwell alone**, like a single bangle. (11.9.6–10)
- **21. Arrow-maker / Smith (iṣukāra)** - So absorbed in shaping an arrow that he did not notice a king's procession pass by — **one-pointed concentration**; the controlled mind, fixed on the Self, perceives nothing else. (11.9.11–13)
- **22. Serpent / Snake (sarpa)** - Roam alone, dwell in no fixed home (it uses others' burrows), reveal no plans, build nothing of its own; the renunciate wanders solitary and inconspicuous. (11.9.14–15)
- **23. Spider (ūrṇanābhi)** - The spider spins its web out of itself and withdraws it back into itself — so the Supreme **emanates the universe from itself and reabsorbs it**, a model of creation and [pralaya](../00-time-and-cosmology/kalpa-and-pralaya.md). (11.9.16–21)
- **24. Wasp / Caterpillar (bhṛṅgī-kīṭa, peśaskṛt)** - The grub, trapped and meditating constantly (in fear) on the wasp that captured it, **becomes the wasp** — by intense, unbroken meditation on a form, one is transformed into that form; so dwell on the Lord/Self and become That. (11.9.22–24)

- **Closing teaching** - The avadhūta adds the **body itself** as a further teacher: it is the field of birth and death yet, rightly used, the vehicle for discrimination and liberation — then he departs, and Yadu, instructed, becomes free of attachment. (Bhāgavata 11.9.25–33)
  - *Variant (count and ordering):* Different editions and retellings (e.g. the **Nārāyaṇīyam** condensation) reorder the gurus or vary the exact list (some swap "osprey/kuraṛa" for "hawk," or distinguish bee vs. beekeeper differently), but the canonical Bhāgavata total is **24**.

## Tradition-by-tradition claims (variant catalog)

- **Vaiṣṇava (avatāra)** - Dattātreya is a **līlā-avatāra of Viṣṇu**, the divine teacher of brahmavidyā; the standard descent born of Atri's Viṣṇu-portion. (Bhāgavata 1.3.11; 2.7.4; 4.1)
- **Nāth / Yogic Śaiva** - Revered as **Ādi-Guru** of the Nāth sampradāya and a fountainhead of **Haṭha Yoga**; texts like the *Yoga-śāstra of Dattātreya* and figures such as Gorakṣanāth are linked to him; the **Jīvanmukti** and **alchemical/rasa** (rasāyana) lore are associated with his name. (Nāth tradition; Yoga texts)
  - *Variant [scholarly — J. Mallinson]:* Dattātreya was **co-opted into the Nāth tradition relatively late (c. 18th c.)** as part of a Vaiṣṇava–Śaiva syncretism, rather than being its original founder.
- **Mahānubhāva (Maharashtra, 13th c.)** - Founded by **Cakradhar Svāmī**, the sect venerates Dattātreya as one of its **Five Kṛṣṇas / pañca-Kṛṣṇa** (the five supreme divine incarnations) and as an Ādi-Guru. (Mahānubhāva tradition)
- **Datta Sampradāya & the Gurucaritra (Maharashtra/Karnataka, 14th–16th c.)** - A distinctively **ascetic-yet-ritually-orthodox** Datta-devotional movement. Its core hagiography, the **Gurucaritra**, recounts two principal historical avatāras of Dattātreya:
  - **Śrīpāda Śrīvallabha** (14th c., Pīṭhāpuram) — the first historical avatāra.
  - **Narasiṃha Sarasvatī** (15th c.) — the second, central to the Gurucaritra.
  - Later regarded as Datta-incarnations: **Mānik Prabhu**, **Akkalkoṭ Svāmī Samartha**, **Vāsudevānanda Sarasvatī (Ṭembe Svāmī)**, and **Śirḍī Sāī Bābā** (c. 1838–1918). (Datta Sampradāya; Britannica)
- **Festival** - **Datta Jayantī** — celebrated on the full-moon (pūrṇimā) of the lunar month **Mārgaśīrṣa**, marking his birth. (regional Hindu calendar)
