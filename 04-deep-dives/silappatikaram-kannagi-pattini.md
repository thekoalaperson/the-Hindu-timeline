# Cilappatikāram & the Kaṇṇaki–Pattini Cult — the Anklet, the Burning of Madurai, and a Goddess Worshipped in Three Lands

> Cosmic address: **Kali Yuga — documented history**. The story is set in early-historic Tamiḻakam under the three crowned dynasties (Chola Pukār, Pāṇḍya Madurai, Chera Vañci), traditionally c. 2nd c. CE; the epic's composition is placed c. 5th c. CE by modern scholarship [scholarly]. A deep-dive episode-file. Up: [04-deep-dives](./) · Parent corpus: [Tamil canon — Sangam & bhakti](../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md) (the summary layer for this text).

The **Cilappatikāram** ("the Tale of the Anklet", *cilampu* "anklet" + *atikāram* "chapter/lore"; also *Silappadikaram*) is the premier Tamil epic: the story of the merchant's wife **Kaṇṇaki**, whose husband **Kōvalaṉ** is wrongly executed in Madurai over her jeweled anklet, who proves his innocence before the Pāṇḍya king, burns Madurai with the power of her chastity, and ascends as the goddess **Pattini** — worshipped to this day in Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Sri Lanka. It is the only Tamil epic to traverse all three Tamil crowns, and (unusually for an epic) its protagonists are commoners, not kings or gods. This file is the canonical deep treatment; the goddess-assimilation side is shared with [Bhadrakālī & the Kerala Bhagavati](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/bhadrakali-kerala-bhagavati.md).

## The text and its two clocks

- **Author and frame** — the epic is attributed to **Iḷaṅkō Aṭikaḷ** ("the venerable ascetic-prince"), presented in the *patikam* (prologue) as the **younger brother of the Chera king Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ** who renounced the world as a **Jain monk** — by the prologue's account, because an astrologer foretold the younger son would rule, and he abdicated the danger away by taking robes at the Kuṇavāyil monastery near Vañci. The poet **Cāttaṉār** (traditionally the author of the sequel *Maṇimēkalai*) is shown relating Kaṇṇaki's story to the two brothers. (Cilappatikāram, patikam)
  - *Variant ([scholarly]):* many scholars regard the patikam as a later prefixed legend and the royal-brother authorship as unverifiable; the author's Jain affiliation is inferred from the text's tone and framing. [disputed]
- **The three stated morals** — the prologue announces the epic's three truths: **(1)** *aṟam* (dharma) itself becomes Yama (death) to a king whose sceptre bends; **(2)** a chaste wife (*pattini*) is worshipped even by the great; **(3)** fate (*ūḻviṉai*, old karma) inexorably works itself out. (Cilappatikāram, patikam)
- **Form** — three books (*kāṇṭam*) named for the three capitals — **Pukārkkāṇṭam**, **Maturaikkāṇṭam**, **Vañcikkāṇṭam** — in ~30 cantos (*kātai*), interleaving narrative verse with song-and-dance genres (the epic showcases *muttamiḻ*: poetry, music, drama). Religiously **syncretic**: a Jain-framed epic hospitable to Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, and folk-goddess material (Koṟṟavai hymns, cowherd Kṛṣṇa-dances). (Cilappatikāram) [scholarly]
- **Dating — the academic clock [scholarly]** — traditional accounts, anchored to Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ, put the epic c. **100–250 CE**; **Kamil Zvelebil** argued it cannot precede the **5th–6th c. CE**; **Iravatham Mahadevan** narrowed to **c. 450–550 CE**. Modern consensus leans **5th c. CE** for the text, while the story-world reflects the earlier Sangam age — keep the two clocks apart. [disputed]
- **The sequel** — the Buddhist epic **Maṇimēkalai** (Cāttaṉār) follows the daughter of Kōvalaṉ and Mātavi into renunciation; the two are paired as the "twin epics" (*iraṭṭaik kāppiyaṅkaḷ*). (Maṇimēkalai)

## Book I — Pukārkkāṇṭam: Kōvalaṉ, Kaṇṇaki, and Mātavi

- **The marriage at Pukār** — in the Chola port-capital **Pukār** (Kāvirippūmpaṭṭiṉam), **Kōvalaṉ**, son of the great merchant Mācāttuvāṉ, weds **Kaṇṇaki**, daughter of the merchant-magnate Māṉāykaṉ; the wedding is celebrated with the city's full pomp and the couple live in prosperous happiness. (Cilappatikāram, Pukārkkāṇṭam, opening cantos)
- **Mātavi's debut** — the courtesan-dancer **Mātavi** (Mādhavi), of the celestial dancing lineage traced to the apsaras Urvaśī, gives her *araṅkēṟṟam* (debut performance) before the Chola king and receives a garland of honor; Kōvalaṉ purchases the garland at great price — by convention, taking Mātavi herself — and abandons his home to live with her. (Cilappatikāram, "Araṅkēṟṟu kātai")
- **The Indra festival and the seashore songs** — during Pukār's great **festival of Indra**, Kōvalaṉ and Mātavi sing alternating love-songs on the beach (*kāṉal vari*); hearing Mātavi sing of another lover (a poetic convention), Kōvalaṉ takes it as real, suspects her heart, and leaves her — his fortune already squandered on her. Mātavi is left carrying his daughter, **Maṇimēkalai**. (Cilappatikāram, "Intiravviḻavūr eṭutta kātai", "Kāṉal vari")
- **The departure for Madurai** — Kōvalaṉ returns, ruined, to Kaṇṇaki, who receives him without reproach and offers her one remaining wealth: her pair of jeweled **anklets** (*cilampu*), as capital to rebuild their life. The couple leave Pukār by night for **Madurai**, joined on the road by the Jain nun **Kavunti Aṭikaḷ** as guide and guardian. (Cilappatikāram, close of Pukārkkāṇṭam)

## Book II — Maturaikkāṇṭam: the anklet trial and the burning of Madurai

- **Lodging with the cowherds** — reaching the Pāṇḍya capital, Kavunti entrusts Kaṇṇaki to the cowherd-woman **Mātari**, in whose settlement Kaṇṇaki is sheltered while Kōvalaṉ goes into the city to sell one anklet; the cowherd girls dance the **kuravai** dance of Kṛṣṇa and the gopīs, amid omens of ill. (Cilappatikāram, Maturaikkāṇṭam; "Āycciyar kuravai")
- **The goldsmith's treachery** — the **royal goldsmith**, who has himself stolen the queen's anklet, sees Kōvalaṉ's anklet, and denounces him to the Pāṇḍya king **Neṭuñceḻiyaṉ** as the thief. The king — without inquiry or trial — orders: *kill the thief and bring the anklet*. Kōvalaṉ is cut down by the executioner's sword. (Cilappatikāram, "Kolaikkaḷak kātai")
- **Kaṇṇaki hears, and comes for justice** — told of her husband's execution, Kaṇṇaki takes the surviving anklet and strides weeping and blazing through Madurai to the palace gate, demanding audience: her city has a king whose sceptre is bent. (Cilappatikāram, "Ūrcūḻ vari")
- **The anklet trial** — before Neṭuñceḻiyaṉ, Kaṇṇaki declares Kōvalaṉ innocent and stakes the proof on the anklets themselves: **the queen's anklet holds pearls; Kaṇṇaki's holds gems (rubies)**. Her anklet is broken open — **a ruby flies out**, striking the truth home. (Cilappatikāram, "Vaḻakkurai kātai")
- **The death of the king** — confronted with his injustice, the Pāṇḍya king declares, *"Am I the king? I who heeded the goldsmith's word — I am the thief"*, and **dies on the spot**, his sceptre fallen; his queen **Kōpperuntēvi** dies with him of grief. The epic offers this as the first stated moral enacted: dharma become Yama to the erring king. (Cilappatikāram, "Vaḻakkurai kātai")
- **The burning of Madurai** — vindicated but unappeased, Kaṇṇaki curses the city, **tears her left breast from her body and hurls it over Madurai**; the fire-god **Agni** rises at her command, and she directs the flames: spare brahmins, the good, cows, chaste women, the old, the young — **consume the wicked**. Madurai burns. (Cilappatikāram, "Vañciṉa mālai"; "Aḻaṟpaṭu kātai")
- **The goddess of Madurai explains the karma** — the city's tutelary goddess **Maturāpati** appears before Kaṇṇaki and reveals the *ūḻviṉai* behind the horror: in a former birth Kōvalaṉ, as **Bharata** in the service of a king, had wrongly denounced and caused the beheading of an innocent merchant, **Saṅkamaṉ**, as a spy — and Saṅkamaṉ's widow's curse has now ripened. The goddess asks Kaṇṇaki to withdraw the fire and foretells that **in fourteen days she will rejoin her husband in heaven**. (Cilappatikāram, "Kaṭṭurai kātai")

## Apotheosis — Kaṇṇaki becomes Pattini

- **The westward walk and ascent** — Kaṇṇaki leaves the ruined city, wanders west along the Vaiyai and up into the **Chera hill-country**, and stands on the hill **Neṭuvēḷ Kuṉṟam** (Tiruccheṅkuṉṟu); at the appointed time the gods descend and **she ascends bodily to heaven with Kōvalaṉ in a celestial chariot**, witnessed by the **Kuṟavar hill-folk**, who dance the *kuṉṟak kuravai* and resolve to worship her. (Cilappatikāram, "Kuṉṟak kuravai")
- **The name Pattini** — she is thereafter the **Pattini** (*pattiṉi*, "the chaste wife" — Skt. *patnī*), the deified embodiment of *kaṟpu* (chastity as sacral power): the second stated moral of the prologue enacted. In Tamil worship she is **Kaṇṇaki Ammaṉ**. (Cilappatikāram; Tamil tradition)

## Book III — Vañcikkāṇṭam: Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ's Himalayan expedition and the temple

- **The king hears of the goddess** — at **Vañci**, the Chera king **Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ**, on an excursion to the Periyār riverbank with his queen **Iḷaṅkō Vēṇmāḷ**, hears from the hill-folk of the wondrous woman who ascended from the hill; the poet Cāttaṉār recounts the full Madurai story. Moved, the queen desires that the chaste one be enshrined; the king resolves to raise her image. (Cilappatikāram, Vañcikkāṇṭam)
- **The Himalayan stone vow** — Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ decrees that the image of Pattini be carved from **stone brought from the Himālaya and bathed in the Gaṅgā** — a deliberate assertion that the southern king's writ runs to the northern mountain. (Cilappatikāram, "Kālkōṭ kātai")
- **The northern war** — marching north, Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ crosses the Gaṅgā and defeats the Āryan kings **Kanaka and Vijaya**, who had mocked the Tamil kings; the captive kings are made to **carry the sacred stone on their heads** back to the Chera country. (Cilappatikāram, "Kālkōṭ kātai"; "Nīrppaṭaik kātai")
- **The consecration at Vañci** — the image of Kaṇṇaki-Pattini is carved and **consecrated in a temple at Vañci** with royal endowments and daily worship; at the festival the deified Kaṇṇaki manifests and grants grace (a devotee-woman, Tēvantikai, is possessed by the goddess and speaks). (Cilappatikāram, "Nāṭukāṇ kātai" to "Varantaru kātai")
- **The Gajabāhu synchronism [scholarly] [disputed]** — the final canto lists among the attending dignitaries **"Kayavāku, king of sea-girt Laṅkā"** — identified with **Gajabāhu I of Anurādhapura** (r. c. 113–135 CE by Mahāvaṃsa-based chronology). This single line is the famous **"Gajabāhu synchronism"**, long used as the sheet-anchor for dating Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ and the Sangam age to the **2nd c. CE**. Critics reply that the epic itself is likely a 5th-c. composition (Zvelebil, Mahadevan), so the synchronism may be a genuine memory, a retrospective construction, or refer to a later Gajabāhu — it remains **contested**, and this corpus records it without resolving it. (Cilappatikāram, "Varantaru kātai"; Mahāvaṃsa chronology; Zvelebil) [disputed]

## The living cult — Pattini in three lands

**Kerala** [regional]
- **Kodungallur Bhagavati = Kaṇṇaki?** — Kerala tradition identifies Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ's Vañci shrine with **Kodungallur**, making the fierce Kodungallur Bhagavati simultaneously **Bhadrakālī and Kaṇṇaki**; the Vañci–Kodungallur identification is traditional and contested in scholarship. Full treatment of the assimilation, the Bharaṇi festival, and the Dārika-cycle: [Bhadrakālī & the Kerala Bhagavati](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/bhadrakali-kerala-bhagavati.md). (Kerala temple tradition) [disputed]
- **Attukal Bhagavati (Thiruvananthapuram)** — the Attukal temple's charter legend holds that **Kaṇṇaki rested at Attukal on her journey from Madurai toward Kodungallur**; the goddess there is worshipped as Kaṇṇaki, and the **Attukal Poṅkāla** — the vast **women-only** offering of sweet rice, Guinness-recorded as the world's largest gathering of women — is her festival ("the Sabarimala of women"). See [Regional temple festivals](../05-concepts-and-dharma/regional-temple-festivals.md). (Attukal temple tradition) [folk]

**Sri Lanka** [regional]
- **Pattini among the Sinhala Buddhists** — **Pattini** is one of the guardian deities of Sinhala Buddhism, with major shrines (devāles) such as Navagamuwa and the Kandy Pattini devāle; her worship is the subject of **Gananath Obeyesekere's** landmark study *The Cult of the Goddess Pattini* (1984). [scholarly]
- **The gammaḍuva** — Pattini is honored in the **gammaḍuva** ("village hall") post-harvest ritual complex, in which ritual specialists sing the Sinhala Pattini corpus (the *pantis kōlmura*, "the thirty-five songs" per Obeyesekere) and enact her myths, including the **shooting of the mango** (*amba viḍamana*) from which Pattini is miraculously born. (Sinhala ritual tradition; Obeyesekere) [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (Sinhala myth-cycle):* in the Sinhala songs Pattini's husband **Palaṅga** (= Kōvalaṉ) is executed in the Pāṇḍi kingdom and Pattini **resurrects him** — a resurrection absent from the Tamil epic, where the reunion is only in heaven. Catalog, do not merge. (Sinhala Pattini texts, per Obeyesekere) [oral tradition]
- **The Gajabāhu importation legend** — late Sinhala chronicle tradition (e.g. the Rājāvaliya) has **Gajabāhu bring the Pattini cult (with her anklet-relic) from the Chola/Tamil country to Laṅkā** along with recovered captives — a foundation-legend paired with, and as disputed as, the synchronism itself. [late text] [disputed]
- **Kaṇṇaki Ammaṉ among Sri Lankan Tamils** — Tamil Hindus of the island's north and east worship her as **Kaṇṇaki Ammaṉ** (notably the Vaṭṭāppaḷai Kaṇṇaki Ammaṉ temple, Mullaitivu, with its annual poṅkal), a Hindu counterpart of the Buddhist Pattini. [regional] [folk]

**Tamil Nadu** [regional]
- **Kaṇṇaki Ammaṉ shrines** — village and border shrines keep her cult alive; the **Maṅgaḷadevī Kaṇṇaki temple** in the Periyār hills (Theni district, on the Tamil Nadu–Kerala border, claimed by some as the site of Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ's shrine) opens for worship **only on Chitrā Paurṇamī** each year. [folk] [disputed]
- **Modern icon** — the **Kaṇṇaki statue on Marina Beach, Chennai** (erected 1968, removed 2001, reinstated 2006) marks her status as an emblem of Tamil womanhood and justice — a modern political afterlife of the epic. [scholarly]

**Scholarly reading** — historians of religion read Pattini/Kaṇṇaki as one stratum in a layered South Indian goddess: the old Dravidian war/mother goddess (Sangam **Koṟṟavai**), the Sanskrit **Kālī/Durgā**, and the epic chaste-wife — one seated presence answering to all registers (see the layered-goddess bullet in the [Bhadrakālī file](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/bhadrakali-kerala-bhagavati.md)). [scholarly]
