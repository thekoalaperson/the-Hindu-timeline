# Haṃsa Avatāra

> Where this sits: a deep-dive on Viṣṇu's **swan-form (haṃsa)** — the philosopher-avatāra who answers the [Four Kumāras](../08-beings-and-bestiary/the-prajapatis.md) on the nature of the Self, a named member of the Bhāgavata's open avatāra roster but one with almost no independent narrative.
>

The **Haṃsa-avatāra** (हंस, "swan / goose") is the form in which [Viṣṇu](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) descends not to slay a demon or rescue the earth, but purely to **expound the nature of the Self** — *adhyātma-vidyā* — to the four mind-born sons of Brahmā. It is one of the most abstract and least dramatized of all the incarnations: the swan is essentially a *teaching*, a single philosophical dialogue with no birth, deeds, or departure of its own. The episode is recounted by **Kṛṣṇa** to **Uddhava** within the [Uddhava Gītā](./uddhava-gita.md) as ancient wisdom he now re-transmits, and it gives that whole discourse its alternate name, the **Haṃsa Gītā**. [scholarly]

## Place in the avatāra lists

- **A member of the "24 avatāras," not the ten** — The Haṃsa does not appear among the fixed canonical [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md); it belongs to the wider open roster of *līlā*-avatāras enumerated in the Bhāgavata's first canto (SB 1.3) and summarized in [SB 11.4](./uddhava-gita.md). (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 1.3; 11.4)
- **Added when the list is extended to 24** — When the standard ten are expanded to twenty-four, the **Haṃsa** (the swan, "expounder of knowledge") is one of the forms commonly added, alongside **Hayagrīva**, the four Kumāras, Nara-Nārāyaṇa, Kapila, Dattātreya, Ṛṣabha, and others. See the discussion in [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) and the form-taxonomy in [Viṣṇu's Forms & Vyūhas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishnu-forms-and-vyuhas.md). (Source: Bhāgavata commentarial tradition; Pāñcarātra)
  - *Variant (counting):* because the "24-avatāra" rosters differ recension to recension, the Haṃsa is present in many but not all of them; some lists drop it in favour of Pṛthu, Mohinī, or Vyāsa. Catalog the membership as roster-dependent, not fixed. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **A "jñāna-avatāra"** — Where most descents are world-rescuing, the Haṃsa is classed (with Kapila, Dattātreya, and Nara-Nārāyaṇa) among the avatāras of **knowledge and teaching** — the Lord descending to deliver doctrine rather than to act. (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa; Vaiṣṇava commentary) [scholarly]

## The Haṃsa-avatāra dialogue (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13)

The avatāra's sole "narrative" is the dialogue Kṛṣṇa relays to Uddhava in **Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13**, the chapter that supplies the Uddhava Gītā with its title **Haṃsa Gītā**.

- **The frame — Kṛṣṇa cites an ancient teaching** — Having exalted *satsaṅga* (11.12), Kṛṣṇa tells Uddhava that the very knowledge he is now imparting is **primordial**: long ago, in a prior age, Viṣṇu himself, taking the form of a **swan (haṃsa)**, taught it to Brahmā's sons. He recites that old conversation. (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13.15–17)
- **The questioners — the Sanaka-kumāras** — The **Four Kumāras** — **Sanaka, Sananda, Sanātana, and Sanat-kumāra** (the *Sanakādi*, mind-born sons of Brahmā) — approach their father with a question of practical metaphysics: **how is the mind to be separated from its sense-objects, since the two seem inextricably bound together?** (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13.18–19)
  - *Variant (who is asked first):* in the Bhāgavata the Kumāras put the question to **Brahmā**, who cannot answer it and so the Lord appears as the swan to resolve it; some retellings have the Kumāras address the swan directly. The Brahmā-unable-to-answer framing is the canonical one. (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13) [scholarly]
- **The Lord appears as the swan** — Unable to settle the doubt, **Brahmā** meditates on the supreme Lord, who manifests before them as a **haṃsa**. The Kumāras ask the swan, "**Who are you?**" (*kas tvaṃ*). (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13.20–22)
- **The swan's reply on identity** — The swan answers with a non-dual riddle: the very question "who are you?" is meaningless if the questioner posits a real difference, for the Self is **one** in all beings; if a difference is posited it is unreal, since all is constituted of the same elements. He is, in essence, **the one Self (ātman)** that all the Kumāras and all beings equally are. (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13.22–24)
- **The teaching — mind, sense-objects, and the witness** — To the original doubt the swan answers that **the objects of the senses and the mind that grasps them are both modifications of the same material nature (the three guṇas)**; they are not the Self. The Self is the **detached witness (sākṣin)**, distinct from both the perceived object and the perceiving mind. Mind-and-objects are mutually entangled precisely because both are *non-Self*; the discrimination (*viveka*) of the witnessing Self from this guṇa-made apparatus **is** the separation the Kumāras sought. (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13.24–34)
- **The three states and the turīya Self** — The swan analyses the **waking, dream, and deep-sleep** states (*jāgrat, svapna, suṣupti*) as conditions of the *buddhi* (intellect/guṇas), and locates the Self as the unchanging consciousness that **witnesses all three** and is bound by none — the true object of self-knowledge. (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13.27–34)
- **The instruction to abide as the witness** — The realized soul, knowing himself as this witness, should give up false identification with body and mind, remain unattached amid sense-experience, and rest in the Self — the *paramahaṃsa* ideal of being "in the water yet unwetted." (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13.35–39)
- **The Kumāras' liberation and the swan's disappearance** — Having received and grasped the teaching, the **Four Kumāras worship the swan**, who then **vanishes**; this is the entire span of the avatāra. (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa 11.13.40–42)

## The avatāra with effectively zero independent story

- **No birth, deeds, or end** — Unlike Matsya, Kūrma, or Narasiṃha, the Haṃsa has **no origin-myth, no exploits, and no departure** beyond the single dialogue; it appears, teaches, and disappears. It is best understood as a **doctrinal descent** — the personification of *adhyātma*-knowledge in swan-shape — rather than an episode with a plot. [scholarly]
- **Why the swan?** — The **haṃsa** is the standing Indic emblem of the discriminating sage: folk belief holds it can **separate milk from water**, i.e. essence (the Self) from dross (the guṇa-made world) — exactly the *viveka* the dialogue teaches; hence the perfected renunciate is the **paramahaṃsa** ("supreme swan"). The swan-form and the swan-symbolism are two faces of the same lesson. (Source: traditional symbolism; cf. [Uddhava Gītā](./uddhava-gita.md)) [folk]
- **Distinct from other "swan" usages** — The cosmogonic **Haṃsa** as a name of the supreme Brahman (Upaniṣadic *haṃsa* / *paramahaṃsa*), Brahmā's **goose-vehicle (haṃsa-vāhana)**, and Sarasvatī's swan are related symbolically but are **not** this avatāra; keep them separate. The avatāra is specifically the SB 11.13 teacher of the Kumāras. (Source: Upaniṣadic and iconographic tradition) [scholarly]
- **Relation to Nara-Nārāyaṇa and the jñāna-avatāras** — Like [Nara–Nārāyaṇa](./nara-narayana.md), the Haṃsa belongs to the family of teaching/penance descents and shares the *paramahaṃsa* ideal; both stand outside the fixed ten and inside the open Bhāgavata roster. (Source: Bhāgavata Purāṇa) [scholarly]

## Two clocks — scriptural vs. historical

- **Scripture-internal placement** — On the tradition's cyclic clock the swan's teaching of the Kumāras belongs to the **early creation**, in a former age, as part of Brahmā's settling of his mind-born sons; Kṛṣṇa cites it as wisdom **older than himself**, which he merely re-transmits at the Dvāpara–Kali cusp (3102 BCE) in the [Uddhava Gītā](./uddhava-gita.md). [traditional]
- **Academic dating** — As a passage of the [Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/smriti-puranas.md), the Haṃsa-avatāra dialogue is text-critically dated with the rest of that work to roughly the **9th–10th c. CE** (South Indian provenance) — far later than its narrated creation-era setting. The two registers must be kept distinct. [scholarly]
