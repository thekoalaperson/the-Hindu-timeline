# Tarakasura and the Birth-War of Kartikeya

> Cosmic address: a first-tier *Śaiva–Skanda asura myth* in which the daitya **Tārakāsura** wins a boon that leaves only a child of Śiva (or, in other tellings, a seven-day-old child, or Agni's son) able to kill him, and thereby forces the **divine wedding** and the **birth of Skanda-Kārttikeya**. Undated within the Manvantara frame; the texts set it in primordial deva-time, conventionally tagged [Satya/Tretā].
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · See also: [Tripura-dāhana](tripura-dahana.md) · [Asura Wars & Roster](../08-beings-and-bestiary/asura-wars-and-roster.md) · [Gaṇeśa & Kārttikeya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md)

The **Tāraka-vadha** ("slaying of Tāraka") is the *narrative engine* of the Purāṇic Śaiva-Skanda cycle. In the *Śiva Purāṇa* and Kālidāsa, Tāraka's boon is calibrated to be unfulfillable: Śiva, having lost **Satī** at Dakṣa's sacrifice, sits in austerity and fathers no one. The gods must therefore engineer his marriage to **Pārvatī** (Satī reborn), send **Kāma** to rouse his desire (and lose him to Śiva's third eye), and at last produce the war-god **Skanda**. The epics frame it differently: in the *Mahābhārata* Anuśāsana-parvan and the *Rāmāyaṇa* Bāla-kāṇḍa, Śiva is already married to Umā, the gods themselves stop the union, and Umā curses them to be childless (*MBh* 13.83.41–51; *Rām.* 1.35.6–23). The *Rāmāyaṇa* does not name Tāraka at all; there the gods simply want a general (*senāpati*, 1.36.1). The episode is inseparable from the [Satī–Dakṣa–Pārvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md) cycle and from the birth-mythology of [Kārttikeya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md). Its fullest narratives are in the *Śiva Purāṇa* (Rudra-saṃhitā, Pārvatī-khaṇḍa and Kumāra-khaṇḍa), the *Skanda Purāṇa* (Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa; Kedāra-khaṇḍa), the *Matsya Purāṇa* (adhyāyas 146–160), the *Vāmana Purāṇa* (31–32), the *Mahābhārata* (Vana, Śalya and Anuśāsana parvans) and Kālidāsa's court epic (*mahākāvya*), the *Kumārasambhava*. **Distinguish Tāraka from his three sons**, Tārakākṣa, Vidyunmālī and Kamalākṣa, whose later war is the separate [Tripura-dāhana](tripura-dahana.md).

---

## I. Tāraka's Tapas and the Boon

- **Lineage of Tāraka** — Tāraka is a **daitya**, son of **Vajrāṅga** (a son of Diti by Kaśyapa, born after Indra had split Diti's earlier embryo into the Maruts) and of **Varāṅgī**, a wife Brahmā created for Vajrāṅga. (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.14.8–27, 2.3.15.1–18; *Matsya Purāṇa* 146.5, 146.41, 146.57–58; *Skanda Purāṇa* Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa 14–15). The Purāṇas that tell the birth agree on this pedigree. The *Mahābhārata* (13.83.54) and Kālidāsa (*Kumārasambhava* 2.32) just call him "the asura Tāraka" without naming parents.
  - *Variant (why Varāṅgī wanted him):* in the *Śiva Purāṇa*, Varāṅgī asks her husband for a son who will conquer the three worlds and bring misery to Viṣṇu (2.3.14.32). In the *Matsya*, Indra has frightened, beaten and tormented her, and she asks for a son, "Tāraka", to rescue her from that sorrow (147.1–2, 147.10–14); the *Skanda* Kaumārikā agrees (15.1–2). (Shastri's translation for *ŚP*; Sanskrit for *Matsya*; Tagare's translation for *Skanda*)
- **The terrible penance** — In the *Śiva Purāṇa*, Tāraka performs tapas in the Madhu forest in hundred-year stages: arms raised on one leg gazing at the sun, then on one big toe, then on water alone, then on air, standing in water, amid fires, upside down, and hanging from a tree. A mass of light rises from his head and scorches the worlds until the gods beg **Brahmā** to go to him (2.3.15.22–35). The *Matsya* has him fast, do the five-fire penance, and live on leaves and water for a hundred years each, then cut flesh from his body day by day and offer it into the fire until he is fleshless (148.10–13). (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.15; *Matsya Purāṇa* 148.10–15)
- **The boon** — The texts disagree on the death-clause itself:
  - *Variant (Śiva Purāṇa 2.3.15.39–41):* Tāraka asks for two boons: that no one in the universe equal him in strength, and that he die only when a son born of Śiva becomes an army commander and hurls weapons at him. He does not first ask for immortality. (Shastri tr.)
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 148.20–23; Skanda Purāṇa Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa 15.49–55):* Tāraka asks to be unkillable by every being and every weapon. Brahmā answers that no embodied being can be free of death and tells him to choose his death. "Deluded by arrogance" (*avalepena mohitaḥ*), Tāraka chooses death at the hands of **a child of seven days** (*śiśor vai saptavāsarāt*). The boon does not name Śiva. Brahmā later tells the gods that this seven-day child will be Śaṃkara's (*Matsya* 154.47–50). [the seven-day motif underlies the accelerated birth-to-war]
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 13.84.5–10):* Tāraka asks not to be slayable by gods, asuras or rākṣasas. Brahmā's loophole is that **Agni's** son, who belongs to none of those classes, will kill him with an unfailing spear.
  - *Variant (Kumārasambhava 2.54–57):* Brahmā says only that he granted the boon and that no one but a portion of Śiva's discharged seed could face Tāraka in battle.
- **Why the boon was thought safe** — No text quoted here gives Tāraka's own reasoning. The *Matsya* and *Skanda* say only that he chose "deluded by arrogance" (*avalepena mohitaḥ*, *Matsya* 148.23; "deluded by false prestige" in Tagare's translation of *Skanda* Kaumārikā 15.55). It is Brahmā who points out the obstacle, Śiva's wifelessness: "Śaṃkara is at present without a wife" (*apatnīkaḥ*, *Matsya* 154.50), and in the *Śiva Purāṇa* Pārvatī serves Śiva, unregarded, during his penance (2.3.16.28–35). [the boon's irony is the whole plot; the "Satī-grief" motive is a reading of the narrative, not a stated calculation]

## II. The Oppression of the Devas

- **Tāraka's conquest of the three worlds** — Crowned king of the three worlds with Śukra's consent, Tāraka extorts the gods' treasures. In the *Śiva Purāṇa* Indra gives up Airāvata, Kubera his nine treasures, Varuṇa his white horses, the sages Kāmadhenu, and the Sun his horse Uccaiḥśravas (2.3.15.43–51). The gods are driven out and their women captured (2.3.16.5–12). Later in the same text Brahmā tells Tāraka that the boon did not include heaven, and Tāraka leaves it to rule the earth from **Śoṇitapura** (2.3.16.37–42; cf. 2.3.15.43). Kālidāsa's gods complain that Indra's horse Uccaiḥśravas has been taken, the Nandana trees have been stripped, and Tāraka's own elephants have beaten Airāvata (*Kumārasambhava* 2.41, 2.47, 2.50). (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.15–16; *Kumārasambhava* II)
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 148.31–45; Skanda Purāṇa Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa 16–21):* Tāraka marches on heaven with a ten-general army under his commander **Grasana**. The generals include Jambha, Kujambha, **Mahiṣa** and **Kālanemi** (*Matsya* 148.38–43). He defeats the gods in a pitched war in which Viṣṇu fights Kālanemi.
- **Inversion of cosmic order** — Under his rule the cosmic powers serve him as courtiers. The sun burns his city only enough to open the lotuses in his ponds, the moon attends him with every digit, and the wind dares blow no harder than a fan. He snatches the sacrificial oblations from Agni's mouth before the gods' eyes. (*Kumārasambhava* 2.33–35, 2.46). The *Śiva Purāṇa* similarly has the sun shine gently, the moon always visible, and the wind blowing in his favour (2.3.15.52), with no sacrifices performed (2.3.16.13). The *Matsya* and *Skanda* make the sun and moon his lamps and the wind his fan (*Matsya* 148.30; *Skanda* Kaumārikā 15.61).
- **The devas petition Brahmā** — The dispossessed gods, led by **Indra**, approach **Brahmā**. He says he cannot destroy what he himself raised up, "even a poison-tree one has reared one may not cut down oneself" (*Kumārasambhava* 2.55; echoed in *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.16.21–22), and reveals the only remedy: **a son of Śiva** (*Kumārasambhava* 2.57–61; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.16.25–31; *Matsya* 154.47–53). [in Kālidāsa the gods' arrival opens canto II (2.1), and Bṛhaspati speaks their lament (2.30–52)]
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 13.84.1–18):* Brahmā sends the gods to look for **Agni**, who escaped Umā's curse because he was absent, and who will beget the slayer on Gaṅgā.
- **The need for a war-general** — The gods ask specifically for a **senānī / senāpati** to lead their host (*Kumārasambhava* 2.51–52), and Brahmā promises that Śiva's son will take the command (*Kumārasambhava* 2.61). In the *Rāmāyaṇa* the whole quest is for a *senāpati* (1.36.1–7). In the *Mahābhārata* Brahmā confers the generalship on the newborn (9.43.48).

## III. The Stratagem — Kāma, the Burning, and Pārvatī's Tapas

- **Pārvatī born to win Śiva** — **Satī**, who gave up her body after her father insulted her, is reborn to **Himavān** (the Himālaya) and **Menā** (*Kumārasambhava* 1.21; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.6). The gods' plan hinges on her uniting with the ascetic Śiva (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.16.28–35; *Matsya* 154.51–53).
- **Indra sends Kāma to break Śiva's trance** — **Indra** summons **Kāmadeva**, who sets out with **Rati** and his friend **Vasanta** (Spring) to aim the flower-arrow at Śiva while Pārvatī attends him. (*Kumārasambhava* 2.63–64, III; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.17–18)
- **The burning of Kāma (Kāma-dahana)** — Śiva's anger at the disturbance bursts as fire from his **third eye**. Before the gods' cry "restrain your anger, lord" has finished echoing, it burns Kāma to ash, leaving Rati a widow and love "bodiless" (*anaṅga*). (*Kumārasambhava* 3.71–72; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.19.14–17) [the Holī/Kāma-dahana motif]
  - *Variant (the promise to Rati):* in the *Śiva Purāṇa*, Śiva himself promises that Kāma will stay bodiless until he is born as **Pradyumna**, son of Kṛṣṇa and Rukmiṇī, who will win Rati back after killing Śambara (2.3.19.38–44). Later in the same text Śiva revives Kāma from the ashes at the wedding (2.3.51.14). In Kālidāsa a bodiless voice tells Rati that Kāma's burning fulfils a curse, earned when he stirred Brahmā's desire for his own daughter, and that Śiva will restore his body once he weds Pārvatī (*Kumārasambhava* 4.40–44). The *Bhāgavata* independently identifies Pradyumna as Kāma, "burned before by Rudra's anger" (10.55.1).
- **Pārvatī's own penance** — Failing to win Śiva by beauty, Pārvatī undertakes severe **tapas**. She lives first on fruit, then on leaves, then gives up even leaves, and so is called **Aparṇā**. She wins Śiva by asceticism, not by Kāma's arrow. (*Kumārasambhava* 5.28; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.22.47–49)
- **Śiva tests her in disguise** — Śiva comes to Pārvatī as a matted-haired **brahmacārin** (*jaṭila*) who runs Śiva down to her face. Her steadfast defence reveals her devotion and he shows his true form. (*Kumārasambhava* 5.30ff; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.26–28) [Kālidāsa's celebrated test-scene. The *Śiva Purāṇa* adds an earlier test by the Seven Sages (2.3.25)]
- **The divine wedding** — Śiva and Pārvatī wed at Himavān's city with the gods attending, and the marriage makes Tāraka's death possible. (*Kumārasambhava* VII; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.3.48) [the cantos generally accepted as Kālidāsa's are I–VII, with VIII, the couple's love-making, usually admitted on Ānandavardhana's citation]

## IV. The Conception and Birth of Skanda-Kārttikeya

- **The interrupted union and the seed of fire** — Śiva and Pārvatī's love-play lasts a thousand divine years. The gods, desperate under Tāraka, gather at Śiva's door, and Śiva lets his seed (**tejas**) fall. **Agni**, in the form of a **dove**, swallows it. (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.4.1.14–63, 2.4.2.1–11)
  - *Variant (the gods stop the union for fear of the child):* in the epics the gods fear that a child of such a pair would leave nothing standing. They ask Śiva to hold back his seed, and he agrees, becoming *ūrdhvaretas* (*MBh* 13.83.40–47; *Rām.* 1.35.6–13). Some seed is spilled anyway and falls on the earth or into Agni (*MBh* 13.83.52–53; *Rām.* 1.35.14–17).
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 158.28–36):* after a thousand years the gods send Agni in the form of a **parrot** (*śukarūpī*) through a window. Śiva makes him drink the half of the seed not yet given to the Devī.
- **Pārvatī's curse** — Robbed of a child, Pārvatī/Umā curses the gods' wives to be barren (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.4.2.14–21; *Rām.* 1.35.20–21). In the *Mahābhārata* the curse falls on the gods themselves: "you shall all be without offspring" (*anapatyā bhaviṣyatha*, 13.83.48–51). In the *Rāmāyaṇa* she also curses the Earth to have many husbands and no joy in a son (1.35.22–23). Agni escapes the curse in the *Mahābhārata* because he was absent (13.83.51, 13.84.8), but is cursed separately in the *Śiva Purāṇa* (2.4.2.20–21).
- **The bearers of the seed** — The seed is too potent for any one bearer. Every text relays it along a different route, and this is the most variable element of the myth. [disputed]
  - *Variant (Śiva Purāṇa 2.4.2.25–68):* the gods, who share Agni's oblations, become pregnant and vomit the seed out. On Nārada's advice Agni puts it into six of the Seven Sages' wives as they warm themselves after the Māgha dawn bath (Arundhatī refuses). Their husbands cast them off. They drop the embryo on Himavat, who throws it into the **Gaṅgā**, and her waves leave it in the **śara**-reed forest. The child is born on the bright sixth of Mārgaśīrṣa (2.4.2.68).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata Śalya-parvan 9.43.6–11):* Agni cannot bear the seed and by Brahmā's order places it in the **Gaṅgā**, who casts it onto a Himālaya peak, where the Kṛttikās find the child in a reed-clump (*śarastamba*).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata Anuśāsana-parvan 13.84.52–13.86.13):* Agni impregnates **Gaṅgā**. She cannot hold the embryo and drops it on **Meru**, where it turns everything to gold (the origin of *jātarūpa*, gold). In a second telling the six **Kṛttikās** carry the embryo themselves and deliver it together, and it becomes one six-based child (13.86.5–11).
  - *Variant (Rāmāyaṇa 1.35.16–1.36.22):* the spilled seed is taken by the Earth, then entered by Agni with Vāyu, forming the white mountain (Śveta) and the reed-forest. Later the gods send Agni to the **Gaṅgā**, who casts the embryo at Himavat's foot, and gold and the other metals are born from it.
  - *Variant (Vāmana Purāṇa 31.3–20):* Agni, weakened by the seed, hands it to the river-goddess **Kuṭilā**. Each carries it for five thousand years, giving Agni his name *Hiraṇyaretas*. Brahmā sends Kuṭilā to the hundred-yojana reed-forest on Mount Udaya, and the child is born after ten thousand years.
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 158.36–159.5):* the swallowed seed bursts out of the gods' bellies and forms a golden lake. Pārvatī drinks its water from a lotus leaf held by the Kṛttikās, on condition that the son also be theirs. The six-faced boy bursts from her **right side** already holding spear and trident. A second boy bursts from her left side, and Indra makes the two into one, who is named Skanda, Viśākha, Ṣaṇmukha and Kārttikeya (159.1–5). [the Matsya's own preview at 146.8–11 gives the river-and-reed version instead]
  - *Variant (Skanda Purāṇa Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa 29.82–122):* Agni enters as a **pigeon** and is made to swallow the seed. It bursts from the gods' bellies as a hundred-yojana lake of mercury. Agni casts it into the **Gaṅgā**, whose waves throw it off, and it becomes the white mountain (Śveta). Agni then desires the Seven Sages' wives, and **Svāhā** takes their forms to unite with him, then flies as a Suparṇā (Garuḍī) to the reed-covered Śveta mountain and leaves the seed in a **golden pot**. She repeats this in the forms of the other wives but cannot take Arundhatī's form (29.96–109). The sages cast off six of their wives, sparing Arundhatī (29.122). (Tagare tr.) [the Mahābhārata Vana motif grafted onto the Gaṅgā route]
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata Vana-parvan 3.213–3.221):* Skanda is **Agni's son by Svāhā**. Svāhā takes the forms of six of the Seven Sages' wives (she cannot copy Arundhatī) and drops Agni's seed six times into a **golden pot** on the white mountain (3.214.10–15). The child is born six-headed (3.214.17). The sages divorce the six wives, who later become the **Kṛttikās** (3.215.5, 3.219.11). The text reconciles this with Śiva by saying Rudra had entered Agni, and Umā Svāhā, so Skanda is also "Rudra's son" (3.218.27–30, 3.220.9).
- **The Kṛttikās nurse him** — The six **Kṛttikā** (Pleiades) mothers each claim the child as theirs. He takes six faces (**Ṣaṇmukha**) to drink from all six at once, whence **Kārttikeya** ("son of the Kṛttikās"). (*MBh* 9.43.10–12, 13.84.76–77; *Rām.* 1.36.23–28; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.4.3.29–33; *Vāmana* 31.22–25). The *Kumārasambhava* tells the birth only in its later cantos IX–XVII, which many manuscripts lack and which scholars since Jacobi generally treat as spurious (exact canto for the Kṛttikā scene not verified). [interpolation; scholarly]
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 9.43.33–39):* when the boy sees Śiva, Umā, Gaṅgā and Agni each hoping he will come to them first, he splits into four, Skanda, Śākha, Viśākha and Naigameṣa, and goes to each. In the *Śiva Purāṇa* the other three instead spring from the boy's right side, left side and heart when **Indra** strikes him with the thunderbolt (2.4.3.22–26, Shastri tr.).
- **Consecration as senāpati** — The gods anoint the prodigious infant **commander of the deva-army**. In the *Mahābhārata* Brahmā grants the generalship and the consecration takes place on the **Sarasvatī** at Samantapañcaka (9.43.48–52, 9.44). In the Vana-parvan Indra anoints him (3.218.42). In the *Śiva Purāṇa* Śiva enthrones and bathes him, the gods give him their weapons, among them a spear from Agni, and Viṣṇu performs the crowning (2.4.5.38–65). (*Rām.* 1.36.30; *Skanda Purāṇa* Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa 29–30)
  - *Variant (how young he was):* the *Rāmāyaṇa* has him defeat the daitya hosts on his very first day (*ekāhnā*, 1.36.29), and the Vana-parvan addresses him as "born six nights ago" (*ṣaḍrātrajāta*, 3.218.6). In the *Matsya* he is "seven days old" when he kills Tāraka (146.11). The *Skanda* Kaumārikā keeps the seven-day boon (15.54–55) and has Tāraka scorn him as "a very small infant" (32.69–71, Tagare tr.).

## V. The War and the Slaying

- **The march on Tāraka** — Skanda leads the gods against Tāraka's army, with **Indra**, **Viṣṇu**, **Vīrabhadra**, the gaṇas and the **Mothers** (*mātṛgaṇa*) in his host. (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.4.7–9; *Skanda Purāṇa* Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa 31–32; *Matsya Purāṇa* 159–160; *Vāmana Purāṇa* 32.82). The *Mahābhārata* likens a hero to "Skanda in the **Tārakāmaya** battle" (8.6.46), and elsewhere calls this war *tārakākhya*, "named after Tāraka, in which Skanda slew Tāraka" (9.42.40).
  - *Variant (the name Tārakāmaya):* the name is ambiguous. The *Matsya*'s list of twelve deva–asura wars counts the Tārakāmaya as the fifth, the war in which **Indra** slew Virocana (47.43, 47.49), and the *Mahābhārata*'s Tripura account calls the "first" deva–asura battle the Tārakāmaya (8.24.3). The *Matsya* also gives the name to the Kṛta-yuga war after Vṛtra's death in which Nārāyaṇa rescues the gods (172.10). The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* names the war over Tārā, Bṛhaspati's wife carried off by Soma, "the Tārakāmaya, caused by Tārā" (*tārānimittas tārakāmayo nāma*, 4.6.16); the *Matsya* tells that abduction (23.30–47) without the name. In the *Skanda* Kaumārikā, Śiva tells Viṣṇu during Skanda's own war that Kālanemi "ought to have been killed by you in the Tārakāmaya battle" (32.43–51), which Tagare's note glosses as the Tārā war [translator's gloss]. [disputed]
- **The duel** — Tāraka mocks the gods for putting a child forward ("this child will slay the enemies!") and fights Indra, Viṣṇu and Vīrabhadra in turn before Skanda stops Vīrabhadra and takes him on himself. (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.4.9.13–54; *Skanda Purāṇa* Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa 32.69–71; Kedāra-khaṇḍa 30)
- **Death by the Śakti / spear** — Skanda kills Tāraka with his **śakti** (spear or lance, the **Vel** of Tamil tradition) and restores the worlds to the devas. (*MBh* 9.45.60–64, 13.86.28 *amoghayā śaktyā*; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.4.10.28–30; *Matsya* 160.22–25; *Vāmana* 32.83)
  - *Variant (the wound):* the spear splits Tāraka's **heart** (*Matsya* 160.25; *Vāmana* 32.83; *Skanda* Kaumārikā 32.172–173). In the *Skanda* Kedāra-khaṇḍa, Kārttikeya cuts off his **head** with the śakti (30.39, Tagare tr.).
  - *Variant (Skanda's reluctance, Skanda Purāṇa Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa 32–33):* Viṣṇu urges him to throw the spear and keep his promise because "the sun is about to set" (32.91), but Skanda refuses to strike a devotee of Rudra, and Viṣṇu's argument that a harasser of beings is no true devotee does not move him (32.92–100). He yields only when he sees Tāraka attacking Śiva's chariot with Viṣṇu in pursuit and an ethereal voice tells him that installing a liṅga will expiate the killing (32.141–144); he then vows to kill Tāraka at once (32.154–155). A goddess named Śakti, won by Tāraka's penance, leaves his head because his merit is spent, and only then does the spear pierce him (32.162–173). Afterwards Skanda grieves, asks for an expiation, and installs liṅgas, among them **Kapāleśvara** at the spot called Śakticchidra, and offers libations to Tāraka (33.1–19, 33.52–66). (Tagare tr.) [the Kaumārikā frame is a tīrtha-māhātmya of the Mahī–sea confluence]
- **Krauñca, Mahiṣa, Bāṇa, Pralamba** — the texts arrange Tāraka's allies and the Krauñca mountain around the central slaying in different ways. Cf. [Asura Wars & Roster](../08-beings-and-bestiary/asura-wars-and-roster.md).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 9.45.64–9.45.79):* with the same śakti Skanda kills Tāraka, **Mahiṣa**, Tripāda and Hradodara with their hosts. Then **Bāṇa**, son of Bali, takes refuge in Mount **Krauñca**, and Skanda splits the mountain with the śakti Agni gave him. In the Vana-parvan the newborn splits Krauñca with arrows (3.214.31), and his great foe is Mahiṣa, whose head the śakti splits (3.221.55–66). Tāraka does not appear there.
  - *Variant (Śiva Purāṇa):* Krauñca is named as Tāraka's commander-in-chief (2.3.16.14, Shastri tr.). After Tāraka's death the mountain **Krauñca** itself seeks Skanda's protection from Bāṇa, and Skanda kills **Bāṇa** and then **Pralamba**, survivors of Tāraka's army (2.4.11.1–28). He installs liṅgas there, among them a Kapāleśvara (2.4.11.13–14).
  - *Variant (Skanda Purāṇa Kaumārikā-khaṇḍa 32.177–182):* Bāṇa, son of Bali, harasses the gods from Krauñca. Skanda's śakti pierces the mountain, burning Bāṇa and his hosts, and the hole through which swans fly to Lake Mānasa is said to remain to this day.
  - *Variant (Vāmana Purāṇa 32.84–110):* **Mahiṣa** is Tāraka's brother. When Tāraka falls he flees into Krauñca, and Bāṇa flees into the ocean. Skanda at first will not kill Mahiṣa because he is kin on his mother's side (32.88–93). Indra cites his own killing of his brother Namuci (32.94–97), and Skanda, angered, quarrels with him over who is stronger. They race round Krauñca, and when the mountain says Indra circled it first, Skanda in anger splits Krauñca together with Mahiṣa (32.98–109).
  - *Variant (Kanda Purāṇam, Tamil, Kacciyappa Civācāriyar):* Tārakāsura is the younger brother of **Sūrapadman**, the chief foe, and Murugan's vel pierces Tāraka together with the illusion-mountain Krauñca. Here Murugan arises from six sparks of Śiva, nursed in the Saravaṇa pool and made one by Pārvatī's embrace. [regional; locus not verified: secondary summaries only]
- **Aftermath: the sons' vengeance** — Tāraka's three sons, Tārakākṣa, Vidyunmālī and Kamalākṣa, survive to do their own penance and raise the three flying cities, setting up the separate war that Śiva ends as **Tripurāntaka**. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.4, where the critical edition spells the eldest *Tārākṣa* and lists him with Kamalākṣa and Vidyunmālī; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.1.7–8, Tārakākṣa eldest). See [Tripura-dāhana](tripura-dahana.md).
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 129.3–5):* the Tripura-builders are a different line-up: **Maya** leads, with Vidyunmālī and Tārakākhya (whom 135.79 nonetheless calls a son of Tāraka), rather than three brothers.

## VI. Theological Reading

- **The boon as plot device** — Like the Mohinī ruse of the [Samudra Manthana](samudra-manthana.md) or the Māyāmoha deception of Tripura, Tāraka's boon is a *designed loophole*: the asura's own clause dictates the theophany (Śiva's son, a seven-day infant, or Agni's child) needed to undo him. [scholarly: boon-engineering pattern]
- **Why Śiva must marry** — In the Purāṇic and Kālidāsan versions, the myth makes the supreme ascetic's *householder life* a cosmic necessity. The war cannot be won by force from outside, only by Śiva's own progeny, which amounts to a Śaiva argument for the generative side of the renouncer. In the epic versions the gods instead try to *prevent* Śiva's offspring and get Skanda through Agni and Gaṅgā. [scholarly]
- **The "two clocks"** — Scripturally the event sits in undated primordial deva-time (conventionally [Satya/Tretā]). There is no historical-calendar anchor, and any yuga tag is a frame-internal convention, not a datable epoch. [scholarly: scriptural vs. historical time]
