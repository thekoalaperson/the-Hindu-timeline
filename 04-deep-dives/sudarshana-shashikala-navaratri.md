# Sudarśana and Śaśikalā: the Navarātri Charter-Story

> *Up to [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · master [index](../README.md). The flagship original narrative of the [Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/devi-bhagavata-upapurana.md) (Skandha III, chs. 14–25): an exiled prince of the Solar line, a princess of [Kāśī](../06-sacred-geography/kashi-varanasi.md), and the Goddess's descent onto a battlefield — the text's own origin-legend for royal **Durgā/Navarātri worship** at Benares and [Ayodhyā](../06-sacred-geography/ayodhya.md). Scripturally a tale of the [Sūryavaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md); academically a late-medieval Śākta composition. [scholarly]*

Unlike most of the Devī Bhāgavata, which re-narrates episodes known from older Purāṇas in Śākta key, the **Sudarśana–Śaśikalā story is the text's own signature narrative** — a dynastic romance built to answer a ritual question: *why do kings worship Durgā, and why especially at Vārāṇasī and Ayodhyā?* Vyāsa tells it to King Janamejaya immediately after the Trimūrti's vision of the supreme Goddess, and follows it directly with the **rules of the Navarātra-vrata** (chs. 26–27) and **Rāma's own Navarātri** (chs. 28–30) — making Skandha III the charter of the autumn festival within this Purāṇa. (Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa III.14–30)

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural time** — the story is told by **Vyāsa to Janamejaya** at the Dvāpara–Kali junction, but is set generations earlier among Ikṣvāku (Solar-line) kings of Kosala. The text itself does not name Sudarśana's yuga; the same genealogical names — **Puṣpa → Dhruvasandhi → Sudarśana → Agnivarṇa** — appear in the standard Purāṇic king-lists of the **post-Rāma Kuśa line** (Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.4; Bhāgavata IX.12; cf. Kālidāsa's Raghuvaṃśa 18–19, where Dhruvasaṃdhi is likewise killed by a lion and the child Sudarśana crowned). On that reckoning the story sits in the **late Tretā / Tretā–Dvāpara stretch of Rāma's descendants** — though the Devī Bhāgavata narrates it without fixing it relative to Rāma. [disputed — internal chronology]
- **Academic time** — the Devī Bhāgavata is a composite **Śākta text of roughly the 9th–14th centuries CE** (Hazra: 11th–12th c.; Pintchman: c. 1000–1200 CE); the Sudarśana narrative is generally read as the compilers' **own composition**, modelled on epic exile-and-svayaṃvara templates, designed to root Navarātri and Durgā-pūjā in Solar-dynasty precedent. [scholarly] [late text]

## The succession crisis in Kosala (III.14–15)

- **King Dhruvasandhi of Kosala** — in the Solar line at Ayodhyā reigns **Dhruvasandhi**, son of Puṣpa, a righteous king with two wives: **Manoramā**, daughter of **Vīrasena**, king of Kaliṅga, and **Līlāvatī**, daughter of **Yudhājit**, king of Ujjayinī. Manoramā bears the elder son **Sudarśana**; Līlāvatī soon after bears **Śatrujit**. (Devī Bhāgavata III.14)
- **Death by lion** — Dhruvasandhi, fond of the chase, is **killed by a lion while hunting**, leaving two child heirs and two rival grandfathers. The ministers, led by the elder statesman **Vidalla**, favour the elder boy Sudarśana. (Devī Bhāgavata III.14–15)
- **The war of the grandfathers** — **Yudhājit of Ujjayinī** marches on Ayodhyā to seat his grandson Śatrujit; **Vīrasena of Kaliṅga** comes to defend Sudarśana's right. In the battle that follows **Vīrasena is slain**, and Yudhājit crowns **Śatrujit** king of Kosala. (Devī Bhāgavata III.15)
- **Manoramā's flight** — fearing for her son's life, the widowed **Manoramā escapes from Ayodhyā** with the boy Sudarśana, a maidservant, and the loyal **Vidalla**; crossing the Gaṅgā (stripped of their valuables on the way, in the text by Niṣāda robbers), they take refuge in the forest **hermitage of the sage [Bharadvāja](./bharadvaja.md)**, who grants them protection. (Devī Bhāgavata III.15–16)
- **Yudhājit's pursuit turned back** — hearing where the heir is hidden, Yudhājit comes to the āśrama intending to seize or kill the boy, but is confronted by **Bharadvāja's inviolable word of shelter**; counselled that killing a child in a ṛṣi's refuge would destroy him, he withdraws to Ayodhyā. (Devī Bhāgavata III.16–17)

## The Kāmarāja bīja: a mantra received by accident (III.17–19)

- **"Klīm" from "klība"** — the five-year-old Sudarśana, playing among the hermitage children, hears his mother's attendants call to **Vidalla** by the word **"klība"** ("eunuch"). The child catches only the first syllable and begins repeating **"klīṁ"** — unknowingly the **Kāmarāja bīja**, the great seed-syllable of the Goddess (the *kāma-bīja* of Śākta mantra-śāstra). He recites it constantly, in play and in earnest, without initiation and without knowing its meaning. (Devī Bhāgavata III.17–18)
- **The Devī's grace** — pleased by the boy's artless, unbroken *japa*, the **Bhagavatī appears to Sudarśana**, and grants him her favour: **divine weapons — bow, inexhaustible arrows, and armour** — with the promise of protection and eventual kingship. The theological point is drawn explicitly: the Goddess's bīja saves even one who utters it unknowingly. (Devī Bhāgavata III.18–19)
- **A charter for mantra-theology** — the episode is the narrative proof-text for the Devī Bhāgavata's Tantric claim that the Goddess's **seed-mantras act by their own power**, independent of the reciter's knowledge — the Śākta counterpart of the accidental-grace tales of Vaiṣṇava bhakti (compare [Ajāmila](./ajamila.md)). [scholarly]

## Śaśikalā of Kāśī and the svayaṃvara (III.19–23)

- **The princess who chose an exile** — **Śaśikalā**, the beautiful daughter of **Subāhu, king of Kāśī** (the [Kāśī line](../03-lineages-vamsha/kashi-line-divodasa.md); *not* the Rāmāyaṇa rākṣasa Subāhu), hears of the virtuous forest-prince Sudarśana and fixes her heart on him; the **Devī then appears to her in a dream**, telling her that Sudarśana, favoured by the Goddess, shall be her husband. A visiting brāhmaṇa who has seen the prince at Bharadvāja's āśrama confirms his beauty and worth. (Devī Bhāgavata III.19–21)
- **The three kinds of svayaṃvara** — Subāhu, unaware of his daughter's vow, proclaims a **svayaṃvara** for her hand; the text pauses to classify svayaṃvaras into three kinds — the **icchā** (free-choice), the **paṇa** (set-condition), and the **śaurya** (valour-price) — declaring Śaśikalā's an *icchā-svayaṃvara*. (Devī Bhāgavata III.20)
- **Sudarśana comes to Kāśī** — informed of the svayaṃvara, Sudarśana sets out for Vārāṇasī with his anxious mother's blessing, trusting the Goddess; in the received text a **Niṣāda chief furnishes the exiled prince a chariot** for the journey. Kings from many lands assemble — among them **Yudhājit**, come to back his grandson **Śatrujit**'s claim and to crush the rival heir. (Devī Bhāgavata III.21–22)
- **The kings' resentment** — the assembled monarchs, and Yudhājit loudest of all, protest that a **throneless forest boy** should not stand among them; Yudhājit threatens violence if the Kāśī princess is given to Sudarśana. Subāhu wavers and begs his daughter to choose safety, but **Śaśikalā refuses every other suitor**, citing the Devī's dream-command and declaring that with the Goddess's grace there is nothing to fear. (Devī Bhāgavata III.22–23)
- **The night wedding** — cornered between his daughter's vow and the kings' wrath, **Subāhu marries Śaśikalā to Sudarśana quietly in his own palace**, bypassing the public svayaṃvara, and sends the couple off with a rich dowry — whereupon the humiliated kings resolve to **ambush the pair on the road** out of Kāśī. (Devī Bhāgavata III.23)

## The Devī on the battlefield (III.24–25)

- **The ambush** — Yudhājit and Śatrujit, with allied kings, fall upon the small wedding party as it leaves Vārāṇasī. Sudarśana, hopelessly outnumbered, does not flee: he **meditates on the Goddess with his Kāmarāja bīja**, and Subāhu's forces rally to his side. (Devī Bhāgavata III.24)
- **The theophany** — the **Devī herself appears on the battlefield, riding her lion**, blazing with weapons and terrible beauty. In the rout that follows, **Śatrujit and Yudhājit are slain** — the usurper-line extinguished by the Goddess in person — and the surviving kings, recognizing whom they face, lay down arms, bow, and **hymn the Goddess**, who, gratified, blesses them. (Devī Bhāgavata III.24–25)
- **Charter-moment for the warrior Goddess** — this is the Devī Bhāgavata's demonstration that the Goddess of the [Devī Māhātmya's cosmic battles](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md) also intervenes in **human dynastic war** for her devotee — the theological bridge from myth to royal cult. [scholarly]

## The charter: Durgā established at Vārāṇasī and Ayodhyā (III.25)

- **Subāhu's boon — the Goddess stays in Kāśī** — King Subāhu worships the manifest Devī and begs her to **remain forever in Vārāṇasī as its guardian**; she consents, and the text presents this as the origin of the great **Durgā Devī of Kāśī**, with Subāhu instituting her shrine and worship in the city. Later tradition identifies this with the **Durgā (Kuṣmāṇḍā) temple complex of Benares**, though the Purāṇa itself gives no architectural detail. (Devī Bhāgavata III.25) [regional — the temple identification]
- **Sudarśana restored** — with Śatrujit dead, the ministers and people of Kosala come to Sudarśana and **escort him to Ayodhyā as king**; he honours his mother Manoramā, forgives the partisans of the usurpation, and rules righteously by the Goddess's grace. (Devī Bhāgavata III.25)
- **Durgā-pūjā instituted at Ayodhyā** — at the Devī's command Sudarśana **establishes the worship of the Goddess in Ayodhyā and throughout Kosala** — images installed, festivals ordained — so that (in the text's own claim) **Devī-worship spread among kings and peoples from these two royal foundations** at Benares and Ayodhyā. This is the Purāṇa's aetiology of royal Śākta cult in the Gangetic heartland. (Devī Bhāgavata III.25–26)

## The Navarātra-vidhi (III.26–27)

- **The rules of the nine nights** — prompted by Janamejaya, Vyāsa lays out the **Navarātra-vrata**: to be kept in the two junction-months of **Caitra (spring) and Āśvina (autumn)** — seasons the text singles out as dangerous "fangs of Yama" (times of disease and death), which the vrata neutralizes; with fasting or single meals, the installed jar (*ghaṭa-sthāpana*), daily pūjā, **homa**, and recitation. (Devī Bhāgavata III.26)
- **Kumārī-pūjā** — the vidhi's centrepiece is the **worship of virgin girls** (ages two to ten), each year of age bearing a distinct name and fruit — **Kumārikā, Trimūrti, Kalyāṇī, Rohiṇī, Kālikā, Caṇḍikā, Śāmbhavī, Durgā, Subhadrā** — with rules on how many to feed and honour daily. (Devī Bhāgavata III.26–27)
- **Open to all** — the text insists the vrata is available to **rich and poor alike**, scaling the offerings to means, and promises kingdoms, sons, and rescue from calamity as its fruits — a deliberately **royal-and-popular** festival charter. (Devī Bhāgavata III.27)
- Compare the calendar frame of the twice-yearly Navarātra in [the Hindu calendar & pañcāṅga](../00-time-and-cosmology/hindu-calendar-and-panchanga.md) and the goddess-roster of [Devī forms & Navadurgā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-forms-and-navadurga.md).

## Rāma's Navarātri (III.28–30)

- **The precedent of precedents** — asked who else kept the vrata, Vyāsa relates that **Rāma himself performed the Navarātra**. After Sītā's abduction ([Āraṇya-kāṇḍa](./ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md)), the grieving Rāma is visited by **[Nārada](./narada.md)**, who instructs him that only the Goddess's grace will win the war against Rāvaṇa, and prescribes the **autumn (Āśvina) Navarātra-vrata**. (Devī Bhāgavata III.28–29)
- **The vrata and the boon** — Rāma performs the nine-night worship with Nārada officiating; the **Devī appears, pleased**, declares Rāma an aṃśa of Nārāyaṇa born to slay Rāvaṇa, and grants the boon of **victory, the recovery of Sītā, and long rule** — after which Rāma marches to war. The text thereby ties **Vijayadaśamī** to Rāma's Goddess-granted victory. (Devī Bhāgavata III.30)
  - *Variant (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa):* the epic itself contains **no Navarātri episode**; Rāma's Goddess-worship before the war is absent from Vālmīki and is a Śākta-era addition. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Kālikā Purāṇa / Bṛhaddharma Purāṇa, Bengal):* in the eastern tradition it is **Brahmā (or Rāma through Brahmā) who "untimely awakens" the Goddess** in autumn — the **akāla-bodhana** — since the gods sleep in that half-year; this is the charter cited for Bengali autumn Durgā-pūjā, elaborated in the [Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ](./ramayana/recensions/krittivasi-ramayan.md), where Rāma offers 108 lotuses and his own eye. [regional]
  - *Variant (emphasis):* the Devī Bhāgavata does not use the akāla-bodhana frame — its autumn rite is *already ordained* (via Sudarśana) and Rāma simply performs it; Bengali tradition instead makes Rāma's autumn worship an *exceptional untimely* awakening. The two aetiologies circulate in parallel. [scholarly]

## Reading the charter [scholarly]

- **A festival given a royal pedigree** — scholars (notably C. Mackenzie Brown and Tracy Pintchman on the Devī Bhāgavata) read Skandha III as a **composed unit**: original narrative (Sudarśana), ritual code (Navarātra-vidhi), and epic warrant (Rāma) — the sequence functioning to legitimize **royal Navarātri/Durgā-pūjā** as ancient Solar-dynasty practice. [scholarly]
- **Geography is the argument** — anchoring the cult at **Vārāṇasī** (Subāhu) and **Ayodhyā** (Sudarśana) plants the Goddess in the two most prestigious sacred-royal cities of the Gangetic plain, complementing the Purāṇa's pīṭha-geography elsewhere (cf. the [Devī Gītā](./devi-gita.md)'s pīṭha lists and the [51 Śakti-pīṭhas](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md)). [scholarly]
- **Names recycled from the king-lists** — the borrowing of Dhruvasandhi/Sudarśana from the post-Rāma Ikṣvāku genealogies gives the fiction genealogical verisimilitude, though it leaves the internal chronology (a Navarātri charter seemingly *before* Rāma, using names listed *after* him) unresolved — a seam the text does not address. [scholarly] [disputed — internal chronology]
