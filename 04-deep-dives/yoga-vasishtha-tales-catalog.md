# Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha — Catalog of Tales

> Where this sits: a **prakaraṇa-keyed index** of the nested parables (*upākhyānas*) of the *Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* (Mahā-Rāmāyaṇa) — six prakaraṇas, ~29,000 verses [disputed], framed in [Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) (sage Vasiṣṭha instructs a despondent young Rāma at Daśaratha's court), but composed in medieval Kashmir, c. 950 CE as the *Mokṣopāya* [scholarly] [late text].
> The canonical **text profile** (structure, doctrine, recensions, dating debate) is [90-literature-corpus/yoga-vasishtha.md](../90-literature-corpus/yoga-vasishtha.md); this node catalogs the *stories*. Back to the [deep-dives index](README.md) · [master index](../README.md).

The Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha teaches almost entirely **by parable**: each doctrine gets a tale, and tales nest inside tales like the mind-made worlds they describe. Where a tale **has a dedicated file** the entry is a pointer; the rest are catalogued here as their canonical home. Sarga (chapter) numbers follow the Nirṇaya Sāgara vulgate; the *Laghu-Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* abridgement and the Kashmiri *Mokṣopāya* differ in extent and numbering, so citations are given at prakaraṇa level except where the vulgate reference is well established. [scholarly]

---

## The frame (Vairāgya & Mumukṣu-vyavahāra, books I–II)

- **Rāma's despondency and Vasiṣṭha's discourse** — Rāma, about sixteen and returned from pilgrimage, falls into radical dispassion; when [Viśvāmitra](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) comes to Daśaratha's court to ask for him (the same juncture the [Bāla Kāṇḍa](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md) narrates), the sages judge his *vairāgya* the perfect vessel, and **Vasiṣṭha** teaches him over successive days before the assembly — every tale below is told inside this sitting. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha I–II)
- **The transmission frame** — the Brahmin **Sutīkṣṇa** questions **Agastya** (works or knowledge? — both, like a bird's two wings); Vālmīki recounts the dialogue to **Bharadvāja** at Brahmā's command. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha I.1–2)
- **Self-effort over fate** — book II's signature teaching, that *daiva* (fate) is nothing but the ripened momentum of one's own prior *pauruṣa* (effort), frames every story of a mind remaking its world. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha II)

## Utpatti-prakaraṇa (book III) — how the world-appearance arises

- **Ākāśaja, the "space-born" brahmin** — Death (Kāla) finds he cannot seize the contemplative brahmin Ākāśaja, and is told the reason: the sage is pure space-consciousness with no ego-knot for Death to grip — the book's opening statement that only mind is bindable. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha III, early sargas)
- **Queen Līlā's nested worlds** — Līlā, granted mind-borne travel by **Sarasvatī**, discovers her dead husband King Padma's whole universe sits inside the hut of a village brahmin who merely *wished* kingship, and that Padma has already died onward into a further world as King **Vidūratha** — worlds inside worlds, each with its own time-rate, until the jīva returns and the corpse revives. The corpus's most elaborate proof that creation is mental projection. **Kept standalone in the gap-list; until a dedicated file exists, the fullest treatment in this corpus is in the [text profile](../90-literature-corpus/yoga-vasishtha.md).** (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha III, Utpatti; commonly cited as III.15–60 [disputed])
- **Karkaṭī / Viśūcikā, the needle-demoness** — the Himālayan rākṣasī **Karkaṭī** (of the [rākṣasa](../08-beings-and-bestiary/rakshasas.md) race), tormented by hunger, wins from Brahmā the form of an iron **needle (sūcikā)** — the piercing colic-sickness **Viśūcikā** — to enter and consume creatures invisibly; Brahmā simultaneously grants beings a protective counter-mantra. Wearied by formless misery, she repents, regains her mountainous body by renewed tapas — now Self-knowing — and tests a night-travelling **king and his minister** with a chain of riddles on the Ātman ("what is the atom that holds worlds?"); satisfied, she becomes the land's protectress, eating only condemned criminals. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha III.68–81)
- **The ten sons of Indu (the Aindavas)** — ten brahmin brothers, sons of **Indu**, resolve in meditation "I am Brahmā" — and each *becomes* a world-creating Brahmā, so that ten complete cosmoses arise from ten convictions; even the "real" Brahmā cannot revoke what sustained imagination has established. The text's boldest statement that a creator-god is a stabilized thought. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha III, Utpatti)
- **Indra and Ahalyā (the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha pair)** — an adulterous couple named **Indra and Ahalyā** remain blissfully unbroken under every punishment King Indradyumna inflicts, because their minds, wholly absorbed in each other, register no body-pain — mind's conviction outweighs the body's world. *Not* the Vedic-epic tale of Gautama's wife: see [Gautama & Ahalyā](gautama-ahalya.md) for that separate, older story. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha III, Utpatti)
- **The three non-existent princes** — a nurse's tale to a child: "In a city that never existed lived three princes — two were never born and the third was never even conceived…"; they bathe in unreal rivers and eat future-cooked food. The self-mocking emblem of how mind furnishes a vivid world out of sheer non-existence and believes it. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha III, Utpatti)

## Sthiti-prakaraṇa (book IV) — how the appearance persists

- **Śukra's mind-made lifetimes** — young **Śukra**, son of Bhṛgu, glimpses an apsarā and follows her *in mind* to Indra's heaven; through purely mental experience he lives enjoyment there and then a whole chain of successive rebirths, while his actual body withers on the hillside. Bhṛgu rises in fury against **Kāla** (Time), who calmly displays the entire chain of imagined lives and the law that mind alone transmigrates; the jīva is recalled, the husk revives — and Śukra goes on to become the asuras' preceptor. Cross-link: [Śukra as guru of the asuras](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/the-gurus-brihaspati-and-shukra.md). (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha IV.5–17)
- **Dāma, Vyāla, and Kaṭa → Bhīma, Bhāsa, and Dṛḍha** — three demons conjured by the asura-magician **Śambara** are invincible precisely while **ego-less** (no "I" to fear for); once battle breeds self-identity and death-fear, the gods rout them, and they fall into miserable rebirths (in the sequel Śambara conjures three *knowing* demons — **Bhīma, Bhāsa, Dṛḍha** — who fight without ego-involvement and remain untouched, withdrawing to liberation when the work is done). Egohood itself is the vulnerability. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha IV.25–34)
- **Dāśūra, the tree-dwelling ascetic** — the over-scrupulous ascetic **Dāśūra**, finding no spot on earth pure enough, dwells atop a kadamba tree; taught that purity is of mind, not place, he instructs his son with the parable of a fanciful king (**Khoṭṭha**, "the unreal one") whose whole kingdom is imagination — as is everyone's. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha IV, Sthiti)
- **Kaca's question** — **Kaca**, Bṛhaspati's son, asks how to renounce *everything*; the answer — renounce the "I"-thought, the seed of all having — is the prakaraṇa-cycle's briefest tale. Placement varies between books across editions. [disputed] (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha, vulgate)

## Upaśama-prakaraṇa (book V) — quiescence of mind

- **Janaka and the siddhas' song** — King **Janaka** of Videha overhears siddhas singing of the Self, is shaken into self-inquiry, passes through despair to poised knowledge, and continues ruling — the text's first full portrait of the **jīvanmukta king**, prefiguring Rāma's own resolution. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha V, Upaśama)
- **Puṇya and Pāvana** — two sage-brothers: when their parents die, younger **Pāvana** grieves inconsolably; the liberated elder **Puṇya** heals him by recalling the *thousands* of mothers, fathers, sons and wives each of them has had across forgotten births — whom shall we mourn, and whom omit? Grief dissolves in the long view of transmigration. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha V.18–20)
- **Bali's inquiry** — the asura emperor **Bali**, sated with world-enjoyment, recalls his father Virocana's teaching (received from Śukra) that consciousness alone is real, and awakens by his own inquiry; Vasiṣṭha uses him as the exemplar that even mid-reign, mid-pleasure, inquiry suffices. For the Vāmana-and-three-steps cycle see [Vāmana & Bali](vamana-bali.md). (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha V, Upaśama)
- **Prahlāda's self-awakening (the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha version)** — here **Prahlāda** attains realization *by his own inquiry*, contemplating Viṣṇu until he sees the Self as Viṣṇu, and sinks into nirvikalpa absorption so deep the asura realm falls kingless into disorder — until **Viṣṇu himself wakes him** and instructs him to rule as a jīvanmukta until the cosmic dissolution. A strikingly different arc from the Purāṇic boy-devotee persecuted by his father — see [Prahlāda & Narasiṃha](prahlada-narasimha.md); the traditions are distinct and are not merged here. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha V, Upaśama)
- **Gādhi's caṇḍāla illusion** — the brahmin **Gādhi**, granted a vision of Viṣṇu's māyā, enters water and instantly lives an entire other life: dead, reborn as the **caṇḍāla Katañja**, by chance made **king of the Kīra country**, exposed, disgraced, self-immolated — then surfaces where only moments have passed, and later finds "evidence" of that life in the world: māyā authenticates itself. *Distinct figure-tradition:* the genealogical **Gādhi**, father of Viśvāmitra, has his own tale at [Gādhi & the caru-swap](../03-lineages-vamsha/gadhi-and-the-caru-swap.md); whether the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha intends the same person is not stated in the text. [disputed] (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha V.44–49)
- **Uddālaka's meditation** — the young sage **Uddālaka** talks himself step by step out of distraction in a celebrated soliloquy ("O mind, whose are you really?"), masters absorption, refuses the celestial fruits that samādhi offers, and abides as a jīvanmukta — the corpus's fullest *practice* narrative, a manual of meditation in story form. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha V, Upaśama)
- **Suraghu and Parīgha** — **Suraghu**, chief of the Himālayan **Kirāta** hill-tribe, agonized that a king's justice must punish and reward, is led by the sage **Māṇḍavya** to self-inquiry and equanimity; later he and the ascetic-envoy **Parīgha** compare states and agree that liberation is neither trance nor activity but unbroken knowing in the midst of either. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha V, Upaśama)
- **Vītahavya's centuries-sleeping body** — the sage **Vītahavya**, disgusted with the body, quits it in meditation; his frame lies preserved in a cave through long ages while consciousness roams; re-entering it (retrieved by yogic means), he demonstrates the jīvanmukta's sovereign indifference to embodiment or disembodiment. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha V, Upaśama, closing sargas)
- **Bhagīratha, the beggar-king who brought the Gaṅgā** — in the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha telling, **Bhagīratha** first *renounces utterly* — gives the kingdom away, begs in his own former capital unrecognized, attains knowledge under the teacher **Tritala** — and only then, desireless, re-accepts kingship and performs the tapas that brings **Gaṅgā** to earth for his ancestors' redemption: only the emptied doer can accomplish the impossible. Cross-frame: the corpus places the Purāṇic-epic Gaṅgā-descent under [Satya Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/01-satya-yuga.md). (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha V–VI, Upaśama/Nirvāṇa; placement varies by recension [disputed])

## Nirvāṇa-prakaraṇa, Pūrvārdha (book VI.1) — liberation, first half

- **Bhuśuṇḍa, the immortal crow** — Vasiṣṭha ascends to a peak of **Meru** to visit **Bhuśuṇḍa**, an ancient crow nested on the **kalpa-taru**: longest-lived of embodied beings, he remembers **repeated kalpas and pralayas** — churnings of the ocean recurring with different outcomes, world-configurations differing cycle to cycle — and survives cosmic fire and flood by **prāṇa-contemplation** (witnessing the prāṇa–apāna movement), resting in the space of consciousness while creation burns. The corpus's chief narrative of **longevity through witness-stillness** and its principal prāṇa-vidyā teaching. Note: he is **absent from the classical eight-name roster** of [the Chiranjīvis](the-chiranjivis.md), which admits his devotional counterpart **Kākabhuśuṇḍi** only in extended folk lists — this entry is his canonical home in the corpus. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.1.14–28)
  - *Parallel (Rāmcaritmānas):* Tulsīdās's crow-narrator **Kākabhuśuṇḍi**, who retells the Rāma-kathā to Garuḍa and likewise remembers many kalpas, is this figure's bhakti transform — see [Rāmcaritmānas](ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md). [late text]
  - *Parallel (pralaya-witness):* compare [Mārkaṇḍeya](markandeya.md), the Purāṇic deathless sage who beholds the dissolution.
- **Ikṣvāku's awakening** — Rāma's own ancestor **Ikṣvāku**, first Solar king, wonders what lies beyond decay and is taught by **Manu** the vision of the one consciousness and the **seven bhūmikās** (stages of knowledge) — knowledge runs in Rāma's blood. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.1, Pūrvārdha)
- **The story of Arjuna foretold** — Vasiṣṭha *prophesies* to Rāma that in Dvāpara Yuga Viṣṇu will descend as **Kṛṣṇa** and his companion **Arjuna** will receive on a battlefield the same non-dual teaching (verses closely echoing the Bhagavad-Gītā appear here); a deliberate internal cross-reference of the two great battlefield-instructions. Cross-link: [Bhagavad-Gītā](bhagavad-gita.md). [late text] (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.1, Pūrvārdha)
- **Cūḍālā and Śikhidhvaja** — the queen-turned-guru who, disguised as the sage **Kumbha**, dismantles her forest-renunciate husband's attachment to renunciation itself, tests him with conjured temptations, and rules beside him as jīvanmukta queen. → **[has file]** [chudala-shikhidhvaja.md](chudala-shikhidhvaja.md); also indexed among the [lesser Gītās](lesser-gitas-catalog.md) milieu of woman-guru dialogues. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.1, Pūrvārdha)

## Nirvāṇa-prakaraṇa, Uttarārdha (book VI.2) — liberation, second half

- **The wood-apple (bilva) and the rock** — twin cosmological parables: the universe is like the undecaying **bilva fruit** whose marrow is consciousness, all worlds mere flavor in it; and within a plain **rock (śilā)** the sage in meditation perceives complete creations — solidity itself contains worlds, because "world" is an idea in the seeming-solid mass of consciousness. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.2, Uttarārdha)
- **Vipaścit's endless quest** — King **Vipaścit**, resolving to find the *edge* of the world, divides into **four bodies** marching to the four horizons through fire, ocean and successive world-shells; lifetimes of exploration find no boundary — appearance has no outside — and one of him, long after, is brought to Rāma's assembly **as a deer**, disenchanted back into human form: a witness examined inside the very discourse we are reading. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.2, Uttarārdha)
- **The monk's dream (the bhikṣu's hundred lives)** — a meditating mendicant imagines being **Jīvaṭa**; Jīvaṭa dreams he is a brahmin; the brahmin dreams he is a prince; the chain runs on through king, swan, god, until whole cascades of dreamers-within-dreamers are live at once — and Vasiṣṭha adds that this monk exists *now*, in a certain monastery, and any inquirer might go and see him: the dream-regress is pointedly pinned to the listener's "waking" world. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.2, Uttarārdha)
- **The Vetāla's riddles** — a **vetāla** (corpse-spirit) seizes a king and offers survival for answers to world-riddles (whose atom holds the universes? what wind carries worlds like dust?); the king answers from Self-knowledge — consciousness is that atom — and the satisfied vetāla releases him and fasts to quiescence. A jñāna-counterpart to the folk *Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati* frame. [folk] (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.2, Uttarārdha)
- **Kuṇḍadanta and the steadfast householder** — the seeker **Kuṇḍadanta** brings to the assembly his puzzle about a tapasvin whose worldly boons all came true and yet who remained unbound; the resolution — that the knower may hold any destiny without being touched — is among the final tales before Rāma's own awakening. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.2, Uttarārdha, closing sargas)
- **Rāma's enlightenment and the charter of jīvanmukti** — Rāma attains direct repose in the Self, is roused from absorption by the assembly, and **chooses to act** — to rule, and in time to fight — while inwardly free; the entire Rāmāyaṇa to come is thereby recast as the career of a jīvanmukta. (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha VI.2, final sargas)
