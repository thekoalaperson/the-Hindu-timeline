# Kāliya-mardana

> *Where this sits: the iconic boyhood līlā of [Kṛṣṇa](./krishna-lila.md) in Vṛndāvana — the subduing of the poison-serpent **Kāliya** in the [Yamunā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/yamuna-and-river-goddesses.md), set in the closing [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) of our 28th Mahāyuga. One of the most-depicted scenes in all Indian art.*
>

The **Kāliya-mardana** ("the crushing of Kāliya"; also **Kāliya-damana**, "the subduing of Kāliya," and **Kāliya-nartana**, "the dance on Kāliya") is the episode in which the boy Kṛṣṇa cleanses a venom-poisoned pool of the river Yamunā by leaping into it, mastering the many-hooded nāga Kāliya, and dancing in triumph upon his hoods. It is told most fully in the **[Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/bhagavata-purana-skandha-map.md) 10.16–17**, with the older **Harivaṃśa** (Viṣṇu Parva) and **Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Book 5** as parallels. For the wider childhood arc see [Kṛṣṇa Bālya-līlā](./krishna-balya-lila.md); for Kāliya as a nāga see [Nāgas & Serpents](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md).

---

## Why Kāliya was in the Yamunā

- **Kāliya's flight from Garuḍa** — Kāliya, a great nāga, originally dwelt in **Ramaṇaka Dvīpa** (the serpents' ocean-island home), but fled the eagle **Garuḍa** — the hereditary devourer of nāgas — and took refuge in a deep pool (*hrada*) of the Yamunā at Vṛndāvana, where Garuḍa was barred from coming (Bhāgavata X.16; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5; Harivaṃśa).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata X.16):* the bar on Garuḍa is explained by a curse — the sage **Saubhari**, fishing-loving and disturbed by Garuḍa preying on the river's fish, cursed the eagle that if he ever ate a fish in that stretch of the Yamunā he would die; knowing this, Kāliya alone of the nāgas dared shelter there. [scholarly]
- **The poisoned pool** — Kāliya's venom boiled the water so that birds flying overhead fell dead, trees on the bank were scorched, and the foam and fumes killed cattle and people who touched it; one whole reach of the Yamunā became a lake of death (Bhāgavata X.16; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5).

## The līlā in the pool

- **The poisoned cows and cowherds** — Krishna, herding with the *gopas*, finds cattle and boys who drank or touched the tainted water lying dead on the bank; he revives them with his nectarean glance (Bhāgavata X.15–16). [In the Bhāgavata this death-and-revival immediately precedes and motivates the descent into the pool.]
- **The leap from the kadamba tree** — Resolving to purge the river, Krishna tightens his garment, climbs a tall **kadamba** tree on the bank (the one tree Garuḍa's nectar-drops had kept alive), and **leaps far out into the venomous pool**, churning it so that it overflows its banks (Bhāgavata X.16; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5; Harivaṃśa).
- **Kāliya's attack** — Roused, Kāliya rushes up and **coils his whole length around Krishna**, binding him; the boy appears motionless and ensnared, and the watching cowherds, cows, parents (Nanda and Yaśodā) and gopīs swoon in terror on the bank, ready to enter the water and die with him (Bhāgavata X.16).
- **Balarāma's reassurance** — Krishna's brother **Balarāma**, knowing his divine nature, restrains the others; Krishna then suddenly **expands his body** so that Kāliya must release his grip (Bhāgavata X.16).
- **The dance upon the hoods (Kāliya-nartana)** — Krishna springs up and **mounts the broad heads of the serpent**, and treading them like a stage **dances**, his lotus feet pressing down each rearing hood as it lifts; the weight of the Lord who bears the universe crushes Kāliya's many hoods until the serpent vomits blood and venom and is broken (Bhāgavata X.16; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5; Harivaṃśa). This *dance* (*tāṇḍava*-like, accompanied in the Bhāgavata by the gods drumming and showering flowers from the sky) is the defining image of the episode.

## The pleas of the Nāga-wives

- **The Nāga-patnīs' prayer** — Seeing their lord trampled near death, Kāliya's **wives (nāga-patnīs / nāga-kanyās)** come up, bow to Krishna, and offer one of the great hymns of surrender in the Bhāgavata — praising him as the Supreme, declaring Kāliya's punishment to be in truth his **grace**, and begging mercy for their husband (Bhāgavata X.16, the *Nāga-patnī-stuti*).
- **Kāliya's own submission** — Battered into recognition, Kāliya himself acknowledges Krishna's lordship and surrenders (Bhāgavata X.16).
- **The banishment to Ramaṇaka** — Krishna spares him on the wives' plea and **orders him to leave the Yamunā with all his kin and return to Ramaṇaka Dvīpa** in the ocean; he promises Kāliya that Garuḍa, seeing on the serpent's hoods the **mark of Krishna's feet**, will no longer attack him (Bhāgavata X.16–17; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5; Harivaṃśa).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa):* the older account is terser and more martial — Krishna subdues and *expels* Kāliya from the river to protect Vraja; the elaborated *Nāga-patnī-stuti* and the bhakti-laden theophany are the Bhāgavata's later devotional overlay. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (regional/Vaiṣṇava):* the **footprint-mark** on Kāliya's hood is often the narrative reason that nāgas thereafter bear an auspicious sign and are worshipped — a motif elaborated in temple lore and folk retelling. [regional]
- **The cleansed river** — Kāliya departs; the Yamunā's waters become sweet and safe again, and Vraja is delivered (Bhāgavata X.17; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5).

## Sequel — the forest fire

- **Davāgni / the swallowing of the forest fire** — In the Bhāgavata the Kāliya episode is immediately followed by a wildfire (*dāvāgni*) that traps the cowherds and cattle sleeping by the now-purified river; Krishna **swallows the blaze** and saves them (Bhāgavata X.17). The two are narrated as a pair — the danger from *water* (poison) answered by the danger from *fire* — and frequently catalogued together in the bālya-līlā. [textual ordering]

## Iconography & afterlife

- **Vīrya and vātsalya together** — The scene fuses two rasas the tradition prizes: the **heroic** (*vīrya*) — a child mastering a cosmic serpent — and the **tender** (*vātsalya*) — the same child the swooning mother fears to lose. The standard murti shows a small Krishna dancing on a multi-hooded cobra, one foot raised, holding a hood or his own raised hand in *abhaya*, the Nāga-patnīs folded in prayer below. [scholarly]
- **Temple and bronze art** — **Kāliya-Kṛṣṇa / Kāliya-mardana** (Tamil: *Kāḷinga-nartana*) is among the most reproduced subjects of South Indian Chola and later **bronze sculpture**, and a fixture of temple relief, miniature painting (Rajput, Pahari, Mewar) and Mughal-era illustration of the Bhāgavata. [scholarly]
- **Dance and performance** — The episode is a staple of **Bharatanāṭyam, Kuchipudi, Kathak and Odissi** repertoire as the *Kāḷinga-nartana / Kāliya-daman* item, and of **Rāslīlā** and **Kṛṣṇa-līlā** folk theatre in the Braj region. [scholarly]
- **Festival** — At **Vārāṇasī** the dance is re-enacted as **Nāg Nathaiyā** (Nāga-naṭhaiyā), when a boy playing Krishna leaps from a kadamba tree into the Ganges onto an effigy of Kāliya, on Kārttika Kṛṣṇa Caturthī. [regional]

## Two clocks

- **Scriptural time:** the līlā falls in Kṛṣṇa's boyhood in the closing **[Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md)** of the current 28th Mahāyuga (traditionally before the onset of Kali Yuga, **3102 BCE**) — placed in the same Vṛndāvana phase as the Govardhana and rāsa episodes.
- **Text-critical time:** the **oldest connected form is the Harivaṃśa** (core c. 1st–2nd c. BCE, growing into the early CE centuries), restated in the **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** (~5th c. CE), and most elaborated and devotionalised in the **Bhāgavata** (~6th–10th c. CE) — which alone develops the Nāga-patnī-stuti, the Saubhari-curse rationale, and the theophanic framing. The skeleton — Kāliya in the Yamunā, the poison, the leap, the dance, the sparing and banishment to Ramaṇaka — is constant across all three. [scholarly]

**Primary texts**
- *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* (Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam), **Canto X.16–17** — the poisoned pool and Saubhari's curse (16), Kāliya's coiling and the dance on the hoods (16), the *Nāga-patnī-stuti* and the banishment to Ramaṇaka (16–17), and the sequel forest-fire (17).
