# Kāliya-mardana

> *Where this sits: the iconic boyhood līlā of [Kṛṣṇa](./krishna-lila.md) in Vṛndāvana — the subduing of the poison-serpent **Kāliya** in the [Yamunā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/yamuna-and-river-goddesses.md), set in the closing [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) of our 28th Mahāyuga. One of the most-depicted scenes in all Indian art.*
>

The **Kāliya-mardana** ("the crushing of Kāliya"; also **Kāliya-damana**, "the subduing of Kāliya," and **Kāliya-nartana**, "the dance on Kāliya") is the episode in which the boy Kṛṣṇa cleanses a venom-poisoned pool of the river Yamunā by leaping into it, mastering the many-hooded nāga Kāliya, and dancing in triumph upon his hoods. It is told most fully in the **[Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/bhagavata-purana-skandha-map.md) 10.15.47–10.17.25**, with the older **[Harivaṃśa](./harivamsha.md)** (critical edition chs. 55–56) and **Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7** as parallels. The three agree on the skeleton but differ in real details (who attacks, how Kṛṣṇa gets free, Balarāma's part, the number of hoods, where Kāliya is sent), catalogued below. Harivaṃśa verse numbers follow the critical text (GRETIL); the vulgate numbering differs. For the wider childhood arc see [Kṛṣṇa Bālya-līlā](./krishna-balya-lila.md); for Kāliya as a nāga see [Nāgas & Serpents](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md).

## Why Kāliya was in the Yamunā

- **Kāliya, son of Kadrū** — Kāliya is one of the nāga sons of **Kadrū**, named in the epic's list of chief serpents (Mahābhārata 1.31.6, crit. ed.); the Bhāgavata calls him *kādraveya* and *kadru-suta* (Bhāgavata 10.17.4, 10.17.7). In the Harivaṃśa, Kaṃsa names Kāliya among the allies he will set to work against the gods' partisans, with Keśin, Pralamba, Dhenuka, Ariṣṭa and Pūtanā (Harivaṃśa 46.25); the Kāliya episode itself makes no link to Kaṃsa.
- **Kāliya's flight from Garuḍa** — Kāliya fled his ocean home for fear of the eagle **Garuḍa**, the hereditary devourer of nāgas, and took refuge in a deep pool (*hrada*) of the Yamunā near Vraja (Bhāgavata 10.16.63, 10.17.1–8; Harivaṃśa 55.48–49). The Harivaṃśa already has Viṣṇu, before his descent, name among the foes he knows on earth "Kāliya, who keeps to a pool of the Yamunā out of fear of Vainateya [Garuḍa]" (*kāliyaṃ cāpi jānāmi yamunāhradagocaram / vainateyabhayāt*, Harivaṃśa 45.7). The Bhāgavata names that home **Ramaṇaka Dvīpa**, "the abode of the nāgas" (*nāgālayaṃ ramaṇakam*, 10.17.1; 10.16.63); the Harivaṃśa says only that he left his dwelling "in the ocean" (*utsṛjya sāgare vāsam*, 55.49).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7):* gives no backstory at all. Kṛṣṇa simply resolves that the serpent, once conquered, "should leave and go to the ocean" (*payonidhim*, 5.7.6); the feud with Garuḍa surfaces only in Kṛṣṇa's closing promise (5.7.78).
- **The quarrel over Garuḍa's tribute** — In the Bhāgavata's fuller account the nāgas each month left a "tree-offering" (*vānaspatyo baliḥ*; "at the base of a tree" in the BBT rendering [translator's gloss]) for Garuḍa, so that he would spare them; Kāliya, drunk on the power of his venom, ate Garuḍa's portion himself. Garuḍa swooped on him, Kāliya bit him, and Garuḍa struck him down with his **left golden wing**; the stricken serpent fled into the Yamunā pool, which Garuḍa could not enter (Bhāgavata 10.17.2–8). [Bhāgavata only]
  - *Variant (Śrīdhara Svāmin's commentary on Bhāgavata 10.17.2, as reported in the BBT purport):* *upahāryaiḥ sarpa-janaiḥ* can also be read as "by the serpent-folk who were liable to be eaten" — humans under the serpents' sway paid the monthly tribute to the nāgas, who passed a share on to Garuḍa. [late text]
- **Saubhari's curse** — Why Garuḍa could not follow: once, in that very pool, the hungry Garuḍa seized a fish although the sage **Saubhari** forbade it; pitying the fish grieving for their slain leader, Saubhari cursed that if Garuḍa ever entered there and ate fish he would die at once. Kāliya alone of the serpents knew this, and so lived there (Bhāgavata 10.17.9–12). The BBT translation adds that Saubhari was "meditating there within the water" [translator's gloss; cf. Saubhari's austerities submerged in the Yamunā at Bhāgavata 9.6.39]. [Bhāgavata only]
- **The poisoned pool** — Kāliya's venom-fire made the water boil, so that birds flying overhead fell into it, and the spray-laden wind killed every living thing, moving or rooted, on the bank (Bhāgavata 10.16.4–5); the Viṣṇu Purāṇa adds trees on the bank scorched and birds burnt by the wind-blown spray (5.7.3–4), so that neither men nor thirsty cattle could use the river (5.7.7). The Harivaṃśa adds that the pool is a *yojana* across and impassable even to the gods, that the birds of the air cannot cross it and even grass that falls on it flares up, that it lies a *krośa* north of the cowherd camp (reading *vrajasya* for the GRETIL text's *vajrasya*, 55.46), and that the surrounding forest is guarded by Kāliya's serpent-ministers (Harivaṃśa 55.40–46, 55.52).

## The līlā in the pool

- **The poisoned cows and cowherds** — Kṛṣṇa goes to the Kālindī (Yamunā) with his friends **but without Balarāma** (*rāmam ṛte*, Bhāgavata 10.15.47; also Harivaṃśa 55.1; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.1). Parched by the summer heat, the cows and cowherd boys drink the poisoned water and fall lifeless on the bank; Kṛṣṇa revives them with his nectar-raining glance (*īkṣayāmṛta-varṣiṇyā*, Bhāgavata 10.15.48–52). [In the Bhāgavata this death-and-revival immediately precedes and motivates the descent into the pool (10.16.1); it is absent from the Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Harivaṃśa.]
- **The leap from the kadamba tree** — Resolving to purge the river, Kṛṣṇa tightens his garment, climbs a **kadamba** tree on the bank and **leaps into the venomous pool**, churning it so that it overflows (Bhāgavata 10.16.6–7; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.10–12; Harivaṃśa 55.57–56.3). In the Bhāgavata the tree is "very tall" (*ati-tuṅgam*), he slaps his arms (*āsphoṭya*) before leaping, and the pool overflows for a hundred bow-lengths (10.16.6–7). In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa the splashed poison-water sets the bankside trees ablaze (5.7.13); in the Harivaṃśa Kāliya's angry fire burns them to ash (56.10).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.14):* the arm-slap comes after the leap: Kṛṣṇa slaps his arm in the serpent's pool, and the sound of it brings the serpent-king up.
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa appendix):* a passage the critical edition relegates to the apparatus says that through the touch of Kṛṣṇa's foot the kadamba would become long-lived, bearing flowers and fruit and worshipped by Kṛṣṇa's devotees, and that it "still shines today" (star passage after Harivaṃśa 55.57). [interpolation]
  - *Variant (later Vaiṣṇava commentary):* the kadamba was the one tree on the bank still alive because Garuḍa, foreseeing the līlā, had put nectar on it — reported by Prabhupāda's *Kṛṣṇa* (ch. 16) as told "in some other Purāṇas," alongside the foot-touch explanation. Not in the verses of Bhāgavata 10.16, Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7 or the Harivaṃśa. [late text] [locus not verified]
- **Kāliya's attack** — Roused by the noise in his home, Kāliya rises, **bites Kṛṣṇa in the vital spots** (*sandaśya marmasu*) and wraps him in his coils; seeing their friend motionless in the serpent's grip, the cowherd boys collapse in grief and the cows and bulls stand crying (Bhāgavata 10.16.8–11).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.14–17; Harivaṃśa 56.4–13):* Kāliya does not come alone. In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa other venomous serpents and hundreds of his wives come with him, and the serpents bind Kṛṣṇa in their coils and bite him (5.7.15–17); in the Harivaṃśa his sons, wives, servants and other great serpents all spew fire, bind and bite him, while he stands unmoving "like a mountain" and does not die (56.11–13).
- **Vraja comes to the bank** — In the Bhāgavata, dreadful omens in Vraja tell Nanda and the elders that Kṛṣṇa, gone out without Balarāma, is in danger; the whole settlement — children, old people and women — rushes out and tracks him by his footprints, marked with the lotus, barley-corn, goad, thunderbolt and flag (Bhāgavata 10.16.12–18). Seeing him in the serpent's coils, they despair; the gopīs feel the three worlds empty, and the gopīs must hold back Yaśodā from following her son into the pool (10.16.19–21).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.18–32; Harivaṃśa 56.14–25):* the frightened cowherd boys run to Vraja crying "Kṛṣṇa has fainted in Kāliya's pool and is being eaten by the serpent-king" (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.19; Harivaṃśa 56.15); Nanda, Yaśodā and the gopīs come (with Balarāma: Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.22; Harivaṃśa 56.18), Nanda stands senseless gazing at his son's face (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.24; Harivaṃśa 56.23), and the gopīs resolve to enter the pool with Yaśodā rather than return to a Vraja without Kṛṣṇa — "what is a day without the sun … what is Vraja without Kṛṣṇa?" (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.26–27; Harivaṃśa 56.24–25). In the Harivaṃśa the women also reproach Yaśodā for her heart of stone (56.21–22).
- **Balarāma and the release** — In the Bhāgavata, Balarāma, knowing his younger brother's power, only smiles and **says nothing** (*prahasya kiñcin novāca*, 10.16.16), and holds back Nanda and the others as they start into the pool (10.16.22). Seeing his people's grief, Kṛṣṇa, who had been imitating human ways, stays a moment longer and then rises from the serpent's bonds by **expanding his body**, so that the tormented Kāliya lets go (10.16.23–24).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.33–43; Harivaṃśa 56.26–29):* Balarāma **speaks to Kṛṣṇa** and reminds him of his divinity. In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa he asks why Kṛṣṇa shows this human mood, says "I, your elder brother, am your portion," and recalls that the gods have descended as the cowherds (5.7.35–42); in the Harivaṃśa he speaks in anger, as "one body made two" with Kṛṣṇa, telling him to subdue the serpent because his kin think him human (56.26–28). Thus reminded, Kṛṣṇa frees himself by **slapping his arms** — there is no body-expansion: in the Viṣṇu Purāṇa he smiles and, slapping his arms (*āsphoṭya*), releases himself from the serpent's bonds (5.7.43); in the Harivaṃśa he sports (*vikrīḍya*), slaps his arms (*āsphoṭayad bāhū*) and bursts the coils (*bhittvā bhoga-bandhanam*, 56.29).
- **The dance upon the hoods (Kāliya-nartana)** — Kṛṣṇa circles the furious serpent as Garuḍa would, wearing him down, then bends down his raised hoods, **mounts the broad heads** and **dances**, his feet reddened by the jewels on the hoods (Bhāgavata 10.16.25–26). Whichever head will not bow he crushes with his stamping feet, until Kāliya vomits blood from mouths and nostrils and poison from his eyes (10.16.28–29), his hoods broken by that "wondrous *tāṇḍava*" (*citra-tāṇḍava*, 10.16.30). Gandharvas, Siddhas, sages, Cāraṇas and the wives of the gods come with drums (*mṛdaṅga*, *paṇava*, *ānaka*), songs, flowers and praise (10.16.27). The serpent is crushed by the weight of the one who holds the universe within him (10.16.31) and in his mind takes refuge in Nārāyaṇa (10.16.30). This *dance* is the defining image of the episode.
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.44–46; Harivaṃśa 56.30–32):* Kṛṣṇa bends down the **middle head** with his hands and dances on it, until the serpent faints and vomits blood (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.44–46); in the Harivaṃśa he presses down the coils with his feet, seizes and bends the head and mounts "that great middle head" (56.30–31). An appendix passage there calls the dance *lāsya* (star passage after Harivaṃśa 56.31). [interpolation]
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 56.6, 56.35):* Kāliya has only **five** heads (*pañcāsya*, 56.6; *pañca-mūrdhan*, 56.35) — against the Bhāgavata's **101** heads (*śataika-śīrṣṇaḥ*, 10.16.28, so the BBT translation), which in the same passage also speaks of a **thousand** hoods (*phaṇā-sahasra*, 10.16.30). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa states no number beyond the "middle" head (5.7.44).

## The pleas of the Nāga-wives

- **The Nāga-patnīs' prayer** — Seeing their lord broken, Kāliya's **wives (*nāga-patnyaḥ*)** come with clothes, ornaments and hair in disarray, set their children before them, prostrate themselves and pray (Bhāgavata 10.16.31–32). Their hymn, one of the great hymns of surrender in the Bhāgavata, declares the punishment just and in truth Kṛṣṇa's **grace** — "even your anger is counted as favour" — praises him as the Supreme, and begs their husband's life (Bhāgavata 10.16.33–53, the *Nāga-patnī-stuti*).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.47–59):* the wives also plead, in a shorter hymn of their own — "women, the foolish and the wretched are to be pitied by the good; forgive this wretch" — with the refrain "the serpent is giving up his life; give us our husband as alms" (*prāṇāṃs tyajati nāgo 'yaṃ bhartṛ-bhikṣā pradīyatām*, 5.7.57–59).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 56.32–35):* the critical text has **no hymn of the wives**; the crushed Kāliya, mouths running with blood, pleads for himself — "in ignorance I showed this anger; I am subdued and my poison gone … grant me my life" — and Kṛṣṇa answers "without anger" (*akruddha eva*, 56.35).
- **Kāliya's own submission** — Released and breathing again, Kāliya with joined palms confesses that serpents are wrathful by birth and nature, a nature hard to give up because Kṛṣṇa himself made it, and leaves his fate to Kṛṣṇa's favour or punishment (Bhāgavata 10.16.55–59). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa gives him a long hymn of his own, with the same plea that this cruel serpent-kind is his nature, not his fault (5.7.60–76, esp. 5.7.71–73); the Harivaṃśa's plea is the brief one above (56.33–34).
- **The banishment** — Kṛṣṇa spares him and **orders him to leave the Yamunā at once for the ocean, with his kin, children and wives, since cows and people use this river**; he promises that Garuḍa, seeing on the serpent's hoods the **mark of Kṛṣṇa's feet**, will no longer attack him (Bhāgavata 10.16.60, 10.16.63; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.77–78; Harivaṃśa 56.36, 56.39). In the Bhāgavata the ocean refuge is understood as Ramaṇaka, the island he had left (10.16.63; 10.16.67 *dvīpam abdheḥ*, "the island of the sea"); the Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Harivaṃśa say only the sea (*samudra-salilam*, Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.77; *arṇava-jalam*, Harivaṃśa 56.36).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 56.37–38):* the sentence is sterner — any of Kāliya's servants or sons seen there again, on land or in water, will be killed by Kṛṣṇa.
  - *Variant (folk / Vaiṣṇava):* the **footprint-mark** on Kāliya's hood is popularly identified with the spectacle-mark on the cobra's hood: cobras have borne Kṛṣṇa's footprint ever since, and Garuḍa will not attack a serpent that carries it — a motif of popular retelling (e.g. Hindu Blog, "Story of Impression on Hood of Cobra Snake and Sri Krishna," 2006), not of the three texts. [folk] [regional]
- **The blessing and the departure** — Only the Bhāgavata adds a blessing on the episode: whoever remembers or recites Kṛṣṇa's command to Kāliya at dawn and dusk will not fear serpents, and whoever bathes in this pool, offers water to the gods, fasts and worships Kṛṣṇa is freed of sins (Bhāgavata 10.16.61–62). Kāliya and his wives then worship Kṛṣṇa with divine garments, jewels, ornaments, unguents and a great lotus garland, circumambulate him and depart (10.16.64–67); Kṛṣṇa comes out of the pool wearing them (10.17.13). [Bhāgavata only]
- **Why the pool had to be cleared** — The Harivaṃśa makes the purpose explicitly practical as well as cosmic: to make the water wholesome (*śiva-jala*) and the river enjoyable for Vraja in every season; for this, and to chastise the wicked who stray from the path, Kṛṣṇa says he dwells in Vraja and took a cowherd birth (Harivaṃśa 55.54–56; cf. Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.8–9).
- **The cleansed river** — Kāliya departs with his family; at once the Yamunā becomes free of poison, its water nectar-like (*sāmṛta-jalā yamunā nirviṣābhavat*, Bhāgavata 10.16.67); the cowherds rejoice to see the river grown auspicious (*śiva-jalā*, Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.82; Harivaṃśa 56.44).
- **Return to Vraja** — The cowherds embrace Kṛṣṇa as one come back from death (Bhāgavata 10.17.14–16; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.81); Balarāma embraces him and laughs, knowing his power (Bhāgavata 10.17.16); at the brāhmaṇas' urging Nanda gives cows and gold in thanks for his son's release (10.17.17–18). In the Harivaṃśa the cowherds circumambulate Kṛṣṇa, congratulate Nanda, and declare Kṛṣṇa their refuge in every danger (56.41–45).

## Sequel — the forest fire

- **Davāgni / the swallowing of the forest fire** — In the Bhāgavata the Kāliya episode is immediately followed by a wildfire (*dāvāgni*): exhausted by hunger and thirst, the people and cattle of Vraja sleep that night by the Yamunā, a fire springs up at midnight in the summer-dry forest and surrounds them, and Kṛṣṇa, at their cry, **drinks the blaze** (*tam agnim apibat*) and saves them (Bhāgavata 10.17.20–25). The two are narrated as a pair — the danger from *water* (poison) answered by the danger from *fire* — and frequently catalogued together in the bālya-līlā. [textual ordering] This is distinct from the second forest fire at Muñjāṭavī (Bhāgavata 10.19). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Harivaṃśa pass straight from Kāliya to the Dhenuka episode (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.8.1; Harivaṃśa 57.1–3).

## Iconography & afterlife

- **Vīra and vātsalya together** — The scene fuses two rasas the tradition prizes: the **heroic** (*vīra*) — a child mastering a cosmic serpent — and the **tender** (*vātsalya*) — the same child the swooning mother fears to lose. In the standard South Indian bronze a small Kṛṣṇa dances on the hood of a multi-hooded serpent, one foot raised, typically **holding the serpent's tail in his left hand** while the right hand is raised in *abhaya*; Kāliya is often shown with a human torso, hands joined in submission. Paintings commonly add the Nāga-patnīs praying in the water. [scholarly]
- **Temple and bronze art** — **Kāliya-Kṛṣṇa / Kāliya-mardana** (Tamil: *Kāḷiṅga-nartana*) is among the most reproduced subjects of South Indian Chola and later **bronze sculpture** (e.g. the late 10th–early 11th c. Chola bronze in the Asia Society collection), and a fixture of temple relief, miniature painting (Rajput, Pahari, Mewar) and Mughal-era illustration of the Bhāgavata. [scholarly]
- **Dance and performance** — The episode is a staple of **Bharatanāṭyam, Kuchipudi, Kathak and Odissi** repertoire as the *Kāḷiṅga-nartana / Kāliya-daman* item, and of **Rāslīlā** and **Kṛṣṇa-līlā** folk theatre in the Braj region. [scholarly]
- **Festival** — At **Vārāṇasī** the dance is re-enacted as **Nāg Nathaiyā** (Nāga-naṭhaiyā) at **Tulsī Ghāṭ**, a custom ascribed to Tulsīdās (16th c.): a kadamba branch is planted at the river's edge and a boy playing Kṛṣṇa leaps from it into the Ganges, standing in for the Yamunā, onto an effigy of Kāliya, on **Kārttika Śukla Caturthī** (the fourth day of the bright fortnight of Kārttika, a few days after Dīpāvalī). [regional]

## Two clocks

- **Scriptural time:** the līlā falls in Kṛṣṇa's boyhood in the closing **[Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md)** of the current 28th Mahāyuga (traditionally before the onset of Kali Yuga, **3102 BCE**) — placed in the same Vṛndāvana phase as the Govardhana (Bhāgavata 10.24–27) and rāsa (10.29–33) episodes.
- **Text-critical time:** the **oldest connected form is the Harivaṃśa** (oldest layers c. 1st–2nd c. BCE, growing into the early CE centuries; dates contested — see [Harivaṃśa](./harivamsha.md)), restated in the **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** (~5th c. CE), and most elaborated and devotionalised in the **Bhāgavata** (~6th–10th c. CE). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa visibly reworks the Harivaṃśa: whole lines recur, such as "what is Vraja without Kṛṣṇa?" (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.27 = Harivaṃśa 56.25) and the promise of the footprints on Kāliya's head (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.7.78 = Harivaṃśa 56.39). The **Nāga-patnī hymn** first appears in the Viṣṇu Purāṇa (it is absent from the Harivaṃśa's critical text) and is greatly expanded in the Bhāgavata; the Bhāgavata alone adds the poisoned cowherds, the Garuḍa-tribute and **Saubhari-curse** backstory, the name **Ramaṇaka**, the silent Balarāma, the body-expansion and the forest-fire sequel. The skeleton — Kāliya in the Yamunā, the poison, the leap from the kadamba, the coils, the dance on the hoods, the sparing and banishment to the ocean under the protection of Kṛṣṇa's footprints — is constant across all three. The Mahābhārata names Kāliya only as one of Kadrū's sons (1.31.6, crit. ed.); a mention of his subduing in its table of contents is a passage the critical edition relegates to the apparatus (star passage after Mahābhārata 1.2.233). [scholarly]
