# The Syamantaka Jewel

> A standalone deep-dive on the **Syamantaka** (*Syamantaka-maṇi*), the sun-gem of Dwārakā — the one extended "detective" episode of Kṛṣṇa's career: the gem's gift to **Satrājit**, the death of **Prasena**, the false accusation against Kṛṣṇa, the cave-fight with the bear-king **Jāmbavān**, the marriages to **Jāmbavatī** and **Satyabhāmā**, the killing of **Śatadhanvā**, Akrūra's keeping of the gem, and the later attachment of the **Gaṇeśa-caturthī** moon-prohibition (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.56–57; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.13; *Harivaṃśa*, Harivaṃśa-parvan, crit. ed. 28–29). Set in the **Dvāpara-yuga** of our [Mahāyuga 28](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md).
> Up one level: [Deep Dives README](README.md). Related: [Kṛṣṇa-līlā](krishna-lila.md) · [Rukmiṇī-haraṇa & the Aṣṭa-bhāryā](rukmini-harana.md) · [Bhāgavata Tales Catalog](bhagavata-tales-catalog.md) · [Harivaṃśa](harivamsha.md).

The Syamantaka saga is unique in the Kṛṣṇa corpus: a self-contained whodunit in which Kṛṣṇa is himself the suspect and must clear his own name by tracking a stolen jewel. It braids together a chain of deaths, two of his eight chief marriages, a direct narrative bridge back to the **Rāmāyaṇa** (Jāmbavān, who served Rāma, now recognises Rāma's later self), and a coda of merit: all three early tellings close by promising that whoever hears or remembers the story is freed from false accusation (Bhāgavata 10.57.42; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.162; Harivaṃśa 28.45), the root of its later recitation on Gaṇeśa-caturthī. It is the marriage-source for both **Jāmbavatī** (mother of Sāmba) and **Satyabhāmā**, who later rides with Kṛṣṇa to the Narakāsura war and whose urging brings home the Pārijāta tree (Bhāgavata 10.59.3, 10.59.39–40; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.29.14, 5.30; see [Narakāsura-vadha](narakasura-vadha.md) and [Pārijāta-haraṇa](parijata-harana.md)). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Harivaṃśa tell the story inside the Yādava genealogy (the line of Nighna, son of Anamitra), not inside their Kṛṣṇa-biography books (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.9–10; Harivaṃśa 28.11; cf. Bhāgavata 9.24.12–13).

## The gem and its owner (Bhāgavata 10.56)

- **Satrājit and Prasena** — **Satrājit** and his brother **Prasena** are the two sons of **Nighna**, son of Anamitra, a Yādava of the Vṛṣṇi line (Bhāgavata 9.24.12–13; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.8–10). In the Harivaṃśa, Anamitra is instead a son of Kroṣṭu by Mādrī (Harivaṃśa 28.10–11).
- **Satrājit's devotion** — Satrājit is a devotee of **Sūrya**, who is his close friend; pleased, the Sun gives him the **Syamantaka** jewel (Bhāgavata 10.56.3). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa tells it as a scene on the seashore: Satrājit praises the Sun, sees him only as a blur of fire, and complains that the god has shown him no special favour (Wilson renders the request "that I may see thee in thy proper form"); Sūrya takes the gem off his neck, appears as a short, copper-bodied figure with reddish eyes, offers a boon, and Satrājit asks for the gem (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.11–18).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 28.12):* in the critical text it is **Prasena** who "obtained the divine gem Syamantaka from the ocean" (*samudrād upalabdhavān*) when Dwārakā was being settled, and Kṛṣṇa later covets it "from Prasena" (28.14). The Sūrya-gift scene appears in the Harivaṃśa only in an unnumbered inserted passage after 28.12; the Bengal recension translated by Dutt carries both statements side by side (Dutt, Harivaṃśa-parva 38.12–24). [interpolation] The Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa, whose chapter runs almost verse for verse with the Harivaṃśa, has the Sūrya scene and Satrājit's gift of the gem to Prasena in its numbered text (Brahmāṇḍa 2.71.21–31, GRETIL numbering).
- **The sun-gem's powers** — Worn at the neck, the Syamantaka makes its wearer blaze like the sun (Bhāgavata 10.56.4). Installed and worshipped in Satrājit's house-shrine, it produces **eight bhāras of gold every day**, and where it is honoured there is no famine, pestilence, calamity, snakes, disease or deceivers (Bhāgavata 10.56.10–11; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.24–26, which names drought among the ills it wards off; cf. Harivaṃśa 28.13, where it pours gold in the Vṛṣṇi-Andhaka settlement and brings timely rain).
  - **The danger to the wearer** — Only the Viṣṇu Purāṇa adds the condition: held by a pure man the gem gives all its gifts, but it **kills an impure wearer** (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.30, restated at 4.13.155). The Bhāgavata gives no such warning.
- **Satrājit's entry into Dwārakā** — He enters the city wearing the gem, so radiant that the citizens run to Kṛṣṇa, who is playing dice, saying that **Sūrya himself** is coming to see him; Kṛṣṇa laughs and tells them it is Satrājit, shining with his gem (Bhāgavata 10.56.4–9; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.19–23). The dice and the laugh are the Bhāgavata's (10.56.5, 10.56.9); in the Viṣṇu Purāṇa the citizens bow to Kṛṣṇa and he tells them to look without fear (4.13.20–23).
- **Kṛṣṇa's request refused** — Kṛṣṇa asks Satrājit to give the gem to the Yadu king (*yadu-rājāya*, i.e. **Ugrasena**); Satrājit, greedy for wealth, refuses, not weighing what the refusal would cost him (Bhāgavata 10.56.12).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.27–29):* Kṛṣṇa never asks. He only thinks the gem fit for King Ugrasena and, though able to take it, refrains for fear of splitting the clan (*gotra-bheda-bhayāt*, 4.13.28). Satrājit, expecting to be asked, passes the gem to his brother Prasena out of greed (4.13.29).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 28.14):* Kṛṣṇa wants the gem from **Prasena**, its owner here, and does not get it; though able to, he does not seize it (*lipsāṃ cakre … na ca taṃ lebhe śakto 'pi na jahāra*).

## The death of Prasena and the false accusation (Bhāgavata 10.56)

- **Prasena's hunt** — Prasena puts the jewel on his neck and rides into the forest to hunt; a **lion kills him and his horse** and takes the gem (Bhāgavata 10.56.13–14; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.30–32; Harivaṃśa 28.15). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa has the lion carry it off in its mouth (4.13.32).
- **Jāmbavān seizes the gem** — As the lion makes for the mountain, the bear-king **Jāmbavān** kills it, takes the Syamantaka into his cave, and gives it to his son as a toy (Bhāgavata 10.56.14–15; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.32–34; Harivaṃśa 28.16). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa names the child **Sukumāra** (4.13.34; Wilson: "his son Sukumára").
- **The slander against Kṛṣṇa** — Satrājit, grieving for his missing brother, says that Kṛṣṇa probably killed Prasena, who had gone to the forest wearing the gem; people pass the charge from ear to ear (Bhāgavata 10.56.15–16). In the other tellings the whole Yadu community draws the inference from Kṛṣṇa's known wish for the gem (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.35; Harivaṃśa 28.17). [This is the slander the whole episode exists to clear.]
- **Kṛṣṇa resolves to clear his name** — To wipe off the stain, Kṛṣṇa sets out with citizens along Prasena's trail; they find Prasena and his horse killed by the lion, and then the lion killed by the bear on the mountainside (Bhāgavata 10.56.17–18). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Harivaṃśa have him follow first the lion's tracks and then the **bear's footprints** to the cave (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.36–40; Harivaṃśa 28.21–22). The Harivaṃśa sends the search through the **Ṛkṣavant** and **Vindhya** ranges (28.19).

## The cave-fight with Jāmbavān (Bhāgavata 10.56)

- **Kṛṣṇa enters alone** — Leaving the people outside, Kṛṣṇa enters the dark cave, sees the gem made into a child's plaything, and stands near the child meaning to take it; the nurse, seeing a stranger, **cries out in fear**, and Jāmbavān rushes in, angry (Bhāgavata 10.56.19–21). In inserted lines at Harivaṃśa 28.25, Balarāma and the Yadus are posted at the cave-mouth. [interpolation]
  - **The nurse's verse** — The Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Harivaṃśa add that, entering, Kṛṣṇa first overhears the nurse soothing the boy: *siṃhaḥ prasenam avadhīt siṃho jāmbavatā hataḥ / sukumāraka mā rodīs tava hy eṣa syamantakaḥ*, "The lion killed Prasena; the lion was killed by Jāmbavān. Don't cry, little Sukumāra, this Syamantaka is yours" (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.41–42; Harivaṃśa 28.22–24). Only after that does the nurse cry for help (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.44). This verse is the one now recited as the remedy on Gaṇeśa-caturthī (see below).
- **The 28-day duel** — Jāmbavān, taking him for an ordinary man, fights his own master; they fight with weapons, stones, trees and bare arms like two hawks over meat, and then with their fists for **twenty-eight days**, day and night without rest (Bhāgavata 10.56.22–24).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.46; Harivaṃśa 28.26):* the fight lasts **twenty-one days** (*ekaviṃśati*). In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Kṛṣṇa is strengthened by the food and water his kin offer in the funeral rites they perform, believing him dead, while Jāmbavān, without food, weakens (4.13.49–51).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata 10.83.10):* in Jāmbavatī's own account to Draupadī, her father fought "for twenty-seven days" (*tri-navahāni*), so the Bhāgavata is not consistent with itself. The counts are recorded here as they stand.
- **Recognition — the Rāmāyaṇa bridge** — Exhausted, Jāmbavān recognises him as **Viṣṇu**, the ancient Person, and in the same breath as the one whose angry glance made the ocean give way, who built the bridge, burned Laṅkā and cut off the Rākṣasas' heads with arrows: that is, as **Rāma**, whom he once served (Bhāgavata 10.56.25–28). In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa he concludes that his opponent must be a portion of Nārāyaṇa, "like our master Rāma," and Kṛṣṇa tells him why he has descended (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.52–53). The critical Harivaṃśa has no recognition scene: Kṛṣṇa simply defeats him (28.28). See [Vānaras and Ṛkṣas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vanaras-and-rikshas.md).
- **Jāmbavatī given in marriage** — Kṛṣṇa touches Jāmbavān with his hand and explains that he came for the gem to clear himself of a false charge; Jāmbavān gladly gives him his daughter **Jāmbavatī**, together with the gem, as a gift of honour (*arhaṇārtham*) (Bhāgavata 10.56.29–32; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.54–56; Harivaṃśa 28.28–29). Kṛṣṇa takes the gem "for his own vindication" (*ātma-saṃśodhanāya*, Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.57; *ātma-viśuddhaye*, Harivaṃśa 28.29). She becomes one of the eight chief queens and the mother of **Sāmba** (Bhāgavata 10.61.11–12). See the [Aṣṭa-bhāryā list](rukmini-harana.md).

## Vindication and the marriage to Satyabhāmā (Bhāgavata 10.56)

- **Kṛṣṇa given up for dead** — His companions wait **twelve days** at the cave-mouth and go home in grief; Devakī, Rukmiṇī, Vasudeva and the kin mourn him, and the citizens, cursing Satrājit, worship the goddess **Durgā Candrabhāgā** for Kṛṣṇa's return (Bhāgavata 10.56.33–35).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.47–49):* the Yadu troops wait only **seven or eight days**, conclude that no fight could last so long, report him dead, and his kin perform the funeral rites. In the critical Harivaṃśa, the party headed by Vasudeva returns and reports him killed, with no count of days (28.27).
- **The jewel restored publicly** — Kṛṣṇa reappears with his new wife; he summons Satrājit to the assembly in the king's presence, tells how he found it, and **hands the Syamantaka back to Satrājit**, clearing himself of the murder charge (Bhāgavata 10.56.36–38; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.58–62; Harivaṃśa 28.30–31).
- **Satrājit's remorse** — Ashamed, Satrājit goes home brooding on how to undo his wrong, and resolves to give Kṛṣṇa both the "jewel among women" and the jewel; he gives him his daughter **Satyabhāmā** and the **gem** (Bhāgavata 10.56.39–43). In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa he gives Satyabhāmā alone, out of fear at having slandered Kṛṣṇa (4.13.64).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 28.32–34):* Satrājit has ten wives and a hundred sons, and gives Kṛṣṇa **three daughters** as wives: **Satyabhāmā**, **Vratinī** and **Padmāvatī** (Dutt's recension reads the third as Praswāpinī, Harivaṃśa-parva 38.47; the Brahmāṇḍa parallel names her Tapasvinī, 2.71.57).
- **Kṛṣṇa keeps the bride, not the gem** — Kṛṣṇa marries **Satyabhāmā** with due rites, but declines the jewel: "We do not want the gem, O king. Let it stay with you, a devotee of the Sun-god; we will share in its fruits" (Bhāgavata 10.56.44–45). [The Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Harivaṃśa have no second offer of the gem, since Kṛṣṇa has already returned it.]
- **The rival suitors** — Satyabhāmā had been sought by many (Bhāgavata 10.56.44). Akrūra and Kṛtavarman complain that Satrājit had promised her to them and then gave her to Kṛṣṇa (Bhāgavata 10.57.4). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa names **Akrūra, Kṛtavarman and Śatadhanvā** as earlier suitors who felt insulted and swore enmity against Satrājit (4.13.65–66), and the Harivaṃśa has Akrūra always wanting both Satyabhāmā and the gem (29.2). This grudge drives the next murder.

## The murder of Satrājit and the theft of the gem (Bhāgavata 10.57)

- **Kṛṣṇa's absence** — News comes that the **Pāṇḍavas** and Kuntī have burned in the lac-house. Kṛṣṇa knows the truth, but goes with **Balarāma** to the Kurus (Hastināpura) to perform the family duties and grieve with Bhīṣma, Kṛpa, Vidura, Gāndhārī and Droṇa (Bhāgavata 10.57.1–2, 10.57.8).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.70; Harivaṃśa 29.6–8):* Kṛṣṇa goes to **Vāraṇāvata**, the site of the fire, to slacken Duryodhana's efforts and stand by the Pārthas (VP), or to make the water-offerings and leave Sātyaki to finish the rites (HV). **Balarāma is not with him**: Kṛṣṇa goes back to Dwārakā to speak to him (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.76; Harivaṃśa 29.9).
- **The conspiracy** — Seizing the moment, **Akrūra** and **Kṛtavarman** incite **Śatadhanvā** to take the gem and kill Satrājit: in the Bhāgavata they ask why Satrājit, who promised them his daughter and then gave her to Kṛṣṇa, should not "follow his brother" (*kasmād bhrātaram anviyāt*, 10.57.4); in the Viṣṇu Purāṇa they tell him outright to kill Satrājit and promise to back him if Kṛṣṇa takes revenge (Bhāgavata 10.57.3–4; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.67–69).
- **Satrājit slain in sleep** — **Śatadhanvā kills the sleeping Satrājit** like a butcher killing animals, while the women wail, and carries off the jewel (Bhāgavata 10.57.5–6; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.71; Harivaṃśa 29.3 has him kill him at night).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 29.3–5):* Śatadhanvā hands the gem to **Akrūra** at once. Akrūra makes him promise never to tell Kṛṣṇa, and boasts that all Dwārakā is under his control.
- **Satyabhāmā's ride** — Grieving, Satyabhāmā lays her father's body in a **trough of oil** (*taila-droṇyām*) and goes to Hastināpura to tell Kṛṣṇa; the two brothers, though they already know, weep and lament as men do (Bhāgavata 10.57.7–9). In the other tellings she drives her chariot to Vāraṇāvata (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.72–73; Harivaṃśa 29.6–7). In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Kṛṣṇa takes the insult as his own: no one kills birds without first assailing the tree they nest in (4.13.75–76).
- **Kṛṣṇa's words to Balarāma** — In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa and Harivaṃśa, Kṛṣṇa returns to Dwārakā and tells Balarāma privately that, with Prasena and Satrājit both dead, the gem will be **theirs in common**, so they must kill Śatadhanvā (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.76–80; Harivaṃśa 29.9–11). This promise is what Balarāma later thinks has been broken.
- **The pursuit of Śatadhanvā** — Śatadhanvā, seeing Kṛṣṇa prepare to kill him, begs **Kṛtavarman** and then **Akrūra** for help; both refuse, Akrūra in a hymn to Kṛṣṇa's power. Śatadhanvā leaves the gem with Akrūra and flees on a horse that can cover a hundred yojanas (Bhāgavata 10.57.10–18; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.81–91, where Akrūra makes him swear to keep the secret even at the point of death). Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma follow in the Garuḍa-bannered chariot (Bhāgavata 10.57.19; the four horses Śaibya, Sugrīva, Meghapuṣpa and Balāhaka are named at Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.92).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 29.12–15):* Kṛṣṇa and Śatadhanvā actually fight; Akrūra, though able, holds back out of guile; and the fugitive's mount is a mare named **Hṛdayā** that covers a hundred yojanas (29.15, *vikhyātā hṛdayā nāma*; Dutt's recension gives the name as "Vijnātāhridayā").
- **Śatadhanvā beheaded** — At **Mithilā** the exhausted horse falls dead and Śatadhanvā runs on foot; Kṛṣṇa follows on foot, cuts off his head with his sharp-rimmed **discus**, and searches his clothes, but the jewel is not there (Bhāgavata 10.57.20–21). In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Balarāma waits in the chariot because the ground is bad for the horses, and Kṛṣṇa throws the discus from two krośas away (4.13.93–99). The Harivaṃśa also leaves Balarāma with the chariot (29.18) but names no weapon (29.17–20). [The verses say only *cakra*; naming it Sudarśana is the usual gloss.]
- **Balarāma goes to Mithilā** — Kṛṣṇa tells Balarāma that Śatadhanvā died for nothing. Balarāma replies that the gem must have been left with someone, tells Kṛṣṇa to go back and find him, and goes into Mithilā to see the king of Videha, his dear friend, where he stays for some years and **Duryodhana** learns the mace from him (Bhāgavata 10.57.22–26). The Bhāgavata shows no quarrel here. Its only trace of distrust comes later, when Kṛṣṇa says, "my elder brother does not fully believe me about the gem" (10.57.38).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.100–107; Harivaṃśa 29.20–29):* an open **rift**. Balarāma demands the gem, and on hearing it is not there cries "Shame on you, so greedy for wealth!", says he forgives Kṛṣṇa only because he is his brother, and renounces Dwārakā, Kṛṣṇa and all his kin, refusing to be won over. He lives in the Videha king's house, where Duryodhana learns the mace from him, until after **three years** Babhru, Ugrasena and others (*babhrūgrasena-prabhṛtibhiḥ*; the Viṣṇu Purāṇa never calls Akrūra Babhru, and its only other Babhru is the son of Devāvṛdha, 4.13.3–5; Wilson omits the name), satisfied that Kṛṣṇa never had the gem, go to Videha and bring him home (VP 4.13.107). In the Harivaṃśa he is won over by the Vṛṣṇi-Andhaka chiefs and Kṛṣṇa himself (29.29).
- **Rites and flight** — Back in Dwārakā, Kṛṣṇa tells Satyabhāmā of Śatadhanvā's death and that the gem was not recovered, and performs her father's funeral rites (Bhāgavata 10.57.27–28). Akrūra and Kṛtavarman, the instigators, flee Dwārakā in fear (Bhāgavata 10.57.29).

## Akrūra and the gem's resting-place (Bhāgavata 10.57)

- **Calamities in Akrūra's absence** — With Akrūra gone, the people of Dwārakā suffer misfortunes of body, mind and the elements, and the elders recall that where Akrūra's father **Śvaphalka** went, the rain followed: the king of Kāśī gave him his daughter **Gāndinī** to end a drought, and Akrūra, their son, has the same power (Bhāgavata 10.57.30–33). The narrator himself demurs: "some say" this, forgetting what was said before; how could misfortune strike the city where the Lord lives? (Bhāgavata 10.57.31).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.108–130):* a longer and different chronology. Akrūra keeps the gem in secret and, using its gold, performs one sacrifice after another, so that for **sixty-two years** (*dviṣaṣṭi-varṣāṇi*, 4.13.110; Wilson's text reads "fifty-two") there is no plague or famine. He leaves only when the Bhojas, his allies, kill **Śatrughna**, great-grandson of Sātvata, and he flees with them (4.13.111). The elder **Andhaka** then tells the story of Akrūra's birth: Gāndinī stayed twelve years in her mother's womb, then promised to come out after three more years if her father gave a brahmin a cow every day; he did, and she was born at the end of that time (4.13.114–127, birth at 4.13.119–123; Wilson: "at the end of three years the damsel came into the world"). Akrūra is promised safety and brought back, and the calamities cease (4.13.129–130).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 29.24–34):* Akrūra (called **Babhru**) performs the "Akrūra-sacrifices" for **sixty years**, armoured by his consecration (29.24–27; Dutt's "sixty thousand years" is not in the critical text). He later leaves with the Andhakas after a killing in battle (the critical text reads Satrājit at 29.30; the inserted lines after it and the Brahmāṇḍa parallel name Śatrughna and Bandhumān, sons of Satrājit's son Bhaṅgakāra, Brahmāṇḍa 2.71.86–88); Kṛṣṇa lets it pass for fear of a family split; Indra withholds rain until the Kukuras and Andhakas win Akrūra back (29.30–33). Akrūra then gives Kṛṣṇa **his sister** in marriage (29.34).
- **Kṛṣṇa's reasoning** — Kṛṣṇa judges that Akrūra's lineage is not the whole cause (Bhāgavata 10.57.34). In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa he reasons from Akrūra's endless, costly sacrifices that the gem must be with him (4.13.131–137); in the Harivaṃśa he knows it by yoga (29.35).
- **The gem produced in assembly** — Kṛṣṇa summons Akrūra, honours him, and tells him with a smile that he has known all along that Śatadhanvā left the gem with him (Bhāgavata 10.57.34–36). Satrājit had no son, so by right his daughter's sons should inherit it, after making his water and rice-ball offerings and clearing his remaining debts (Bhāgavata 10.57.37). "Still, it is hard for others to hold; let it stay with you, man of good vows. But my elder brother does not quite believe me: show it, and bring your kinsmen peace" (Bhāgavata 10.57.38–39). Akrūra brings out the sun-bright gem from under his cloth; Kṛṣṇa shows it to his kin, clears himself, and **gives it back to Akrūra** (Bhāgavata 10.57.40–41; Harivaṃśa 29.36–40, where Akrūra then wears it and shines like the sun).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.138–161):* a fuller scene. Akrūra says he has held it with great difficulty, expecting Kṛṣṇa to ask for it any day, and has been unable to enjoy anything; he produces it from a small gold box hidden in the cloth at his belly (4.13.141–146). Seeing it, **Balarāma** wants it as the share Kṛṣṇa promised him, and **Satyabhāmā** wants it as her father's property (4.13.150–151). Kṛṣṇa rules that it belongs jointly to Balarāma and himself and is Satyabhāmā's inheritance, but that it may be held only by someone pure and celibate. He, with **sixteen thousand wives**, cannot hold it; Satyabhāmā would not accept the conditions; Balarāma would have to give up wine and every pleasure. So he asks Akrūra to keep it for the good of the whole realm (4.13.154–160), and from then on Akrūra wears it openly, shining like the sun (4.13.161).
- **The merit of the tale** — All three texts end by saying that the story protects from slander. Whoever reads, hears or remembers it is freed of ill fame and sin (Bhāgavata 10.57.42: *duṣkīrtiṃ duritam apohya yāti śāntim*); never suffers even a slight false accusation and is freed of all sin (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.162); or is never touched by false curses (Harivaṃśa 28.45, placed after the Jāmbavān half).

## The Gaṇeśa-caturthī moon-curse aetiology

- **Why Kṛṣṇa was slandered at all** — A widely-attached frame explains the false accusation as the effect of Kṛṣṇa's having **seen the moon on Bhādrapada Śukla Caturthī** (Gaṇeśa-caturthī), in some tellings as a reflection in a vessel of milk. Whoever sees the moon that night is fated to suffer a false charge (*mithyā-doṣa*), and in popular tellings it is the sage **Nārada** who explains this to Kṛṣṇa and has him keep the Caturthī fast. This frame is absent from Bhāgavata 10.56–57, Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13 and Harivaṃśa 28–29. [folk / vrata-kathā] [locus not verified: popular sources often name the Skanda Purāṇa for it, but no chapter could be found]
- **The origin of the moon's curse** — The prohibition itself is traced to **Gaṇeśa**: the **moon (Candra)** laughs at the pot-bellied god (in many tellings after he falls from his mouse), and Gaṇeśa, or in some versions Pārvatī, curses him so that anyone who looks at him incurs disgrace, later softening the curse to the one Caturthī night (popular *vrata* literature; see [Gaṇeśa and Kārttikeya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md)). [late text / regional] [locus not verified: the attribution to the Gaṇeśa Purāṇa or the Mudgala Purāṇa (one secondary source gives Mudgala 4.6) could not be checked against an edition]
- **The recitation-remedy** — Hence the Syamantaka narrative is **recited or heard on Gaṇeśa-caturthī**, and by anyone who has accidentally seen the moon that night, to **avert false accusation**. The mantra in common use is the nurse's verse *siṃhaḥ prasenam avadhīt…* (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.42 = Harivaṃśa 28.24). The promise of protection is scriptural, given in the texts' own closing verses (Bhāgavata 10.57.42; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.13.162; Harivaṃśa 28.45); tying it to the moon and to the Caturthī is the later *vrata* layer. [folk]
  - *The naming and the stones:* the inadvertent moon-sighting is called *mithyā-doṣa*, and the night itself *kalaṅk cauth* ("blemish fourth", Hindi). In Maharashtra the day is also known as *dagaḍī cautha* ("stone fourth"): whoever sees the moon throws stones onto a neighbour's roof to avert the curse. [regional / folk] The common explanation, that the stones are meant to provoke abuse that cancels the curse, is [locus not verified].
