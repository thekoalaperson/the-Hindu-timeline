# Andhakasura-Vadha: The Blind Asura and the Matrikas

> Cosmic address: a first-tier **Śaiva deva–asura war** of primordial / past-manvantara time — the asura **Andhaka** ("the blind one"), born from a single bead of sweat off [Pārvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md)'s palms when she playfully covered [Śiva](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md)'s eyes and plunged the cosmos into darkness, who later lusts after his own (unknowing) mother Pārvatī and is impaled on Śiva's **triśūla** until, drained of his blood and his pride, he is redeemed and made the gaṇa **Bhṛṅgi**. It is the charter myth of the **Saptamātṛkā / Bhairava** blood-drinking host and a twin to the [Tāraka](tarakasura-vadha.md), [Jalandhara](jalandhara-vrinda.md), and [Tripura](tripura-dahana.md) wars.

The **Andhakāsura-vadha** ("slaying of the blind asura") is one of the great Śaiva war-myths, told most fully in the *Śiva Purāṇa* (Rudra-saṃhitā, Yuddha-khaṇḍa), the *Matsya Purāṇa*, the *Kūrma Purāṇa*, the *Liṅga Purāṇa*, and the *Vāmana Purāṇa*. Its peculiar horror — an asura who is the *son* of the very goddess he lusts after — makes it the Śaiva counterpart to the Oedipal and a charter for two great cultic complexes: the **Saptamātṛkā** (Seven Mothers) summoned to lap his multiplying blood, and the redeemed-demon-as-devotee motif that fuses Andhaka with the gaṇa **Bhṛṅgi**. All major variants are catalogued below; traditions are kept separate, not merged.

---

## A. The birth of Andhaka — darkness from Pārvatī's hands

- **The covering of Śiva's eyes** — On Kailāsa, Pārvatī playfully steps behind Śiva and covers his three eyes with both palms; instantly the entire universe is plunged into darkness, for Śiva's eyes are the sun and moon and the fire of cosmic light. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Rudra-saṃhitā*; *Matsya Purāṇa*).
- **The drop of sweat** — From the heat and exertion of holding Śiva's brow, a bead of **perspiration** falls from Pārvatī's hands (or her body); from it, in the unnatural dark, is born a deformed, **blind, hairy, hideous** boy who is therefore named **Andhaka** ("the blind"). Because he is *born of darkness*, he is sightless. (*Śiva Purāṇa*; *Matsya Purāṇa*; *Kūrma Purāṇa*).
  - *Variant (Śiva's own sweat):* in some tellings the sweat is Śiva's, or the child springs from the contact of both their bodies in the dark — making him the joint offspring of the divine couple. (*Matsya Purāṇa*). [regional]
- **A child of the goddess** — Crucially, Andhaka is in origin **Pārvatī's son**, a fact that converts his later lust into the unwitting violation of his own mother and supplies the myth's moral charge. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*).

## B. Andhaka adopted by the asura Hiraṇyākṣa

- **Given to the childless Hiraṇyākṣa** — The daitya **Hiraṇyākṣa** (brother of Hiraṇyakaśipu) performs austerities for a son; Śiva grants him the dark boy. Andhaka is reared as Hiraṇyākṣa's heir and grows into a mighty asura king, his asura identity now overlaying his divine birth. (*Śiva Purāṇa*; *Matsya Purāṇa*; *Vāmana Purāṇa*).
  - *Variant (a different asura genealogy):* some Purāṇic lists make Andhaka a son or descendant of Hiraṇyākṣa by ordinary asura begetting, with the sweat-birth as a parallel/esoteric account. [disputed]
- **The boon of near-invincibility** — By long penance Andhaka wins from Brahmā a boon: he can be slain only if he lusts after a chaste woman who is in truth his own mother — a "secret door" framed so that he believes himself effectively immortal. (*Matsya Purāṇa*; *Śiva Purāṇa*). [the boon encodes the very transgression that dooms him]

## C. The lust for Pārvatī and the demand for the goddess

- **Conquest of the three worlds** — Andhaka, blind yet vastly powerful, overruns the devas, expels Indra, and seizes sovereignty over the three worlds, ruling as an asura tyrant. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*).
- **He spies the goddess on Kailāsa** — Andhaka's spies (or he himself) behold the supremely beautiful **Pārvatī** alone on the mountain while Śiva is absent; inflamed, Andhaka resolves to seize her as his consort — not knowing she is his mother. (*Śiva Purāṇa*; *Matsya Purāṇa*; *Kūrma Purāṇa*).
- **The embassy and Śiva's wrath** — Andhaka sends an emissary (in some tellings **Prahlāda**, his kinsman, who tries to dissuade him) demanding the goddess; refused, he marches on Kailāsa. Śiva confronts him to defend Pārvatī, and the deva–asura war is joined. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*; *Vāmana Purāṇa*).
  - *Variant (Prahlāda's counsel):* the *Vāmana Purāṇa* gives Prahlāda a moralizing role, warning Andhaka that the woman he covets is the World-Mother and that no good can come of the war. [late text]

## D. The blood-drop war and the summoning of the Mātṛkās

- **The impalement on the triśūla** — In single combat Śiva at last transfixes Andhaka upon his **trident (triśūla)**, lifting him aloft on its three prongs. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*; *Matsya Purāṇa*; *Kūrma Purāṇa*).
- **The multiplying blood (the core motif)** — But each **drop of Andhaka's blood** that falls to the earth instantly springs up as a new, full-grown **Andhaka-clone**; the battlefield fills with countless duplicate Andhakas, so that the more Śiva wounds him the more enemies arise — he cannot be killed by wounding alone. (*Matsya Purāṇa*; *Śiva Purāṇa*; *Kūrma Purāṇa*). [the same raktabīja-logic as the Mātṛkā/Cāmuṇḍā myths]
- **Śiva creates the blood-drinkers** — To stop the multiplication, Śiva (or the Devī) brings forth a host of fierce **Mother-goddesses / Bhairavas** whose sole task is to **drink up every drop of blood before it touches the ground**. (*Matsya Purāṇa*; *Kūrma Purāṇa*).
  - *Variant (the Saptamātṛkā emanate from the gods):* the **Seven Mothers (Saptamātṛkā)** — Brāhmī, Māheśvarī, Kaumārī, Vaiṣṇavī, Vārāhī, Indrāṇī/Aindrī, and Cāmuṇḍā — are emanated from Śiva and the great gods specifically for this war, and afterward beg to remain in the world to feed; this is a principal **charter of the Mātṛkā cult**. (*Matsya Purāṇa*; *Vāmana Purāṇa*). See [Mātṛkās & 64 Yoginīs](../08-beings-and-bestiary/matrikas-and-64-yoginis.md).
  - *Variant (a single Mātṛkā / Yogeśvarī):* other tellings have Śiva create one terrible Śakti (**Yogeśvarī** or his own Bhairava-form) from his mouth/flame to lap the blood, with the Seven Mothers as her retinue. (*Kūrma Purāṇa*; *Liṅga Purāṇa*). [regional]
  - *Variant (the Mothers grow ravenous):* a striking sequel: having drained Andhaka, the Mātṛkās' hunger turns against the worlds, and Śiva must pacify them (manifesting **Narasiṃha** or a soothing form) — a tale shared with the Devī-Māhātmya milieu. (*Vāmana Purāṇa*). [late text]

## E. The prolonged impalement and Andhaka's redemption

- **Held aloft until drained** — With the blood now drunk before it can fall, no new clones arise; Śiva keeps Andhaka **impaled on the trident for a vast span of time** (in some texts many years/aeons), suspended between earth and sky, his blood and his strength slowly exhausted. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*; *Matsya Purāṇa*).
- **The dance of Śiva (Andhakāsura-mūrti)** — In iconography Śiva spears Andhaka while dancing — the fierce **Andhakāsuravadha-mūrti** / **Andhakāntaka** form, an eight-armed Śiva piercing the small asura aloft on his trident, often catching the blood in a *kapāla* (skull-bowl). (Śilpa/Āgama tradition; *Kāraṇāgama*). [iconographic]
- **Penitence on the trident** — Drained and humbled, Andhaka — who is in truth the goddess's son — at last perceives the divine majesty of Śiva and Pārvatī; he praises Śiva with a hymn (in some texts a long stotra) and begs forgiveness, his asura pride burnt away by the long ordeal. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*; *Kūrma Purāṇa*).
- **Śiva's grace** — Moved by the asura's devotion, Śiva lowers him, forgives him, and grants him not death but **liberation through service** — transforming the demon-enemy into a devotee. (*Śiva Purāṇa*; *Liṅga Purāṇa*).

## F. Andhaka becomes the gaṇa Bhṛṅgi

- **Adoption as a gaṇa** — Andhaka, redeemed, is made a **leader of Śiva's gaṇas** and, in the dominant Śaiva tradition, is identified with the emaciated gaṇa **Bhṛṅgi** (Bhṛṅgin / Bhṛṅgiriṭi), Śiva's fervent attendant. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*; *Liṅga Purāṇa*).
  - *Variant (Bhṛṅgi as a separate figure):* an independent tradition makes Bhṛṅgi a sage so devoted to Śiva alone that he refuses to honor Pārvatī's half in the Ardhanārī, is reduced to skin-and-bone (kept upright by a third leg), and is unconnected to Andhaka. The fusion of the two is a specifically Śaiva-Purāṇic identification. [disputed]
- **From mother-lust to mother-service** — The arc closes its own circle: the asura who would have violated the goddess-mother becomes the gaṇa who serves Śiva-and-Pārvatī forever — the transgression inverted into eternal devotion. [scholarly: a redemption-of-the-enemy pattern, cf. the spared Maya in Tripura]

## G. Theological significance

- **The raktabīja blood-multiplication** — Andhaka shares the **"seed-of-blood" (raktabīja)** motif with the [Mātṛkā](../08-beings-and-bestiary/matrikas-and-64-yoginis.md)/Cāmuṇḍā cycle: an enemy that multiplies from his own spilled blood and can be stopped only by a blood-drinking goddess-host. The Andhaka war is one of the principal **charter myths of the Saptamātṛkā** and of the Bhairava blood-cults. [scholarly]
- **The blindness as moral allegory** — Purāṇic and later commentary reads **Andhaka ("the blind") as spiritual blindness / ignorance (avidyā)** — the soul that, sightless, lusts after the very Divine Mother it sprang from, and is "cured" only by being pinned and emptied; his redemption is the conquest of *tamas* by grace. [scholarly]
- **Redemption versus annihilation** — Unlike Tāraka (slain outright by Skanda) or Tripura (incinerated), Andhaka is **not destroyed but converted** — the Śaiva myth most fully expressing deliverance-by-grace of the enemy himself. (*Śiva Purāṇa*.)
- **Two clocks** — Scripturally the war sits in **primordial / a past manvantara** of deva-asura conflict (alongside Tāraka, Jalandhara, Tripura), narrated as cosmology; historically it is undatable myth. But its **cultic residue** — the Saptamātṛkā shrines, the Andhakāntaka-mūrti in temple sculpture (notably at Ellora and Elephanta), and the gaṇa Bhṛṅgi — is living, observable practice. The narrative clock and the cultic clock are distinct.

## Sources

**Primary scripture**
- *Śiva Purāṇa*, **Rudra-saṃhitā, Yuddha-khaṇḍa** — the fullest Śaiva account: Andhaka's birth from Pārvatī's sweat in the dark, his rearing by Hiraṇyākṣa, the conquest of the worlds, the lust for Pārvatī, the war with Śiva, the impalement on the triśūla, his penitence and redemption, and his adoption as the gaṇa Bhṛṅgi.
- *Matsya Purāṇa* — the covering of Śiva's eyes and the sweat-born child; the boon; the blood-multiplication and the summoning of the Mātṛkās to drink the falling blood.
- *Kūrma Purāṇa* — the impalement, the raktabīja blood-clones, and the blood-drinking Śakti/Mātṛkā host; Andhaka's praise of Śiva.
- *Liṅga Purāṇa* — the single fierce Śakti/Bhairava form created to lap the blood; Śiva's grace and Andhaka's enrollment among the gaṇas (Bhṛṅgi).
- *Vāmana Purāṇa* — Prahlāda's dissuasion of Andhaka; the Saptamātṛkā emanated for the war and their subsequent ravenousness pacified by Śiva.

**Tradition & iconography**
- Śilpa / Āgama tradition (e.g. *Kāraṇāgama*) — the **Andhakāsuravadha-mūrti / Andhakāntaka** image of dancing Śiva impaling Andhaka and catching his blood; sculptural exemplars at **Ellora** and **Elephanta**.
- Śākta / Mātṛkā tradition — the Andhaka war as a charter for the **Saptamātṛkā** and the Bhairava blood-cults.
