# Nārada's māyā — the sage becomes Suśīlā, wife of King Tāladhvaja

> **Where this sits:** A deep-dive into the great **māyā-parable** of the *Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa* (**Skandha VI, chs. 28–31**): the deathless sage **[Nārada](narada.md)**, wishing to *see* the Goddess's power of illusion, bathes in a lake, rises as the woman **Suśīlā**, marries **King Tāladhvaja**, lives out a whole married lifetime — bearing sons and grandsons and losing them all to war — and is restored to himself in what proves to have been, on the bank, a mere moment. On the cosmic clock this is a **timeless / [cross-yuga]** demonstration by Bhagavān Viṣṇu (Hari); the *text* recording it is a late-Kali Śākta Upapurāṇa (c. 9th–14th c. CE [scholarly]).
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · Up to [master index](../README.md) · Kin-stories: [Nārada](narada.md) · [Nārada-moha (Śiva Purāṇa)](narada-moha-svayamvara.md) · [Māyā & Avidyā](../05-concepts-and-dharma/maya-and-avidya.md)

The story in one line: **Nārada asks Viṣṇu to explain *māyā*; Viṣṇu answers not with words but with an experience** — the sage forgets he is Nārada, becomes a woman, tastes a full human life of love, motherhood, and bereavement, and awakens to find it was all the Goddess's illusion, spun in an instant. It is the *Devī Bhāgavata*'s definitive lesson that **even the gods are bound by Mahāmāyā**, and that the plural, grieving world is a passing dream over an unchanging Self. The tale is a Śākta cousin of the Vaiṣṇava [Brahmā-vimohana](brahma-vimohana.md) (where Kṛṣṇa deludes Brahmā) and of the *[Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha](lila-story-yoga-vasishtha.md)*'s dream-lives; distinguish it sharply from the *Śiva Purāṇa*'s [Nārada-moha](narada-moha-svayamvara.md), a **different** deluding of Nārada that ends in his curse on Viṣṇu and the Rāma-avatāra.

## The frame — where the tale sits in the Devī Bhāgavata

- **Vyāsa narrates to Janamejaya (DBP VI)** — Like the whole *Devī Bhāgavata*, the episode is spoken by **Vedavyāsa** to King **Janamejaya** (great-grandson of Arjuna) at the Dvāpara–Kali junction; Book VI is a cycle on the Goddess's overriding power, running through **Vṛtra**, **Triśiras/Viśvarūpa**, and **Nahuṣa** before closing on this proof that *Māyā rules even Viṣṇu*. (Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa VI.28–31) [Śākta frame]
- **The doctrinal thesis it illustrates** — The Śāktas' claim is bolder than the Vaiṣṇava one: it is not merely that Viṣṇu *wields* māyā over lesser beings, but that Viṣṇu himself, when he tries to *describe* Mahāmāyā to Nārada, must confess she is beyond telling — and can only make Nārada *undergo* her. The wielder here is ultimately **the Goddess (Bhagavatī / Mahāmāyā)**, Viṣṇu her instrument. (DBP VI.28) [the point the Śākta editors are pressing against the Vaiṣṇava Bhāgavata]
- **"Two clocks" note** — *In-narrative* the demonstration is timeless: Nārada is a [cross-yuga] deathless sage and the episode floats free of any single age. *Text-critically* the *Devī Bhāgavata* is a composite **Upapurāṇa**, dated by R. C. Hazra to the **11th–12th c. CE**, by Tracy Pintchman to **c. 1000–1200 CE**, in a broader range of **9th–14th c.** — never to be confused with the "eternal, Vyāsa-authored" claim of the tradition. [scholarly] [late text]

## Nārada's question — "show me your māyā" (DBP VI.28)

- **The sage's request** — Wandering the worlds as ever, **Nārada** comes before **Bhagavān Hari (Viṣṇu)** and, having heard endlessly *of* the Goddess's Mahāmāyā, asks the Lord to **reveal it to him directly** — to let him see the power by which the One appears as the deluded many. (DBP VI.28)
- **Viṣṇu's warning** — The Lord replies that **Māyā cannot be told, only felt**; that even the gods do not fathom her; and (in the telling's irony) half-consents, knowing the sage's curiosity will teach him more sharply than any discourse. He sets out with Nārada across the country. (DBP VI.28)
- **The thirst and the lake** — Journeying, Viṣṇu feigns fatigue and thirst near a beautiful **lake / tank (saras)** on the outskirts of a city, and asks Nārada to **go down and fetch water**. This ordinary errand is the hinge of the whole parable — the threshold between the sage's true self and the dream. (DBP VI.28)

## The plunge — Nārada rises as the woman Suśīlā (DBP VI.28)

- **The transforming bath** — Nārada steps into the water and **immerses himself**; the instant he rises, he has **forgotten that he is Nārada** and stands on the bank as a **beautiful young woman**, with no memory of the sage, the vīṇā, or the Lord waiting nearby. The male ascetic is simply *gone*; the woman is all that is. (DBP VI.28)
  - *On the name:* the woman is called **Suśīlā** ("the well-conducted, the virtuous") in this and most retellings; some English summaries leave her unnamed or give other names, since the text foregrounds her *condition*, not her label. Treat the name as traditional rather than as a fixed verse-datum. [the corpus catalogs, does not resolve, such variance]
- **The total amnesia** — Crucially, the illusion is **complete, not partial**: unlike a disguise, Suśīlā does not "know" she is really Nārada. This is the theological core — *māyā is not a costume but an amnesia*; the jīva under Māyā forgets its own divine nature exactly so. (DBP VI.28) [the doctrinal payload of the plunge]

## The married life — Tāladhvaja, sons, and grandsons (DBP VI.29)

- **King Tāladhvaja finds her** — **Tāladhvaja** ("he of the palmyra-palm banner"), king of that country, comes upon the solitary, weeping woman by the lake, is smitten by her beauty, and asks who she is and whose she is; she can only say she does not know. He takes her home and **marries her**, making Suśīlā his queen. (DBP VI.29)
- **A long, happy household** — Suśīlā and Tāladhvaja live in deep mutual love for many years; she bears the king **many sons**, who grow, marry, and give her **grandsons** — a full, ripening domestic life of festivals, weddings, and increase, the whole ordinary sweetness of *saṃsāra* experienced from the inside. (DBP VI.29)
- **The felt duration** — Within the dream, **decades pass**: an entire human lifetime of attachment accrues, love layered on love, until Suśīlā is a matriarch surrounded by children and children's children. The reader alone remembers that on the lake-bank a god still waits with a water-pot. (DBP VI.29) [the "lifetime as a moment" motif — see §"the awakening"]

## The catastrophe — war, and the cremation ground (DBP VI.29)

- **The war** — A **conflict breaks out** between Tāladhvaja and a rival king; in the fighting **all of Suśīlā's sons and grandsons are slain**, and her kinsmen with them — the household she spent a lifetime raising is annihilated. (DBP VI.29)
- **The wailing on the burning-ground** — Suśīlā goes to the **cremation ground**, gazes on the heaped pyres of her sons and grandsons, and **breaks down in unbearable grief**, lamenting her ruined life — the very peak of *saṃsāric* sorrow, attachment turned to anguish. Her weeping is the dramatic climax of the parable. (DBP VI.29)
- **The old brahmin's consolation** — There **Viṣṇu appears as an aged brahmin** (still unrecognized), sits beside the grieving woman, and gently reasons with her: *why weep for these bodies, who are not truly your own — kinship itself is Māyā's knot; the world's relations are a river's meeting and parting of driftwood.* He counsels her to rise and **perform the funerary ablution** in the nearby tank for the departed. (DBP VI.29) [the jñāna-teaching delivered inside the illusion, before it is broken]

## The awakening — restored to Nārada in a moment (DBP VI.29)

- **The second bath dissolves the spell** — Suśīlā descends into the same lake to make the water-offering — and rising from it is **Nārada once more**, the male sage, memory flooding back: the vīṇā, the errand, the Lord. The woman, the marriage, the sons, the grief — **all vanish** in the instant, along with the very pyres and corpses she had been weeping over. (DBP VI.29)
- **Viṣṇu on the bank, smiling** — **Bhagavān Hari stands where Nārada left him**, water-pot still in hand, gently smiling — for on *his* clock **only a moment has passed** while Nārada lived out an entire lifetime; the illusion consumed no real time at all. The felt decades were spun and unspun in the space of an errand. (DBP VI.29)
  - *Variant (Tāladhvaja's own bewilderment):* in the fuller telling **King Tāladhvaja** too comes searching for his lost queen and is confronted with the truth — his "wife," his "sons," his whole grief, were as unreal as Nārada's; the delusion is shown to bind not one dreamer but a shared world. [DBP VI.29; the shared-dream detail]
- **The lesson stated** — Viṣṇu tells Nārada plainly: **this is Mahāmāyā** — the power he asked to *see*. It cannot be argued; it can only be **suffered and woken from**. The sage who moves untouched through every loka has now known, from the inside, the amnesia, love, and bereavement of an ordinary bound soul — and knows why the scriptures call the world a dream. (DBP VI.29; the doctrinal glory of Mahāmāyā then elaborated in chs. 30–31) [the moral the frame drives home]

## Reading the parable — what it teaches, and its kin

- **Māyā as amnesia, not disguise** — The engine of the story is that Nārada *forgets he is Nārada*. This is the exact model of the individual soul (*jīva*) under **avidyā**: not a god play-acting a mortal, but consciousness that has genuinely lost the memory of its own nature — and can recover it only by "bathing again," i.e., by knowledge/grace. See the doctrine mapped in [Māyā & Avidyā](../05-concepts-and-dharma/maya-and-avidya.md). [scholarly]
- **The compression of time** — A whole lifetime lived in a moment is the parable's signature and its most quoted feature; the same "long life inside an instant" structure powers the *[Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha](lila-story-yoga-vasishtha.md)* dream-lives (Līlā, Gādhi) and, in Vaiṣṇava register, the year that is a moment in the [Brahmā-vimohana](brahma-vimohana.md). The corpus keeps these **parallel, not merged** — each belongs to its own text and theology. [scholarly]
- **The gender-transformation motif** — Nārada's becoming a woman by entering water places the tale in the same thematic family as **[Ilā ↔ Sudyumna](../03-lineages-vamsha/ila-sudyumna.md)** (the king who becomes a woman in Śiva's grove), though the *purpose* differs utterly: here the sex-change is a vehicle for the *experience of bound life*, not a curse or boon in its own right. [regional/thematic parallel — do not conflate the two episodes]
- **Śākta vs Vaiṣṇava ownership of the deluder** — In the *Devī Bhāgavata*'s framing the ultimate agent is **the Goddess (Mahāmāyā)**, Viṣṇu merely her instrument who cannot even *describe* her; contrast the Vaiṣṇava [Brahmā-vimohana](brahma-vimohana.md), where **Kṛṣṇa himself** is the sovereign wielder of *yoga-māyā*. The **same narrative logic** is claimed by rival theologies — a deliberate sectarian counter-move, characteristic of the Devī Bhāgavata's "real Bhāgavata" polemic. [disputed] [Śākta reading]
  - *Variant (popular retelling):* many modern folk and discourse versions attach this very story to **Kṛṣṇa** ("Nārada asks Kṛṣṇa to explain māyā"), collapsing the Śākta Viṣṇu-frame into a Vaiṣṇava one; the *scriptural* locus in the Devī Bhāgavata names **Bhagavān Hari / the Goddess**, not the cowherd Kṛṣṇa. [folk] [oral tradition]
