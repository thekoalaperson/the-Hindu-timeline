# Jalandhara and Vrinda

> Cosmic address: a first-tier **Śaiva deva–asura war** of primordial / past-manvantara time — the asura **Jalandhara**, born of the fire of [Śiva](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md)'s third eye cast into the ocean, made near-invincible by the *pativratya* (chastity-power) of his wife **Vṛndā**, undone when **Viṣṇu** impersonates him to break her vow, then slain by Śiva. It is the **charter myth** of the **Tulsī** plant, of the **Tulsī-Vivāha** festival, and of the city **Jālandhara** (a [Śakti Pīṭha](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md)). Belongs to the same tier as Andhaka, Tāraka, and Tripura.
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · See also: [Asura Wars & Roster](../08-beings-and-bestiary/asura-wars-and-roster.md) · [Tripura-dahana](tripura-dahana.md) · [Śiva Myth-Cycle](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md)

The **Jalandhara–Vṛndā** narrative is one of the great Śaiva war-myths, told most fully in the *Śiva Purāṇa* (Rudra-saṃhitā, Yuddha-khaṇḍa) and the *Padma Purāṇa*, with parallels in the *Skanda* and *Devī Bhāgavata*. Its theological core is a paradox: the asura is **unkillable while his wife's chastity is intact**, so the gods win not by force but by the violation of a *pativratā*'s vow — a morally fraught deception (cf. the same *māyā*-by-Viṣṇu pattern in [Bhasmāsura/Vṛkāsura](bhasmasura-and-vrikasura.md) and [Samudra Manthana / Mohinī](samudra-manthana.md)). All major variants are catalogued below; traditions are kept separate, not merged.

---

## A. Birth and rise of Jalandhara

- **Origin from Śiva's brow-fire** — When Indra (with Bṛhaspati) once provokes Śiva's wrath, Śiva opens his **third eye**; the **fire/energy** that bursts forth is too potent to be consumed. Śiva (or Brahmā) directs it into the **ocean (samudra)**, where the sea-god **Samudra** gestates it; a boy is born and named **Jalandhara** ("born of/holding water," *jala* = water). (*Śiva Purāṇa, Rudra-saṃhitā, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*).
  - *Variant (the brow-fire's secondary use):* The same released energy, in some tellings, is what Brahmā later shapes into the demon — the "fire that Indra could not bear to look upon." (*Śiva Purāṇa*.)
- **Reared by the Ocean; Brahmā's prophecy** — The infant grips Brahmā's beard so fiercely that tears come; Brahmā names him **Jālandhara** and decrees he will be an immense, near-unconquerable king of the asuras, slain only by Śiva himself. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*).
- **King of the asuras; marriage to Vṛndā** — Grown to vast power, Jalandhara is crowned lord of the daityas. He weds **Vṛndā** (Bṛndā), a *pativratā* of supreme chastity — in most accounts the **daughter of the asura Kālanemi**. Her unbroken fidelity becomes the **invisible armor** that makes her husband unkillable. (*Śiva Purāṇa*; *Padma Purāṇa*).
  - *Variant (Vṛndā's parentage):* Some texts make her a daughter of Kālanemi; others give her simply as a *daitya* maiden of perfect virtue. [regional]

## B. The provocations and the war

- **The grievance over the Churning** — Jalandhara learns that the **Samudra Manthana** ([Churning of the Ocean](samudra-manthana.md)) despoiled his "father" the Ocean of its treasures — **Lakṣmī, the moon, the airāvata, the pārijāta, and the amṛta** taken by the gods. As the Ocean's son he demands their return; refused, he goes to **war on the devas**. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*).
- **Defeat of Indra and the gods** — Jalandhara routs Indra's host; the devas flee. He becomes effectively sovereign of the three worlds, ruling with (in many tellings) a measure of justice — a "righteous tyrant" whose only fatal flaw is hubris. (*Śiva Purāṇa*).
- **The embassy to Śiva; lust for Pārvatī** — Emboldened, Jalandhara sends a messenger (often **Rāhu**) to Śiva on Kailāsa demanding that Śiva surrender his "jewel," **Pārvatī**, as the Ocean's due. Enraged, Śiva manifests from his fury a being (in some texts the origin of **Kīrtimukha** / the *grāha*) that nearly devours Rāhu. War between Śiva and Jalandhara becomes inevitable. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*).
  - *Variant (Pārvatī's counter-ruse):* While Śiva battles, Jalandhara assumes **Śiva's own form** to deceive Pārvatī and seize her; she detects the fraud and withdraws (saving her chastity), then incites Viṣṇu to repay the trick in kind upon Vṛndā — establishing a tit-for-tat symmetry. (*Śiva Purāṇa*; *Padma Purāṇa*). [the symmetry is the moral hinge of the tale]

## C. The breaking of Vṛndā's vow — Viṣṇu's deception

- **The invincibility lock** — In open battle Śiva cannot finish Jalandhara, because **Vṛndā's *pativratya*** shields him: so long as her vow of fidelity holds, no weapon can kill her husband. The gods turn to **Viṣṇu**. (*Śiva Purāṇa*; *Padma Purāṇa*).
- **The illusion of her husband's death** — Viṣṇu (with the gods, often via sages or *māyā*-monkeys) conjures a vision before Vṛndā: her husband **slain, his severed head** brought before her, to shake her resolve. (*Padma Purāṇa*; *Śiva Purāṇa*).
- **Viṣṇu impersonates Jalandhara** — Viṣṇu then takes the **exact form of Jalandhara** and approaches the grieving Vṛndā as her returning, living husband. Believing him her lord, she **embraces him** — and in that instant her vow of sole-fidelity is broken; her *pativratya*-power lapses. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*; *Padma Purāṇa*).
  - *Variant (degree of violation):* Tellings range from a mere touch/embrace that nullifies the vow to fuller cohabitation; the more decorous Purāṇic versions stress that the *intent and the disguise*, not consummation, are what break the *pativratya*. [disputed]
- **The simultaneous death of Jalandhara** — At the very moment Vṛndā's chastity fails, Jalandhara's protection vanishes on the battlefield. Śiva, with the **discus (cakra) struck from his own toe** — or in some texts the **Sudarśana / a Śiva-cakra** — **beheads Jalandhara**; his radiant energy (*tejas*) is reabsorbed, returning into Śiva. (*Śiva Purāṇa, Yuddha-khaṇḍa*).
  - *Variant (the weapon):* The cakra "created from Śiva's toe" is the *Śiva Purāṇa* image; other tellings name the **Sudarśana-cakra** or have Śiva slice him with a fiery discus born of his own power. [regional]

## D. Vṛndā's curse, self-immolation, and rebirth as Tulsī

- **Vṛndā discovers the fraud** — When the disguise drops and Vṛndā realizes she has been deceived by **Viṣṇu**, she **curses him**: that he shall become a **stone (the śālagrāma/black śilā)**, and (in many versions) be **separated from his own wife** and forced to wander — the seed of the [Rāma](ramayana/README.md) exile, where Viṣṇu-as-Rāma loses Sītā. (*Padma Purāṇa*; *Śiva Purāṇa*).
  - *Variant (curses multiplied):* Some texts add curses making him **stone (śālagrāma), grass (kuśa/darbha), and a tree** — etiologies for the sacred objects of Vaiṣṇava worship. (*Padma Purāṇa*.) [late text]
- **Self-immolation** — Stricken with grief and shame, Vṛndā **enters fire (sati / self-immolation)** and dies. (*Śiva Purāṇa*; *Padma Purāṇa*).
- **Rebirth as the Tulsī plant** — From her ashes (or by Viṣṇu's boon, contrite over the deception) springs the **Tulsī (holy basil)** plant; Viṣṇu declares that **Tulsī will be dearest to him above all flowers and leaves**, eternally united with him as the **śālagrāma-stone** (his form by her own curse). The mutual curse becomes a mutual blessing — Lakṣmī-like devotion. (*Padma Purāṇa*; *Śiva Purāṇa*).
  - *Variant (Tulsī as a separate goddess):* In the *Brahma-Vaivarta* and some Vaiṣṇava streams, **Tulasī/Vṛndā** is a distinct goddess (sometimes conflated with a gopī of [Vṛndāvana](../06-sacred-geography/mathura-vrindavan-braj.md)), and the basil is her embodiment. [regional / sectarian]

## E. Charter myths and afterlife

- **Tulsī-Vivāha** — The episode is the charter for **Tulsī-Vivāha**, the ceremonial **marriage of the Tulsī plant to Viṣṇu (as śālagrāma or as Kṛṣṇa)**, celebrated on **Prabodhinī/Devotthāna Ekādaśī (Kārttika, śukla-pakṣa)**, marking the close of *Cāturmāsya* and the opening of the Hindu wedding season. Vṛndā's union with Viṣṇu is ritually re-enacted. (Festival tradition, rooted in *Padma Purāṇa*).
- **Veneration of Tulsī and śālagrāma** — The myth grounds the daily Hindu **worship of the Tulsī plant** (the courtyard *tulsī-vṛndāvana*) and the pairing of **Tulsī leaf with śālagrāma** in Viṣṇu-pūjā. (Ritual tradition.)
- **The city Jālandhar** — The Punjab city **Jālandhar** is traditionally named for the asura Jalandhara, said to be his capital/realm; it is counted among the **[51 Śakti Pīṭhas](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md)** (where a body-part of Satī fell), enshrining the goddess **Tripuramālinī / Jvālāmukhī-associated** *pīṭha*. (Sthala-purāṇa & Śākta tradition.) [regional]
  - *Variant (etymology):* Some derive the name from *jālandhara* "(land) within the waters" rather than the asura directly; the asura-etymology is the dominant local tradition. [disputed]

## F. Theological significance

- **Victory by deception, not force** — Like [Vāmana](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md), [Mohinī](samudra-manthana.md), and the [Bhasmāsura/Vṛkāsura](bhasmasura-and-vrikasura.md) tales, the asura is undone by **Viṣṇu's *māyā***, not weapons — but here uniquely the casualty is an innocent *pativratā*, making it the most morally costly of the deception-myths. The tradition itself registers the wrong via Vṛndā's curse, which **rebounds on Viṣṇu** (his stone-form, his loss of Sītā). [scholarly]
- **Pativratya as cosmic power** — The myth literalizes the Purāṇic conviction that a wife's **chastity (*sati*/*pativratya*) is a tangible force** capable of making even an asura invincible — the same power-logic as Anasūyā, Sāvitrī, and Sītā. (*Śiva Purāṇa*.)
- **Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava interlock** — Śiva supplies the demon's origin (brow-fire) and the killing blow; Viṣṇu supplies the stratagem; the goddess (Pārvatī's near-violation answered by Vṛndā's actual violation) frames the whole. The tale binds the **Trimūrti** into one tightly-meshed action and births a **Vaiṣṇava cult object (Tulsī/śālagrāma)** out of a **Śaiva war** — a striking sectarian synthesis. [scholarly]
- **Two clocks** — Scripturally the war sits in **primordial / a past manvantara** of asura-conflicts (alongside Andhaka, Tāraka, Tripura), narrated as deva-asura cosmology; historically it is undatable myth — but its **ritual residue** (Tulsī worship, Tulsī-Vivāha, the Jālandhar pīṭha) is living, observable practice. The narrative clock and the festival clock are distinct.

## Sources

**Primary scripture**
- *Śiva Purāṇa*, **Rudra-saṃhitā, Yuddha-khaṇḍa** — the fullest Śaiva account: Jalandhara's birth from the third-eye fire cast into the ocean, Brahmā's prophecy, marriage to Vṛndā, the grievance over the Churning, the war on the devas, the demand for Pārvatī, Pārvatī's and Vṛndā's mirrored deceptions, Viṣṇu's impersonation, and Śiva's slaying of Jalandhara with the toe-born cakra.
- *Padma Purāṇa* — the Vṛndā/Tulsī material: the illusion of Jalandhara's death, Viṣṇu's disguise, Vṛndā's curse (śālagrāma-stone; separation from his wife → Rāma's loss of Sītā), her self-immolation, and rebirth as the **Tulsī** plant; charter for **Tulsī-Vivāha**.
- *Skanda Purāṇa* — parallel telling of the Jalandhara war and the Tulsī etiology; *sthala-purāṇa* associations.
- *Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa* — Śākta framing of the conflict and of the goddess-power (Vṛndā's *pativratya*; the Śakti-pīṭha tradition).

**Tradition & ritual**
- *Brahma-Vaivarta Purāṇa* and Vaiṣṇava lore — **Tulasī/Vṛndā as goddess**; pairing of Tulsī with śālagrāma and Kṛṣṇa.
- Festival tradition — **Tulsī-Vivāha** on **Prabodhinī Ekādaśī (Kārttika)**; daily Tulsī worship.
- *Sthala-purāṇa* / Śākta tradition — the city **Jālandhar** as Jalandhara's realm and a **[Śakti Pīṭha](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md)**.
