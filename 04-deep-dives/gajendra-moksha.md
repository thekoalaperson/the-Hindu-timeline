# Gajendra Mokṣa — the Liberation of the Elephant-King

> Cosmic address: a *Manvantara-frame tale* narrated by Śuka to Parīkṣit at the opening of the 8th Canto, set in the **[4th — Tāmasa — Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-04-tamasa.md)**, when Viṣṇu appeared as **Hari**, son of Harimedhas and Hariṇī (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.1.30*). It is one of the great liturgical hymns of [Viṣṇu](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) and the paradigm of *śaraṇāgati* (total surrender).
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · See also: [Concepts: karma / saṃsāra / mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md) · [Trimūrti and Tridevī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md)

The episode of **Gajendra Mokṣa** ("the liberation of the lord-of-elephants") is told in the **Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Canto 8, Chapters 2–4** (SB 8.2–8.4). It frames a metaphysical lesson — that no created being, however mighty, can save itself; deliverance comes only through unconditional surrender — inside a tale of an elephant seized by a crocodile, a thousand-year struggle, a hymn, and a god who flies to the rescue. It is recited daily across Vaiṣṇava traditions and is one of the most beloved of all Purāṇic *stotras*. Verse numbers below follow the BBT/Vedabase numbering of the Bhāgavata.

---

## Why it sits in the Manvantara frame

- **Narrative placement (SB 8.1.27–8.1.33)** - Canto 8 of the Bhāgavata catalogs the fourteen Manus and their Manvantaras. Listing the 4th Manu, **Tāmasa**, Śuka mentions that in that age the Lord was born as **Hari** to Harimedhas and Hariṇī and "freed Gajendra from the crocodile" (*harir ity āhṛto yena gajendro mocito grahāt*, 8.1.30). **Parīkṣit then asks to hear the story in full** (8.1.31–32), and Śuka narrates it as an inset before resuming the Manu list with the 5th Manu, Raivata, at 8.5 (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.1.27–8.1.33, 8.5.2*).
- **A bridge tale** - It immediately precedes the [Samudra Manthana](samudra-manthana.md) cycle (SB 8.5–8.12, set in the 6th, Cākṣuṣa, Manvantara — 8.5.7–10), positioning Viṣṇu as the constant rescuer who acts both for an individual devotee (Gajendra) and for the cosmos (the churning, the Kūrma and Vāmana avatāras). (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Canto 8*)

---

## The setting (SB 8.2)

- **Mount Trikūṭa** - A magnificent mountain ten thousand *yojanas* high and as broad, surrounded by the Ocean of Milk, with three principal peaks of **iron, silver and gold**, its caves frequented by Siddhas, Gandharvas, Apsarās and other celestials, full of forests, streams and lakes (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.2.1–8.2.8*).
- **The garden Ṛtumat** - In a valley of Trikūṭa lay **Ṛtumat**, a garden belonging to **Varuṇa**, the sporting-ground of the celestial women, its trees in flower and fruit in every season; in it was a vast lake of golden lotuses, lilies and water-birds (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.2.9–8.2.19*).
- **Gajendra, lord of the herd** - The leader of the elephants who lived in the mountain's forest came, with his she-elephants and calves, heated, in musth (*mada*), shaking the mountain with his weight; other beasts fled at his scent. Thirsty, he entered the lake, bathed, drank, and sprayed his wives and young with water — "like a householder" attached to his family, "deluded by the Lord's *māyā*" (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.2.20–8.2.26*).

## The seizure and the thousand-year struggle (SB 8.2)

- **The crocodile's attack** - "Impelled by destiny" (*daiva-codita*), a powerful crocodile (*grāha*; also *nakra*, 8.2.29) angrily seized the elephant by the foot (*caraṇe*). The elephant, supremely strong, fought with all his might to free himself (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.2.27*).
  - *Note on the word:* the Bhāgavata says *grāha* / *nakra*; the name *makara* used in some retellings is not in these verses. It is Pothana's word in Telugu (*makari*, *makarēndruḍu*, "lord of makaras," who "seized the elephant-king": *okka makarēndruḍu ibharāju noḍisi paṭṭe*) (Pothana, *Āndhra Mahābhāgavatamu*, Skandha 8, Gajendra-mokṣaṇa section). [retelling usage] [regional]
- **The herd's helplessness** - Seeing their lord dragged by superior strength, his she-elephants cried out in distress, and the other elephants tried to pull him from behind but could not free him (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.2.28*). Gajendra himself later reflects that neither his kinsmen elephants nor his wives could save him (*8.2.32*).
  - *Retelling elaboration:* many popular versions add that the herd finally **abandoned** him as his end neared — a stock image of how kin fail at death. The verses describe their failure to help, not a desertion. [retelling variant]
- **A thousand years** - Pulling one another into and out of the water, the elephant and the crocodile fought "for a thousand years" (*samāḥ sahasram*), and the gods looked on in wonder (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.2.29*). Over that long time the elephant's mental and bodily strength wasted, while the crocodile, in its own element, grew stronger (*8.2.30*). The text gives the figure as plain years; readings of them as "celestial years" in some modern retellings are not in the verse. [retelling gloss]
- **The turning inward** - Helpless, Gajendra reflected long and resolved: since no kin could save him and the crocodile was "the noose of the Ordainer," he would take refuge in the Supreme, from whom even Death runs in fear (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.2.31–8.2.33*). Fixing his mind in his heart, he then chanted a supreme prayer "learned in a former birth" (*prāg-janmany anuśikṣitam*) (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.1*).

## The Gajendra-stuti — the hymn of surrender (SB 8.3)

- **The prayer of the lord-of-elephants** - Gajendra's hymn (the **Gajendra-stuti**, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.2–8.3.29*) is the heart of the episode and one of the most-recited *stotras* in the tradition. It is chanted with the mind fixed within (8.3.1); the famous lifted lotus comes **after** it, when the Lord appears (8.3.32, below).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa 1.194; Vāmana Purāṇa 85):* the elephant takes the lotus in his trunk **first** and then recites his hymn. In the Viṣṇudharmottara he "offered it with devotion to Kṛṣṇa" and then spoke the stotra (*gṛhītvā sa karāgreṇa sarasaḥ kamalottamam / nivedya bhaktyā kṛṣṇāya gajaḥ stotram udīrayat*, 1.194.28); in the Vāmana he takes up a golden lotus and, seeking release, recites it (*pragṛhya puṣkarāgreṇa kāñcanaṃ kamalottamam … gajaḥ stotram udīrayat*, 85.30). [variant]
- **To the un-named Absolute** - Gajendra does **not** invoke a sectarian name but addresses *that Supreme Being from whom the universe has its conscious life*: *oṃ namo bhagavate tasmai yata etac cid-ātmakam / puruṣāyādi-bījāya pareśāyābhidhīmahi* — roughly "Oṃ, obeisance to that Lord from whom this [world] is conscious; on the Person, the primal seed, the supreme ruler, we meditate" (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.2*; the BBT translation's insertion of "Vāsudeva" here is a translator's gloss, not in the verse).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa 1.194; Vāmana Purāṇa 85):* the elephant's hymn in these texts is a different stotra and **names** the god throughout — Nārāyaṇa, Puruṣottama, Acyuta, Vāsudeva, Keśava, Janārdana, Madhusūdana, holder of Śārṅga, discus, sword and mace (*namo namaḥ kāraṇavāmanāya nārāyaṇāya … śrī-śārṅga-cakrāsi-gadā-dharāya*, VDh 1.194.29–48; Vāmana 85.31–59, which opens *oṃ namo mūlaprakṛtaye ajitāya mahātmane* and adds Govinda). The two stotras share many verses. The Bhāgavata's point that the nameless prayer is why only Hari came (8.3.30) is therefore particular to its own telling. [variant]
- **Themes of the hymn** - It praises the Lord as the ground, material and maker of the world yet beyond cause and effect (8.3.3), the witness who manifests and withdraws the cosmos by his *māyā* (8.3.4), what remains beyond the darkness of dissolution (8.3.5), unknowable even to gods and sages, like an actor in costume (8.3.6), without birth, deed, name or form yet taking forms for the world's sake (8.3.8–9), the inner witness / *kṣetrajña* (8.3.10, 8.3.13), hard to reach for those attached to self, home, wealth and kin (8.3.18), and in the *neti-neti* climax "neither god nor demon, human nor beast, female, male nor neuter … what remains after all negation" (*niṣedha-śeṣa*, 8.3.24) (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.3–8.3.29*).
- **The cry of the surrendered** - Its emotional core is self-surrender: "I take refuge in him" (*taṃ prapadye*, 8.3.3); obeisance to the one who "frees a surrendered beast like me from the noose" (*mādṛk prapanna-paśu-pāśa-vimokṣaṇāya*, 8.3.17); to "the protector of the surrendered" (*prapanna-pālāya*, 8.3.28); "I have come to that Lord" (8.3.29) — making it the scriptural archetype of **śaraṇāgati** / **prapatti** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.2.32–8.2.33, 8.3.3, 8.3.17, 8.3.28–8.3.29*).
- **Asking for more than rescue** - Strikingly, Gajendra says he does not even wish to go on living in an elephant's body, "covered within and without by ignorance"; what he asks is liberation from the covering of the self that time does not destroy (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.25*; cf. 8.3.19).
- **No demigod responds — only Hari** - Because the prayer described the attributeless Supreme and named no particular deity, **Brahmā and the other gods** (*brahmādayaḥ*), each identified with a distinct form, did not come forward; **Hari**, who embodies all the gods, appeared (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.30*). (The BBT translation's list "Brahmā, Śiva, Indra and Candra" expands *brahmādayaḥ*; translator's gloss.)

## The rescue (SB 8.3, end)

- **Viṣṇu on Garuḍa** - Understanding Gajendra's plight and hearing the hymn, the Lord, disc in hand (*cakrāyudha*), came swiftly on the Veda-bodied **Garuḍa** (*chandomayena garuḍena*), accompanied by gods singing his praise (*divijaiḥ saha saṃstuvadbhiḥ*) (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.31*).
- **The lotus and the name** - Seeing Hari in the sky on Garuḍa, the agonized Gajendra lifted a lotus in his trunk and, with difficulty, cried: **"Nārāyaṇa, teacher of all, Lord, obeisance to you!"** (*nārāyaṇākhila-guro bhagavan namas te*) — the first time in the episode that he utters a deity's proper name, the hymn having used only titles such as *bhagavat*, *brahman* and *īśvara* (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.32*; cf. 8.3.2, 8.3.9, 8.3.18, 8.3.30).
- **The Lord's descent and the Sudarśana** - Seeing him in pain, the unborn Lord **leapt down at once** from Garuḍa (*sahasāvatīrya*), **drew the elephant out of the lake together with the crocodile**, and, as the gods watched, freed Gajendra by **splitting open the crocodile's mouth with his discus** (*grāhād vipāṭita-mukhād ariṇā*) (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.33*). The BBT renders this "severed the crocodile's mouth from its body."
  - *Variant (Pothana's Telugu Bhāgavatam; many retellings):* the discus **cuts off the crocodile's head** and takes its life (*bhīmaṃbai tala druñci prāṇamula bāpeṃ jakram*; prose: *sudarśanaṃbu makari tala druñcu*) (Pothana, *Āndhra Mahābhāgavatamu*, Skandha 8, Gajendra-mokṣaṇa section — padya number not verified). [regional]
  - *Note:* the Vāmana Purāṇa's Sanskrit agrees with the Bhāgavata rather than with the beheading. Viṣṇu pulls both animals out of the lake and, on dry land, **tears the crocodile apart** with his discus (*sthalasthaṃ dārayāmāsa grāhaṃ cakreṇa mādhavaḥ*, Vāmana Purāṇa 85.62–63). V. D. N. Rao's condensed English says "cuts off the neck," which is a translator's rendering.
- **In art** - The image of a god descending from his mount to save a drowning beast is among the most depicted in Vaiṣṇava art (the "Gajendra-mokṣa" iconography of Viṣṇu on Garuḍa wielding the discus). (Iconographic tradition.)

## The two curse-backstories (SB 8.4)

When the crocodile was slain it resumed a celestial form; **Śuka** (not Viṣṇu) then tells Parīkṣit the past lives of both combatants (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4.3–8.4.12*). Other Purāṇas tell the backstories differently — **variants are catalogued below.**

### The elephant: King Indradyumna, cursed by Agastya

- **Indradyumna the Pāṇḍya king** - Gajendra had formerly been **Indradyumna**, a king of the **Pāṇḍyas**, "best of the Draviḍas," devoted to Viṣṇu-vows (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4.7*).
- **The slight to Agastya** - Having become an ascetic with matted hair at a hermitage on a *kulācala* (a principal mountain range; the BBT glosses it as the **Malaya hills** — translator's gloss), Indradyumna was worshipping Hari at the hour of worship while keeping a **vow of silence** (*gṛhīta-mauna-vrata*). The sage **Agastya** arrived with his disciples; seeing the king silent and offering no reception, he grew angry (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4.8–8.4.9*). He cursed him: "This ill-minded, untrained man has insulted a brāhmaṇa; let him enter blind darkness, a dull-witted elephant" (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4.10*).
- **The grace within the curse** - Śuka adds that the royal sage accepted the curse as destiny (*diṣṭam*); born into the elephant-womb that destroys self-memory, he nevertheless retained the memory (of his prayer) through the power of his past worship of Hari (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4.11–8.4.12*; cf. 8.3.1).
  - *Commentarial reading:* the curse is really a blessing, since it brought Indradyumna (and Hūhū) to the Lord within a single life. (A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami, purports to SB 8.4.1 and 8.4.3–4.) [commentarial gloss]
  - *Variant (Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa 1.194):* The elephant was not a human king but the **gandharva Hāhā**, a chief among gandharvas, cursed by **Devala** to become an elephant; after the crocodile's death he regains gandharva form (*gajo gandharvatāṃ gataḥ*), attains release and goes to heaven (*divam*); touched by Viṣṇu, both receive divine garlands and garments and go by a celestial car (*vimāna*) to the gods' abode (*tridaśālaya*), while Viṣṇu departs. This is not the *sārūpya* form of the Bhāgavata (*Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa 1.194.19, 1.194.51–52, 1.194.64–66*, Mārkaṇḍeya to Vajra). The text states no cause for Devala's curse; a singing-contest quarrel between Hāhā and Hūhū, offered in secondary retellings (after S. A. Dange, *Purāṇic Myth and Culture*), is not in the verses. [variant]

### The crocodile: the gandharva Hūhū, cursed by Devala

- **Hūhū the gandharva** - The crocodile at once took a wondrous form: he had been **Hūhū**, "best of the gandharvas," freed now from the **curse of Devala** (*mukto devala-śāpena hūhūr gandharva-sattamaḥ*). He bowed, sang the Lord's praises, circumambulated him, and returned to his own world freed of sin (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4.3–8.4.5*). The Bhāgavata verses do **not** say what provoked the curse.
- **The prank on the sage** - The well-known cause comes from commentary and retelling: Hūhū, sporting in the water with women, pulled the leg of the sage **Devala** as he bathed, and the angry sage cursed him to become a crocodile. (BBT chapter summary to SB 8.4; popular retellings — locus in a Purāṇic verse not found.) Some retellings add that Devala was worshipping Sūrya at the time — locus not verified. [commentarial / retelling]
- **The promised deliverance** - When Hūhū begged pardon, the sage foretold that he would be freed when Viṣṇu delivered Gajendra. (BBT chapter summary to SB 8.4.) V. D. N. Rao's condensed English of the Vāmana Purāṇa likewise has the muni foretell that Nārāyaṇa himself would slice off the crocodile's head. The Sanskrit of Vāmana Purāṇa 85 has no such forecast; it says only that Hūhū became a crocodile by Devala's curse and went to heaven after being killed by Kṛṣṇa (85.64). [commentarial / condensed translation]
  - *Variant (Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa 1.194):* Hūhū becomes the crocodile "through Devala's curse" (*hūhūr jagāma grāhatvaṃ śāpād vai devalasya tu*), but here his partner-in-curse **Hāhā** is the elephant, and the lake lies on **Trikūṭa**, the triple summit of the *kulācala* **Śṛṅgavān** in Jambudvīpa. Its three peaks are of gold (the Sun's abode), silver (the Moon's), and all jewels in the middle (Brahmā's), not the Bhāgavata's iron, silver and gold (*Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa 1.194.2–5, 1.194.18, 1.194.21*; cf. *Bhāgavata* 8.2.2–3). [variant]
- **Both liberated** - Thus the single act of the Sudarśana releases **both** beings at once: Hūhū regains his gandharva form and departs (*8.4.3–8.4.5*), and Gajendra, by the Lord's touch, is freed from the bondage of ignorance and receives a form like the Lord's — four-armed, in yellow garments (*sārūpya*) (*8.4.6*). The Lord, praised by gandharvas, siddhas and gods, returns on Garuḍa to his own abode (*sva-bhavanam*) taking Gajendra with him as an attendant (*pārṣada*) (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4.13*; "Vaikuṇṭha" is the usual name for that abode in the tradition).

## The fruit of recitation (phala-śruti, SB 8.4)

- **Śuka's phala-śruti** - Hearing this account of the elephant-king's release brings heaven and fame, removes the taint of Kali, and **destroys bad dreams** (*duḥsvapna-nāśa*); the twice-born who wish their good should rise in the morning, purified, and recite it to allay bad dreams (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4.14–8.4.15*).
- **The Lord's own promise** - Viṣṇu, pleased, tells Gajendra before all: those who rise at the end of night and remember him, Gajendra, this lake, mountain and grove, the Milk Ocean, Śvetadvīpa, his emblems and weapons, Garuḍa, Śeṣa, Śrī, Brahmā, Nārada, Śiva, Prahlāda, his avatāras, the sacred rivers, Dhruva, the seven ṛṣis and others are freed from all sin; and to those who praise him at night's end **with Gajendra's hymn**, he grants the highest state (*vipulāṃ gatim*) at death. He then blows his conch and mounts Garuḍa (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4.16–8.4.26*).
- **Place in daily liturgy** - On the strength of these verses the Gajendra-stuti / Gajendra-mokṣa is widely recited at dawn and grouped among the foremost Viṣṇu hymns, and is popularly held to give protection in fear and mortal danger. (Vaiṣṇava liturgical tradition; living practice.) [living tradition]

## Other tellings and regional traditions

- **Vāmana Purāṇa 85 (*Gajendramokṣaṇa*)** - Pulastya tells Nārada the four *japyas* that Prahlāda recited, and the Gajendra-mokṣaṇa comes first (85.1–3). Trikūṭa, son of Meru, rises from the Milk Ocean, with peaks of gold (the Sun's), silver (the Moon's) and a jewelled peak that is Brahmā's seat, and a lake of golden lotuses (85.4–18). The elephant is seized by the crocodile. Devoted to Nārāyaṇa through practice in birth after birth (*janmajanmāntarābhyāsāt*, 85.28), he takes up a golden lotus and recites a named stotra (85.30–59). Viṣṇu comes on Garuḍa, pulls both out and tears the crocodile apart with the discus on land (85.61–63). The crocodile was **Hūhū**, cursed by Devala (85.64). Touched by Viṣṇu, the elephant becomes a man with a divine body (85.65, 85.73–74). Viṣṇu (85.68–72) and then Brahmā (85.79–82) declare the fruits of recitation. The chapter does **not** name the elephant's former identity. The "Pāṇḍya king" and the beheading appear only in V. D. N. Rao's condensed English ("chapter 19"). (Vāmana Purāṇa 85, *pulastya-nārada-saṃvāde … gajendramokṣaṇaṃ nāma pañcāśītitamo 'dhyāyaḥ*, chapter number as in the sanskritdocuments transcription; other editions number it differently. Rao's condensation places it in Prahlāda's tīrtha-yātrā, a frame not checked in the Sanskrit.) See [Vāmana Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/vamana-purana.md).
- **Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa 1.194** - A separate *Gajendramokṣaṇa* chapter (Mārkaṇḍeya to Vajra) with its own stotra and with **Hāhā and Hūhū** as elephant and crocodile — see the variants above. [variant]
- **Pothana's Telugu Bhāgavatam** - In Bammera Pothana's *Āndhra Mahābhāgavatamu* (15th c., Skandha 8), Viṣṇu, hearing the cry in Vaikuṇṭha, rushes out so fast that he tells Lakṣmī nothing, takes up neither conch nor discus, does not wait for Garuḍa, and still holds the end of Lakṣmī's garment — Lakṣmī, the conch, the discus and Garuḍa follow after him, and the discus cuts off the crocodile's head. The scene opens with the famous *ala vaikuṇṭhapuramulō* padya, in which the Lord, sporting with Ramā by the nectar-lake in his Vaikuṇṭha palace, hears the elephant's *pāhi pāhi*. The haste is the next padya, *sirikiñ jeppaḍu*: he does not tell Śrī, does not take conch and discus in his hands, does not call his retinue, does not ready Garuḍa, and does not let go of Śrī's garment-end. Then *tanaveṇṭan siri* has Śrī, the palace women, Garuḍa, bow, mace, conch and discus, Nārada and Viṣvaksena follow him, and *bhīmaṃbai tala druñci* has the discus behead the crocodile. The Sanskrit Bhāgavata instead has him come on Garuḍa, disc in hand (8.3.31). (Pothana, *Āndhra Mahābhāgavatamu*, Skandha 8, Gajendra-mokṣaṇa section; padya numbers not verified.) [regional]
- **Sacred sites** - Several places claim the episode: in Tamil tradition the Divya Deśam of **Kapisthalam (Tirukkavittalam)**, whose deity is worshipped as Gajendra-varada ("boon-giver to Gajendra"); elsewhere other local sthala-purāṇas name their own lakes. See [108 Divya Deśams](../06-sacred-geography/108-divya-desams.md). [regional]

## Theological significance: the model of śaraṇāgati

- **Helplessness as qualification** - Gajendra is the paradigm devotee precisely because he is utterly powerless: his strength and herd fail, and only when he turns to the Lord does grace act. This is the scriptural template for **prapatti / śaraṇāgati** in Śrī-Vaiṣṇava and Gauḍīya thought. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.2.31–8.3.33*; theological tradition.)
- **The non-sectarian call** - Because Gajendra invokes the Absolute without a sectarian name, the tale is read as teaching that sincere surrender — not correct labeling — draws the divine response (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.3.30*).
- **Crocodile of saṃsāra** - Allegorically: the elephant is the *jīva* (soul), the lake is *saṃsāra*, the crocodile is the grip of material entanglement / time / death, the herd is unreliable worldly relations, and the lotus offered to the arriving Lord is the soul's surrender. The text itself compares Gajendra to an attached householder (8.2.26) and calls the crocodile "the noose of the Ordainer" (8.2.32). (Standard commentarial allegory; see [karma / saṃsāra / mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md).)

**Primary scripture**
- *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* (Śrīmad Bhāgavatam), **Canto 8, Chapters 1–4** — the Tāmasa-manvantara notice and Parīkṣit's request (8.1.27–33); setting on Trikūṭa, the seizure and the thousand-year struggle (8.2); the Gajendra-stuti and rescue (8.3); Hūhū/Devala, Indradyumna/Agastya, the liberation and the two *phala-śrutis* (8.4).
- *Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa* **1.194** (*Gajendramokṣaṇa*) — the Hāhā/Hūhū variant, lotus before the hymn, named stotra.
- *Vāmana Purāṇa* **85** (*Gajendramokṣaṇa*, Pulastya to Nārada; vulgate numbering) — the Vāmana telling, read in Sanskrit; V. D. N. Rao's condensed English used only where noted.
- Bammera Pothana, *Āndhra Mahābhāgavatamu*, Skandha 8, Gajendra-mokṣaṇa section — the Telugu regional telling, read in the Telugu Wikisource text (no padya numbers).
