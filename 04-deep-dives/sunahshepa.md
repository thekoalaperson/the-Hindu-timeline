# Sunaḥśepa — the Boy Offered in Sacrifice

> Cosmic address: a *kṣatriya/brāhmaṇa* legend of the Solar (Ikṣvāku) line, set in the present **Vaivasvata Manvantara** of the current Kalpa, in an early generation of the Ayodhyā dynasty. It survives first as a Vedic ritual *ākhyāna* (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa) and is later retold as an inset in the [Rāmāyaṇa Bāla-kāṇḍa](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md). A deep-dive episode-file. Up: [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · Related: [Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md).

The tale of **Sunaḥśepa** ("dog's tail" / "dog's penis") is one of the oldest narrated stories in Sanskrit literature and the locus classicus for Hindu attitudes to *narabali* (human sacrifice). Its primary form is **Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.13–18** (of the Ṛgveda), embedded in the *Rājasūya* royal-consecration liturgy, where it is recited at the king's anointing. A separate, divergent retelling appears at **Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla-kāṇḍa, sarga 61–62** (Critical Ed.; ~1.61–62 in vulgate). The variant policy here is to catalogue **all** versions attributed to source. The story turns on a vow to **Varuṇa**, a substitute victim bought for cattle, and rescue by the mantras of **Viśvāmitra**, ending in adoption and the splitting of Viśvāmitra's lineage.

## Version I — Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.13–18 (the primary, Vedic form)

The frame is ritual: this is the *narāśaṃsī* / *ākhyāna* prescribed for recitation by the **Hotṛ** priest at the **Rājasūya**, with the **Adhvaryu** giving responses, so that the consecrated king is purified of the very sin the story dramatizes. [scholarly]

- **Hariścandra's childlessness and the vow** - King **Hariścandra Vaidhasa** of the Ikṣvāku line has a hundred wives but no son. On the counsel of **Nārada**, he prays to **Varuṇa** for a son, vowing: *"If a son be born to me, I will sacrifice him to thee."* Varuṇa grants the boon and a son, **Rohita**, is born (AB 7.13–14). Chronological placement: an early Solar-dynasty generation, present Vaivasvata Manvantara.
- **The deferred sacrifice** - Varuṇa repeatedly demands the promised offering; Hariścandra repeatedly **postpones** it on successive ritual pretexts — *wait till he is ten days old, till his teeth come, till they fall, till they regrow, till a kṣatriya's armour fits him* — buying years of delay (AB 7.14).
- **Rohita refuses and flees** - When at last told he is to be the victim, the grown **Rohita takes his bow and flees to the forest (araṇya)**, wandering a full year (AB 7.14).
- **Varuṇa's curse — dropsy** - For the broken vow, **Varuṇa seizes Hariścandra with dropsy / belly-disease (jalodara, a swollen-belly illness)** (AB 7.14). The king's suffering is the engine that forces the substitution.
- **Indra's refrain — "Keep moving"** - Five times across five years, as Rohita thinks to return and ransom his father, the god **Indra** appears (as a brahmin) and urges him onward with the famous verse **"caraiveti, caraiveti"** — *"Wander, keep wandering!"* — praising the wanderer's life. This wandering-refrain is one of the celebrated passages of the Brāhmaṇa (AB 7.15).
- **Ajīgarta sells his son** - In his sixth year Rohita meets a starving brahmin ṛṣi, **Ajīgarta Sauyavasi**, with three sons. Rohita offers **a hundred cows for one son** to die in his place. The father will not give the **eldest** (Madhyama is sometimes given as eldest's name) and the mother will not give the **youngest (Śunaḥpuccha)**; so the **middle son, Sunaḥśepa**, is sold (AB 7.15). [The three brothers' names — Śunaḥpuccha (tail), Sunaḥśepa (penis), Śunolāṅgūla (tail) — are all "dog" names.]
- **Brought to the altar** - Rohita brings Sunaḥśepa to his father; **Varuṇa accepts the substitute** ("a brahmin is worth more than a kṣatriya") and the *Rājasūya*-style sacrifice is arranged with the boy as victim (AB 7.15–16).
- **The four priests refuse** - The officiants are **Viśvāmitra (Hotṛ), Jamadagni (Adhvaryu), Vasiṣṭha (Brahman), and Ayāsya (Udgātṛ)** (the four great priestly families). **No one will bind the boy to the sacrificial post (yūpa)** (AB 7.16).
- **Ajīgarta binds his own son for more cattle** - The father **Ajīgarta volunteers to bind Sunaḥśepa for another hundred cows**, and does so (AB 7.16).
- **Ajīgarta offers to butcher his son** - When the bound boy still has no slaughterer, **Ajīgarta offers to kill his own child for a third hundred cows**, and advances **whetting his knife**, drunk on the promised cattle — the story's image of paternal depravity (AB 7.16). This shamelessness becomes proverbial. [The Brāhmaṇa condemns Ajīgarta sharply.]
- **Sunaḥśepa's recourse to the gods** - Seeing he will be slain *"as though he were not a man,"* Sunaḥśepa resolves to take refuge in the deities and praises them with **Ṛgvedic hymns**, in ritual sequence: **Prajāpati, Agni, Savitṛ, Varuṇa**, then Agni again, the **Viśvedevas**, and finally **Uṣas (the Dawn)** (AB 7.16–18). The hymns are identified with actual Ṛgveda sūktas (notably RV 1.24–30 attributed to "Sunaḥśepa Ājīgarti").
- **The bonds fall away** - With each verse to **Varuṇa** a fetter loosens (Varuṇa's *pāśa*, his noose, is the disease and the binding alike); with the **final hymn to Uṣas** the **last bond falls**, the boy is **freed**, and **Hariścandra is simultaneously cured of his dropsy** (AB 7.18).
- **The Soma-pressing redemption** - Sunaḥśepa then completes the morning Soma-pressing for the priests, sealing the rescue within the rite (AB 7.17).
- **Adoption as Devarāta** - **Viśvāmitra adopts Sunaḥśepa**, renaming him **Devarāta** ("god-given"), and makes him **eldest** of his sons and heir to his Vedic knowledge and the Gāthins' lineage (AB 7.17–18).
- **Sunaḥśepa rejects Ajīgarta** - Ajīgarta tries to **reclaim** his son for the cows; Sunaḥśepa refuses, declaring that a man who took up a knife to kill his own child, and did it for cattle, is no father — choosing Viśvāmitra instead (AB 7.18).
- **Viśvāmitra's hundred sons split** - Viśvāmitra orders his **hundred sons** to accept Devarāta as eldest. The **fifty younger sons agree** and are blessed; the **fifty elder sons refuse** and are **cursed to become the ancestors of the despised, beyond-the-pale peoples** — named as **Āndhras, Puṇḍras, Śabaras, Pulindas, and Mūtibas** ("dasyus in great number"), said to be exiled beyond Āryāvarta (AB 7.18). [This is the Brāhmaṇa's etiology for those border peoples — culturally loaded, [disputed] as ethnography.]

## Version II — Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla-kāṇḍa 61–62 (the epic retelling)

Told by **Śatānanda** to Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa as part of the legend of **Viśvāmitra**'s greatness, this version **changes the king, the cause, the price, the kinship, and the saving hymns**. See [ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md). [variant — late relative to AB]

- **King Ambarīṣa, not Hariścandra** - The patron is **King Ambarīṣa of Ayodhyā**, performing a great sacrifice (Rām. Bāla 61). The Varuṇa-vow/childlessness frame is absent.
- **Indra steals the victim** - **Indra carries off the sacrificial animal (the consecrated victim/horse)**. The officiating priest tells Ambarīṣa he must **recover the beast or procure a human victim** to avert ruin (Rām. Bāla 61).
- **The search and Ṛcīka** - Searching, Ambarīṣa comes upon the sage **Ṛcīka** (Bhārgava) on Mount Bhṛgutuṇḍa with his wife and **three sons**. The king offers **a hundred thousand cows (and gold)** for a son to be the victim. Ṛcīka will not give the **eldest**; the **mother will not give the youngest**; so the **middle son, Sunaḥśepa**, offers/agrees to go (Rām. Bāla 61).
  - *Variant — kinship:* here Sunaḥśepa is a **Bhārgava (son of Ṛcīka)** and is **Viśvāmitra's sister's son (maternal nephew)**, not a stranger bought from Ajīgarta. There is **no Ajīgarta** in this telling, and thus none of the father's knife-whetting depravity. [variant]
- **Refuge with Viśvāmitra at Puṣkara** - En route, at **Puṣkara**, Sunaḥśepa meets his uncle **Viśvāmitra** and begs to be saved. Viśvāmitra, moved, first asks his **own sons to take the boy's place** as victim (Rām. Bāla 62).
- **The sons refuse; the curse** - Viśvāmitra's sons **scornfully refuse**, comparing self-sacrifice to **eating one's own/ dog's flesh**. Enraged, Viśvāmitra **curses them to be reborn as outcaste, dog-flesh-eating Vāsiṣṭhas (or Muṣṭikas) for a thousand years** — the epic's parallel to the AB lineage-split (Rām. Bāla 62).
- **The two secret hymns to Indra & Viṣṇu** - Instead of the AB sequence of Ṛgvedic deities, Viśvāmitra teaches Sunaḥśepa **two divine *gāthās* / mantras** (in some texts hymns to **Indra and Viṣṇu**, or to **Indra and Upendra**) to recite while bound at the post (Rām. Bāla 62).
- **Indra grants long life** - At the sacrifice Sunaḥśepa recites the mantras; **Indra is pleased, appears, grants him long life**, and rewards Ambarīṣa with the **fruit of the completed sacrifice**. The boy is spared (Rām. Bāla 62).
  - *Variant — the saving deity:* the rescue is effected through **Indra/Viṣṇu** (epic) rather than the climactic hymn to **Uṣas** (Brāhmaṇa). [variant]

## Secondary retellings & echoes

- *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa, 9th skandha):* the **Hariścandra–Rohita–Sunaḥśepa** version is retold within the Hariścandra cycle; Sunaḥśepa is again bought and rescued and adopted by Viśvāmitra as **Devarāta**, reaffirming the Vedic frame over the epic one. [late text]
- *Variant (Sāṅkhyāyana / Śāṅkhāyana & other Brāhmaṇa/Śrauta sources):* the Sunaḥśepa *ākhyāna* recurs in the *Rājasūya* liturgical tradition tied to recitation of **RV 1.24–30**, the hymns ascribed to **Sunaḥśepa Ājīgarti** as their seer (*ṛṣi*) — i.e., the rescued boy is canonized as a Ṛgvedic poet. [scholarly]
- *Variant (Purāṇic genealogies — Viṣṇu, Bhāgavata):* **Devarāta** is listed among Viśvāmitra's lineage as adopted eldest, transmitting the **Gāthina/Kauśika** Vedic line; the cursed elder sons are made progenitors of various dasyu/border peoples, echoing AB 7.18. [late text]
- *Folk/etymological echo:* the three brothers' "dog" names (**Śunaḥpuccha**, **Sunaḥśepa**, **Śunolāṅgūla**) have invited symbolic readings (the bound victim as a sacrificial "dog"); David Gordon White and others read astral/ritual symbolism into the dog-and-dawn motifs. [scholarly]

## Interpretation & significance (scholarly)

- **Ritual function** - The whole *ākhyāna* is liturgically recited at the **Rājasūya** so the newly consecrated king is freed from sin and debt — Sunaḥśepa's release from Varuṇa's noose models the king's release. [scholarly]
- **A charter against human sacrifice** - Many scholars read AB 7.13–18 as **substituting** Vedic recitation/Soma for *narabali*, dramatizing its **abolition** rather than endorsing it; others read it as evidence the practice once existed. [disputed]
- **Three models of fatherhood** (David Shulman) - the tale contrasts **Hariścandra's reluctant, loving** fatherhood, **Ajīgarta's mercenary cruelty**, and **Viśvāmitra's conditional, adoptive** fatherhood. [scholarly]
- **Varuṇa's noose (pāśa)** - the disease (dropsy), the binding to the *yūpa*, and the cosmic *ṛta*-debt are one and the same "bond," loosed verse by verse — a unified image of sin-as-fetter. [scholarly]
