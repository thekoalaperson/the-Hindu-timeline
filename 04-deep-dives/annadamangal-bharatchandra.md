# Annadāmaṅgal — Īśvarī Pāṭunī and the Vidyā–Sundara Romance

> Cosmic address: a **regional-Kali** node on the **historical clock** — the courtly climax of Bengal's *maṅgal-kāvya* tradition, composed 1752 CE for the Nadia court, glorifying the goddess **Annadā (Annapūrṇā)**; only its Śaiva-Śākta frame (Śiva–Pārvatī, Annapūrṇā of Kāśī) touches the cyclic clock.
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Genre home: [Regional & Oral Epics](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md) · Sister cycle: [Caṇḍīmaṅgal — Kālketu & Śrīmanta](chandimangal-kalketu-and-srimanta.md) · The goddess: [Devī-Māhātmya & the Great Goddess](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)

The **Annadāmaṅgal** (*Annadā-maṅgal*, "the auspicious poem of Annadā the Food-Giver") of **Bhāratcandra Rāy "Guṇākar"** — completed **1752 CE** (1159 Bengali San) at the court of **Rāja Kṛṣṇacandra Rāy of Nadia** (Krishnanagar) — is the last and most polished of the great Bengali *maṅgal-kāvyas*, the courtly consummation of a folk-devotional genre. Where the [Caṇḍīmaṅgal](chandimangal-kalketu-and-srimanta.md) and [Manasāmaṅgal](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/manasa.md) are village-and-market poems sung over pūjā nights, Bhāratcandra's is a **connoisseur's poem** — witty, erudite, dense with Sanskrit ornament and Persian-laced idiom — that nonetheless carries the genre's oldest hooks into Bengali memory: the ferryman **Īśvarī Pāṭunī** and his proverbial boon, and the tunnel-romance of **Vidyā and Sundara** whom **Kālī** saves at the execution-ground. Its goddess is **Annadā = Annapūrṇā**, the form of Pārvatī who holds the ladle and feeds even Śiva.

---

## Two Clocks — Text, Poet, and Frame

- **Historical clock (composition)** — Bhāratcandra Rāy (c. **1712–1760** [scholarly]), a Brahmin of **Pheṇo-Bhūrśuṭ** (Bhurishrestha, Howrah district), driven by poverty and a spell in Burdwan's revenue jail, found patronage with **Rāja Kṛṣṇacandra Rāy** (1710–1783) of Nadia, who conferred on him the title **"Rāy-Guṇākar."** He completed the Annadāmaṅgal in **1752 CE**. (Annadāmaṅgal, colophon) [scholarly] [regional]
- **Scriptural / cyclic clock (the poem's frame)** — the Deva-frame is **timeless Śaiva-Śākta myth**: Dakṣa's sacrifice, Satī's self-immolation, Pārvatī's penance and marriage to Śiva, and the **Kāśī legend of Annapūrṇā** who feeds the begging Śiva — belonging not to dated history but to the recurring divine cycle (see [Satī, Dakṣa & Pārvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md)). [regional]
- **Documented history inside the poem** — the third part, **Mānsiṃha**, is set in real **c. 1600 CE**: the Mughal general **Rāja Mān Siṃha** (Akbar's Rajput commander), the Bengal rebel **Pratāpāditya of Jessore**, and Emperor **Jahāṅgīr** — history refracted through devotional-legendary lens to glorify the patron's ancestor. The two clocks must not be collapsed: the goddess-frame is cyclic myth, the Mān Siṃha material is (embroidered) Kali-yuga chronicle. [scholarly] [regional]
- **The maṅgal logic** — like every *maṅgal-kāvya*, the plot *is* the propagation of a cult: the goddess Annadā, slighted or ignored, manifests her power (the Pāṭunī theophany, the rescue of Sundara, the deliverance of Bhavānanda) and ends with her **worship established** and her patrons enriched. Bhāratcandra inherits the frame from Mukundarāma's Caṇḍīmaṅgal even as he transforms its tone from rustic realism to courtly sophistication. [scholarly]

## Part I — Annadāmaṅgal: the Goddess Annadā (Annapūrṇā)

- **The Deva-frame** — the poem opens with the origin and glory of the goddess as **Annadā/Annapūrṇā**, retelling the Śaiva myth-cycle: **Dakṣa's yajña**, **Satī's** death, her rebirth as **Pārvatī**, her austerities, and her marriage to **Śiva** — the standard Purāṇic scaffolding that every maṅgal poet lays before the local action. (Annadāmaṅgal, Annadā-maṅgal khaṇḍa) [regional]
- **Annapūrṇā of Kāśī — the anna theology** — the goddess's defining act is **feeding Śiva**: in the Kāśī legend the ascetic Śiva, bowl in hand, begs alms and **Annapūrṇā (Annadā) fills his begging-bowl** — the doctrine that the Great Goddess is the giver of *anna* (food, grain, sustenance) on which even the Absolute depends. This is the theological heart that names the poem. (Annadāmaṅgal; cf. [Kāśī / Vārāṇasī](../06-sacred-geography/kashi-varanasi.md)) [regional]
- **Īśvarī Pāṭunī and the ferry** — the poem's single most beloved episode: the goddess, wandering in disguise as a radiant young woman, comes at night to the bank of the **Bhāgīrathī (Gaṅgā)** and asks the low-caste ferryman **Īśvarī Pāṭunī** to row her across. Wary of her beauty and the hour, he ferries her; mid-stream or on the far bank she **reveals her divine four-armed form** and offers him a boon. (Annadāmaṅgal, Pāṭunī episode) [regional]
- **"Dudhe-bhāte" — the proverbial boon** — the simple boatman, asked what he desires, wants no wealth or heaven but only: **"āmār santān jena thāke dudhe-bhāte"** — *"may my children live ever in milk and rice."* The line became one of the most-quoted blessings in the Bengali language, a byword for humble, sufficient prosperity — the folk voice speaking through Bhāratcandra's courtly verse. (Annadāmaṅgal, Pāṭunī episode) [regional] [folk]
- **The goddess enters Bengal** — the Pāṭunī theophany is the narrative hinge by which Annadā's worship crosses from Kāśī into **Bengal**, preparing the ground for her patronage of **Bhavānanda Majumdār** and, through him, the Nadia Rāj — tying the goddess's advent directly to the patron's lineage. (Annadāmaṅgal) [regional]
- *Parallel:* the deity in disguise ferried or served by a humble mortal who then wins grace is a maṅgal-genre commonplace — compare the goddess's descent to hunter and merchant in the [Caṇḍīmaṅgal](chandimangal-kalketu-and-srimanta.md); here the reward is not a kingdom but the archetypal Bengali wish for a full rice-bowl. [scholarly]

## Part II — Vidyāsundar (Kālikāmaṅgal): the Tunnel Romance

- **The Kālikāmaṅgal inheritance** — the middle part is a self-contained **Vidyāsundar** (also **Kālikāmaṅgal**, "the auspicious poem of Kālī"), a pre-existing Bengali romance-cycle Bhāratcandra reworked to its most famous form; its goddess is **Kālī**, whose devotee-hero she rescues. (Annadāmaṅgal, Vidyā-Sundar khaṇḍa) [regional]
- **Vidyā of Burdwan** — **Vidyā**, daughter of **Rāja Vīrasiṃha** of **Bardhamān (Burdwan)**, is a paragon of beauty and Sanskrit learning who vows to wed only a suitor who can **defeat her in poetic-scholarly debate** — none has succeeded. (Annadāmaṅgal, Vidyā-Sundar khaṇḍa) [regional]
- **Sundara of Kāñcī** — **Sundara**, handsome and learned prince of **Kāñcī (Kanchipuram)** in the south and a fervent **devotee of Kālī**, hears of Vidyā and travels north to Burdwan to win her, arriving in disguise. (Annadāmaṅgal) [regional]
- **Hīrā the Mālinī** — Sundara lodges with the flower-woman (mālinī) **Hīrā**, through whose garland-baskets he sends Vidyā **flowers wrapped in clever love-verses**, opening a secret courtship; Hīrā the go-between is one of the poem's most vivid comic-realist figures. (Annadāmaṅgal) [regional]
- **The suraṅga (tunnel)** — by Kālī's favour Sundara **digs a secret underground tunnel** (*suraṅga*) from Hīrā's house into Vidyā's guarded palace-chamber; the couple meet in scholarly contest, she is won, and they marry by the **gāndharva rite**, Sundara passing nightly through the tunnel. (Annadāmaṅgal) [regional]
- **Discovery** — Vidyā **conceives**, the pregnancy betrays the secret, and the outraged Vīrasiṃha sets the **kotwāl (city police-chief)** to hunt the unknown intruder; tracing the tunnel, the kotwāl seizes Sundara and presents him to the king **as a thief**, his royal identity unknown. (Annadāmaṅgal) [regional]
- **The death-sentence and Kālī's rescue** — condemned to execution, Sundara is led to the **śmaśāna (cremation/execution ground)**, where he pours out a hymn to **Kālī**; the goddess **manifests and stays the executioners**, revealing him as the prince of Kāñcī. The terrified Vīrasiṃha releases him, recognizes the match, and **Vidyā and Sundara are properly wed** — the maṅgal pattern of the devotee saved on the scaffold, exactly as Kālī/Caṇḍī rescues Śrīmanta in the [Caṇḍīmaṅgal](chandimangal-kalketu-and-srimanta.md). (Annadāmaṅgal, Vidyā-Sundar khaṇḍa) [regional]
- **Sanskrit and Bengali antecedents** [scholarly] — the Vidyāsundar is not Bhāratcandra's invention but the last flowering of an older tradition:
  - *Source-legend (Sanskrit):* the tale descends ultimately from the **Caurapañcāśikā ("Fifty Stanzas of the Thief")** attributed to the Kashmiri poet **Bilhaṇa** (11th c.), whose poet-lover is caught with a princess and sentenced to death — the neutral deliverance later recast as Kālī's rescue. [scholarly] [disputed]
  - *Bengali predecessors:* earlier Vidyāsundar / Kālikāmaṅgal poets include **Sābirid Khān** (16th c.), **Kṛṣṇarām Dās**, and the Śākta poet **Rāmprasād Sen** — Bhāratcandra's version simply eclipsed them all. (Kālikāmaṅgal tradition) [scholarly] [regional]
- *Afterlife:* the Vidyāsundar became a staple of **jātrā (folk-opera)** and, in the 19th century, one of the very first **Bengali printed books** and a subject of Kalighat painting and Battala woodcut — its erotic wit made it both wildly popular and a target of Victorian-era moral censure. [regional] [scholarly]

## Part III — Mānsiṃha: the Bhavānanda Frame

- **The historical setting** — the third part shifts to **c. 1600 CE** and the Mughal conquest of Bengal: **Rāja Mān Siṃha**, Akbar's great Rajput general, campaigns against the rebel **Pratāpāditya**, the Bāra-Bhuiyā chief of **Jessore/Yaśohar**. (Annadāmaṅgal, Mānsiṃha khaṇḍa) [scholarly] [regional]
- **Bhavānanda Majumdār** — the poem's mortal hero is **Bhavānanda Majumdār**, ancestor of the patron Kṛṣṇacandra, a devotee of **Annadā** who aids Mān Siṃha — guiding, provisioning and supporting the Mughal army — and is protected throughout by the goddess's grace. (Annadāmaṅgal, Mānsiṃha khaṇḍa) [regional]
- **Delhi, Jahāṅgīr, and the imperial grant** — Mān Siṃha takes Bhavānanda (with the captured Pratāpāditya) to the imperial court; through a sequence in which **Annadā's power delivers Bhavānanda** from danger and imprisonment, Emperor **Jahāṅgīr** grants him honours and **zamindari rights** — the poem's charter-myth for the founding of the **Nadia Rāj**. (Annadāmaṅgal, Mānsiṃha khaṇḍa) [regional]
- **Poem as dynastic panegyric** — the whole Annadāmaṅgal is thus framed to **glorify Kṛṣṇacandra** by sacralizing his forefather's fortunes: the goddess who fed Śiva and blessed a ferryman also raised the poet's patron's house — devotion, poetry and political legitimacy fused. (Annadāmaṅgal) [scholarly]
  - *Historical caution:* the Mān Siṃha–Bhavānanda–Jahāṅgīr sequence is **devotional legend built on a historical skeleton**, not a reliable chronicle; the campaign against Pratāpāditya is historical, but the goddess-driven episodes and the exact terms of the family's ennoblement are courtly embellishment. [disputed] [scholarly]

## Why Annadāmaṅgal Matters

- **The genre's climax and courtly turn** — Bhāratcandra converts the *maṅgal-kāvya* from a sung village-vrata form into a **literary showpiece**: layered *payār* and *tripadī* metres, virtuoso Sanskrit ornament, and a trilingual ear that switches to **Persian- and Hindustani-laced diction** for the Mughal characters of the Mānsiṃha part. He is widely reckoned the finest Bengali poet before the modern age. [scholarly]
- **The proverbial "dudhe-bhāte"** — Īśvarī Pāṭunī's wish that his children live "in milk and rice" passed out of the poem into everyday Bengali speech as the archetypal humble blessing — the folk register that keeps this most courtly of maṅgals rooted in common life. [regional] [folk]
- **A poem read against its own patron** — later critics note the paradox that a poem commissioned as **dynastic flattery** produced its most durable lines in the mouths of a low-caste ferryman and a scheming flower-woman — the maṅgal genre's persistent voice from below surviving even its courtly makeover. [scholarly]

> Note: the Annadāmaṅgal is **regional Bengali literature of the historical Kali clock** (composed **1752 CE** [scholarly]). Its Deva-frame (Śiva–Pārvatī, Annapūrṇā) is timeless Śaiva-Śākta myth; its Vidyāsundar is a legendary "present"; its Mānsiṃha part embroiders **documented c. 1600 CE** history.
