# Śiva loses to Pārvatī at dice — the dyūta-krīḍā and the Śabarī huntress

> Where this sits: the **closing diptych of the Kedāra-khaṇḍa** — the "engine-room" Śaiva narrative that names the whole [Skanda Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md). On Kailāsa, in the timeless domestic register of the divine marriage, **Pārvatī beats Śiva at dice**, strips him of every ornament, curses the tale-bearing gaṇa, and drives the humiliated god into the forest — then wins him back in the disguise of a **Śabarī (tribal) huntress**. It is the textual charter of the **Umā-Maheśvara dyūta-krīḍā** ("the dice-play of Śiva and the Goddess") sculpted across medieval Śaiva India.
>

## The text and where it sits

- **Source and location** — the whole cycle is **Skanda Purāṇa, Māheśvara-khaṇḍa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa, chapters 34–35** (AITM / Motilal vulgate, tr. G. V. Tagare). Chapter 34 (*"Śiva Loses to Pārvatī in a Game of Dice"*) is the game and the rupture; chapter 35 (*"Pārvatī as Śabarī Brings Back Śiva; Śiva's Coronation"*) is the reconciliation — it is the **concluding chapter of the Kedāra-khaṇḍa**. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34–35*)
- **The khaṇḍa it closes** — the Kedāra-khaṇḍa (~35 chapters) is the **Śaiva mythic core** of the Skanda Purāṇa, carrying the Śiva–Pārvatī marriage, the burning of [Kāma](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/kamadeva-and-minor-deities.md), and the [Skanda / Tārakāsura](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md) charter. The dice-game is deliberately placed as its **coda**: after the marriage is made and the war-god begotten, the text stages the marriage's most human quarrel — a game between husband and wife — and resolves it in Śiva's re-coronation. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34–35*)
- **Frame and instigator** — the narrator is **Sūta** to the ṛṣis of Naimiṣa; the mischief-maker inside the story is **Nārada**, who interrupts the couple's embraces on Kailāsa to propose that there is "great happiness in the game of dice" (*akṣa-krīḍā*), and sets them playing. The whole disaster is thus a sage's *līlā*-seed. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34.61*)
- **Dating [scholarly]** — the Skanda's Śaiva core is placed **c. late-6th / early-7th c. CE (Vārāṇasī)**, but the seven-khaṇḍa vulgate's individual khaṇḍas accreted as late as the **13th–15th c. CE**; the Kedāra-khaṇḍa in its printed form belongs to this developed medieval Śaiva layer [late text]. Traditionally, of course, it is timeless Śiva-vākya transmitted by Vyāsa. The two clocks are kept apart throughout. [scholarly]

## The game — Śiva loses everything (chapter 34)

- **The stakes** — goaded by Nārada, Śiva and Pārvatī wager the god's own regalia. He stakes and loses, in turn, the **crescent moon** on his brow, his **great necklace** (the serpent **Vāsuki**), his **pair of ear-rings** (the nāgas **Kambala and Aśvatara**), his **elephant-hide** garment, and finally his **loincloth** — the ascetic reduced, throw by throw, to nakedness. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34.76 ff.*)
- **"Fraudulent play"** — the text says plainly that Pārvatī defeats him by *chala* (guile/fraud): the Goddess, who is Prakṛti and Māyā herself, cannot lose a game of chance to her own consort, because the dice answer to her will. The humour and the theology are the same joke — **the God of yoga cannot master the play of the Goddess**. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34*)
- **The mounting quarrel** — as Śiva loses, **Nārada laughs**, and **Bhṛṅgī** — Śiva's fanatically Śiva-only gaṇa, who worships the god and not the Goddess — intervenes verbally to defend his lord's supremacy and to insist Śiva was cheated. Pārvatī, already victorious, turns "excessively furious" at the laughter and the partisan witness. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34.72 ff.*)

## The curse of Bhṛṅgī and Śiva's flight (chapter 34)

- **Bhṛṅgī cursed to be fleshless** — enraged at his bias, Pārvatī **curses Bhṛṅgī to be devoid of flesh and blood** — reduced to skin and bone. This is the Kedāra-khaṇḍa's etiology for the gaṇa's famous **emaciated, three-legged skeletal form**: unable to stand on two shrunken legs, he is granted (in the wider tradition) a third for a prop. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34.115–116*)
  - *Variant (the witness cursed):* a widespread **[folk]** telling makes the biased referee not Bhṛṅgī but **Nandin (Nandi) the bull**, who declares Śiva the winner and is cursed by Pārvatī with an incurable disease, relenting only when he propitiates [Gaṇeśa](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md). The corpus records both; the printed Kedāra-khaṇḍa names **Bhṛṅgī**, not Nandi. [folk] [regional]
- **The stripping of Śiva** — victorious and furious, Pārvatī humiliates her husband by **removing his remaining garment**, leaving the great ascetic exposed before the assembly. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34*)
- **Śiva storms off** — shamed and angry, **Śiva abandons Pārvatī and Kailāsa** and withdraws to the **Siddhavaṭa forest**, where he sits in yogic posture, alone, refusing his wife and his home — the god in a sulk that is also a return to his native asceticism. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34.139–153*)

## Pārvatī as the Śabarī huntress (chapter 35)

- **The Goddess's dejection and stratagem** — bereft on Kailāsa, Pārvatī resolves to fetch Śiva back not as his queen but by seduction in **disguise**. She assumes the form of a **young Śabarī / Śabara huntress** (a tribal *kirātī* / forest-woman): dark-complexioned, adorned in leaves and forest ornaments, carrying **bow and quiver** — the very antithesis of the Himālayan princess. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 35*)
- **Śiva smitten** — the ascetic who fled a woman is undone by one: seeing the huntress, **Śiva is at once "afflicted with love"** and **seizes the huntress by the hand** — whereupon she **vanishes**, teasing his desire and drawing him after her. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 35*)
- **"Ask my father"** — the huntress deflects him to seek her hand from her **father** — the **Mountain Himavān**, "stationed on the peak of Kailāsa." The pointer is the disclosure: the Śabarī's father is the same **Himālaya** who fathered Pārvatī — the disguise names its own truth, and the tribal maiden *is* the mountain-king's daughter. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 35*)
  - *Note (theology of the disguise):* the episode literalizes a standing Śaiva paradox — Śiva flees the wife and pursues the stranger who is the same Goddess; the "other woman" who reclaims the ascetic is his own consort. Nārada, appearing again as counsellor, "cautions Śiva about the dangers of young women," and it is by his counsel that Śiva **awakens from the delusion** and recognizes the whole affair as a "mysteriously wonderful" divine play (*līlā*/*māyā*). (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 35*)
- **Reconciliation and coronation** — Pārvatī (and the mountains) **entreat Śiva's pardon**, which he grants; the estrangement is healed. The Kedāra-khaṇḍa then closes on a note of restored order — **Śiva's ceremonial coronation at Gandhamādana**, performed by **Aṅgiras and the sages**, with celestial music and a shower of blossoms from the devas: the reunited couple re-enthroned. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 35*)

## Variant endings — Viṣṇu and the "rigged" rematch

- **The Vaiṣṇava-inflected retelling** — a widely-circulated version (retold in popular Śaiva anthologies and cited to "the Skanda / Śiva Purāṇa" tradition) inserts **[Viṣṇu](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishnu-forms-and-vyuhas.md)** where the Kedāra-khaṇḍa has Nārada: Śiva, having lost everything and withdrawn to the forest, **meets Viṣṇu, who offers to help**. In a **rematch**, Śiva now wins back all he had lost — provoking Pārvatī to accuse *him* of cheating. Viṣṇu then discloses the secret: *"my spirit entered the dice; they moved not by your throw but by my will"* — neither had Śiva truly won nor Pārvatī truly lost. The game was illusion, the quarrel a product of *moha* (delusion). (*retelling tradition; cf. Skanda Purāṇa*) [disputed]
  - *How the corpus handles this:* this file follows the **printed Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34–35** as canonical — there the arbiter/instigator is **Nārada**, the cursed witness is **Bhṛṅgī**, there is **no dice-rematch**, and the reconciliation turns on the **Śabarī disguise** and Śiva's coronation. The **Viṣṇu-rigs-the-dice** ending is a **parallel telling**, valuable for making explicit the theology the Kedāra-khaṇḍa only implies (the dice as Māyā, the outcome as *līlā*) — but its Viṣṇu-as-revealer is not in the vulgate chapters. Both are recorded, not merged. [disputed]

## The dyūta-krīḍā in stone — Umā-Maheśvara iconography

- **A distinct iconographic type** — the "dice-play" is one of the most beloved sub-forms of the seated **Umā-Maheśvara** (Śiva-and-the-Goddess-together) icon: the divine couple seated on Kailāsa **playing at dice** (the cross-and-circle board, *caupaṛ*/*pāśaka*), Pārvatī often **turning her face away in mock pique** as she wins, with **Nandin, gaṇas, and attendants** looking on. It is a domesticity-in-grandeur image — the cosmic ascetic caught in a board-game he is losing. [scholarly]
- **Where it survives** — dice-play panels of Śiva and Pārvatī are carved at the great Rāṣṭrakūṭa and post-Gupta Śaiva sites — notably at **Elephanta** and in the **Ellora** cave-temples (**c. 8th c. CE**) — and recur through medieval temple sculpture, testifying that the narrative was a living theme well before the Kedāra-khaṇḍa reached its printed form. [scholarly]
- **Why it mattered to sculptors** — the *dyūta-krīḍā* let artists show the Goddess as **Śiva's equal and better** in a single frame: not the demure bride but the winner, with the ash-smeared god as her playful loser. The theme is the visual charter for which the Kedāra-khaṇḍa is the literary charter. [scholarly]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — the dice-game carries **no yuga-anchor**; it is a **recurring Kailāsa līlā** of the divine marriage, set in the mythic antiquity of the present 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara. Its "output" is doctrinal and iconographic rather than calendrical: the dice as an image of **Prakṛti / Māyā** overruling the yogic will, and the standing Umā-Maheśvara dice-icon. (*Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra-khaṇḍa 34–35*)
- **Academic / historical frame [scholarly]** — the Kedāra-khaṇḍa dice narrative is a **late-medieval Śaiva composition** within the ever-accreting Skanda vulgate [late text]; but the theme's **sculptural attestation from c. 8th c. CE** (Ellora, Elephanta) shows the *story* long predates its surviving textual form. Modern scholarship reads the episode as Śaiva theology in domestic dress — the God "inside out," mastered at play by the Goddess who is his own power. [scholarly]
