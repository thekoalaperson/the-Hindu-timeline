# Yuddha (Laṅkā) Kāṇḍa — Book of War

> Cosmic address: [Vaivasvata Manvantara](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md) → 28th Mahāyuga → **[Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** → the Rāma-avatāra cycle, its climax.
> Parent: **[Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive](README.md)** · Previous: [Sundara Kāṇḍa](5-sundara-kanda.md) · (Next: Uttara Kāṇḍa — the disputed epilogue)
> See also: [Daśāvatāra (Rāma)](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) · [Sūryavaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) · [Devas, Asuras & the Lokas](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md) · [Itihāsa-Epics corpus](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)

The sixth and longest book of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa, **128 sargas / ≈5,600–5,700 ślokas** in the vulgate — half again the length of any other kāṇḍa. Sarga counts vary by recension: some Southern printings run to **131**, the Baroda Critical Edition to **≈116**. Every citation on this page is vulgate/Southern numbering. Called *Yuddha* ("War") or *Laṅkā Kāṇḍa* ("Book of Laṅkā"). It carries the epic from Hanumān's report through the bridging of the ocean, the siege and battles of Laṅkā, the deaths of Kumbhakarṇa, Indrajit, and Rāvaṇa, Sītā's fire-ordeal, the flight north in the Puṣpaka, and Rāma's coronation. In the **Critical Edition this is the last book**; the *Uttara Kāṇḍa* (Sītā's exile, Lava–Kuśa, Rāma's departure) is widely held [scholarly] to be a later addition.

## I. Council, Embassies and the Defection of Vibhīṣaṇa (sargas 1–19)

- **Rāma honors Hanumān; the war-council** - On hearing Hanumān's report from Laṅkā, Rāma despairs of crossing the ocean but is rallied; the vānara host marches south to the shore (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 1–4).
- **Vibhīṣaṇa's counsel rejected in Laṅkā** - Rāvaṇa convenes his court; **Vibhīṣaṇa** (his youngest brother) urges returning Sītā, warning that righteousness is with Rāma. Rāvaṇa scorns and insults him and finally spurns him outright (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 9–11, 14–16).
- **Kumbhakarṇa at the first council** - Roused briefly for the council, Kumbhakarṇa reproaches his brother for having acted first and consulted afterwards — the abduction should have been debated before it was done, not after — and then pledges to kill Rāma himself regardless. The rebuke and the loyalty are one speech (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 12).
- **Why Rāvaṇa cannot force Sītā — the Puñjikasthalā curse** - Answering the court's puzzlement that Sītā is held and not taken, Rāvaṇa confesses the reason himself: long ago he had violated the apsaras **Puñjikasthalā** as she went to Brahmā's abode, and **Brahmā** cursed him that if he ever again forced a woman unwilling **his head would split into a hundred pieces**. It is the war-book's own, undisputed answer to the question the epic's audiences ask most, and it is distinct from the later Nalakūbara curse of *Uttara* 26 — whose term is **seven** pieces, not a hundred (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 13).
- **Vibhīṣaṇa's defection (śaraṇāgati)** - Spurned, Vibhīṣaṇa flies across the sea with four companions and surrenders to Rāma. A debate erupts among the vānaras over whether to accept an enemy's brother; Rāma rules that **one who comes seeking refuge must never be turned away** — a touchstone verse of the doctrine of *śaraṇāgati* — and accepts him, pre-crowning him king of Laṅkā (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 17–19).
  - *Variant (suspicion):* Sugrīva alone counsels rejecting Vibhīṣaṇa as a spy; Hanumān argues for acceptance — the balance of counsel differs by retelling.
  - *Variant ([Rāmcaritmānas](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)):* Tulsīdās dwells on Vibhīṣaṇa as the model devotee whom the Lord receives unconditionally; the episode is a major bhakti set-piece.

## II. Bridging the Ocean — Nala-Setu / Rāma-Setu (sargas 20–23)

- **Rāvaṇa's embassy to Sugrīva** - Rāvaṇa sends the rākṣasa **Śuka**, in the form of a bird, to detach Sugrīva from Rāma with an appeal to rākṣasa-vānara common cause; the vānaras seize and maul him, and Rāma orders him released unharmed — an envoy is inviolable, the same law Vibhīṣaṇa had cited to save Hanumān (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 20, 24).
- **Rāma's penance before the sea** - Rāma lies in supplication for three days asking Sāgara (the ocean) to grant passage; ignored, he raises a fiery āgneyāstra/brahmāstra to dry the sea (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 21).
- **Sāgara appears and names Nala** - The ocean-god appears in terror, pleads his nature — he cannot make himself passable and stay what he is — and offers instead to **bear the bridge**: he will hold still and carry it as far as the army needs to cross. He names the builder: the vānara **Nala**, born of Viśvakarman on Mandara, whose father's boon on his mother was "a son equal to me" — the boon is **craft**, not buoyancy. Rāma diverts the unspent weapon to the northern desert/Drumakulya region (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 22).
- **The Setu built in five days** - Under Nala's direction the vānaras hurl mountains, trees, and boulders into the sea; the **100-yojana-long, 10-yojana-wide bridge** is completed in five days, and the army crosses to Laṅkā (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 22).
  - *Note (nothing floats):* Vālmīki's sarga 22 contains **no floating stone and no buoyancy of any kind**. The rocks and trees go into the water, Nala's engineering holds them, and Sāgara underneath bears the weight. The bridge is a feat of construction, not of levitation.
  - *Variant (stones float by the Name):* the famous image of rocks floating because *Rāma* was written on them belongs to [Tulsīdās's *Rāmcaritmānas*](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md) and to oral tradition, where it carries the doctrine that the Name is greater than the one who bears it. [devotional]
  - *Variant (the squirrel):* A widely-told [folk tradition] adds a small squirrel carrying pebbles and rolling in sand to help; Rāma strokes its back, leaving the three white stripes seen on Indian palm squirrels today — not in Vālmīki.
  - *Variant (Śiva-liṅga at Rāmeśvaram):* Tradition holds Rāma installed/worshipped a Śiva-liṅga at the bridgehead (Rāmeśvaram), one of the [jyotirliṅgas](../../06-sacred-geography/) — a later sthala-purāṇa overlay onto the crossing.
- **The portents read on the far shore** - Landed, Rāma points out to Lakṣmaṇa the omens standing over both armies — his own right eye and arm throbbing, the carrion-birds circling Laṅkā — and reads them as the sign of a great slaughter in which they will prevail (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 23).

## III. Suvela, the Spies, and the Opening of the Siege (sargas 25–50)

- **The spy-catalogue** - Back in Laṅkā, **Śuka and Sāraṇa** — caught scouting in disguise and exposed by Vibhīṣaṇa, then released by Rāma — stand on the ramparts and name the vānara host to Rāvaṇa chief by chief: the roll of commanders, their fathers, their strengths, their numbers. **Śārdūla** is sent next and comes back with the same answer. It is the epic's own order-of-battle, delivered by the enemy (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 25–30).
- **Rāma occupies Suvela** - The army climbs **Mount Suvela**, the peak overlooking Laṅkā, and encamps there; Rāma and the chiefs survey the city and its gardens from the summit, and Rāma sees Rāvaṇa on the rampart for the first time (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 31, 38–39).
- **Sugrīva's leap at Rāvaṇa** - From Suvela, Sugrīva springs unaided onto the tower where Rāvaṇa stands, **strikes the crown from his head**, and grapples with him hand to hand before escaping back through the air. Rāma rebukes him for hazarding the campaign on a private impulse (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 40).
- **Rāvaṇa's deception of Sītā** - To break Sītā's will, Rāvaṇa has the sorcerer Vidyujjihva conjure an **illusory severed head of Rāma and his bow**, shown to her as proof of his death (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 32); **Saramā** — Vibhīṣaṇa's wife — consoles her, exposes the illusion, and goes to overhear the council for her (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 33–35).
- **Mālyavān's counsel** - Only now, with the enemy encamped on Suvela, does the aged minister **Mālyavān** — Sumālī's elder brother, and so Rāvaṇa's maternal great-uncle — rise in council to argue from omens and statecraft that a king at war with dharma cannot win, and that Sītā must go back. Rāvaṇa abuses him and he withdraws. His counsel is **post-crossing**, not part of the earlier Vibhīṣaṇa debate (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 35–36).
- **Aṅgada's embassy** - Rāma sends the prince **Aṅgada** (Vālin's son) as envoy to Laṅkā, demanding Sītā's release on pain of Rāvaṇa's death and Vibhīṣaṇa's enthronement; Rāvaṇa orders him seized, and Aṅgada shakes off the guards and departs defiantly (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 41).
  - *Variant (the planted foot):* A famous [folk tradition] / southern retelling has Aṅgada plant his foot in Rāvaṇa's court and challenge anyone to lift it; none can, humbling the rākṣasas — popular in [Rāmcaritmānas](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md) and theatre, not in Vālmīki.
- **The first assault** - The vānaras storm Laṅkā's gates; Rāvaṇa's general Prahasta and others sally. Furious single combats and mass battle fill the days (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 42–43).
- **Aṅgada breaks Indrajit's chariot** - In the open fighting **Aṅgada smashes Indrajit's chariot, kills his horses and his charioteer**, and forces him off the field. It is the humiliation that drives Indrajit to abandon open combat for sorcery, and it is the immediate cause of the nāga-pāśa that follows (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 44).
- **Indrajit's first nāgāstra** - Returning **invisible**, Indrajit binds Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa with the serpent-arrows (nāga-pāśa); both fall as if slain, dismaying the army, and Rāvaṇa applauds his son (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 44–46).
- **Sītā flown over the corpse-field** - Rāvaṇa has Sītā carried up in the Puṣpaka to look down on the two brothers lying bound and apparently dead among the fallen; she breaks down. **Trijaṭā** — again the one rākṣasī who steadies her — reads the field back to her correctly: the vānaras are not routed, the horses are not unyoked, the brothers are not dead but bound, and the chariot they were shown Sītā from would not fly for a widow (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 47–48).
- **Garuḍa frees them** - The eagle **Garuḍa**, enemy of serpents, descends; at his approach the nāga-bonds dissolve and the brothers revive (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 50).

## IV. The Fall of the Rākṣasa Champions (sargas 51–67)

- **Dhūmrākṣa, Vajradaṃṣṭra, Akampana, Prahasta** - In successive sorties Rāvaṇa's commanders are killed: Dhūmrākṣa and Akampana by Hanumān, Vajradaṃṣṭra by Aṅgada, and the chief general **Prahasta** by Nīla (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 51–58).
- **Rāvaṇa takes the field** - Rāvaṇa himself sallies and strikes Lakṣmaṇa down with a **śakti given him by Brahmā** — the first of the two lances that fell Lakṣmaṇa in this book, and not to be confused with the second (see §VI). Hanumān lifts Lakṣmaṇa clear, and then **carries Rāma on his shoulders** into the duel, Rāma fighting from the vānara's back against the chariot-borne king. Humiliated and disarmed — Rāma spares him, telling him to return rested — Rāvaṇa retreats (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 59).
- **The waking of Kumbhakarṇa** - Rāvaṇa orders his colossal brother **Kumbhakarṇa** roused with drums, elephants, and mountains of food (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 60).
- **The two sleep-etiologies, kept apart** - Vālmīki explains the sleep **twice**, in two sargas of different age, and the corpus keeps them distinct rather than fusing them:
  - *The older layer (Uttara 10):* when the three brothers came to take their boons, the gods begged Brahmā to forestall Kumbhakarṇa, and **Sarasvatī entered his mouth** — whereupon he asked, in his own words and with **no stated intent** and **no commutation clause**, for sleep for many years. A plain slip of the tongue, and nothing more.
  - *The later layer (Yuddha 61):* Vibhīṣaṇa, asked by Rāma who this giant is, gives the developed version — Kumbhakarṇa had meant to ask *nirdevatvam* (the unmaking of the gods) and Sarasvatī turned it to *nidrā* (sleep); Rāvaṇa then petitions Brahmā, who commutes the sleep to **six months asleep and one day awake**. The pun and the commutation belong here, not to the boon-scene.
  - *Variant (Indrāsana/nidrāsana):* the popular telling in which he means to ask for **Indra's seat** (*indrāsana*) and says **a bed of sleep** (*nidrāsana*) is a later gloss on the *Yuddha* 61 pun, not Vālmīki's wording. [folk/Purāṇic gloss]
- **Kumbhakarṇa's rampage and death** - Awakened, Kumbhakarṇa first repeats his council-rebuke and counsels peace, rebukes the minister Mahodara for flattery, then loyally goes to war; he devours and scatters vānaras, withstands Hanumān and others, until **Rāma severs his arms, legs, and finally his head** with celestial arrows (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 62–67).

## V. Indrajit's Sorceries and the Sañjīvanī (sargas 68–91)

- **Death of Rāvaṇa's sons and kin** - The champions fall in a named order: **Narāntaka** by Aṅgada's fist (69); **Devāntaka** and **Triśiras** by Hanumān, **Mahodara** by Nīla, **Mahāpārśva** (called Matta) by Ṛṣabha with the rākṣasa's own mace (70); and the giant **Atikāya**, armored by Brahmā's boon, by Lakṣmaṇa with the brahmāstra (71). Rāvaṇa's grief over Atikāya closes the sequence (72) (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 68–72).
  - *Note (duplicated names):* a **second** Mahodara and a **second** Mahāpārśva fight and die in Rāvaṇa's final sortie at *Yuddha* 97–98, and the **Virūpākṣa** killed there at 96 shares his name with one of the five senāpatis Hanumān killed at *Sundara* 46. The vulgate reuses these names for distinct rākṣasas and never flags it; do not merge the bearers.
- **Indrajit's brahmāstra fells the host** - Indrajit, fighting unseen, looses the brahmāstra and lays low the whole vānara army, Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa among them (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 73).
- **The first Sañjīvanī flight** - **Jāmbavān** bids Hanumān fly north for four healing herbs; the herb-mountain in this sarga stands **between Mount Ṛṣabha and Kailāsa** and Vālmīki gives it no name at all. Unable to tell the glowing herbs apart, Hanumān **carries the whole mountain** back, reviving the fallen, then returns it (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 74).
  - *Note (the name "Droṇagiri"):* **"Droṇagiri" is not a Vālmīki place-name anywhere in the epic.** The mountain of the first flight is unnamed; the mountain of the second (*Yuddha* 101) is named **Oṣadhi / Mahodaya**. Droṇagiri belongs to later tradition and to the Uttarakhand sthala-legend, and is the standard name in retellings. [later tradition]
  - *Variant (Bharata's arrow):* A southern/folk addition has Bharata in Ayodhyā, mistaking the airborne Hanumān for a demon, shooting him down, then aiding him — absent from Vālmīki.
  - *Note:* Vālmīki has **two** herb-fetching episodes, in different sargas, to different mountains, prescribed by different people (here Jāmbavān for the brahmāstra wounds; at 101 Suṣeṇa for the śakti wound). Popular retellings merge them into one flight; the corpus keeps them apart.
- **The night-burning of Laṅkā** - With the brothers revived, **Sugrīva** orders the assault renewed at nightfall: the vānaras go to the city with torches in their hands and fire it street by street, thousands of houses burning at once — the second burning of Laṅkā, this one by the whole army rather than by one tail (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 75).
- **Kumbha and Nikumbha** - Kumbhakarṇa's sons come out to avenge their father: **Kumbha** is killed by Sugrīva, who takes his blows and then breaks him; **Nikumbha**, wielding a great iron mace, is killed by Hanumān (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 76–77).
- **Makarākṣa** - **Makarākṣa**, son of Khara — the son of the man Rāma killed at Janasthāna, come for the debt — challenges Rāma directly and hurls a trident given him by Śiva; Rāma breaks it and kills him with the **āgneyāstra** (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 78–79).
- **Māyā-Sītā slain** - To demoralize the army, Indrajit conjures an **illusory Sītā (Māyā-Sītā)** on his chariot and beheads her before Hanumān's eyes; the vānaras reel until Vibhīṣaṇa reveals it was sorcery — Rāvaṇa would never kill the woman he covets (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 81, 83–84).
  - *Variant (the "real" shadow-Sītā doctrine):* Several traditions ([Kūrma Purāṇa], [Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa], and [Rāmcaritmānas](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)) hold that the *true* Sītā was hidden with Agni before the abduction and a **Chāyā/Māyā-Sītā** stood in her place all along — this same shadow is later "burned away" at the agni-parīkṣā to restore the real Sītā. See §VIII.
- **Nikumbhilā, and the yajña** - **Nikumbhilā** is the *caitya* — the sanctuary and fire-altar — inside Laṅkā that is the city's own sacrificial ground and Indrajit's private one: it is where he had performed the **seven sacrifices** (agniṣṭoma, aśvamedha, bahusuvarṇaka, rājasūya, gomedha, vaiṣṇava, and the māheśvara that men cannot undertake) which won him his boons — the sky-going chariot, the māyā that veils the field in darkness so that he fights unseen, and inexhaustible quivers — with **Uśanas (Śukra)** officiating (*Uttara* 25). That is why it and no other altar is his death-ground. He retreats there now to complete the rite that would make him unassailable; by the boon's own terms he can be killed only if struck **before** the oblations are finished, and Vibhīṣaṇa, who grew up in the city, is the one man in Rāma's camp who knows the place (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 84–86).
- **Indrajit slain by Lakṣmaṇa** - Guided by Vibhīṣaṇa, Lakṣmaṇa, with Hanumān and Vibhīṣaṇa, reaches Nikumbhilā, disrupts the yajña, and after a tremendous duel **beheads Indrajit with the Aindrāstra** (Indra's weapon), invoked on the vow that "if Rāma is true to dharma, let this slay him" (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 88–90).
  - *Variant (Sulocanā / satī):* Later texts and theatre add **Sulocanā**, Indrajit's widow (a nāga-princess), performing satī and the severed head speaking — a popular [late text]/folk cycle (*Sati Sulochana*), not in Vālmīki. She does not appear in the *Rāmcaritmānas* either; the cycle is Kṛttivāsī-adjacent Bengali, Marathi and Rāmlīlā material.
- **Suṣeṇa's field-surgery** - After the duel the vānara physician **Suṣeṇa** treats Lakṣmaṇa and the wounded chiefs with herbs, and the host is put back on its feet before Rāvaṇa's last sortie (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 91).

## VI. Lakṣmaṇa, the Maya-Śakti, and the Second Sañjīvanī (sargas 92–101)

- **Rāvaṇa's grief; Supārśva restrains him** - Devastated by Indrajit's death, Rāvaṇa takes up his sword and starts for the Aśoka-vana to kill Sītā where she sits. The minister **Supārśva** blocks him and argues him out of it — a king does not murder a woman he has kept, and the answer to a son's death is the battlefield, not the grove. Rāvaṇa turns back and arms for the field (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 92).
- **The gāndharva-astra** - In the fighting that follows, Rāma cuts through Rāvaṇa's massed troops with the **gāndharva-astra**, whose illusion makes him appear everywhere at once: the rākṣasas see a Rāma on every side and cannot tell where the arrows come from. The rākṣasīs of the city lament their dead by name (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 93–94).
- **The last champions** - In Rāvaṇa's final sortie **Virūpākṣa** and the second **Mahodara** are killed by Sugrīva, and the second **Mahāpārśva** by Aṅgada, stripping the king of his last commanders (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 95–98).
- **Lakṣmaṇa struck by the śakti** - In the duel Rāvaṇa hurls a **śakti fashioned by Maya the dānava** — a conjured, unfailing lance hung with **eight loudly clamouring bells**, gold-crowned, blazing like a thunderbolt — at Lakṣmaṇa, who falls mortally wounded, fated to die at sunrise (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 100).
  - *Note (not the Vāsavī Śakti):* Vālmīki names no *Vāsavī Śakti* here. The **Vāsavī Śakti** — Indra's once-only javelin — belongs to the *Mahābhārata*, where Indra gives it to **Karṇa** in exchange for his armour and Karṇa spends it on Ghaṭotkaca; the corpus's own [divyāstra register](../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/divyastras-and-divine-weapons.md) assigns it there. Rāvaṇa's two lances in this book are **Brahmā-given** (*Yuddha* 59) and **Maya-made** (*Yuddha* 100), and neither is Indra's.
- **Second Sañjīvanī flight** - The physician **Suṣeṇa** names the herbs — **viśalyakaraṇī, sāvarṇyakaraṇī, saṃjīvakaraṇī, sandhānī** — and this time the mountain is named in the text: **Oṣadhi**, also called **Mahodaya**. Hanumān flies for it and, again unable to choose among the herbs, brings the whole peak; Lakṣmaṇa is healed before dawn and the mountain returned (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 101).

## VII. The Slaying of Rāvaṇa (sargas 102–112)

- **Indra's chariot and Mātali** - Seeing Rāma on foot against the chariot-borne Rāvaṇa, Indra sends his own **celestial chariot driven by the charioteer Mātali**, with his bow, armor, and weapons, to equalize the fight; the duel opens with Rāvaṇa's serpent-missile answered by Rāma's Garuḍa-arrows (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 102). Wounded, Rāvaṇa is carried off the field by his own charioteer against his will and returns raging (103–104).
- **The Āditya-Hṛdayam** - As Rāma flags, the sage **Agastya** appears and teaches him the **Āditya-Hṛdayam**, a hymn to Sūrya; performing the ācamana and chanting it thrice, Rāma is renewed with vigor for the final assault (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 105). *(A celebrated stotra recited in its own right; printings differ on whether it stands at sarga 105 or 107, and it is outside the Critical Edition's constituted text.)*
- **The regenerating heads** - The duel rages seven days; **each head Rāma severs rises again**, a hundred heads cut off in all, and Rāma is left wondering why arrows that killed Khara, Kabandha and Vālī will not kill this one. The sarga ends with Mātali about to speak (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 107).
- **The Brahmā-arrow to the heart** - **Mātali** gives the prompt: the hour of Rāvaṇa's destruction has come, discharge the missile presided over by Brahmā. Rāma fits the **Brahmā-made arrow given him by Agastya** — wind in its feathers, fire and sun at its point, its shaft of ether, weighted with the mass of Meru and Mandara — and looses it. It falls on Rāvaṇa's chest and **tears out his heart** (108.18); Rāvaṇa drops dead from his chariot with his bow, and the arrow, blood-smeared and its work done, returns silently to Rāma's quiver (108.20). The gods rain flowers (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 108).
  - *Note (no amṛta, no navel):* sarga 108 contains **no nectar and no navel**. The shot is to the chest, the killing detail is the heart, and the only counsel given is Mātali's. Nothing in Vālmīki makes Rāvaṇa's life depend on amṛta stored in his body, and nothing makes Vibhīṣaṇa the discloser of a bodily secret.
  - *Variant (the amṛta in the navel):* the famous version in which **Vibhīṣaṇa reveals a pot of nectar lodged in Rāvaṇa's navel** and Rāma dries it with an arrow to the navel belongs to [Tulsīdās's *Rāmcaritmānas*](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md) and to the regional and stage retellings that follow it, where it carries the theme of the brother who knows the secret. It is the best-known telling of Rāvaṇa's death and it is not Vālmīki's. [devotional / regional]
  - *Variant (Rāvaṇa as devotee/guru):* Some traditions have the dying Rāvaṇa, a supreme Śiva-bhakta and scholar, give a dharma-discourse; Lakṣmaṇa is sent to learn statecraft at his deathbed [folk/regional, esp. Tamil and oral].
- **Mandodarī's lament; honorable cremation** - Rāvaṇa's consorts and then his chief queen **Mandodarī** mourn over the body; Rāma directs Vibhīṣaṇa to give Rāvaṇa full funeral rites, declaring enmity ends at death — "he was a mighty being, worthy of honor" (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 109–111).
- **Vibhīṣaṇa crowned king of Laṅkā** (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 112).

## VIII. The Reunion and the Fire-Ordeal (sargas 113–121)

- **Sītā summoned; her mercy to the rākṣasīs** - Hanumān brings word of the victory to Sītā in the Aśoka-vana and asks leave to kill the rākṣasīs who had threatened and tormented her for ten months. **She forbids it**, and gives him a bear-and-hunter parable for his answer: a noble being does not count the offences of the wicked, the guards acted under their master's orders, and her own misfortune is the fruit of her own past acts. It is the moral high point of her portrayal in the war-book (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 113). She is then bathed, adorned, and brought before Rāma (114).
- **Rāma's harsh disavowal** - Rāma, before the assembled hosts, coldly declares he fought to redeem his honor, not for her, and that he cannot take back a wife who dwelt in another's house; he bids her go where she will. Sītā answers him in kind — she was carried off by force, not by consent, and he is speaking to her like a common man to a common woman — and asks Lakṣmaṇa to build a pyre (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 115–116).
- **"I think of myself as a man"** - As Sītā stands at the fire the gods come down and reproach Rāma for treating her so, telling him he is Nārāyaṇa; Rāma answers with the epic's most-quoted self-description: **"I think of myself as a man, Rāma the son of Daśaratha"** (*ātmānaṃ mānuṣaṃ manye*) — and asks Brahmā to say who he truly is. The avatāra is revealed to him, not claimed by him (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 117).
- **The Agni-parīkṣā (fire-ordeal)** - Sītā, declaring her purity, enters the blazing fire. **Agni himself rises out of the pyre bearing Sītā unburned**, sets her in Rāma's hands, and testifies to her chastity; Rāma receives her, saying he never doubted her but had to prove her purity to the world (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 118).
  - *Variant (the shadow-Sītā restored — Chāyā/Māyā-Sītā):* Per [Kūrma Purāṇa], [Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa], the *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa* and [Rāmcaritmānas](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md), Rāma had entrusted the **real Sītā to Agni before the abduction**, leaving an illusory double that Rāvaṇa carried off; the fire-ordeal is the device by which **Agni returns the true Sītā** — recasting the ordeal as restoration, not a test, and shielding Sītā from any taint of captivity.
  - *Variant (theology of the ordeal):* Bhakti commentators read the agni-parīkṣā as lokasaṅgraha (a public demonstration for the world's sake), Rāma never having doubted her; rationalist/[scholarly] readings treat it (and its harsher Uttara-Kāṇḍa sequel) as a later patriarchal interpolation.
- **Daśaratha appears; Kaikeyī's repudiation withdrawn** - Śiva bids Rāma look, and **Daśaratha** stands before him in a celestial chariot, released from grief by his son's victory. Rāma asks him for one thing: to **take back the curse and disowning he had laid on Kaikeyī and Bharata** in his last days. Daśaratha revokes them, blesses Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa, and departs (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 119).
- **The fallen revived** - Rāma asks **Indra** — not the gods collectively — for the boon, naming it exactly: let every vānara who died for his sake rise and live. Indra answers that the boon is a hard one and that he has never yet revoked an offer, and grants it; the dead vānaras and ṛkṣas stand up as if from sleep, and the land bears flower, fruit and water out of season (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 120).

## IX. Return to Ayodhyā and Coronation (sargas 122–128)

- **Flight north in the Puṣpaka** - The fourteen-year exile nearly ended, Rāma (with Sītā, Lakṣmaṇa, Vibhīṣaṇa, Sugrīva, Hanumān, and the chief vānaras) boards Rāvaṇa's reclaimed flying chariot **Puṣpaka-vimāna** and flies to Ayodhyā, Rāma pointing out to Sītā the bridge, Kiṣkindhā, Ṛṣyamūka, the Daṇḍaka sites, the hermitages, and the rivers below; they halt at **Bharadvāja's** āśrama, where the sage grants the boon that every tree on the road to Ayodhyā bear fruit out of season (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 122–124).
- **Hanumān precedes them; Bharata's vow** - Hanumān is sent ahead to Nandigrāma to forewarn **Bharata**, who had ruled as regent with Rāma's sandals on the throne and had vowed to enter fire if Rāma did not return on the appointed day; Hanumān recounts the whole forest years to him, and Bharata, overcome, offers him wealth, villages and sixteen brides on the spot (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 125.43–45, 126).
- **The reunion at Nandigrāma** - Rāma is reunited with Bharata, Śatrughna, and the mothers; Bharata returns the kingdom, and Ayodhyā is made ready to receive its king (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 127).
- **The Rāja-abhiṣeka (coronation)** - Rāma is consecrated **King of Ayodhyā** by Vasiṣṭha and the assembled sages, and the vānara and rākṣasa allies are honored and dismissed with gifts. Sītā, given a **pearl necklace** by Rāma, looks at Hanumān and then at her husband, and Rāma tells her to give it where her heart is — she bestows it on **Hanumān**, and the text stops to itemize the ten virtues the necklace answers to in him (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 128.82).
  - *Note (where the chiranjīvi boon actually sits):* Hanumān asks nothing at this coronation, and *Yuddha* 128 contains **no** grant of deathlessness. The boon of living **as long as Rāma's story is told among men** is given at the **Mahāprasthāna, *Uttara* 108** — Rāma's own parting disposition, alongside Vibhīṣaṇa's term of the sun and moon and Jāmbavān's until the coming of Kali. Placing it at the coronation is a retelling's compression.
- **Rāma-rājya** - Rāma's reign inaugurates the archetypal age of perfect dharma — timely rains, no disease or want, no untimely death — the political ideal invoked ever after as *Rāma-rājya* (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 128).
- **Phala-śruti** - The book closes with the fruits of reciting the Rāmāyaṇa (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 128). The story continues — disputedly — into the [Uttara Kāṇḍa].

## X. The Rākṣasa Command and the Ground of the War

Below Rāvaṇa, Kumbhakarṇa, Indrajit, Vibhīṣaṇa and Mandodarī the war-book carries a whole court and command staff who pass as bare names in most retellings. Listed here with kinship, office, and the sarga each is settled at.

- **Prahasta** - Son of Sumālī, Rāvaṇa's maternal uncle, prime minister and commander-in-chief, and the counselor whose advice carried the abduction. Father of **Jambumālī** and of the seven sons Hanumān killed at the gate (*Sundara* 44–45). Takes the field when the first sorties fail and is killed by **Nīla** (*Yuddha* 57–58).
- **Mālyavān** - Eldest of the three founding rākṣasa brothers (Mālyavān, Sumālī, Mālī) and Rāvaṇa's maternal great-uncle; the senior political voice, who argues from omens that a king at war with dharma cannot win, and is abused for it (*Yuddha* 35–36). He does not fight.
- **Dhūmrākṣa · Vajradaṃṣṭra · Akampana** - The three commanders sent out in succession as the siege opens: Dhūmrākṣa killed by Hanumān with a mountain-peak (51–52), Vajradaṃṣṭra by Aṅgada (53–54), Akampana by Hanumān with a sāla tree (55–56). The Akampana who brings the news of Janasthāna to Laṅkā and first proposes the abduction (*Araṇya* 31) bears the same name and is generally read as the same rākṣasa.
- **Atikāya** - Rāvaṇa's son, given impenetrable armor and a bow by Brahmā; the one champion Lakṣmaṇa must use the brahmāstra on (71).
- **Narāntaka · Devāntaka · Triśiras (of Laṅkā)** - Rāvaṇa's sons: Narāntaka killed by Aṅgada (69), Devāntaka and the three-headed Triśiras by Hanumān (70). Distinct from the **Triśiras of Janasthāna**, Khara's captain, whom Rāma kills in the forest (*Araṇya* 27).
- **Akṣakumāra** - Rāvaṇa's youngest son, killed by Hanumān in the grove-fighting before the war begins (*Sundara* 47).
- **Kumbha · Nikumbha** - Kumbhakarṇa's sons, killed by Sugrīva and Hanumān (76–77).
- **Makarākṣa** - Khara's son, come for his father's death; killed by Rāma with the āgneyāstra (78–79).
- **Khara · Dūṣaṇa · Triśiras — the Janasthāna command** - Rāvaṇa's standing garrison in Daṇḍaka: **Khara**, his maternal cousin (*Uttara* 24) and not his brother, posted over fourteen thousand rākṣasas with Śūrpaṇakhā; his general **Dūṣaṇa**; his captain **Triśiras**. All three, and the fourteen thousand, are killed by Rāma alone (*Araṇya* 26–29) — the provocation the whole war answers.
- **Vidyujjihva** - The sorcerer who conjures the illusory head of Rāma and the bow for Sītā (32). Vālmīki does not say whether he is the Vidyujjihva who married Śūrpaṇakhā (*Uttara* 12).
- **Śuka · Sāraṇa · Śārdūla** - Rāvaṇa's intelligence: Śuka goes to Sugrīva as envoy in bird-form and is released on Rāma's order (20, 24); he and Sāraṇa, caught scouting the camp and exposed by Vibhīṣaṇa, are released to report, and their forced recitation of the vānara order-of-battle from the rampart is the fullest muster-roll the epic gives (25–29). Śārdūla goes next and returns with the same answer (30).
- **Mahodara · Mahāpārśva · Supārśva · Virūpākṣa** - Rāvaṇa's ministers and household champions. **Supārśva** is the one who talks him out of murdering Sītā after Indrajit's death (92); the rest die in the sorties, and the names recur on more than one bearer (see §V).
- **Laṅkinī** - The city's own tutelary guardian, felled at the gate by Hanumān, whose defeat Brahmā had long before named as the sign of the rākṣasas' ruin (*Sundara* 3).
- **Saramā · Trijaṭā** - The two rākṣasīs who take Sītā's side: **Saramā**, Vibhīṣaṇa's wife and daughter of the gandharva king Śailūṣa (*Uttara* 12), who exposes the illusory head and spies on the council for her (33–35); and **Trijaṭā**, who reads first the omen-dream (*Sundara* 27) and then the corpse-field (48) correctly against every appearance.

**The ground itself** — two places the war turns on, neither of them a general landscape:

- **Suvela** - The mountain overlooking Laṅkā from which Rāma's army besieges the city: the host climbs it after the crossing and encamps on the summit, and it is from Suvela that Rāma first sees Rāvaṇa on the rampart and Sugrīva springs at him (31, 38–40). The whole siege is fought from this peak.
- **Nikumbhilā** - The sanctuary (*caitya*) and fire-altar inside Laṅkā, the city's sacrificial ground and Indrajit's private one — the site of the seven sacrifices, Uśanas (Śukra) officiating, that won him the sky-going chariot, the darkening māyā and his inexhaustible quivers (*Uttara* 25). The oblations he pours here are **flesh and blood, offered to the bhūtas**, and the ground the text gives it is a **great thicket with one banyan (*nyagrodha*) terrible as a black cloud**, the tree Lakṣmaṇa is told to shoot before Indrajit can reach it (*Yuddha* 82.24–26, 87.2–4). Vibhīṣaṇa, who grew up in the city, is the one man in Rāma's camp who can find it. Because the boons were taken there, that is the only ground on which they can be broken, and interrupting a rite at Nikumbhilā is the sole condition under which he can be killed (84–90). Full treatment: [Nikumbhilā](../../06-sacred-geography/nikumbhila.md).
  - *Variant (the presiding goddess and the quarter):* later tradition gives the altar a deity of its own and the rite her name — **Nikumbhilā-devī**, identified by the commentators with **Bhadrakālī** and in southern and tantric retellings with **Pratyaṅgirā** — so that Indrajit's interrupted sacrifice reads as a goddess-invocation rather than a fire-oblation. The same stratum sites the grove in Laṅkā's **western quarter**. Vālmīki names **no deity and no quarter**; the thicket and the banyan, by contrast, are his own. [later tradition / regional]

## XI. Major Variants Across Traditions

- **Where the epic ends** - Vālmīki's **Critical Edition closes here** (with the coronation); the *Uttara Kāṇḍa* (Sītā's later banishment, Lava–Kuśa, her descent into the earth, Rāma's departure) is [scholarly] regarded as a later book, though canonical in tradition.
- **[Rāmcaritmānas](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md) (Tulsīdās, Avadhi, 16th c.)** - *Laṅkākāṇḍa* foregrounds bhakti: the floating Name-inscribed stones, the shadow-Sītā (so no agni-parīkṣā stigma), Vibhīṣaṇa-śaraṇāgati as devotional centerpiece; ends with Rāma's homecoming and the *Uttarakāṇḍa* given over largely to devotion rather than Sītā's exile.
- **Kamban's *Irāmāvatāram* (Tamil, 12th c.)** - *Yuddha/Pōr Kāṇṭam* with distinctive Tamil amplifications, ornate battle poetry, and elevated treatment of Rāvaṇa.
- **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** - Advaitic/devotional retelling embedding the Chāyā-Sītā doctrine and treating the whole war as Rāma's līlā.
- **Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas** - In Jain versions (e.g., *Paümacariya*) **Lakṣmaṇa**, not Rāma (who is non-violent), kills Rāvaṇa; Rāvaṇa is a noble but flawed Vidyādhara; no monkeys-as-animals (the "Vānaras" are a human clan). The Buddhist *Dasaratha Jātaka* omits the war and the abduction entirely.
- **Southeast Asian retellings** - The Thai *Ramakien*, Khmer *Reamker*, and others recast the war with local heroes and added episodes (e.g., the mermaid Suvannamaccha obstructing the bridge-building).
