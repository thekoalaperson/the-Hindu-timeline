# Araṇya Kāṇḍa — Book of the Forest

> Cosmic address: [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) ← but the Rāmāyaṇa itself unfolds in the [Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current Vaivasvata Manvantara. Rāma is the 7th [Daśāvatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) of [Viṣṇu](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md), of the [Sūryavaṃśa / Ikṣvāku line](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
> Parent: [Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive index](README.md) · Part of [04-deep-dives](../README.md). Primary source: Vālmīki [Rāmāyaṇa](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md). Previous: [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](2-ayodhya-kanda.md) · Next: [Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa](4-kishkindha-kanda.md).

The **Araṇya Kāṇḍa** ("Book of the Forest") is the third of the seven kāṇḍas of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa. The vulgate (Southern recension) numbered throughout this file has 75 sargas; the Baroda Critical Edition (CE) constitutes 71, and among the vulgate passages it lacks are the whole of sarga 31 and the Ayomukhī episode, both flagged below. It covers the heart of Rāma's fourteen-year exile in the **Daṇḍaka** wilderness with Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa: their passage through the ashrams of the great forest sages, the slaying of demons that infest the woods, the settling at **Pañcavaṭī**, the **Śūrpaṇakhā** episode and the war with **Khara–Dūṣaṇa**, **Mārīca**'s golden-deer ruse, **Rāvaṇa**'s abduction of Sītā, the death of **Jaṭāyu**, and the despairing brothers' first steps toward the alliance that fills the next kāṇḍa. It is the structural hinge of the epic — the kāṇḍa in which the central catastrophe occurs.

## Entering Daṇḍaka: The Sages of the Forest (sargas 1–11)

- **Into the Daṇḍakāraṇya** - After Citrakūṭa, Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa cross south into the vast **Daṇḍaka** forest, a belt of ascetic hermitages plagued by rākṣasas; the sages beg Rāma's protection as a kṣatriya-in-exile (Vālmīki, Araṇya 1).
- **Slaying of Virādha** - The man-eating rākṣasa **Virādha**, who names himself the son of Java and Śatahradā, seizes Sītā. A boon from Brahmā means no weapon can cut, pierce or kill him, so the brothers sever his arms, Lakṣmaṇa the left and Rāma the right, and Rāma resolves to bury him in a deep pit. Recognising Rāma, the dying Virādha asks for exactly that, burial being the age-old custom for dead rākṣasas. He reveals that he is the **gandharva Tumburu**, cursed by Kubera (Vaiśravaṇa) for failing to attend him while infatuated with the apsaras **Rambhā**; the curse was to end when Rāma killed him in a fight. He sends the brothers on to the sage Śarabhaṅga (Vālmīki, Araṇya 2–4; 3.5–6, 4.5, 4.16–23).
- **Śarabhaṅga's self-immolation** - At sage **Śarabhaṅga**'s hermitage, the sage — who has just refused Indra's offer to carry him to Brahmaloka, wishing to see Rāma first — enters the fire and ascends in a radiant body, having gained Rāma's darśana (Vālmīki, Araṇya 5).
- **Vow to the ṛṣis** - The forest sages tell Rāma that their communities are being slaughtered as though they had no protector, and bid him come and see the bodies of the many sages the rākṣasas have killed, a massacre that reaches from the Pampā and the Mandākinī to Citrakūṭa. Rāma **vows to clear Daṇḍaka of demons**, the dharmic pledge that motivates the whole kāṇḍa (Vālmīki, Araṇya 6; 6.15–17).
  - *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa / devotional reading):* the vow is framed as Viṣṇu's deliberate descent to unburden the Earth of the rākṣasa scourge, not merely a knight's promise.
- **Sutīkṣṇa's hermitage** - The trio reach the ashram of **Sutīkṣṇa**, who has likewise awaited Rāma and offers him the worlds he has won by austerity; Rāma answers that he will win those worlds himself and asks only for a place to live. When the sage says the only trouble there is the herds of wild animals, Rāma offers to shoot them, then decides to move on rather than distress a sage so fond of them. They pass the night there and take leave at dawn (Vālmīki, Araṇya 7–8; 7.12–14). Vālmīki does not call Sutīkṣṇa a disciple of Agastya in these sargas.
- **Sītā's ahiṃsā counsel** - Setting out again, Sītā warns Rāma of the three subtle vices born of desire — falsehood, another man's wife, and **violence without enmity** — and tells the parable of the ascetic whom Indra ruined by leaving a sword in his keeping: the weapon in the hand bends the mind toward slaughter, and the quarrel of the Daṇḍaka sages is not his to fight. It is her longest ethical argument in the epic (Vālmīki, Araṇya 9).
- **Rāma's answer: the vow outranks his life** - Rāma replies that he has given the ṛṣis his word before their faces and will not take it back; he would sooner give up Lakṣmaṇa, or Sītā, or his own life than break a promise once made, and the bow exists for exactly this — the protection of the afflicted (Vālmīki, Araṇya 10).
- **The lake of the five apsarases** - On the road the travellers hear song and instruments from under a lake. A sage travelling with them explains that it is **Pañcāpsaras**, made by the ascetic **Māṇḍakarṇi**, who spent ten thousand years in austerity living on air in the water. The alarmed gods sent five chief apsarases to break his penance; they became his wives, and he lives with them in a house hidden beneath the water, from which the music comes (Vālmīki, Araṇya 11.11–19; CE 3.10.11).
- **Ten years' wandering** - Rāma then moves from hermitage to hermitage, staying ten months in one, a year in another, a few months elsewhere, until **ten years** have passed among the sages. He returns to Sutīkṣṇa, asks the way to Agastya, and on the road visits the hermitage of **Agastya's brother** (Vālmīki, Araṇya 11; CE 3.10.23–26).

## Agastya, Anasūyā, and the Divine Weapons (sargas 11–14)

- **Anasūyā and Sītā** - At **Atri**'s hermitage, immediately before the forest crossing, the saintly **Anasūyā** instructs Sītā on **pativratā-dharma** (wifely devotion) and gifts her celestial unfading garments, ornaments, and cosmetics (Vālmīki, **Ayodhyā** 117–119: arrival at Atri's ashram and Anasūyā's instruction at 117, the gifts and Sītā's story at 118, departure at 119). See [Atri and Anasūyā](../atri-anasuya.md).
  - *Note:* the episode stands at the **close of the Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa** in the vulgate; the Araṇya Kāṇḍa opens after it. Readers often connect Anasūyā's gift with the ornaments Sītā drops to the vānaras during the abduction-flight (Araṇya 54) and that Sugrīva later shows Rāma (Kiṣkindhā 6), but Vālmīki does not say the bundle held Anasūyā's gifts: at 54.2 they are simply "auspicious ornaments" wrapped in her golden silk upper cloth. [interpretation]
- **Arrival at Agastya's ashram** - They reach the hermitage of the great sage **Agastya**, the "tamer of the Vindhyas" and drinker of the ocean. On the way, as they approach the hermitage of Agastya's brother, **Rāma himself narrates** to Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa how Agastya made the southern forests habitable by digesting the rākṣasa **Vātāpi**, whom his brother **Ilvala** served up to brahmin guests as a ram, and then burning Ilvala with a glance (Vālmīki, Araṇya 11.55–66; arrival at Agastya's at 12). See [Agastya](../agastya.md).
- **The arsenal from Agastya** - Agastya hands over the deposit he has held for this hour: the great bow **of Viṣṇu**, wrought with gold and diamond by **Viśvakarmā**; **one** unfailing arrow blazing like the sun, **given by Brahmā**; **two** quivers whose shafts never run out, **given by Indra**; and a gold-decorated sword in a **golden sheath**. With this bow Viṣṇu once killed the great asuras, and the arms have been laid up with Agastya until Rāma should need them (Vālmīki, Araṇya 12.32–36).
  - *Note:* **"Brahmadatta" is not the arrow's name** — *brahma-datta* is the adjective "Brahmā-given." Vālmīki keeps Agastya's shafts distinct and the registry should too: this Brahmā-given arrow is the one Mātali later prompts Rāma to loose at Rāvaṇa, and the Yuddha passage names Agastya as its giver ([Yuddha](6-yuddha-kanda.md) 108.3–4). The arrow that kills Khara is described only as **given by Indra** (*dattaṃ maghavatā*, Araṇya 30.25; CE 3.29.25); that it came out of Agastya's Indra-given quivers is a reasonable inference, not something the verse states. Four items, three donors — not one undifferentiated gift.
- **Directed to Pañcavaṭī** - Agastya advises Rāma to settle at **Pañcavaṭī** on the **Godāvarī** river — fertile, scenic, and suited to a hermit's life (Vālmīki, Araṇya 13).
- **Meeting Jaṭāyu** - On the way the family meets the aged vulture-king **Jaṭāyu**, who greets Rāma as a friend of his father Daśaratha. Asked who he is, he recites the descent of all creatures from the daughters of Dakṣa married to Kaśyapa and names himself the son of **Aruṇa** (Garuḍa's brother) by **Śyenī**, with **Sampāti** as his elder brother. He offers to stay with them and guard Sītā when the brothers are away — a promise that foreshadows his death (Vālmīki, Araṇya 14; 14.3, 14.32–34).

## Life at Pañcavaṭī (sargas 15–16)

- **The hermitage built** - Lakṣmaṇa constructs a leaf-and-bamboo cottage at Pañcavaṭī; the three live in idyllic forest peace through the seasons (Vālmīki, Araṇya 15–16).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's seasonal hymn** - A celebrated lyrical passage describing the **Hemanta (winter)** season at Pañcavaṭī — among the epic's finest nature poetry (Vālmīki, Araṇya 16).
- **Kaikeyī defended** - In the middle of the winter-piece Lakṣmaṇa thinks of Bharata keeping his vows in Ayodhyā and asks how a mother whose husband is Daśaratha and whose son is Bharata could be so cruel. Rāma will not hear it: their middle mother is never to be blamed, and Lakṣmaṇa should speak only of Bharata (Vālmīki, Araṇya 16.35–37).

## Śūrpaṇakhā Mutilated (sargas 17–18)

- **Śūrpaṇakhā's advance** - **Śūrpaṇakhā** ("she of winnowing-fan nails"), sister of **Rāvaṇa**, sees Rāma and, smitten, proposes marriage. Vālmīki sets her ugliness against his beauty feature by feature, and she offers to eat Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa with her, so that she and Rāma can roam Daṇḍaka together (Vālmīki, Araṇya 17; 17.26–28).
- **The mocking deflection** - Rāma playfully redirects her to the "unmarried" Lakṣmaṇa, who in turn sends her back to Rāma; the byplay enrages her (Vālmīki, Araṇya 18).
- **The mutilation** - When she lunges at Sītā, Rāma tells Lakṣmaṇa that one does not jest with the cruel and orders him to disfigure her; **Lakṣmaṇa cuts off her ears and nose** with his sword (Vālmīki, Araṇya 18.19–21). This single act is the spark that ignites the war.
  - *Variant (Kamban's Irāmāvatāram, Tamil):* the encounter is elaborated. She comes to Rāma in a beautiful form and, asked who she is, says she is the daughter of Brahmā's grandson and the younger sister of Kubera and of Rāvaṇa who lifted the silver mountain, and calls herself **Kāmavalli**, an unmarried girl (Kamban, Āraṇiya kāṇṭam, Cūrppaṇakaip paṭalam, v. 39). Her demonic nature shows only when she is rebuffed. [regional]
  - *Variant (extent of the wound):* in **Kamban**, Lakṣmaṇa cuts off her nose, her ears and the tips of her breasts (Cūrppaṇakaip paṭalam, v. 94). Vālmīki has ears and nose only (18.21), though he gives the triple mutilation to the rākṣasī **Ayomukhī** later in this kāṇḍa (69.17). The tradition that Nāsik takes its name from the cut nose (*nāsikā*) is a local folk etymology. [regional] [folk tradition]

## War of the Demons: Khara, Dūṣaṇa, Triśiras (sargas 19–30)

- **Khara roused** - Mutilated, Śūrpaṇakhā flees to her brother **Khara**, ruler of Janasthāna (the Daṇḍaka demon-colony) with his fourteen thousand rākṣasas (Vālmīki, Araṇya 19).
- **The fourteen and the vanguard** - Khara first dispatches **fourteen** rākṣasas with Śūrpaṇakhā; Rāma annihilates them, and she returns shrieking again (Vālmīki, Araṇya 20–21).
- **The host of fourteen thousand** - Khara marches with his full army and his generals **Dūṣaṇa** and **Triśiras** ("three-heads"); dire omens attend their advance (Vālmīki, Araṇya 22–23).
- **Rāma sends the others to a cave** - Rāma stations Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa in a hidden mountain cave and faces the army **single-handed** — one of the epic's great solo-combat set-pieces (Vālmīki, Araṇya 24).
- **Dūṣaṇa slain** - Rāma destroys Dūṣaṇa and his contingent with a storm of arrows (Vālmīki, Araṇya 26).
- **Triśiras slain** - The three-headed Triśiras is beheaded (Vālmīki, Araṇya 27).
- **Khara slain** - In the last stage of the duel, Khara's chariot and bow gone, Rāma takes up an arrow blazing like fire, like a second staff of Brahmā, **given by Indra**, and looses it; it strikes Khara's chest and he falls burning, like Andhaka burned by Rudra. All **fourteen thousand** demons of Janasthāna lie dead, accomplished by Rāma alone (Vālmīki, Araṇya 28–30; 30.24–27). The gods rain flowers.
- **The sages' design revealed** - The royal and great sages, Agastya among them, gather round Rāma and tell him that this was why Indra came to Śarabhaṅga's hermitage, and that the sages brought him to this region by design to destroy these rākṣasas (Vālmīki, Araṇya 30.33–35; CE 3.29.29–31, where Agastya is not named among them). Lakṣmaṇa and Sītā come out of the cave, and Sītā embraces her husband.
- **Akampana's escape** - The rākṣasa **Akampana** alone gets clear of Janasthāna and races to Laṅkā to report the slaughter to Rāvaṇa (Vālmīki, Araṇya 31.1).

## Rāvaṇa Provoked (sargas 31–41)

- **Akampana's counsel and the first visit to Mārīca** - Akampana tells Rāvaṇa that Rāma cannot be beaten in open battle and that the one way to destroy him is to **carry off Sītā**, whose loss will kill him of grief. Rāvaṇa sets out alone with his charioteer in his mule-drawn chariot for the hermitage of **Mārīca** — the demon Rāma had hurled a hundred yojanas into the sea in the [Bāla Kāṇḍa](1-bala-kanda.md) — and Mārīca, who has already felt those arrows, **talks him out of it**; Rāvaṇa turns back to Laṅkā (Vālmīki, Araṇya 31; 31.31–34, 31.40–49).
  - *Note:* Vālmīki's vulgate Rāvaṇa goes to Mārīca **twice**. The first approach, on Akampana's advice, is dissuaded and abandoned; only the second, after Śūrpaṇakhā's goading, produces the golden deer. Retellings that fuse the two erase Mārīca's one successful refusal — and with it the reason his second refusal must be broken by threat.
  - *Recension:* this whole sarga, Akampana's report and the first visit with it, is **absent from the Critical Edition**, where Khara's death (CE 3.29) is followed directly by Śūrpaṇakhā's flight to Laṅkā (CE 3.30.1–3). In the CE, Rāvaṇa goes to Mārīca only once. The valmikiramayan.net editors note the excision and the debate over whether the sarga is original. [scholarly]
- **Śūrpaṇakhā's incitement** - Śūrpaṇakhā reaches Laṅkā, rebukes Rāvaṇa before his court for a king asleep on his throne, and goads him — describing both Rāma's lethal prowess and Sītā's incomparable beauty, planting the desire that will doom him (Vālmīki, Araṇya 32–34).
- **Rāvaṇa seeks Mārīca again** - Rāvaṇa mounts his **golden, mule-yoked** aerial chariot, crosses to the far shore, and comes to Mārīca a second time, the deer-ruse already formed in his mind (Vālmīki, Araṇya 35).
- **Mārīca's warning** - Mārīca warns Rāvaṇa that Rāma is no ordinary man and that abducting Sītā means certain destruction of himself, his city, and his kin, and he urges restraint. He has felt Rāma's arrows twice: once as a youth at Viśvāmitra's sacrifice, and again when he and two other rākṣasas roamed Daṇḍaka in the shape of flesh-eating deer and Rāma loosed three arrows that killed his companions while he alone escaped (Vālmīki, Araṇya 36–39; 38, 39.2–13).
- **Coerced compliance** - Rāvaṇa threatens to kill Mārīca on the spot; Mārīca, choosing "death by Rāma over death by Rāvaṇa," agrees to play the golden deer and rides back with him to Pañcavaṭī (Vālmīki, Araṇya 40–42).

## The Golden Deer and the Abduction (sargas 42–49)

- **The golden deer (māyā-mṛga)** - Mārīca becomes a dazzling **golden, silver-spotted deer** and grazes near the hermitage; Sītā, enchanted, begs Rāma to capture it (alive for a pet, or its hide) (Vālmīki, Araṇya 42–43).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's suspicion** - Lakṣmaṇa warns it is surely a rākṣasa illusion (recalling Mārīca); Sītā insists, and Rāma — half-suspecting a trick yet resolving to kill the demon if it is one — pursues, leaving Lakṣmaṇa to guard Sītā (Vālmīki, Araṇya 43).
- **The deer slain, the false cry** - Rāma chases the deer deep into the forest and pierces it with an arrow; dying, Mārīca resumes his demon form and cries out **"Hā Sītā! Hā Lakṣmaṇa!"** in a perfect imitation of Rāma's voice (Vālmīki, Araṇya 44).
- **Sītā sends Lakṣmaṇa away** - Hearing the cry, Sītā panics; despite Lakṣmaṇa's assurance that Rāma is invincible, she accuses him with harsh words (even of desiring her) until, anguished, he goes to find Rāma (Vālmīki, Araṇya 45).
  - *Variant — the Lakṣmaṇa Rekhā:* In Vālmīki there is **no protective line**; Lakṣmaṇa simply departs. The famous **Lakṣmaṇa Rekhā** (a line drawn with bow or foot that Sītā must not cross) is a **later, non-Vālmīki addition** found in regional and popular tellings, among them Rādheśyām Kathāvācak's Hindi *Rāmāyaṇ*, in which Sītā crosses it absent-mindedly. It is **absent even from Tulsīdās' Araṇya Kāṇḍa** in the [Rāmcaritmānas](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md). The Bengali **Kṛttivāsa Rāmāyaṇa** and the Telugu **Raṅganātha Rāmāyaṇa** are often named as early homes of the motif, but that attribution could not be confirmed here, and the earliest attestation remains unsettled. [scholarly] [folk tradition] [unverified]
  - *Note (the Mānas's own seam):* the Rāmcaritmānas never stages the line in its Araṇya Kāṇḍa, yet its **Laṅkā Kāṇḍa** assumes one — Mandodarī taunts Rāvaṇa that he could not so much as cross the line Lakṣmaṇa drew. The motif was evidently current in Tulsīdās's world without being written into his own abduction scene. See [Rāmcaritmānas](recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md).
- **Rāvaṇa as the mendicant** - With both brothers gone, Rāvaṇa approaches disguised as a wandering **saṃnyāsin / brahmin ascetic** begging alms; Sītā, observing hospitality, comes out to serve him (Vālmīki, Araṇya 46).
- **Sītā's account of herself** - Taking the beggar for a guest owed courtesy, Sītā tells him who she is: Janaka's daughter, wed to Rāma, **twelve years** in her husband's house before the exile fell, the consecration planned in the thirteenth year, and **Rāma twenty-five and herself eighteen** when they left Ayodhyā (Vālmīki, Araṇya 47.4–5, 47.10).
  - *Note:* these figures do not reconcile with [Bāla](1-bala-kanda.md) 20's *ūnaṣoḍaśavarṣo me rāmo* ("my Rāma is not yet sixteen") at Viśvāmitra's request. The twelve years do agree with what Sītā tells Hanumān at [Sundara](5-sundara-kanda.md) 33.17. The Critical Edition reads differently here: Sītā says she lived **one year** (*saṃvatsaram*) in Rāma's house, after which the consecration was planned (CE 3.45.4–5), and the age verse keeps Rāma's twenty-five but has no line for Sītā's eighteen (CE 3.45.10). The CE's Sundara passage still says twelve years (CE 5.31.13), so even the critical text does not agree with itself. Vālmīki never resolves the arithmetic; the corpus records the discrepancy rather than smoothing it. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The abduction** - Rāvaṇa reveals himself, declares his love and power, and — when Sītā scorns him — **seizes her by the hair with his left hand and by the thighs with his right**, sets her in his chariot, and drives up into the sky southward toward Laṅkā as she screams (Vālmīki, Araṇya 48–49; 49.17–20).
  - *Note on the vehicle:* Vālmīki's abduction-car is a **golden, mule-yoked** chariot that goes where its master wills (Araṇya 35.6, 49.19). Jaṭāyu smashes it to splinters and kills the mules (51), after which Rāvaṇa completes the flight **bodily**, carrying Sītā in his arms through the air (51–52, 54). The **Puṣpaka**, Kubera's aerial car, is a different vehicle, but it is not absent from this kāṇḍa: the poet's portrait of Rāvaṇa as Śūrpaṇakhā finds him at court recalls how he took it from Kubera (32.14–15), Rāvaṇa boasts to Sītā of the Puṣpaka "by which I travel the sky" (48.6), and in Laṅkā he invites her to ride in it with him (55.29–30). The story of its capture is told in the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](7-uttara-kanda.md), and Hanumān sees it in Rāvaṇa's palace in the [Sundara Kāṇḍa](5-sundara-kanda.md). The abduction-car itself is never called Puṣpaka.
  - *Variant (Kamban's Irāmāvatāram, Tamil):* Rāvaṇa does not touch Sītā, mindful of a word Brahmā once spoke against him; with his pillar-like arms he digs up the ground she stands on, a yojana deep, and sets it with her on his chariot (Kamban, Āraṇiya kāṇṭam, Irāvaṇaṉ cūḻccip paṭalam, vv. 72–73). The device keeps her untouched by him. [regional]

### The Māyā-Sītā / Shadow-Sītā Tradition [variant cycle]

A major theological variant holds that **Rāvaṇa never abducted the true Sītā at all** — only an illusory double — preserving her absolute purity and pre-explaining her later **Agni-parīkṣā** (fire-ordeal).

- *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, embedded in the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa):* Foreknowing the abduction, Rāma instructs Sītā to **enter the fire (Agni)** and leave a **Māyā-Sītā / Chāyā-Sītā** (shadow-Sītā) in her place; it is this illusory Sītā whom Rāvaṇa carries off. After Rāvaṇa's death, at the fire-ordeal the Māyā-Sītā **vanishes into the fire** and Agni restores the real Sītā — Agni declaring the duplicate was created to bring about Rāvaṇa's destruction.
- *Variant (Kūrma Purāṇa):* the **earliest** text known to contain the motif (so Maithreyi Krishnaraj, as summarised in Wikipedia, *Maya Sita*). As Rāvaṇa arrives, Sītā prays to **Agni**, who makes an exact double and takes the real Sītā into his keeping; at the fire-ordeal Agni restores her and the double is consumed. That summary reports no Vedavatī link for this version.
- *Variant (Brahma-vaivarta Purāṇa):* Agni, disguised as a brahmin, asks Rāma to let him exchange the real Sītā for a **Māyā-Sītā** before the abduction and to take her back after the ordeal. The shadow-Sītā is linked to **Vedavatī**, who rebuffed Rāvaṇa and was to be the cause of his death, and after austerities at Puṣkara she is reborn as **Draupadī** (chapter 14 of the Kamakoti *Essence of Brahma Vaivarta Purana* summary).
- *Variant (Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa):* follows the same scheme: Agni asks Rāma for the exchange and the secret is kept; it is the Māyā-Sītā who longs for the deer and is carried off, and she is called **Trihāyaṇī**, the woman of three ages, as Vedavatī, Māyā-Sītā and Draupadī (Wikipedia, *Maya Sita*, citing Swami Vijnanananda and Vettam Mani).
- *Variant (Tulsīdās' Rāmcaritmānas):* explicitly adopts the Adhyātma scheme — the "Sītā" abducted is the shadow; the real Sītā hides in fire and returns at the ordeal, a key device of the Vaiṣṇava-devotional tradition.
- *Variant (Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa; Balarāma Dāsa's Odia Jagamohana Rāmāyaṇa and Upendra Bhañja's Vaidehīśa Vilāsa; Ezhuthachan's Malayalam Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇam, in which Vedavatī volunteers to be the one carried off):* further regional elaborations of the illusory-Sītā concept. Bengali tellings are sometimes added to this list [unverified].
- *Contrast (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa):* the base text has **no** shadow-Sītā — the real Sītā is abducted and her fidelity is later vindicated by the fire-ordeal itself. The Māyā-Sītā is a Purāṇic/devotional overlay. [scholarly]

## Jaṭāyu's Last Stand (sargas 49–51)

- **Jaṭāyu intercepts** - Hearing Sītā's cries, the aged vulture-king **Jaṭāyu** rouses himself and challenges Rāvaṇa in mid-air, invoking Rāma and dharma, and demanding he release her (Vālmīki, Araṇya 49–50).
- **The aerial battle** - Jaṭāyu fights ferociously — breaking Rāvaṇa's bow, **smashing the chariot to pieces**, killing his mules, tearing off his charioteer's head with his beak, and wounding the demon — a heroic stand despite his age (Vālmīki, Araṇya 51; 51.10–18).
- **Jaṭāyu mortally wounded** - Rāvaṇa finally **severs Jaṭāyu's wings, feet, and flanks** with his sword; the great bird falls, dying. His car destroyed under him, Rāvaṇa takes Sītā **in his arms** and rises again into the sky on his own strength (Vālmīki, Araṇya 51–52; 51.42).

## Carried to Laṅkā (sargas 52–56)

- **Sītā's defiance in flight** - Held aloft in Rāvaṇa's arms she does not plead but arraigns him: a thief who struck while her protectors were drawn away, too small a creature to have faced Rāma in the field, and a fool who has swallowed his own death — she calls out to the forest, the Godāvarī, and the deer to tell Rāma which way she has gone (Vālmīki, Araṇya 52–53).
- **The tokens dropped** - Seeing **five vānaras** seated on a hilltop, Sītā wraps her auspicious ornaments in her golden silk upper cloth and **lets the bundle fall** among them, in the hope that they will tell Rāma; Rāvaṇa, in his haste, does not notice. It becomes the clue Sugrīva later shows Rāma ([Kiṣkindhā](4-kishkindha-kanda.md) 6) (Vālmīki, Araṇya 54.1–4). Itemised lists of what she dropped come from commentators, not from the verse.
- **The eight spies to Janasthāna** - Inside Laṅkā, Rāvaṇa first lodges Sītā in his own inner apartments under rākṣasī guard, then dispatches **eight** picked rākṣasas to re-occupy the emptied **Janasthāna**, watch Rāma's movements, and take any chance to kill him (Vālmīki, Araṇya 54).
- **The palace tour** - Rāvaṇa walks Sītā through his palace and city — the terraces, the treasuries, the aerial gardens, the retinue of women — and offers her the rank of chief queen over all of it; she rejects the whole display and bids him give her back (Vālmīki, Araṇya 55).
- **The blade of grass and the twelve-month ultimatum** - Sītā lays a **blade of grass** between herself and Rāvaṇa and answers him past it, refusing to address him directly; she does the same in the Sundara Kāṇḍa. Rāvaṇa gives her **twelve months** to submit, threatening that if she has not yielded by then his cooks will cut her up for his morning meal (Vālmīki, Araṇya 56.1, 56.24–25; CE 3.54.1, 3.54.22).
  - *Note:* this clock runs across the kāṇḍas. When Rāvaṇa comes to her in the [Sundara Kāṇḍa](5-sundara-kanda.md) he reminds her that **two months remain** of the term (Sundara 22.8), and Sītā gives Hanumān the same reckoning: the tenth month is running and two are left (Sundara 33.31, 37.8). It is the deadline that makes the war urgent rather than merely inevitable.
- **Committed to the Aśoka grove** - Rāvaṇa has her taken from the palace to the **Aśoka-vana** and set under a guard of misshapen rākṣasīs, ordered to keep her there in secret and to break her pride by threats and coaxing, as one tames a wild she-elephant — the confinement in which the Sundara Kāṇḍa will find her (Vālmīki, Araṇya 56.26–32).

## The Brothers' Grief and the First Clues (sargas 57–75)

- **Return to an empty hermitage** - Rāma, having killed the deer and met the returning Lakṣmaṇa, reproaches him for leaving Sītā alone, races back to find Pañcavaṭī empty, and descends into wild grief, questioning trees, animals, and rivers about Sītā (Vālmīki, Araṇya 57–63; the questioning of the trees at 60).
- **Rāma's rage against the three worlds** - At the Godāvarī he finds her fallen flowers, the footprints of a huge rākṣasa, and the wreckage of a fight — a broken bow, a shattered chariot. Grief turns: Rāma declares he will burn up the whole creation — earth, sky, the worlds of the gods — and empty the universe of every living thing if Sītā is not returned to him, and takes up his bow to do it. It is the epic's darkest picture of him (Vālmīki, Araṇya 64; 64.53–71).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's restraint speech** - Lakṣmaṇa argues him down: the worlds have not wronged him, and a king's anger must fall on the guilty party alone; let them first find who took her, and then strike. He presses the argument again in a second speech on fortitude in misfortune. The pattern of the whole epic — the younger brother holding the elder inside dharma — is set here (Vālmīki, Araṇya 65–66).
- **Finding Jaṭāyu** - The brothers find the dying Jaṭāyu, who names **Rāvaṇa** as the abductor and reports the southward flight (Vālmīki, Araṇya 67–68).
- **The Vinda muhūrta** - With his last breath Jaṭāyu adds the one piece of hope in the kāṇḍa: Sītā was carried off in the hour called **Vinda**, and what is lost in that muhūrta is soon regained by its owner, while the thief perishes like a fish that has swallowed the hook. He then expires; Rāma performs his **funeral rites** with his own hands, and the bird attains the higher worlds by Rāma's grace (Vālmīki, Araṇya 68; 68.12–13; CE 3.64.12–13).
- **Ayomukhī** - Moving on through the Krauñca forest toward Mataṅga's hermitage, the brothers are set upon by the rākṣasī **Ayomukhī**, who embraces Lakṣmaṇa and claims him for a husband; he cuts off her ears, nose, and breasts and she flees howling — a deliberate echo of Śūrpaṇakhā, and the beat immediately before Kabandha's arm closes on him (Vālmīki, Araṇya 69.8–18).
  - *Recension:* the Ayomukhī episode is **not in the Critical Edition**, whose sarga passes straight from the Krauñca forest to Lakṣmaṇa's omens and Kabandha (CE 3.65.5–15). Kamban, by contrast, gives her a whole canto, the *Ayōmukip paṭalam*. [scholarly] [regional]
- **Kabandha slain** - The headless monster **Kabandha** — a trunk with a mouth in its belly, one eye in its chest, and **two arms a yojana long** — seizes both brothers; they hew off the arms, and, dying, he asks to be **cremated**. He tells Lakṣmaṇa to know him as the **son of Danu** (*danoḥ putram*), and the narrator, and later Rāma speaking to Śabarī, call him simply Danu (71.20, 71.26, 74.19). Once of surpassing beauty, he took a monstrous shape to terrify the forest sages until the ṛṣi **Sthūlaśiras**, whom he had frightened, cursed him to keep it, the curse to end when Rāma cut off his arms and burned him. Proud of the long life Brahmā had granted him, he then attacked Indra, whose thunderbolt drove his head and thighs into his body (71.2–15). From the pyre he rises in a shining form and **counsels Rāma to ally with the vānara Sugrīva** at Ṛśyamūka, naming him the one being who can find Sītā, and describes the road to Pampā, Mataṅga's grove and the waiting Śabarī (Vālmīki, Araṇya 69–73; the counsel at 72–73). Full treatment: [Kabandha](../characters/kabandha.md).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Āraṇyaka-parvan, Rāmopākhyāna):* the radiant being who rises from Kabandha's body names himself the **gandharva Viśvāvasu**, born into a rākṣasa-frequented form by a *brahma*-curse (*gandharvo viśvāvasur ahaṃ … prāpto brahmānuśāpena yoniṃ rākṣasasevitām*, MBh 3.263.38; the compound can mean a curse of Brahmā or of a brahmin), and directs Rāma to Sugrīva. Vālmīki's text never gives the name Viśvāvasu; summaries that give Vālmīki's Kabandha that name are blending the two accounts. Cataloged, not adopted.
- **Śabarī's devotion** - Following Kabandha's directions to the west shore of Pampā, the brothers come to Mataṅga's grove and the aged **Śabarī**, whom Vālmīki calls a **śramaṇī** (a woman ascetic). She served Mataṅga's disciples, who were carried to heaven when Rāma reached Citrakūṭa, and has waited for him on their word. She offers him the forest fruit she has gathered on the shores of Pampā, shows him the grove and the altars where her teachers worshipped, and, with his leave, **gives herself to the fire** and rises in a radiant form to the world of her teachers (Vālmīki, Araṇya 74; 73.26, 74.7, 74.14–17, 74.32–35). The directions to Pampā and Sugrīva are Kabandha's (72–73), not hers. Full treatment: [Śabarī](../characters/shabari.md).
  - *Note:* the familiar "Niṣāda / Bhīl" labeling of Śabarī is later framing; the base text's word for her is *śramaṇī*, and her name marks her as a woman of the Śabara people.
  - *Variant (later devotional tradition):* the beloved image of **Śabarī's tasted berries**, in which she bites each fruit first to be sure it is sweet, is in neither Vālmīki nor the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa nor the Rāmcaritmānas (in the Mānas she simply brings roots and fruits). Devdutt Pattanaik traces the tasted fruit to the Odia Rāmāyaṇa of Balarāma Dāsa (of the fifteenth-century Pañcasakhā poets), where it is a mango, and the jujube (*ber*) form to Priyādās's Braj commentary *Bhaktirasabodhinī* (c. 1712), where Philip Lutgendorf also finds Śabarī tasting the jujubes and setting the sweetest aside for Rāma. The valmikiramayan.net commentary on Araṇya 74.17 instead assigns the tasted fruit to the Padma Purāṇa, quoting a verse without a location [unverified]. The **navadhā-bhakti** (nine forms of devotion) discourse Rāma gives her is a separate amplification, found in the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa (Araṇya 10.20–31, per Lutgendorf) and, closely following it, in the Rāmcaritmānas. Neither is in Vālmīki; both are central images of bhakti transcending caste. [late text / devotional]
- **Toward Pampā** - Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa proceed to the lotus-covered **Pampā** lake near **Ṛśyamūka** mountain, where Sugrīva and Hanumān dwell — the kāṇḍa ends on the threshold of the alliance that will recover Sītā (Vālmīki, Araṇya 75).
