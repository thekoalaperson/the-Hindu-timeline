# Uttara Kāṇḍa — The Final Book

> Cosmic address: [Vaivasvata Manvantara](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md) → 28th Mahāyuga → **[Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** → the Rāma-avatāra cycle (its closing chapter and aftermath).
> Parent: **[Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive](README.md)** · Previous: [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](6-yuddha-kanda.md) · (last book)
> See also: [Daśāvatāra (Rāma)](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) · [Sūryavaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) · [Itihāsa-Epics corpus](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)

The seventh and last book of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa. The vulgate (Southern-recension) text has **111 sargas**, and every citation in this file follows its numbering. The vulgate also prints thirteen unnumbered *prakṣipta* (interpolated) chapters, 23b–f, 37b–f and 59b–d. The Baroda **Critical Edition (CE) constitutes 100 sargas and 2,690 verses** (counted from the CE text on GRETIL). Where a citation adds "CE 7.x.y", the passage was checked in that Sanskrit. The two numberings agree through sarga 36 and then drift apart (vulgate 43 = CE 42, vulgate 76 = CE 67, vulgate 108 = CE 98), because the CE leaves out whole vulgate sargas; passages missing from the CE are flagged where they appear. Named *Uttara* ("Latter / Final / Beyond"), the book is a double appendix: a long **flashback** filling in the deep prehistory of the rākṣasas, Rāvaṇa, and the vānaras (sargas 1–36), and the **aftermath of the war** — Rāma's reign (Rāma-rājya), the banishment of Sītā, the twins Lava and Kuśa, Sītā's return to the earth, and Rāma's departure to heaven (sargas 37–111). It also recasts the entire epic as a story sung *back to its own hero*.

> **Status — [disputed] / [scholarly]:** Since Hermann Jacobi (*Das Rāmāyaṇa*, 1893), most modern philologists have held the Uttara Kāṇḍa, with the Bāla Kāṇḍa, to be **later** than a five-book core. The case rests on the text itself, not on missing manuscripts: the Baroda editors found the book in their manuscripts and constituted it. The arguments are these. Rāma is openly Viṣṇu in Books 1 and 7 but, in Jacobi's phrase, "durchaus Mensch" (wholly human) in Books 2–6 (quoted in Brockington 1976). The Yuddha Kāṇḍa closes with its own *phala-śruti*, as if the poem ended there (vulgate *Yuddha* 128). The frame ("the sages ask Rāma about Rāvaṇa") reads like an appendix. And many readers feel that the killing of Śambūka and the banishment of Sītā **contradict the dharma and characterization** of the Rāma of the central books. *Counter-view:* the Bāla Kāṇḍa already knows this book. Its table of contents lists "the sending-away of Vaidehī" and says Vālmīki told "whatever was still to come" in the *uttara* poem (CE 1.3.28–29), and the vulgate counts "six kāṇḍas and the Uttara" (*Bāla* 4.2). Traditional commentators and devotional Hinduism treat the book as integral and canonical, and astronomical-dating advocates argue that it is authentic. This file records the contents as transmitted and flags the controversy rather than resolving it.

## I. Frame: the sages question Rāma (sargas 1–3)

- **The ṛṣis pay homage** - After the coronation (the end of *Yuddha Kāṇḍa*), Agastya and the assembled sages come to congratulate Rāma, arriving from the four quarters. Kauśika, Yavakrīta, Raibhya, Cyavana and Kaṇva come from the east; Svastyātreya, Namuci and Pramuci come with Agastya from the south; Pṛṣadgu, Kavaṣa, Dhaumya and Raudreya come from the west; and the seven great ṛṣis, Vasiṣṭha, Kaśyapa, Atri, Viśvāmitra, Gautama, Jamadagni and Bharadvāja, come as well (CE 7.1.1–5). Rāma asks them to explain how Rāvaṇa, Kumbhakarṇa and Indrajit grew so mighty. Agastya becomes the narrator of the rākṣasa prehistory (Vālmīki *Uttara* 1–2).
- **Frame function** - This Q-and-A device lets the book retro-fit a full genealogy and backstory onto enemies already slain in Book 6 — a structural marker many [scholarly] readers cite for its appendix status.

## II. The rākṣasa prehistory — Rāvaṇa's ancestry & rise (sargas 2–34)

- **Pulastya → Viśravas** - The rākṣasa line descends from the Prajāpati / Saptarṣi **Pulastya**, a mind-born son of Brahmā. While he does tapas at the hermitage of the royal sage **Tṛṇabindu** on Meru, the young women who come to play there disturb him. He declares that any girl who comes into his sight will conceive. Tṛṇabindu's daughter has not heard the warning; she wanders in, hears him reciting the Veda, and finds herself pregnant. Her father gives her to Pulastya, and the son is named **Viśravas** because the Veda was "heard" (*viśruta*) by her (Vālmīki *Uttara* 2; CE 7.2.10–28). See [Pulastya](../characters/pulastya.md) and [Viśravas](../characters/vishravas.md).
- **The two wives of Viśravas** - The sage **Bharadvāja** gives Viśravas his daughter **Devavarṇinī**, and she bears **Kubera (Vaiśravaṇa)**. Brahmā himself names the child, after his likeness to his father (CE 7.3.3–7). Later the rākṣasī **Kaikasī**, daughter of Sumālī, is sent by her father to choose Viśravas for herself. She approaches him at a "dreadful hour" (*dāruṇā velā*) while he is at his fire-offering, which is why her sons are rākṣasas (Vālmīki *Uttara* 9; CE 7.9.1–18).
  - *Variant (Purāṇic):* the Purāṇas name Kubera's mother **Iḍaviḍā (Ilavilā)** and make her Tṛṇabindu's daughter, giving Viśravas the wife whom Vālmīki gives his father. Vālmīki's Uttara has no Ilavilā.
- **Kubera, lord of the north** - Kubera does austerity for thousands of years; Brahmā makes him a **Loka-pāla** (world-guardian of the north) alongside Indra, Varuṇa and Yama, gives him the lordship of wealth and the flying chariot **Puṣpaka** — and nothing else. Laṅkā is not Brahmā's gift: asked by his son where he should dwell, **Viśravas** names the golden city on **Mount Trikūṭa**, built by Viśvakarmā and standing empty and unguarded since Viṣṇu drove the rākṣasas down to Rasātala (Vālmīki *Uttara* 3; CE 7.3.14–27).
- **Before Rāvaṇa: the first rākṣasas** - Rāma is surprised to hear that Laṅkā had rākṣasa owners before Rāvaṇa's line, and asks who they were (CE 7.4.1–7). Agastya's answer reaches back to creation. Brahmā made beings to guard the waters. Those who said "we will guard" (*rakṣāmaḥ*) became **rākṣasas**, and those who said "we will worship" (*yakṣāmaḥ*) became **yakṣas** (CE 7.4.9–13). The line runs as follows:
  - The brothers **Heti** and **Praheti** come first. Heti marries **Bhayā**, sister of Kāla, and their son **Vidyutkeśa** marries **Sālakaṭaṅkaṭā**.
  - She abandons their newborn **Sukeśa**. **Śiva**, moved by **Pārvatī**'s pity, makes the child as old as his mother and gives him a flying city (CE 7.4.14–29).
  - Sukeśa's three sons, **Mālyavān, Sumālī and Mālī**, win invincibility from Brahmā. **Viśvakarmā** directs them to Laṅkā on Trikūṭa, a city he had built at Indra's command, and they marry the three daughters of the gandharvī **Narmadā**. Sumālī's children by **Ketumatī** include **Kaikasī** and **Kumbhīnasī** (CE 7.5.13–36).
  - When they harass the gods, Śiva sends the gods on to **Viṣṇu**. Viṣṇu's discus beheads Mālī, and the survivors flee with their wives to Pātāla (Vālmīki *Uttara* 4–8; CE 7.7.37–39, 7.8.21).
  - Agastya ends by telling Rāma that these older rākṣasas were stronger than Rāvaṇa, that only Nārāyaṇa can kill rākṣasas, and that **Rāma is that Nārāyaṇa** (CE 7.8.23–25). See [Sumālī and Mālyavān](../characters/sumali-and-malyavan.md).
- **Birth of the brood** - From Kaikasī are born, in this order, **Rāvaṇa** (ten-headed, twenty-armed, born among evil omens and named Daśagrīva by his father), **Kumbhakarṇa** (the gigantic glutton), the sister **Śūrpaṇakhā**, and last the gentle **Vibhīṣaṇa**, whom Viśravas had promised her would be "worthy of my line" (Vālmīki *Uttara* 9; CE 7.9.20–27). See [Kaikasī](../characters/kaikasi.md).
- **The great tapas & Brahmā's boon** - Spurred by Kaikasī's envy of Kubera's splendour, the three brothers do extreme penance at Gokarṇa. Rāvaṇa fasts for ten thousand years and offers one head to the fire at the end of each thousand. Nine heads are gone, and he is about to cut off the tenth, when Brahmā appears and later restores them all. Rāvaṇa asks that **suparṇas, nāgas, yakṣas, daityas, dānavas, rākṣasas and devas** be unable to kill him. That is the list he dictates himself, and it contains no gandharvas. He dismisses "men and the other creatures" as straw. The text names **men** here and **not monkeys**, and this is the loophole that dooms him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 10; CE 7.10.10–22).
  - *Vulgate addition:* Brahmā also grants him the power to take any form at will. The CE lacks this boon.
  - *Variant / cross-link:* this omission is the doctrinal reason Viṣṇu incarnates specifically as a **man** ([Rāma](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md)) aided by **vānaras** — see the deva-petition frame in [Bāla Kāṇḍa](1-bala-kanda.md).
- **Kumbhakarṇa's boon of sleep** - The devas ask Brahmā to refuse Kumbhakarṇa any boon. They give the record: seven apsarases of Nandana and ten of Indra's attendants, besides ṛṣis and men, already devoured (CE 7.10.31–34). Brahmā calls **Sarasvatī**, who **enters his mouth**. Asked his wish, Kumbhakarṇa says only "to sleep for innumerable years," and Brahmā answers "so be it." When the goddess leaves him he wonders aloud how such words came out of his mouth (CE 7.10.35–41). Vālmīki gives no waking-clause here: the goddess on the tongue is the whole mechanism, and this is the older of the epic's two sleep-etiologies (Vālmīki *Uttara* 10). Later Rāvaṇa has a sleeping-palace a yojana wide built for him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 13; CE 7.13.1–7).
  - *Later layer (Vālmīki* Yuddha *61):* on the battlefield the sleep is re-explained as a **pun** — Kumbhakarṇa meant to ask for *nirdevatvam* (godlessness, the annihilation of the gods) and Sarasvatī twisted it to *nidrātvam* (sleep) — and Rāvaṇa is said to have begged the term commuted to **six months asleep, one day awake**. Both the pun and the commutation belong to that later sarga, not to the boon-scene; see [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](6-yuddha-kanda.md) and [Kumbhakarṇa](../characters/kumbhakarna.md).
- **Vibhīṣaṇa's boon** - The righteous brother asks only that his mind stay on dharma in every circumstance. Pleased, Brahmā adds **immortality unasked**, because a rākṣasa-born mind that never turns to adharma deserves it (Vālmīki *Uttara* 10; CE 7.10.27–30).
- **The cession of Laṅkā** - Sumālī comes up from Rasātala and urges Rāvaṇa to take Laṅkā back. At first Rāvaṇa refuses, saying Kubera is "our elder". **Prahasta** argues that heroes have no brotherhood and cites the gods' own war on their half-brothers the daityas, and Rāvaṇa sends him as envoy to ask for the city. Kubera is willing to give it up but consults their father first. **Viśravas counsels Kubera to yield**: he has already rebuked Rāvaṇa, he says, and the boon has made his son reckless. Kubera withdraws to **Mount Kailāsa**, and Laṅkā passes to Rāvaṇa without a blow struck (Vālmīki *Uttara* 11; CE 7.11.10–41).
- **Maya's daughter and the śakti dowry** - **Śūrpaṇakhā** is given first, to the Kālakeya dānava **Vidyujjihva**. Then, while hunting, Rāvaṇa meets the dānava architect **Maya**, who is looking for a husband for his daughter by the apsaras **Hemā**. Rāvaṇa marries **Mandodarī**, sister of Maya's sons **Māyāvī and Dundubhi**, the two Vālī later kills (see [Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa](4-kishkindha-kanda.md)). With her he receives the **infallible śakti** (javelin) "with which he struck down Lakṣmaṇa" (CE 7.12.19). Kumbhakarṇa marries **Vajrajvālā**, Virocana's *daughter's daughter*, and Vibhīṣaṇa marries **Saramā**, daughter of the gandharva king Śailūṣa. Saramā is named from her mother's cry to the Mānasa lake, *saro mā vardhata*, "lake, do not rise". Mandodarī then bears **Meghanāda** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 12; CE 7.12.2–28). See [Mandodarī](../characters/mandodari.md) and [Saramā](../characters/sarama.md).
- **The murdered envoy and the war on the yakṣas** - Kubera, hearing of his brother's crimes, sends an envoy urging him back to dharma. The message includes Kubera's own story: he looked at Pārvatī and lost his left eye, becoming **Ekākṣipiṅgala** ("one-eyed, tawny"), and by a long vow he became Śiva's friend. Rāvaṇa hears it as a threat. He cuts the envoy down and gives him to the rākṣasas to eat, then marches on Kailāsa. He breaks the yakṣa army, strikes Kubera down with a mace, and carries off the **Puṣpaka** as spoil — the war the peaceful cession had avoided (Vālmīki *Uttara* 13–15; CE 7.13.11–38, 7.15.23–31).
- **Origin of the name "Rāvaṇa"** - In pride after defeating Kubera, Rāvaṇa finds the Puṣpaka stopped in mid-air above Śiva's mountain. He tries to uproot Kailāsa, and Śiva presses it down with his toe, pinning Rāvaṇa's arms beneath it. The rākṣasa's roar (*rava*) fills the three worlds, and the pleased god **names him Rāvaṇa**, "he who made the worlds cry out" (Vālmīki *Uttara* 16; CE 7.16.17–30). Deep treatment: [Rāvaṇa at Kailāsa](../ravana-kailasa-ravananugraha.md).
  - *Vulgate additions:* Rāvaṇa praises Śiva with hymns and "laments for a thousand years", and Śiva gives him the sword **Candrahāsa**. The CE has neither the thousand years nor the sword.
  - **Nandī's curse** - Mocked as a monkey-faced creature when he barred the mountain path, Śiva's attendant **Nandī** curses Rāvaṇa that **monkeys** of his own shape and strength will be born to destroy his race. This is the second, and more specific, naming of the doom already left open in Brahmā's boon (Vālmīki *Uttara* 16; CE 7.16.12–15).
- **Rāvaṇa's world-conquest (digvijaya)** - He overruns the three worlds, defeating kings, yakṣas and nāgas and forcing captive women into the Puṣpaka (Vālmīki *Uttara* 18–23).
  - **Marutta's sacrifice** - At King **Marutta**'s Māheśvara sacrifice at Uśīrabīja, with Bṛhaspati's brother **Saṃvarta** officiating, the attending gods flee Rāvaṇa by taking animal forms: Indra a peacock, Yama a crow, Kubera a chameleon and Varuṇa a swan. Saṃvarta stops the consecrated king from fighting, and Rāvaṇa eats the assembled sages. Afterwards each god blesses the creature whose shape saved him. The peacock gets Indra's thousand eyes in its tail and dances in the rain. The crow is freed from disease and from death unless men kill it, and the dead in Yama's realm are fed when it is fed. The swan becomes moon-white, and the chameleon gets a golden head (Vālmīki *Uttara* 18; CE 7.18.2–32). The vulgate adds that the peacock "will have nothing to fear from serpents".
  - **The march on Yama** - **Nārada** meets Rāvaṇa in the clouds and asks why he troubles a world of mortals who are already Death's. He steers him toward Yama, then follows to watch, curious (Vālmīki *Uttara* 20; CE 7.20.1–25). Rāvaṇa invades Yamaloka, sees the torments of the hells, and shatters Yama's host. When Yama raises the **kāla-daṇḍa**, Brahmā intervenes. The rod is infallible, so if it fell either Rāvaṇa would die and Brahmā's boon would be false, or he would live and the rod would be false. Yama withdraws rather than make Brahmā a liar (Vālmīki *Uttara* 21–22; CE 7.22.32–41).
  - **The Nivātakavacas** - A stalemate of more than a year in Rasātala against the **Nivātakavacas** ends in a Brahmā-brokered, fire-witnessed pact of friendship. From them Rāvaṇa learns **ninety-nine māyās** (*śatam ekonam*, "a hundred less one") — the arsenal his son will later inherit (Vālmīki *Uttara* 23; CE 7.23.7–14).
  - **Varuṇa's sons** - Rāvaṇa then storms the palace of **Varuṇa**, passing the wish-cow Surabhi, whose milk is the Ocean of Milk. Varuṇa's sons and grandsons fight him in the air, and **Mahodara** smashes their chariots. Varuṇa's minister Prabhāsa tells Rāvaṇa that the lord of waters has gone to Brahmaloka to hear gandharva music. Rāvaṇa shouts his own name in triumph and leaves (Vālmīki *Uttara* 23; CE 7.23.16–46).
  - **The Kālakeyas and the widowing of Śūrpaṇakhā** - Rāvaṇa slaughters four hundred **Kālakeya** dānavas at Aśmanagara (CE 7.23.15). Among the dead is **Vidyujjihva**, the husband his own sister **Śūrpaṇakhā** had married. When she reproaches him, he pleads that in the heat of battle he could not tell kin from foe. He sends her to **Daṇḍaka** under the protection of their maternal cousin **Khara**, whom he sets over the forest with fourteen thousand rākṣasas and **Dūṣaṇa** as general. The forest, he notes, was cursed long ago by Uśanas (Vālmīki *Uttara* 23–24; CE 7.24.18–35). See [Śūrpaṇakhā](../characters/shurpanakha.md) and [Khara](../characters/khara-and-the-janasthana-command.md).
  - **The curse of the abducted women** - The chaste captives heaped into the Puṣpaka curse him: since he delights in other men's wives, **a woman shall be the cause of his death**. This collective śāpa outranks any single grievance in the doom-ledger (Vālmīki *Uttara* 24; CE 7.24.15).
  - *Prakṣipta (vulgate 23b–f):* the vulgate also prints five extra chapters of conquests [interpolation]. Rāvaṇa meets the imprisoned dānava **Bali** in Pātāla. He challenges the **Sun**, whose doorkeeper Daṇḍī gives him a formal "defeat". He clashes with the Ikṣvāku emperor **Māndhātā**. Brahmā gives him a protective mantra in the Moon's region. And he is humbled by the dancing hosts of **Kapila**, the "Mahāpuruṣa". None of this is in the CE. The Māndhātā encounter also sits uneasily with sarga 67, where Māndhātṛ dies by Lavaṇa's trident.
- **Vedavatī's curse** - In the Himālayan forest Rāvaṇa finds the ascetic **Vedavatī**. She is the daughter of the brahmarṣi **Kuśadhvaja**, Bṛhaspati's son, and was born from her father's Veda-recitation. Her father had meant her for **Viṣṇu** and was murdered by the daitya Śambhu for refusing other suitors. Rāvaṇa seizes her by the hair. She cuts off the hair with her own hand and enters the fire, vowing to be **reborn, not from a womb, for his destruction**. Agastya then identifies her outright: the Vedavatī of the Kṛta age is the one born in the Tretā "in a furrow struck by the plough" and called **Sītā** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 17; CE 7.17.7–31). See [Vedavatī](../characters/vedavati.md).
  - *Variant ("Māyā-Sītā / Chāyā-Sītā"):* in several Purāṇas (Kūrma, Brahmavaivarta, Devī-Bhāgavata) and [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](README.md), the **real Sītā is hidden in fire** and a shadow-Sītā (sometimes equated with Vedavatī) is abducted — harmonizing this backstory with Sītā's purity. [late text]
- **The Anaraṇya curse** - The Ikṣvāku king **Anaraṇya**, struck down by a single blow of Rāvaṇa's palm and dying, curses him by his gifts, oblations and good rule that a king born in his own line will take Rāvaṇa's life (Vālmīki *Uttara* 19; CE 7.19.16–24).
- **Madhu and Kumbhīnasī** - Returning to Laṅkā, Rāvaṇa finds his son in the **Nikumbhilā** grove, finishing **seven sacrifices** under **Uśanas** (Śukra). In the Māheśvara rite Paśupati himself has given Meghanāda a sky-going chariot, the **tāmasī** darkness-māyā that hides him in battle, inexhaustible quivers and an unconquerable bow. Rāvaṇa grumbles that the rites honoured his enemies, the gods (CE 7.25.2–15). **Vibhīṣaṇa** then rebukes him for the abducted women and tells him that, while he was away, the rākṣasa **Madhu** carried off their cousin **Kumbhīnasī**. Rāvaṇa marches on Madhupura. Kumbhīnasī begs for her husband's life, and Rāvaṇa spares Madhu and enlists him for the war on heaven (Vālmīki *Uttara* 25; CE 7.25.18–49). Kumbhīnasī becomes the mother of **Lavaṇa** — see [Madhu, Lavaṇa and the founding of Mathurā](../madhu-lavana-and-mathura.md).
- **Nalakūbara's curse** - For violating the apsarā **Rambhā** — who was on her way to Nalakūbara and had named herself his wife, making her Rāvaṇa's own nephew's consort — Kubera's son **Nalakūbara** curses him that if he ever again touches a woman who does not desire him, his head will **split into seven pieces** (*saptadhā*) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 26; CE 7.26.43–44).
- **Brahmā's curse over Puñjikasthalā** - A second and separate curse to the same effect, and the one Rāvaṇa himself cites in the war. Explaining to Mahāpārśva why he does not force Sītā, he recalls violating the apsarā **Puñjikasthalā** as she went to Brahmā's palace. Brahmā's sentence was that if he forced another woman, his head would **burst into a hundred pieces** (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 13.11–15). The two curses are distinct in text, giver, sarga and number — see [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](6-yuddha-kanda.md) and [Tapas, boons & curses](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/tapas-boons-and-curses.md).
- **The death of Sumālī** - In the war with the devas, Rāvaṇa's grandfather **Sumālī** is struck on the head by the mace of **Sāvitra**, eighth of the Vasus, and reduced to ash; no bone or flesh is left. The rākṣasa host breaks and flees, leaving the field to Rāvaṇa's son (Vālmīki *Uttara* 27; CE 7.27.34–42).
- **Indrajit conquers Indra** - **Meghanāda**, named for a birth-cry like thunder, first fights Indra's son **Jayanta**. Then, invisible by māyā, he binds **Indra** himself and carries him to Laṅkā (Vālmīki *Uttara* 28–29; CE 7.28.7–12, 7.29.26–40). Brahmā comes to ransom Indra and renames the son **Indrajit** ("conqueror of Indra"). Indrajit first asks for immortality. Refused, he chooses a substitute: that he be unbeatable whenever he has first worshipped the fire before battle, and that he perish if he fights *before* the japa-homa is complete. This is the clause the war-book turns on (Vālmīki *Uttara* 30; CE 7.30.5–14). In the Uttara this fire-clause is Brahmā's grant; the Nikumbhilā grove belongs to the earlier sacrifices of sarga 25. See [Indrajit](../characters/indrajit-meghnad.md).
- **Why Indra could be beaten** - Brahmā explains the defeat as the ripening of an old debt: it is **Gautama's curse for the seduction of Ahalyā** that stripped the king of the gods of his invulnerability, tying the Laṅkā war back to the Bāla Kāṇḍa's [Ahalyā episode](1-bala-kanda.md) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 30).
  - *Internal variant:* this telling differs from Bāla 48–49. In the Uttara, **Brahmā made Ahalyā** from the finest part of every creature and held her in trust with Gautama before giving her to him. Gautama's curse makes Indra fall into enemy hands in battle and carry half the sin of every future adultery among men. Ahalyā loses her uniqueness: beauty is shared out among all creatures. Indra is cleansed by a **Vaiṣṇava sacrifice**, and Jayanta, the text adds, was carried safe into the ocean by his grandfather (CE 7.30.17–41). See [Ahalyā](../characters/ahalya.md).
- **Defeat by Kārtavīrya Arjuna** - On the Narmadā, the thousand-armed Haihaya king of Māhiṣmatī, **Kārtavīrya Arjuna**, fells Rāvaṇa with a mace and binds him with his thousand arms. He releases him only at the request of Rāvaṇa's grandfather **Pulastya**, and the two make a fire-witnessed friendship. Agastya draws the moral: there is always someone stronger (Vālmīki *Uttara* 31–33; CE 7.32.61–33.22).
- **Defeat by Vālī** - The vānara king **Vālī** tucks Rāvaṇa under his arm and circles the four oceans during his prayers; humbled, Rāvaṇa makes a fire-pact of friendship with him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 34; CE 7.34.11–40) — back-explaining Vālī's might shown in [Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa](4-kishkindha-kanda.md). See [Sugrīva and Vālī](../characters/sugriva-and-vali.md).

## III. The vānara & Hanumān backstory (sargas 35–36, with an appendix at 37)

- **Rāma's question** - Rāma has watched Hanumān cross the sea, burn Laṅkā and win the war almost alone. He asks Agastya why such a being did not simply burn up Vālī "like a creeper" when the feud with Sugrīva broke out, and stood by while his king was hounded. He concludes that Hanumān must not have known his own strength. The whole birth-account is Agastya's answer, and the question is what makes the curse of forgetting the point of the telling (Vālmīki *Uttara* 35; CE 7.35.2–16).
- **Birth on Sumeru** - Hanumān is born on **Sumeru**, where **Kesarī** rules, to Kesarī's beloved wife **Añjanā**, and **Vāyu** is his begetter (Vālmīki *Uttara* 35; CE 7.35.19–20). The Uttara does not say that Añjanā was a fallen apsarā; that backstory belongs to Jāmbavān's speech in [Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa](4-kishkindha-kanda.md) 66.
- **The leap at the sun** - Left alone and hungry while his mother gathers fruit, the infant takes the rising sun for a fruit and leaps "many thousands of yojanas" toward it (CE 7.35.29). It is an **eclipse day**: **Rāhu**, arriving to seize the sun, is grabbed by the child and flees to **Indra**. Indra rides out on **Airāvata** with Rāhu ahead of him, and Hanumān lunges at the elephant, taking it for another fruit. **Indra's vajra** drops him on a mountain and breaks his **left jaw** (*hanu*). **Sūrya**, seeing an innocent child, does not burn him, and **Vāyu** follows his son, cooling him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 35; CE 7.35.21–47).
- **Vāyu's strike** - In grief the Wind withdraws from all creatures (the vulgate takes him into a cave with the child). Breath stops in every body, rites fail, and creation begins to suffocate. Brahmā revives the child with a touch, and the gods come one by one to win the Wind back (Vālmīki *Uttara* 35–36; CE 7.35.48–36.6).
- **The boons, itemized** - Each god gives a boon (Vālmīki *Uttara* 36; CE 7.36.10–23):
  - **Indra:** a lotus garland, the name **Hanumān** from the broken jaw, and immunity to the vajra.
  - **Sūrya (Mārtāṇḍa):** a hundredth part of his own splendour, and the śāstras, so that the boy will be eloquent when he is old enough to study.
  - **Varuṇa:** no death by his noose or by water for a million years.
  - **Yama:** immunity to his rod, freedom from disease, and freedom from despondency in battle.
  - **Kubera:** his mace shall never kill him.
  - **Śiva:** immunity to Śiva and his weapons.
  - **Viśvakarmā:** immunity to every weapon he has made for the gods.
  - **Brahmā:** immunity to every *brahma-daṇḍa* (Brahmā's weapon and the brahmins' curse alike), and long life. He then tells Vāyu that his son will be unconquerable and will do deeds for Rāma's sake.
- **The sages' curse of forgetfulness** - Grown overbearing with these boons, the boy wrecks the ladles, fire-altars and bark-cloth of the hermitages. The sages bear it for a while, since Brahmā has made him proof against their curses. In the end the ṛṣis descended from **Bhṛgu and Aṅgiras** curse him mildly that "for a long time" (*dīrghakālam*) he will not know the strength he leans on. That is why he never used it during the Vālī–Sugrīva feud (Vālmīki *Uttara* 36; CE 7.36.28–40).
  - *Recensional difference:* the **release-clause** — "when it is recalled to you, you shall wield it again" — is in the vulgate's sarga 36 but **not in the CE's constituted verse**, which says only "for a long time". It is the clause that makes Jāmbavān's rousing at [Kiṣkindhā 65–66](4-kishkindha-kanda.md) work as a key turning in a lock; see [Sundara Kāṇḍa](5-sundara-kanda.md) and [Hanumān](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/hanuman.md). [recensional]
  - **The grammarian** - Agastya adds that Hanumān once followed the Sun, walking backwards facing him from the eastern to the western mountain, to learn grammar from him (CE 7.36.42). He also says that all the great vānaras were "created by the gods for your sake" (CE 7.36.44).
- **The birth of Vālī and Sugrīva — appendix material** - The vulgate prints, as the **appendix chapters 37b–c** after sarga 37, Agastya's account of **Ṛkṣarajas**, a vānara born of tears that Brahmā wiped from his eyes. Ṛkṣarajas sees his reflection in an enchanted lake on Meru, plunges in to fight it, and surfaces as a woman. **Indra** fathers **Vālī** on her, named because the seed fell on her hair (*vāla*), and **Sūrya** fathers **Sugrīva**, named from her neck (*grīvā*). Sūrya assigns Hanumān to Sugrīva's service, and Brahmā installs Ṛkṣarajas, now male again, at Kiṣkindhā. The block stands **outside the Critical Edition's constituted text**; it is *prakṣipta* even by the Uttara Kāṇḍa's own standard, a stratum later than the kāṇḍa around it. The constituted text has the simpler picture. CE 7.36.35 names Ṛkṣarajas plainly as "father of Vālī and Sugrīva", and the Araṇya already calls Sugrīva both "son of Ṛkṣarajas" and Sūrya's "own (*aurasa*) son" (CE 3.68.16). The appendix spells out the divine fatherhood rather than inventing it. [interpolation]
- **Why Rāvaṇa took Sītā — appendix material** - In the rest of the same block (37c–f) Agastya passes on a story Nārada had from **Sanatkumāra** [interpolation]:
  - Sanatkumāra tells the young Rāvaṇa that rākṣasas killed by the gods return to rebirth, but those killed by **Viṣṇu** reach his world. He foretells Nārāyaṇa's birth as Rāma and Lakṣmī's birth from the earth as Sītā.
  - Rāvaṇa goes looking for a fight on **Śvetadvīpa**, where the island's women toss him from hand to hand "like an insect".
  - Agastya draws the conclusion: Rāvaṇa **carried Sītā off desiring death at Rāma's hand**, and guarded her "as his own mother".
  - None of this is in the CE. It is a Rāmāyaṇa source, inside the vulgate itself, for the devotional reading of Rāvaṇa as a *virodha-bhakta*, one who reaches God through enmity. Its claim that he guarded Sītā as a mother sits awkwardly with the Sundara Kāṇḍa's threats.

## IV. The reign of Rāma — Rāma-rājya (sargas 37–42)

- **Dispersal of the allies** - Rāma rewards the kings and sends them home, among them Pratardana of Kāśi (CE 7.37.1). Then he sends home Sugrīva, Vibhīṣaṇa (now king of Laṅkā), the vānaras and the ṛkṣas (Vālmīki *Uttara* 38–40).
  - **Hanumān's first vow** - At this leave-taking, Hanumān asks to keep his life for as long as Rāma's story is told on earth. Rāma embraces him and grants it: "as long as the worlds endure, so long will my story endure… and so long will the breath stay in your body". He then hangs his own moon-bright necklace, with a beryl at its centre, on Hanumān's neck (Shastri's vulgate: a string of pearls) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 40; CE 7.39.16–20). This is the vow that Rāma's last command at sarga 108 tells him to keep.
- **Kubera gives the Puṣpaka back** - Rāma had dismissed the Puṣpaka to Kubera after the return to Ayodhyā, as the chariot itself recalls. Kubera will not keep it: the chariot was won by Rāma, he says, and it must carry him. So the Puṣpaka comes back, speaking out of the sky, to serve Rāma. Rāma worships it and dismisses it with a standing summons: *"Go where you will, and come when I call you to mind."* This is the clause that reconciles his later use of the Puṣpaka in this same kāṇḍa with its formal return (Vālmīki *Uttara* 41; CE 7.40.3–11).
- **Rāma-rājya, the ideal kingdom** - The reign becomes the proverb of perfect rule. No widow laments, no beast or disease afflicts, no thief walks the roads, the old never perform rites for the young, the clouds rain in season, and the trees are always in flower and fruit (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 128.98–104). The Uttara has its own, shorter versions. As the Puṣpaka departs, **Bharata** reports that for more than a month no one has fallen ill, even the very old do not die, women bear sons, Indra rains in season, and the winds are kind (Vālmīki *Uttara* 41; CE 7.40.13–17). After Sītā is gone, the text repeats it: rain in season, no untimely death, no disease, no adharma "while Rāma ruled" (Vālmīki *Uttara* 99; CE 7.89.8–10). The private side of the reign opens on Rāma and Sītā's felicity in the Aśoka grove (Vālmīki *Uttara* 42). The tradition reads the reign through the yuga frame of the dharma-bull standing on **three legs** in the Tretā (cf. [yugas](../../00-time-and-cosmology/yugas-and-mahayuga.md)).
- **Sītā's pregnancy-wish** - In the grove Rāma sees that Sītā is carrying a child and asks what she longs for. She says she wants to visit the ascetics' hermitages on the Gaṅgā and spend even one night at the feet of sages who live on roots and fruit. He promises that she will go "tomorrow" (Vālmīki *Uttara* 42; CE 7.41.21–26). This wish becomes the cover for the banishment.

## V. The banishment of Sītā (sargas 43–50)

- **Bhadra's report (loka-apavāda)** - **Bhadra** is one of the companions who amuse the king with stories. Rāma asks him what the people say of him, of Sītā and of his brothers, and presses him for the bad along with the good. Bhadra reports the talk of the squares, markets, streets and groves: the people praise the bridge and the victory, and then ask what pleasure a king can take in a wife whom Rāvaṇa held on his lap and kept in Laṅkā. Now, they say, "we too will have to put up with this from our wives, for as the king does, the people follow". The others present confirm it. In Vālmīki the informant is a named companion and the slander is **general city talk**; no washerman appears anywhere in the sarga (Vālmīki *Uttara* 43; CE 7.42.1–22).
  - *Variant (Padma Purāṇa, Pātāla-khaṇḍa 55–58):* the famous **washerman (rajaka)** comes from here. Rāma's spies overhear him refusing to take back his own wife: he is not Rāma, to take back a woman who lived in a stranger's house. The Purāṇa then gives the slander a past life. As a girl in Janaka's garden, Sītā caught a pair of parrots who were repeating the prophecy of her marriage. The female died in captivity, cursing Sītā to be separated from Rāma while pregnant, and the grieving male drowned himself and was reborn as the washerman **Krodhana**. The washerman is the single scene most often quoted as Vālmīki's, and Vālmīki does not have it; many oral tellings go on to curse or punish him. [Purāṇic / folk]
- **The abandonment** - Rāma calls his brothers together. He tells them that Agni, Vāyu, the Sun and the Moon all declared Sītā sinless in Laṅkā, and that "my inner self knows Sītā is pure". Yet the public scandal cuts him to the quick, and he would give up his own life, or theirs, for fear of disgrace, let alone Sītā. Though she is **pregnant**, he orders **Lakṣmaṇa** to take her by chariot, with Sumantra driving, across the Gaṅgā. He is to leave her near **Vālmīki's hermitage** on the Tamasā, using her own wish to see the hermitages as the pretext. He binds them all by oath not to plead for her (Vālmīki *Uttara* 44–45; CE 7.44.2–21).
- **Sītā's lament; Vālmīki's shelter** - On the far bank Lakṣmaṇa falls weeping to the ground before her and then tells her the truth (CE 7.46.3–18). Sītā says she will not drown herself in the Gaṅgā, because the royal line she carries would be lost. She sends Rāma a message: treat the citizens as you treat your brothers; she grieves not for her own body but for his good name (CE 7.47.8–12). The hermit boys bring **Vālmīki**. He has known her innocence through his tapas-vision, and he entrusts her to the women ascetics of the āśrama, telling them "sinless, she has been abandoned by her husband" (Vālmīki *Uttara* 46–49; CE 7.48.1–20). Lakṣmaṇa tells her that Vālmīki was a close friend of Daśaratha (CE 7.46.16).
  - *Variant (Tamil):* Kamban's *Irāmāvatāram* ends with the coronation and does **not narrate** the banishment. The Tamil Uttara-kāṇḍam that is printed after it is credited to the poet **Oṭṭakkūttar**.
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās,* Rāmcaritmānas*):* the Mānas Uttarakāṇḍ is a different book, made of the Rāma-rājya, Kākabhuśuṇḍi's discourse and teaching. It never tells of the banishment and simply records that "Sītā bore two fine sons, Lava and Kuśa", alongside the two sons each of the other brothers (the chaupāīs beginning *sevahiṃ sānukūla saba bhāī*).
  - *Variant (Adhyātma / Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa & folk):* Sītā is said to receive reassurances before she leaves, or the **shadow-Sītā** narrative softens the act. [late text]
  - *Variant (modern / Malayalam — C. N. Sreekantan Nair, "Kāñcana Sītā", 1961):* a celebrated reinterpretation centring Sītā's perspective, filmed by G. Aravindan (1977). [scholarly / modern]
- **The prophecy that made it fated** - On the way home Lakṣmaṇa asks why the gods allow such grief. **Sumantra** breaks a secret Daśaratha had told him to keep. Durvāsā, at Vasiṣṭha's hermitage, had told Daśaratha that Rāma would one day give up Sītā, and his brothers too. He would reign **eleven thousand years** (*daśa varṣa sahasrāṇi daśa varṣa śatāni ca*), perform horse-sacrifices, found many royal lines and then go to Brahmaloka (Vālmīki *Uttara* 50–51; CE 7.49.10–12, 7.50.2–17).
  - *Vulgate addition (sarga 51, "Viṣṇu is cursed by Bhṛgu"):* in the vulgate, Durvāsā also gives the cause. In a war of gods and asuras, Bhṛgu's wife sheltered the daityas, and Viṣṇu cut off her head with his discus. Bhṛgu cursed him to be born among men and live long separated from his wife, and Viṣṇu accepted the curse "for the good of the worlds". The CE passes straight from Daśaratha's question to the prophecy (CE 7.50.10–11), so this curse-etiology of the banishment is **not in the constituted text**. [interpolation]

## VI. Lava and Kuśa; the Aśvamedha (sargas 66, 71, 91–94)

- **Birth of the twins** - On the very night Śatrughna lodges at the hermitage on his way to fight Lavaṇa, Sītā bears twin sons. At midnight Vālmīki makes a protective rite against evil spirits with a handful of *kuśa* grass and its cut end (*lava*). The elder is to be wiped with the kuśa and named **Kuśa**, the younger with the lava and named **Lava**. Śatrughna hears the news and rejoices (Vālmīki *Uttara* 66; CE 7.58.1–11). Fuller treatment of the twins' cycle: [Uttara-Rāmāyaṇa: Lava and Kuśa](../uttara-ramayana-lava-kusha.md).
- **They are taught the Rāmāyaṇa** - Vālmīki trains the boys to sing his poem to the vīṇā in the proper modes, ignorant that it is their own father's tale (Vālmīki *Uttara* 93; CE 7.84.3–14).
  - *Vulgate addition (sarga 71):* in the twelfth year, on his way back to Ayodhyā, Śatrughna stops at Vālmīki's hermitage and hears the Rāma-story sung to strings. His soldiers ask whether they are dreaming. The CE says only that he made "seven or eight halts" on the way (CE 7.63.3); the scene is not in the constituted text.
- **The horse-sacrifice (Aśvamedha)** - Rāma holds the Aśvamedha in the **Naimiṣa forest on the Gomatī** and invites Sugrīva, Vibhīṣaṇa and the kings. He has taken no other wife, and a **golden image of Sītā** (*kāñcanī patnī*, the "kāñcana-Sītā") serves as the consecrated wife. This poignant motif of his unbroken fidelity is repeated "at every sacrifice" afterwards (Vālmīki *Uttara* 91–92, 99; CE 7.82.14–19, 7.89.4).
- **The twins sing the epic** - At the sacrifice Lava and Kuśa sing the poem from Nārada's visit onward, twenty sargas a day. The audience whispers that but for their matted hair and bark-cloth they would be indistinguishable from Rāma. The boys refuse the eighteen thousand gold pieces Rāma offers: "what would forest-dwellers do with gold?" They tell him that the author is Vālmīki, who has come to the sacrifice, and that the poem runs to **five hundred sargas** "up to the end of your life". Hearing the song over many days, Rāma recognizes them as Sītā's sons (Vālmīki *Uttara* 93–94; CE 7.85.8–86.2).
  - *Recensional difference:* in the vulgate the boys also give the figures **24,000 verses, a hundred episodes, six kāṇḍas "together with the Uttara"**. The CE's verse names only the five hundred sargas (CE 7.85.20).
  - *Variant (Purāṇic, drama & folk / Lava-Kuśa cycle):* the boys **capture the sacrificial horse** and defeat Rāma's champions in battle before they are recognized. Vālmīki has no such episode; the horse-capture is not in his text at all. The motif is developed in the Padma Purāṇa's Pātāla-khaṇḍa and the Jaimini *Aśvamedha*, and in Bhavabhūti's play *Uttararāmacarita* (Act V, Lava against Lakṣmaṇa's son Candraketu), which also ends with Sītā restored to Rāma. Regional tellings and films expand it into battles with Lakṣmaṇa, Bharata, Śatrughna and Hanumān. [Purāṇic / folk]

## VII. Sītā returns to the Earth (sargas 95–98)

- **The summons & the final oath** - Rāma sends word to Vālmīki: if Sītā is pure, let her prove it before the assembly tomorrow, with the sage's consent. Vālmīki agrees, "for a husband is a woman's god" (CE 7.86.3–10). He leads her in, she in ochre robes, and vouches for her. "These twins are your sons," he says. "I am the tenth son of Pracetas and do not remember ever speaking a lie." He stakes his thousands of years of austerity on her innocence. Rāma answers that he already believed her, from her ordeal before the gods. He abandoned her, knowing her sinless, for fear of the people, and he asks the sage's pardon (Vālmīki *Uttara* 95–97; CE 7.87.13–88.4).
- **Bhūmi receives her daughter** - With the gods assembled, Sītā speaks with downcast eyes: "As I have never thought of any man but Rāghava even in my mind, so may the goddess **Mādhavī** (the Earth) open a place for me." A divine throne rises from the ground, borne on the heads of nāgas. The Earth-goddess takes Sītā in her arms, welcomes her and seats her on it, and Sītā **descends into Rasātala** under a rain of flowers. It is her final, unanswerable proof and her refusal to return (Vālmīki *Uttara* 97; CE 7.88.9–14).
  - *Note:* Sītā is the **earth-born** (found in a furrow — see [Bāla/marriage backstory](1-bala-kanda.md)); her return *to* the earth closes that ring. See [Sītā](../characters/sita.md).
  - *Vulgate addition (sarga 98):* Rāma rages at the Earth, his "mother-in-law", to give Sītā back or be destroyed. Brahmā calms him with a reminder that he is Viṣṇu and a promise of reunion in heaven. He also bids him hear the rest of the poem, and the sages of Brahmaloka stay to hear the "Uttara Kāṇḍa". This sarga is **absent from the CE**, which moves straight from Sītā's disappearance to the end of the sacrifice and Rāma's grief (CE 7.89.1).

## VIII. The Śambūka episode (sargas 73–76)

- **The dead brāhmaṇa boy** - An old brahmin of the countryside brings the body of his five-year-old son to the palace gate. A child dying before his father, he says, can only be the king's fault (CE 7.64.2–14). **Nārada** gives the yuga-scheme (CE 7.65.9–23). In the Kṛta age only brahmins did tapas. In the Tretā, kṣatriyas joined them and the others served. In the Dvāpara, vaiśyas join in, and tapas by **śūdras** belongs only to the Kali age. A śūdra is now practising austerities at the edge of Rāma's realm, and that adharma is killing the child. Rāma has the body kept in oil, calls the Puṣpaka to mind, and searches the west, north and east before finding the ascetic in the south. He is hanging head-down by a lake north of the Śaivala mountain (Vālmīki *Uttara* 73–75; CE 7.66.2–13).
- **Rāma slays Śambūka** - Asked his birth and his aim, the ascetic answers truthfully that he is a śūdra named **Śambūka** and seeks to become a god "in this very body" (*saśarīraḥ*). Rāma draws his sword and **beheads him** while he is still speaking, and at that moment the boy revives (Vālmīki *Uttara* 76; CE 7.67.2–5). In the vulgate the gods then rain flowers and say that "by your grace this śūdra will not attain heaven". Rāma uses their boon to ask for the boy's life, though the boy has already revived. In both texts Agastya then greets Rāma, saying the gods report him as the slayer of the śūdra (CE 7.67.10).
  - *Status — [disputed]:* one of the most controversial passages in all of Hindu literature, central to **anti-caste critique**. Reformers have attacked or rewritten it. T. Ramaswamy Chowdary (Tripuraneni) wrote the Telugu play *Śambūka Vadha* (1920), and Kuvempu's Kannada *Śūdra Tapasvi* (1944) lets Śambūka live. **B. R. Ambedkar** cites it in *Annihilation of Caste* (1936) as proof that caste is enforced by force, and E. V. Ramasamy (Periyar) used it against Rāma. Others read it as the strongest evidence of the kāṇḍa's **late, interpolated** character. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (the "salvation" reading):* the defence that Śambūka gains a higher state by Rāma's hand is **not in Vālmīki**, whose vulgate says the opposite. It comes from the classical poets. Kālidāsa writes that "punished by the king himself, the śūdra won the goal of the good, which he could not win by austerity that overstepped his own path" (*Raghuvaṃśa* 15.53). Bhavabhūti's *Uttararāmacarita* (Act II) brings the slain Śambūka back in a divine form to greet Rāma. [kāvya]

## IX. The reign's other episodes (sargas 51–92, 99–108)

- **Story of King Nṛga** - Rāma has neglected the petitioners for four days, and he tells Lakṣmaṇa a warning tale. The pious king **Nṛga** gave away a poor brahmin's stray cow by mistake among thousands of gifts, and two brahmins came to him to settle the dispute. Kept waiting at the palace gate for days, they cursed him to live unseen as a **lizard in a pit**, to be freed only when Viṣṇu is born among the Yadus as Vāsudeva. Nṛga installed his son Vasu and went down into the pit (Vālmīki *Uttara* 53–54). In Vālmīki the fault is the **king who will not give audience**, not the double gift that the Mahābhārata and Bhāgavata tellings stress.
  - *Recensional status:* the whole run from Nṛga to Yayāti (vulgate 53–59) is **absent from the CE's constituted text**. The CE passes from Lakṣmaṇa consoling Rāma (CE 7.51) directly to the ascetics of the Yamunā at the gate (CE 7.52, = vulgate 60). In the vulgate these are main-sequence numbered sargas, not marked appendices, but the CE treats them as later. [interpolation]
- **Nimi, Vasiṣṭha and Urvaśī** - The mutual curse of King **Nimi** and his purohita **Vasiṣṭha** — each cursing the other to bodilessness — and the cursing of the nymph **Urvaśī**; Nimi thereafter dwells in the eyelids of all creatures. Deep treatment: [Nimi & Vasiṣṭha's mutual curse](../../03-lineages-vamsha/nimi-vasishtha-mutual-curse.md) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 55–57; not in the CE).
- **Śukra curses Yayāti** - The lunar king's premature old age and **Pūru**'s exchange of his youth for his father's — the Rāmāyaṇa's own telling of a story the Mahābhārata makes famous (Vālmīki *Uttara* 58–59; not in the CE). See [Yayāti](../yayati.md).
  - *Prakṣipta (vulgate 59b–d):* the vulgate follows with three unnumbered chapters of court-cases. A dog with a gashed skull brings a complaint against the brahmin who struck it. A vulture and an owl dispute a nest, and Rāma judges between them. These "Rāma the judge" tales are absent from the CE. [interpolation]
- **The Śatrughna–Lavaṇa–Mathurā arc** - The ascetics of the Yamunā, led by **Cyavana**, come to Rāma for relief from **Lavaṇa**, son of Madhu. Lavaṇa holds the Madhu-vana with the trident **Rudra** gave his father, which turns any challenger to ash, and he eats men (CE 7.53.3–21). **Śatrughna** asks for the war and is consecrated king of the country to be won. He travels out by way of Vālmīki's āśrama, arriving, as it happens, on the night **Kuśa and Lava are born**. He catches Lavaṇa away from the trident and kills him. He founds **Madhupura (Mathurā)** on the site and rules there **twelve years** before visiting Ayodhyā (Vālmīki *Uttara* 60–72; CE 7.63.1–7). It is Śatrughna's only independent arc, and the corpus's forward link to the Mathurā of the Kṛṣṇa cycle. Full treatment, with the Mālyavān → Analā → Kumbhīnasī → Lavaṇa kinship chain: [Madhu, Lavaṇa and the founding of Mathurā](../madhu-lavana-and-mathura.md).
  - **Māndhātṛ's end** - Embedded in the arc is the story **Cyavana** tells Śatrughna at the Yamunā hermitage on the night before the fight. The great Ikṣvāku emperor **Māndhātṛ** wanted Indra's throne, and Indra taunted him that Lavaṇa did not yet obey him. Māndhātṛ marched on Lavaṇa's forest and was burnt to ash, with his army, by the same trident (Vālmīki *Uttara* 67; CE 7.59.1–23).
  - **Saudāsa (Kalmāṣapāda)** - Also embedded is Vālmīki's own explanation of the ruined sacrificial ground beside his hermitage. Young **Mitrasaha Saudāsa** killed one of two tiger-shaped rākṣasas while hunting, and the survivor took revenge. Disguised first as Vasiṣṭha and then as the cook, he had human flesh served to the real Vasiṣṭha. Vasiṣṭha cursed the king to live on such food, then limited the curse to **twelve years** when he learned of the trick (Vālmīki *Uttara* 65; CE 7.57.9–34).
- **Śveta, the corpse-eating king** - After Śambūka, Rāma visits **Agastya**, who gives him a divine ornament made by Viśvakarmā and then tells where it came from. Long ago Agastya himself came to a lake in an empty forest. There he saw a shining being step down from a celestial car and eat a well-fed corpse. The being was King **Śveta** of Vidarbha, son of Sudeva. Śveta had reached Brahmaloka by austerity, but because he had **given nothing away**, hunger followed him there. Brahmā told him to eat his own preserved body until Agastya came to that forest. Śveta begged Agastya to accept the ornament, and the moment Agastya took it, the corpse vanished and Śveta went to heaven satisfied. It is **Agastya's acceptance of the gift**, not Rāma's touch, that frees him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 76–78; CE 7.67.11–69.27). Deep treatment: [Śveta, the corpse-king, and Agastya](../shveta-corpse-king-and-agastya.md).
- **Daṇḍa, Arajā and the making of Daṇḍakāraṇya** - **Daṇḍa**, youngest of Ikṣvāku's hundred sons, is given the country between the Vindhya and the Śaivala mountains, with **Madhumanta** as his city and **Uśanas** (Śukra) as his purohita. He rapes **Arajā**, his preceptor's eldest daughter. Uśanas curses the kingdom: within seven nights Indra will bury it under a rain of dust for a hundred yojanas around. Only Arajā's lake and the creatures who shelter by her that night are spared. The blasted land is **Daṇḍakāraṇya**, and the ascetics who later settle there make it **Janasthāna**; the forest of the exile is demon-country by curse rather than by nature (Vālmīki *Uttara* 79–81; CE 7.70.14–72.18). Full treatment: [Daṇḍa, Arajā and the making of Daṇḍakāraṇya](../danda-araja-and-dandakaranya.md).
- **Bharata argues down the Rājasūya** - Rāma proposes the imperial **Rājasūya**; **Bharata** dissuades him — the rite destroys royal houses, and a king who is already lord of the world need not ruin the world to prove it. The **Aśvamedha** is chosen instead, and **Lakṣmaṇa** makes the case from the story of **Vṛtra**: Indra, stained with brahmin-murder for killing him, was cleansed by a horse-sacrifice (Vālmīki *Uttara* 83–86).
- **Ila / Ilā** - Rāma tells the story of **Ila**, son of Prajāpati **Kardama** and lord of **Bāhli**. Hunting, Ila strays into the grove where Śiva has made every male a female to please Pārvatī. Śiva will grant anything but manhood. Pārvatī grants half, so that Ila is a man one month and the woman **Ilā** the next, remembering neither state in the other (CE 7.78.3–29). As Ilā she bears **Purūravas** to **Budha**, the head of the lunar line, and her women become the kiṃpuruṣīs (CE 7.79.23–80.23). An **Aśvamedha** restores Ila's manhood for good. He leaves Bāhli, where Śaśabindu then reigns, and founds **Pratiṣṭhāna** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 87–90; CE 7.81.17–24). That is the point of the tale: it is told to show what a horse-sacrifice can do. Vālmīki's Ila is Kardama's son, not Manu's child as the Purāṇas have him, so the two genealogies disagree at the root. Cross-link: [Ila / Sudyumna](../../03-lineages-vamsha/ila-sudyumna.md), [Chandravaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **Death of the queen-mothers** - After Sītā is gone, Rāma performs horse-sacrifices for ten thousand years (CE 7.89.5). Then **Kausalyā, Sumitrā and Kaikeyī** die and rejoin Daśaratha in heaven, and Rāma gives great gifts in their name. The reign runs on without the generation that made it (Vālmīki *Uttara* 99; CE 7.89.11–15).
- **Reign-settlements** - The settlements of the princes are as follows:
  - **Bharata:** at the request of Kaikeyī's brother **Yudhājit** of Kekaya, Rāma sends Bharata against the gandharvas of the Sindhu country. Bharata conquers the land, which is **Gāndhāra**, and installs **his own sons**, **Takṣa at Takṣaśilā** and **Puṣkala at Puṣkalāvatī** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 100–101; CE 7.90.16, 7.91.9–10). See [Bharata's gandharva conquest](../bharata-gandharva-conquest-takshashila.md).
  - **Lakṣmaṇa:** his sons **Aṅgada** and **Candraketu** are given **Aṅgadīyā** in Kārupatha (CE *Kārāpatha*, to the west) and **Candrakāntā** in the Malla country (to the north) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 102; CE 7.92.2–13).
  - **Śatrughna:** his sons **Subāhu** and **Śatrughātin** are installed at **Mathurā** and **Vidiśā** only at the very end, when Śatrughna is summoned to follow Rāma (Vālmīki *Uttara* 108; CE 7.98.9).
  - **Kuśa and Lava** are settled last (see §X).
- **The chronology seam** - The kāṇḍa's own intervals do not close. The twins are born at sarga 66. Śatrughna rules Madhupura twelve years and returns, and the boys are still boys at the Aśvamedha. Yet the text then lets **ten thousand years** of horse-sacrifices pass before the queens die (CE 7.89.5). The span of the whole reign is given twice in the Uttara itself, as **eleven thousand years**, *daśa varṣa sahasrāṇi daśa varṣa śatāni ca*, "ten thousand years and ten hundred". It is first given in Durvāsā's prophecy (CE 7.50.13) and again in Kāla's message (CE 7.94.12), and Nārada's summary in the Bāla gives the same formula (CE 1.1.76). The vulgate [Yuddha](6-yuddha-kanda.md) has "ten thousand years" (128.96). So the human arc of the twins' childhood and the Tretā-scale arc are two clocks the text never reconciles. [scholarly]

## X. The departure of Rāma — Mahāprasthāna (sargas 103–111)

- **Kāla comes as a messenger** - **Time (Kāla)** comes to the palace gate disguised as an ascetic. He asks for a private audience on the condition that anyone who sees or hears them must be killed, and Rāma posts **Lakṣmaṇa** at the door (CE 7.93.1–16). Kāla says that in a former existence he was Rāma's own son, "born of māyā" (CE 7.94.2, *tavāhaṃ pūrvake bhāve putraḥ*; Shastri's vulgate translation reads "his son", Brahmā's). He brings Brahmā's message: Rāma once fixed his own stay among men at eleven thousand years; that term is full, and he may stay longer if he wishes, or return to the gods. Rāma, smiling, chooses to go back "whence I came". The message names the world of the gods (*suraloka*), not Vaikuṇṭha (Vālmīki *Uttara* 103–104; CE 7.94.1–19).
- **Durvāsā and Lakṣmaṇa's doom** - The irascible sage **Durvāsā** arrives demanding to see Rāma at once. If he is kept waiting, he says, he will curse the land, the city, Rāma, Bharata and all their offspring. He has just finished a fast of a thousand years and wants food. Lakṣmaṇa reasons that one death is better than the ruin of all, and he interrupts the sealed meeting. Rāma feeds the sage. Afterwards Lakṣmaṇa himself urges Rāma to keep his vow and kill him, and **Vasiṣṭha** counsels abandonment. Rāma pronounces "I let you go; for the good, banishment and death are the same" (CE 7.96.13). Lakṣmaṇa goes to the Sarayū and stops his breath. **Indra bears him bodily to heaven**, and the gods worship "the fourth part of Viṣṇu" returned — the text's own ending for him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 105–106; CE 7.95.1–96.18).
  - *Variant (Vaiṣṇava devotional):* he enters the water and is taken up as **Śeṣa / Ananta**, resuming the serpent-form that is his true nature. This is the gloss the tradition remembers, and it differs from the sarga, which makes him a quarter of Viṣṇu and has Indra carry him off. See [Lakṣmaṇa](../characters/lakshmana.md).
- **Succession** - Rāma first means to crown **Bharata**, but Bharata refuses any kingdom without Rāma and asks that the twins be crowned instead (CE 7.97.2–7). Rāma divides Kosala between them. **Kuśa** rules southern Kosala from **Kuśāvatī** on the slope of the Vindhya, and **Lava** rules northern Kosala from **Śrāvastī**. He consecrates them both before he leaves (Vālmīki *Uttara* 107; CE 7.97.17, 7.98.4–5).
- **Rāma's last commands** - Before he goes, Rāma disposes of his allies by name:
  - **Vibhīṣaṇa** is to stay in Laṅkā "as long as creatures endure" and rule by dharma (CE 7.98.22–23). The vulgate adds that his empire will last as long as sun and moon shine and men speak of Rāma, and charges him to worship the Ikṣvāku family deity, Jagannātha.
  - **Hanumān** is told to keep his own vow: *"as long as my stories circulate in the world, O lord of monkeys, keep your life and hold to your promise"* (CE 7.98.24–25). This **confirms the chiranjīvi grant** Rāma made at the leave-taking of sarga 40 (CE 7.39.16–19); the grant is made twice in the Uttara, and both times by Rāma himself.
  - **The bears and monkeys** are invited to come with him (CE 7.98.26).
  - *Vulgate additions:* **Jāmbavān** receives his own command, *"till the Kali Yuga begins, continue to dwell on earth"*, and **Sugrīva**, refusing to be left behind, reports that he has already installed **Aṅgada** on the Kiṣkindhā throne. Neither is in the CE (Vālmīki *Uttara* 108).
  - Cross-link: [The Chiranjīvis](../the-chiranjivis.md), [Jāmbavān](../characters/jambavan.md).
- **The Great Departure into the Sarayū** - At dawn Vasiṣṭha performs the rites of the great departure. Rāma walks out of Ayodhyā in silk, holding kuśa-grass and reciting the Veda, silent (and, in the vulgate, barefoot), toward the **Sarayū**. The retinue Vālmīki personifies outright follows him:
  - **Śrī** with her lotus, and the goddess **Vyavasāya** (Resolve). The vulgate puts Śrī on his right and Vyavasāya on his left. The CE puts Śrī on his left, **Hrī** (Modesty) on his right and Vyavasāya in front (CE 7.99.6).
  - His arrows and bow, walking in human form.
  - The **Vedas in the persons of brahmins**, the **Sāvitrī** (the vulgate's Gāyatrī), the syllable **Oṃ** and the invocation **Vaṣaṭ**.
  - Behind them come the women of the palace, Bharata and Śatrughna, the brahmins with their sacred fires, the ministers, the citizens, the vānaras and ṛkṣas, and the country people. No one is downcast (CE 7.99.4–18).
  - A yojana and a half on, at the river, Brahmā and the gods meet him. Brahmā hails him, "Come, Viṣṇu", and bids him enter whatever form he wishes. Rāma, **with his body and with his brothers, enters the Vaiṣṇava radiance** (the *jala-samādhi* / Mahāprasthāna) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 109–110; CE 7.100.1–10).
- **Where the followers go** - At Viṣṇu's request Brahmā gives those who followed him the **Sāntānika worlds**, "next to Brahmaloka". Even animals that die thinking of Rāma will dwell there. The vānaras and ṛkṣas return to the gods whose portions they were (in the vulgate **Sugrīva** enters the orb of the **sun**, his father). At the **Gopratāra** ford everyone who entered the water dropped the human body and boarded a celestial car (Vālmīki *Uttara* 110; CE 7.100.14–24).
- **The ending, and the phala-śruti** - The CE ends at 7.100.26 with a single colophon verse: "this much is the tale, with its Uttara, honoured by Brahmā, called the Rāmāyaṇa, made by Vālmīki". The vulgate adds a whole closing sarga of merits (offspring, long life, the removal of sin, Viṣṇu's world) won by hearing the Rāmāyaṇa (Vālmīki *Uttara* 111). This is a hallmark of an integral closing frame, but the constituted text does not have it.
