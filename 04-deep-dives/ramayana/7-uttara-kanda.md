# Uttara Kāṇḍa — The Final Book

> Cosmic address: [Vaivasvata Manvantara](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md) → 28th Mahāyuga → **[Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** → the Rāma-avatāra cycle (its closing chapter and aftermath).
> Parent: **[Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive](README.md)** · Previous: [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](6-yuddha-kanda.md) · (last book)
> See also: [Daśāvatāra (Rāma)](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) · [Sūryavaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) · [Itihāsa-Epics corpus](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)

The seventh and last book of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa, **111 sargas / ~2,690 ślokas** in the vulgate (Southern-recension) numbering — the convention every citation in this file follows. The Baroda **Critical Edition constitutes 100 sargas**; the vulgate additionally prints *prakṣipta* (interpolated) appendix chapters — 23b–f, 37b–f, 59b–d — which stand outside the constituted text and are flagged as such where they appear below. Named *Uttara* ("Latter / Final / Beyond"), the book is a double appendix: a long **flashback** filling in the deep prehistory of the rākṣasas, Rāvaṇa, and the vānaras (sargas 1–36), and the **aftermath of the war** — Rāma's reign (Rāma-rājya), the banishment of Sītā, the twins Lava and Kuśa, Sītā's return to the earth, and Rāma's departure to heaven (sargas 37–111). It also recasts the entire epic as a story sung *back to its own hero*.

> **Status — [disputed] / [scholarly]:** The Uttara Kāṇḍa (with the Bāla Kāṇḍa) is widely held by modern philology to be a **later addition** to a once five- or six-book core. It is **absent from the oldest manuscript strata**, its frame ("the sages ask Rāma about Rāvaṇa") is appendix-like, and episodes such as the killing of Śambūka and the banishment of Sītā are felt by many to **contradict the dharma and characterization** of Rāma in the central books. *Counter-view:* the makers of the **Baroda Critical Edition did not excise it**, traditional commentators (and devotional Hinduism) treat it as integral and canonical, and astronomical-dating advocates argue for its authenticity. This file records the contents as transmitted, flagging the controversy rather than resolving it.

## I. Frame: the sages question Rāma (sargas 1–3)

- **The ṛṣis pay homage** - After the coronation (the end of *Yuddha Kāṇḍa*), Agastya and the assembled sages come to congratulate Rāma; he asks them to explain how Rāvaṇa, Kumbhakarṇa and Indrajit grew so mighty. Agastya becomes the narrator of the rākṣasa prehistory (Vālmīki *Uttara* 1–2).
- **Frame function** - This Q-and-A device lets the book retro-fit a full genealogy and backstory onto enemies already slain in Book 6 — a structural marker many [scholarly] readers cite for its appendix status.

## II. The rākṣasa prehistory — Rāvaṇa's ancestry & rise (sargas 2–34)

- **Pulastya → Viśravas** - The rākṣasa line descends from the Prajāpati / Saptarṣi **Pulastya**, a mind-born son of Brahmā. Practicing tapas at the hermitage of Tṛṇabindu, he is disturbed by the women there; his curse / circumstance with Tṛṇabindu's daughter produces the sage **Viśravas** ("he who hears all") (Vālmīki *Uttara* 2–3).
- **The two wives of Viśravas** - Viśravas marries **Ilavilā** (Bharadvāja's daughter), begetting **Kubera (Vaiśravaṇa)**; later the rākṣasī **Kaikasī** (daughter of Sumālī) approaches him at an inauspicious twilight hour, begetting the rākṣasa brood (Vālmīki *Uttara* 4–9).
- **Kubera, lord of the north** - Kubera does austerity for thousands of years; Brahmā makes him a **Loka-pāla** (world-guardian of the north) alongside Indra, Varuṇa and Yama, gives him the lordship of wealth and the flying chariot **Puṣpaka** — and nothing else. Laṅkā is not Brahmā's gift: asked by his son where he should dwell, **Viśravas** names the golden city on **Mount Trikūṭa**, built by Viśvakarmā and standing empty and unguarded since Viṣṇu drove the rākṣasas down to Rasātala (Vālmīki *Uttara* 3).
- **Birth of the brood** - From Kaikasī are born **Rāvaṇa** (the ten-necked), **Kumbhakarṇa** (the gigantic glutton), the gentle **Vibhīṣaṇa**, and the sister **Śūrpaṇakhā** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 9–10).
- **The great tapas & Brahmā's boon** - Spurred by Kaikasī's envy of Kubera's splendour, the three brothers do extreme penance at Gokarṇa. Rāvaṇa offers his ten heads one by one into the fire over ten thousand years; Brahmā grants that he shall not be slain by **suparṇas, nāgas, yakṣas, daityas, dānavas, rākṣasas or devas** — the list he himself dictates, and one that contains no gandharvas. He disdains to name **men and monkeys**, the loophole that dooms him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 10).
  - *Variant / cross-link:* this omission is the doctrinal reason Viṣṇu incarnates specifically as a **man** ([Rāma](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md)) aided by **vānaras** — see the deva-petition frame in [Bāla Kāṇḍa](1-bala-kanda.md).
- **Kumbhakarṇa's boon of sleep** - Dreading what the glutton would ask for, the devas petition **Sarasvatī**, who **enters his mouth**; when Brahmā asks his desire he says only "to sleep for innumerable years," and Brahmā answers "so be it." Vālmīki attributes no contrary intention to him here and attaches no waking-clause — the goddess on the tongue is the entire mechanic, and this is the older of the epic's two sleep-etiologies (Vālmīki *Uttara* 10).
  - *Later layer (Vālmīki* Yuddha *61):* on the battlefield the sleep is re-explained as a **pun** — Kumbhakarṇa meant to ask for *nirdevatvam* (godlessness, the annihilation of the gods) and Sarasvatī twisted it to *nidrātvam* (sleep) — and Rāvaṇa is said to have begged the term commuted to **six months asleep, one day awake**. Both the pun and the commutation belong to that later sarga, not to the boon-scene; see [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](6-yuddha-kanda.md) and [Kumbhakarṇa](../characters/kumbhakarna.md).
- **Vibhīṣaṇa's boon** - The righteous brother asks only to remain ever on the path of dharma even in calamity; pleased, Brahmā adds **immortality unasked** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 10).
- **The cession of Laṅkā** - On grandfather Sumālī's urging, Rāvaṇa sends the minister **Prahasta** to demand Laṅkā from Kubera. Rather than see brothers fight, **Viśravas counsels Kubera to yield**; Kubera withdraws to **Mount Kailāsa** and Laṅkā passes to Rāvaṇa without a blow struck (Vālmīki *Uttara* 11).
- **Maya's daughter and the śakti dowry** - Crossing the Vindhyas, Rāvaṇa meets the dānava architect **Maya** and marries his daughter **Mandodarī**, receiving with her the **infallible śakti** (javelin) that will one day fell Lakṣmaṇa; Kumbhakarṇa is married to **Vajrajvālā**, daughter of Virocana, and Vibhīṣaṇa to **Saramā**, daughter of the gandharva king Śailūṣa. **Śūrpaṇakhā** is given to the dānava **Vidyujjihva** of the Kālakeyas in the same sarga (Vālmīki *Uttara* 12).
- **The murdered envoy and the war on the yakṣas** - Kubera, hearing of his brother's crimes, sends an envoy urging him back to dharma; Rāvaṇa kills the messenger and has him eaten, then marches on Kailāsa. He breaks the yakṣa army, strikes Kubera down with a mace, and carries off the **Puṣpaka** as spoil — the war the peaceful cession did *not* involve (Vālmīki *Uttara* 13–15).
- **Origin of the name "Rāvaṇa"** - Lifting Mount Kailāsa to shake off Śiva (in pride after defeating Kubera), Rāvaṇa is pinned under it by Śiva's toe; he howls (*rava*) for a thousand years until Śiva, pleased, releases him and **names him Rāvaṇa** ("he who makes the worlds scream") and gives him the sword Candrahāsa (Vālmīki *Uttara* 16).
  - **Nandī's curse** - Mocked as a monkey-faced creature when he barred the mountain path, Śiva's attendant **Nandī** curses Rāvaṇa that **monkeys** of his own shape will destroy his race and his city — the second, and more specific, naming of the doom already left open in Brahmā's boon (Vālmīki *Uttara* 16).
- **Rāvaṇa's world-conquest (digvijaya)** - He overruns the three worlds, defeating kings, yakṣas and nāgas and forcing captive women into the Puṣpaka (Vālmīki *Uttara* 18–23).
  - **Marutta's sacrifice** - At the Maheśvara sacrifice of King **Marutta**, the attending gods flee Rāvaṇa by taking the forms of animals — Indra a peacock, Yama a crow, Kubera a chameleon, Varuṇa a swan — and each afterwards blesses the creature whose shape saved him: the peacock's thousand-eyed tail and safety from serpents, the crow's freedom from disease, the swan's moon-white form, the chameleon's head of gold (Vālmīki *Uttara* 18).
  - **The march on Yama** - Rāvaṇa invades Yamaloka, sees the tortures of the naraka regions, and shatters Yama's host; when Yama raises the **kāla-daṇḍa**, Brahmā intervenes — the rod cannot fall without making his own boon a lie — and Yama withdraws rather than be falsified (Vālmīki *Uttara* 21–22).
  - **The Nivātakavacas** - A year-long stalemate in Rasātala against the **Nivātakavacas** ends in a Brahmā-brokered pact of friendship, from whom Rāvaṇa learns a **hundred māyās** (illusion-arts) — the arsenal his son will later inherit (Vālmīki *Uttara* 23).
  - **The Kālakeyas and the widowing of Śūrpaṇakhā** - Rāvaṇa slaughters the **Kālakeya** dānavas, killing among them **Vidyujjihva**, the husband his own sister **Śūrpaṇakhā** had married; he consoles the widow with gifts and posts her in **Daṇḍaka** under the protection of their cousin **Khara**, whom he sets over the forest with fourteen thousand rākṣasas (Vālmīki *Uttara* 23–24).
  - **The curse of the abducted women** - The chaste captives heaped into the Puṣpaka curse him with one voice: **a woman shall be the cause of his destruction** — a collective śāpa that outranks any single grievance in the doom-ledger (Vālmīki *Uttara* 24).
- **Vedavatī's curse** - Rāvaṇa molests the chaste ascetic **Vedavatī** (intended bride of Viṣṇu); she immolates herself, vowing to be **reborn as the cause of his death** — i.e. as **Sītā** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 17). Agastya explicitly identifies Sītā as the reborn Vedavatī.
  - *Variant ("Māyā-Sītā / Chāyā-Sītā"):* in several Purāṇas (Kūrma, Brahmavaivarta, Devī-Bhāgavata) and [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](README.md), the **real Sītā is hidden in fire** and a shadow-Sītā (sometimes equated with Vedavatī) is abducted — harmonizing this backstory with Sītā's purity. [late text]
- **The Anaraṇya curse** - The Ikṣvāku king **Anaraṇya**, defeated and dying, curses Rāvaṇa that a future descendant of his line (Rāma) will kill him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 19).
- **Nalakūbara's curse** - For violating the apsarā **Rambhā** — who was on her way to Nalakūbara and had named herself his wife, making her Rāvaṇa's own nephew's consort — Kubera's son **Nalakūbara** curses him that if he ever again touches a woman who does not desire him, his head will **split into seven pieces** (*saptadhā*) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 26).
- **Brahmā's curse over Puñjikasthalā** - A second and separate curse to the same effect, and the one Rāvaṇa himself cites in the war: confessing to his council why Sītā cannot be forced, he recalls violating the apsarā **Puñjikasthalā** as she went to Brahmā's court, and Brahmā's sentence that should he force another unwilling woman his head would **burst into a hundred pieces** (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 13). The two curses are distinct in text, giver, sarga and number — see [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](6-yuddha-kanda.md) and [Tapas, boons & curses](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/tapas-boons-and-curses.md).
- **The death of Sumālī** - In the war with the devas, Rāvaṇa's grandfather **Sumālī** is beaten to dust by **Savitra**, eighth of the Vasus, and the rākṣasa host breaks and flees — the rout that leaves the field to Rāvaṇa's son (Vālmīki *Uttara* 27).
- **Indrajit conquers Indra** - Rāvaṇa's son **Meghanāda** (named for a birth-cry like thunder) defeats and binds **Indra** himself and carries him to Laṅkā; Brahmā ransoms Indra and renames the son **Indrajit** ("conqueror of Indra"), granting him the fire-rite at **Nikumbhilā** that makes him unassailable — with the clause the war-book turns on: if he fights *before* completing the oblations, he perishes (Vālmīki *Uttara* 29–30).
- **Why Indra could be beaten** - Brahmā explains the defeat as the ripening of an old debt: it is **Gautama's curse for the seduction of Ahalyā** that stripped the king of the gods of his invulnerability, tying the Laṅkā war back to the Bāla Kāṇḍa's [Ahalyā episode](1-bala-kanda.md) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 30).
- **Defeat by Kārtavīrya Arjuna** - The thousand-armed Haihaya emperor **Kārtavīrya Arjuna** captures and imprisons Rāvaṇa, releasing him only at Pulastya's request (Vālmīki *Uttara* 31–33).
- **Defeat by Vālī** - The vānara king **Vālī** tucks Rāvaṇa under his arm and circles the four oceans during his prayers; humbled, Rāvaṇa makes a fire-pact of friendship with him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 34) — back-explaining Vālī's might shown in [Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa](4-kishkindha-kanda.md).

## III. The vānara & Hanumān backstory (sargas 35–36, with an appendix at 37)

- **Rāma's question** - Having watched Hanumān carry a mountain and burn a city, Rāma asks Agastya why such a being ever needed rousing — why he did not simply take Laṅkā alone. The whole birth-account is Agastya's answer, and the question is what makes the curse of forgetting the point of the telling (Vālmīki *Uttara* 35).
- **Birth on Sumeru** - Hanumān is born to **Kesarī**, chief of the vānaras on **Sumeru**, and his wife **Añjanā**, an apsarā fallen to vānara form; **Vāyu** is his begetter, and the child is a wind-god's son in a monkey house (Vālmīki *Uttara* 35).
- **The leap at the sun** - Hungry, the infant springs three thousand yojanas at the rising sun, taking it for fruit. It is an **eclipse day**, and **Rāhu**, arriving for his own portion, is charged by the child and flees to **Indra**. Indra rides out on **Airāvata**; Hanumān lunges at the elephant instead, and **Indra's vajra** breaks his left jaw (*hanu*) and drops him on a mountain. **Sūrya**, seeing an infant, withholds his heat, and **Vāyu** cools him as he falls (Vālmīki *Uttara* 35).
- **Vāyu's strike** - In grief the Wind gathers his son into a cave and **withdraws from the worlds**; breath stops in every body and the creation begins to suffocate. Brahmā revives the child, and the gods come one by one to buy the Wind back (Vālmīki *Uttara* 35).
- **The boons, itemized** - **Brahmā**: no weapon of his shall harm him. **Indra**: immunity to the vajra, and the name **Hanumān** from the broken jaw. **Sūrya (Mārtāṇḍa)**: a hundredth part of his own splendour, and eloquence and mastery of the śāstras when the boy comes to him to learn. **Varuṇa**: no death by noose or water. **Yama**: no sickness, and no death in battle. **Kubera**: his mace shall never wound him. **Śiva**: his weapons shall be restrained from him. **Viśvakarmā**: proof against all celestial weapons, and long life (Vālmīki *Uttara* 36).
- **The sages' curse of forgetfulness** - Grown unbearable in the hermitages, the boy is cursed by the ṛṣis descended from **Bhṛgu and Aṅgiras** to **become unaware of his own strength** — with the release-clause written into the curse itself: *when it is recalled to you, you shall wield it again*. This is core text, not devotional accretion, and it is what makes Jāmbavān's reminding at [Kiṣkindhā 65–66](4-kishkindha-kanda.md) work at all — see [Sundara Kāṇḍa](5-sundara-kanda.md) and [Hanumān](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/hanuman.md) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 36).
- **The birth of Vālī and Sugrīva — appendix material** - Some vulgate printings carry, as an **appendix to sarga 37** (Shastri's 37b, first of the unnumbered 37b–f block), Agastya's account of **Ṛkṣarajas**, a vānara born of Brahmā's tears, who sees his reflection in an enchanted lake on Meru, plunges in, and surfaces as a woman; **Indra** fathers **Vālī** on her and **Sūrya** fathers **Sugrīva**, after which Ṛkṣarajas resumes his own shape and rears them both. The block stands **outside the Critical Edition's constituted text** — *prakṣipta* even by the Uttara Kāṇḍa's own standard, a stratum later than the kāṇḍa around it. The oldest layer contradicts it flatly: **Kiṣkindhā 57.5** and **Sundara 63** both make Ṛkṣarajas simply their father in the ordinary way. [interpolation]

## IV. The reign of Rāma — Rāma-rājya (sargas 37–42)

- **Dispersal of the allies** - Rāma rewards and sends home Sugrīva, Vibhīṣaṇa (now king of Laṅkā), the vānaras and ṛkṣas; the Puṣpaka chariot is returned to its owner **Kubera** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 39–40).
- **Kubera gives the Puṣpaka back** - Kubera will not keep it: he sends the chariot — which speaks for itself out of the sky — back to Rāma to serve him and carry him about the world. Rāma dismisses it with a standing summons: *"Go where you will, but come again whenever I call you to mind."* This is the clause that reconciles his later use of the Puṣpaka in this same kāṇḍa with its formal return (Vālmīki *Uttara* 41).
- **Rāma-rājya, the ideal kingdom** - The reign becomes the proverb of perfect rule: no widow laments, no beast or disease afflicts, no thief walks the roads, the clouds rain in season and the trees stand always in flower and fruit. The set-piece itself belongs to the coronation sarga of the previous book, not to the Uttara — the Uttara opens the reign instead on Rāma and Sītā's private felicity in the Aśoka grove (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 128.99–106; *Uttara* 42). The dharma-bull standing (in Tretā) on **three legs** is the yuga-frame the tradition reads it through (cf. [yugas](../../00-time-and-cosmology/yugas-and-mahayuga.md)).

## V. The banishment of Sītā (sargas 43–50)

- **Bhadra's report (loka-apavāda)** - Rāma asks his friend and courtier **Bhadra** what is said of him in the highways, markets, streets and parks. Bhadra answers that the city blames the king for taking back a wife who had dwelt in Rāvaṇa's house, and mutters that they must now tolerate the same in their own wives. In Vālmīki the informant is a named courtier and the slander is **general city talk** — no washerman appears anywhere in the sarga (Vālmīki *Uttara* 43–44).
  - *Variant (Padma Purāṇa & folk / regional retellings):* the famous **washerman (rajaka)**, throwing out his own wife and declaring "I am not Rāma, to take back a woman who lived in a stranger's home," is the tradition's later dramatization of Bhadra's report — the single scene most often quoted as Vālmīki's and the one Vālmīki does not have. Many oral tellings go on to curse or punish the washerman, reading his slander as a past-life grudge. [folk]
- **The abandonment** - Though Sītā is **pregnant**, Rāma — to protect the unimpeachable honour of the throne — orders **Lakṣmaṇa** to convey her to the forest under pretext of a pilgrimage and leave her near **Vālmīki's hermitage** on the Gaṅgā (Vālmīki *Uttara* 45–47).
- **Sītā's lament; Vālmīki's shelter** - Abandoned and weeping, Sītā is taken in and protected by the sage **Vālmīki** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 48–49).
  - *Variant (Tamil / Kamban's* Irāmāvatāram *omits the whole Uttara material):* Kamban ends with the coronation; the banishment is **not narrated**.
  - *Variant (Adhyātma / Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa & folk):* before leaving, Sītā receives reassurances or it is the **shadow-Sītā** narrative that softens the act. [late text]
  - *Variant (modern / Malayalam — C. N. Sreekantan Nair, "Kāñcana Sītā"):* a celebrated reinterpretation centring Sītā's perspective. [scholarly / modern]

## VI. Lava and Kuśa; the Aśvamedha (sargas 66, 71, 91–94)

- **Birth of the twins** - In the hermitage Sītā bears twin sons; Vālmīki names them **Kuśa** and **Lava**, performing their birth-rites (Vālmīki *Uttara* 66).
- **They are taught the Rāmāyaṇa** - Vālmīki composes the **24,000-verse Rāmāyaṇa** and trains the boys to chant it perfectly to musical measure, ignorant that it is their own father's tale (Vālmīki *Uttara* 71, 93).
- **The horse-sacrifice (Aśvamedha)** - Childless of an acknowledged consort at court, Rāma performs a great Aśvamedha using a **golden image of Sītā** (*hema-Sītā / kāñcana-Sītā*) as the ritual wife — a poignant motif of his unbroken fidelity (Vālmīki *Uttara* 91).
- **The twins sing the epic** - At the sacrifice Lava and Kuśa recite the Rāmāyaṇa before Rāma's assembly; their resemblance and the story reveal them as his sons (Vālmīki *Uttara* 93–94).
  - *Variant (folk / Lava-Kuśa cycle):* a vast popular tradition (and films) elaborates the **boys capturing the sacrificial horse** and **defeating Lakṣmaṇa, Bharata, Śatrughna and Hanumān** in battle before recognition — only lightly present in Vālmīki. [folk tradition]

## VII. Sītā returns to the Earth (sargas 95–98)

- **The summons & the final oath** - Rāma sends for Sītā; Vālmīki leads her into the assembly and vouches for her, and Rāma asks her to once more **publicly swear her purity** to satisfy the people (Vālmīki *Uttara* 95–96).
- **Bhūmi receives her daughter** - Sītā, declaring "as I have never thought of any but Rāma, may **Mādhavī (Bhūmi-Devī), my mother, receive me**," calls on the earth; a golden throne rises, the goddess of Earth embraces her, and Sītā **descends into the ground forever** — her final, unanswerable proof and her refusal to return (Vālmīki *Uttara* 97).
  - *Note:* Sītā is the **earth-born** (found in a furrow — see [Bāla/marriage backstory](1-bala-kanda.md)); her return *to* the earth closes that ring. Rāma's grief and threat to destroy the world are calmed by Brahmā, who promises reunion in heaven.

## VIII. The Śambūka episode (sargas 73–76)

- **The dead brāhmaṇa boy** - A brāhmaṇa's son dies prematurely; the father blames an *adharma* in the realm. Nārada explains that in Tretā a **śūdra performing tapas** (forbidden to his varṇa in that age, per the text's logic) is the cosmic cause (Vālmīki *Uttara* 73–74).
- **Rāma slays Śambūka** - Rāma finds the ascetic **Śambūka** doing penance, learns he is a śūdra seeking heaven in the body, and **beheads him**; the brāhmaṇa boy is instantly revived; the gods rain flowers (Vālmīki *Uttara* 76).
  - *Status — [disputed]:* one of the most controversial passages in all of Hindu literature, central to **anti-caste critique** (and rewritten sympathetically by reformers, e.g. **Tripuraneni** and **B. R. Ambedkar's** critiques). Defenders read it allegorically (Śambūka attains *mokṣa* by Rāma's hand) or as the strongest evidence of the kāṇḍa's **late, interpolated** character. [scholarly]

## IX. The reign's other episodes (sargas 51–92, 99–108)

- **Story of King Nṛga** - Rāma tells Lakṣmaṇa of the pious king who gave the same cow twice in charity and was turned into a lizard in a pit for the error, waiting ages for release — a dharma-cautionary tale, and plain main-sequence text of the kāṇḍa, not appended matter (Vālmīki *Uttara* 53–54).
- **Nimi, Vasiṣṭha and Urvaśī** - The mutual curse of King **Nimi** and his purohita **Vasiṣṭha** — each cursing the other to bodilessness — and the cursing of the nymph **Urvaśī**; Nimi thereafter dwells in the eyelids of all creatures. Deep treatment: [Nimi & Vasiṣṭha's mutual curse](../../03-lineages-vamsha/nimi-vasishtha-mutual-curse.md) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 55–57).
- **Śukra curses Yayāti** - The lunar king's premature old age and **Pūru**'s exchange of his youth for his father's — the Rāmāyaṇa's own telling of a story the Mahābhārata makes famous (Vālmīki *Uttara* 58–59).
- **The Śatrughna–Lavaṇa–Mathurā arc** - The ascetics of the Yamunā come to Rāma for relief from **Lavaṇa**, son of Madhu, who holds the Madhu-vana with **Śiva's invincible trident** and eats men. **Śatrughna** asks for the war, is consecrated king of the country to be won, and travels out by way of Vālmīki's āśrama — arriving, as it happens, on the night **Kuśa and Lava are born**. He catches Lavaṇa away from the trident, kills him, founds **Madhupura (Mathurā)** on the site and rules there **twelve years** before returning to Ayodhyā. It is Śatrughna's only independent arc, and the corpus's forward link to the Mathurā of the Kṛṣṇa cycle (Vālmīki *Uttara* 60–72). Full treatment, with the Mālyavān → Analā → Kumbhīnasī → Lavaṇa kinship chain: [Madhu, Lavaṇa and the founding of Mathurā](../madhu-lavana-and-mathura.md).
  - **Māndhātṛ's end** - Embedded in the arc: the great Ikṣvāku emperor **Māndhātṛ**, having conquered the earth and marched on Lavaṇa's forest, is burnt to ash by the same trident — the warning Śatrughna is given before he goes (Vālmīki *Uttara* 67).
  - **Saudāsa (Kalmāṣapāda)** - Also embedded: the Ikṣvāku king cursed by Vasiṣṭha to eat men for offering him human flesh — the story that stands behind the line's later reputation (Vālmīki *Uttara* 65).
- **Śveta, the corpse-eating king** - On his tour after Śambūka, Rāma meets a shining ascetic in Agastya's forest who must devour his own body daily: as King **Śveta** he practised austerity but gave nothing away, and so earned heaven with an unappeasable hunger. Rāma's touch frees him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 77–78).
- **Daṇḍa, Arajā and the making of Daṇḍakāraṇya** - The youngest of Ikṣvāku's hundred sons, **Daṇḍa**, given the country between the Vindhya and Śaivala, violates **Arajā**, daughter of his own preceptor **Śukra**; Śukra's curse buries the kingdom and its people under seven days of dust, sparing only the grove where Arajā was left. The blasted land is **Daṇḍakāraṇya** — the forest of the exile, demon-country by curse rather than by nature (Vālmīki *Uttara* 79–81). Full treatment: [Daṇḍa, Arajā and the making of Daṇḍakāraṇya](../danda-araja-and-dandakaranya.md).
- **Bharata argues down the Rājasūya** - Rāma proposes the imperial **Rājasūya**; **Bharata** dissuades him — the rite destroys royal houses, and a king who is already lord of the world need not ruin the world to prove it. The **Aśvamedha** is chosen instead, and the case is made from the story of **Vṛtra**: Indra, brahminicide-stained for killing him, was cleansed by a horse-sacrifice (Vālmīki *Uttara* 83–86).
- **Ila / Ilā** - The king who alternates month by month between man and woman by Pārvatī's word, and as Ilā bears **Purūravas** to Budha — the head of the lunar line. Vālmīki's Ila is the son of **Kardama, king of Bāhli**, not Manu's child as the Purāṇas have him; the two genealogies disagree at the root. Cross-link: [Ila / Sudyumna](../../03-lineages-vamsha/ila-sudyumna.md), [Chandravaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 87–90).
- **Death of the queen-mothers** - **Kausalyā, Sumitrā and Kaikeyī** die and go to Daśaratha's world; the reign runs on without the generation that made it (Vālmīki *Uttara* 99).
- **Reign-settlements** - Rāma sends **Bharata** against the gandharvas of the Sindhu country and installs his sons **Takṣa at Takṣaśilā** and **Puṣkala at Puṣkalāvatī**; **Lakṣmaṇa's** sons **Aṅgada** and **Candraketu** are given Kārupatha and Candrakāntā in the Malla country; **Śatrughna's** sons **Subāhu** and **Śatrughātin** take Mathurā and Vidiśā; Kuśa and Lava are settled last (Vālmīki *Uttara* 100–108).
- **The chronology seam** - The kāṇḍa's own intervals do not close: the twins are born at sarga 66, Śatrughna rules Madhupura twelve years and returns, the boys are still boys at the Aśvamedha, and Rāma walks to the Sarayū a few sargas later — yet the only figure given for the reign anywhere is the **ten thousand years** of [Yuddha](6-yuddha-kanda.md) 128.96 and 128.106 (the familiar **eleven thousand** is the traditional reading of *daśa varṣa sahasrāṇi daśa varṣa śatāni ca*, "ten thousand years and ten hundred"); the Uttara's own close names no span at all. The human arc and the Tretā-scale arc are two clocks the text never reconciles. [scholarly]

## X. The departure of Rāma — Mahāprasthāna (sargas 103–111)

- **Kāla comes as a messenger** - **Time (Kāla / Yama-Mṛtyu)**, disguised as an ascetic, requests a private audience with Rāma to announce that his earthly task is complete and **Viṣṇu must return to Vaikuṇṭha**; their meeting must not be interrupted on pain of death (Vālmīki *Uttara* 103–104).
- **Durvāsā and Lakṣmaṇa's doom** - The irascible sage **Durvāsā** arrives demanding instant audience, threatening to curse the realm. To save the kingdom, **Lakṣmaṇa** interrupts the sealed meeting, accepting the death-penalty himself; Rāma, bound by dharma, "abandons" him (banishment = death for the noble). Lakṣmaṇa goes to the Sarayū, and **Indra bears him bodily to heaven** — the text's own ending for him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 105–106).
  - *Variant (Vaiṣṇava devotional):* he enters the water and is taken up as **Śeṣa / Ananta**, resuming the serpent-form that is his true nature — the gloss the tradition remembers, and the reverse of what the sarga says. See [Lakṣmaṇa](../characters/lakshmana.md).
- **Succession** - Rāma divides Kosala between the twins — **Kuśa at Kuśāvatī** in the south, **Lava at Śrāvastī** in the north — and consecrates them both before he leaves (Vālmīki *Uttara* 107).
- **Rāma's last commands** - Before he goes, Rāma disposes of his allies by name. **Vibhīṣaṇa** is to stay in Laṅkā "as long as people exist," his empire enduring as long as the moon and sun shine and men speak of Rāma, and is charged to worship the Ikṣvāku deity Jagannātha. **Hanumān** is ordered to live on — *"as long as my story is told in the world, O Foremost of Monkeys, so long mayest you be happy"* — and answers that he will remain while that history circulates: this is the **chiranjīvi grant**, made here by Rāma himself and nowhere else in Vālmīki. **Jāmbavān** receives the same command with a different term: *"till the Kali Yuga begins, continue to dwell on earth."* The rest of the bears and monkeys are simply invited to follow, and **Sugrīva**, refusing to be left behind, has already installed **Aṅgada** on the Kiṣkindhā throne (Vālmīki *Uttara* 108). Cross-link: [The Chiranjīvis](../the-chiranjivis.md).
- **The Great Departure into the Sarayū** - Robed in white, holding kuśa-grass, reciting the Veda, Rāma walks out of Ayodhyā to the **Sarayū** at the **Goprataraka** ghāṭ, followed by a retinue Vālmīki personifies outright — **Śrī-Lakṣmī** with her lotus on his right and the goddess **Vyavasāya** on his left, the **Vedas in the persons of brahmins**, **Gāyatrī**, the syllable **Oṃ** and the invocation **Vaṣaṭ**, and his own bow and arrows walking as beings — with his brothers, the citizens, the vānaras and ṛkṣas, and much of the city behind; Brahmā and the gods receive him as he enters the water and he **resumes his form as Viṣṇu** (the *jala-samādhi* / Mahāprasthāna) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 109–110).
- **Where the followers go** - Not everyone goes to Vaikuṇṭha: Brahmā assigns the men, animals and birds who walked into the river the **Sāntānika worlds**, the heaven above Brahmaloka reserved for the selfless; **Sugrīva** enters the orb of the **sun**, his father; the vānaras and ṛkṣas return to the gods whose portions they were (Vālmīki *Uttara* 110).
- **The phala-śruti** - The book closes with a recitation of the merit (offspring, long life, removal of sin, attainment of Viṣṇu's world) gained by hearing the Rāmāyaṇa — a hallmark of an integral closing frame (Vālmīki *Uttara* 111).
