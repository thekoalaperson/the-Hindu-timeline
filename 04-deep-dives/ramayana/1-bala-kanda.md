# Bāla Kāṇḍa — Book of Childhood

> Cosmic address: [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) ← but the Rāmāyaṇa itself unfolds in the [Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current Vaivasvata Manvantara. Rāma is the 7th [Daśāvatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) of [Viṣṇu](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md), of the [Sūryavaṃśa / Ikṣvāku line](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
> Parent: [Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive index](README.md) · Part of [04-deep-dives](../README.md). Primary source: Vālmīki [Rāmāyaṇa](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md).

The **Bāla Kāṇḍa** ("Book of Childhood/Youth") is the first of the seven kāṇḍas of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa. It has **77 sargas** in the vulgate / Southern recension and **76** in the Baroda Critical Edition (CE), whose Bāla Kāṇḍa has 1,941 numbered verses (a count of its GRETIL verse IDs). Every sarga number on this page is **vulgate**, the convention this corpus uses throughout; CE numbers are added in brackets where the two recensions differ in substance. The kāṇḍa frames the whole epic and narrates Rāma's birth and boyhood, his tutelage under Viśvāmitra, the slaying of his first demons, the redemption of Ahalyā, the breaking of Śiva's bow, the four-fold marriage at Mithilā, and the confrontation with Paraśurāma. Its embedded legends have **two different narrators**. Viśvāmitra tells the tīrtha-origin cycle on the road (sargas 23–25, 29, 32–49). The long account of Viśvāmitra's own life (51–65) is told at Mithilā by **Śatānanda**, Janaka's house-priest and Ahalyā's son.

## The Frame Story (sargas 1–4)

- **Nārada's epitome** - The sage Vālmīki asks Nārada whether any man now living has a list of some sixteen virtues: guṇa, vīrya, knowledge of dharma, gratitude, truthful speech, firm vows and the rest. Nārada answers "Rāma of the Ikṣvākus" and recites a compressed summary of the whole story in the rest of the sarga's 100 verses (the *Saṃkṣipta Rāmāyaṇa*) (Vālmīki, Bāla 1.1–4, 1.8ff).
- **The first śloka** - At the Tamasā river, near the Gaṅgā, Vālmīki watches a niṣāda shoot one of a pair of mating krauñca birds while the hen cries over it. His grief (*śoka*) bursts out as metrical verse (*śloka*), the birth of the anuṣṭubh meter and of poetry itself (Vālmīki, Bāla 2.3–15; the verse is 2.15, CE 1.2.14).
  - *On its form:* the utterance is cast as an imprecation — *mā niṣāda pratiṣṭhāṃ tvam agamaḥ śāśvatīḥ samāḥ*, "Niṣāda, may you never attain standing through the endless years" — so commentators have long debated whether the first verse of Sanskrit poetry is formally a **śāpa** (curse). The text never applies the word, and some commentators reject the reading precisely on the ground that an epic must not open with a curse. [disputed]
- **Brahmā's commission** - Brahmā appears and commands Vālmīki to compose Rāma's life in this new meter, granting him divine insight into every event (Vālmīki, Bāla 2).
- **Vālmīki's vision of the whole poem** - Seated on darbha grass with water sipped and mind gathered, Vālmīki is granted yogic sight of Rāma's entire life. The sarga then runs the contents of all seven kāṇḍas as a table of contents before a line of the poem has been sung (Vālmīki, Bāla 3).
  - *Recension note:* the famous simile that he saw it all "as a myrobalan fruit in his own palm" (*pāṇāv āmalakaṃ yathā*, vulgate 3.6) is not in the CE text, which moves straight from the darbha seat (CE 1.3.2) to the contents list. [recensional]
- **Lava and Kuśa** - Vālmīki teaches the completed poem, 24,000 verses in 500 sargas and six books plus an "uttara", to Rāma's twin sons (4.2). They sing it among the sages and in the streets of Ayodhyā. **Within this same sarga** Rāma sees them, brings them to his palace, seats himself on the golden throne among his brothers and ministers, and listens. The frame therefore opens and closes in Bāla 4 itself. The Uttara Kāṇḍa later narrates the same performance at length (Vālmīki, Bāla 4.2, 4.27–36; CE 1.4.21–27; the Uttara scene at CE 7.85–86).

## The Childless King and the Putrakāmeṣṭi (sargas 5–17)

- **Daśaratha of Ayodhyā** - The aged Ikṣvāku king of Kosala, ruling from Ayodhyā on the Sarayū, has three chief queens — **Kausalyā, Kaikeyī, Sumitrā** — but no son to continue the [Sūryavaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) (Vālmīki, Bāla 5–6). His eight ministers, the eighth being Sumantra, and his two chief priests, Vasiṣṭha and Vāmadeva, are portrayed in sarga 7, and in sarga 8 he resolves on a horse sacrifice for sons.
- **The Sanatkumāra prophecy** - Sumantra recalls an old prophecy of **Sanatkumāra**: Daśaratha's sons will come through the sage **Ṛśyaśṛṅga**, son of Vibhāṇḍaka (Vālmīki, Bāla 9.1–19). The prophecy is resumed in sarga 11, where it also foretells Daśaratha's friendship with the king of Aṅga and his visit to him (Vālmīki, Bāla 11.1–6).
- **Ṛśyaśṛṅga brought** - The tale itself follows. The celibate boy with a deer's horn had never seen a woman. Courtesans lure him out of the forest to break Aṅga's drought under king **Romapāda**, rain falls the moment he enters the kingdom, and Romapāda gives him **his own daughter Śāntā** in marriage. Daśaratha then fetches the couple from Aṅga (Vālmīki, Bāla 9.18–19, 10.28–33, 11.3–6; Daśaratha calls her "Śāntā, your daughter" to Romapāda at 11.19, CE 1.10.19).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.23.7–8):* Śāntā is **Daśaratha's own daughter**, given by him to his childless friend Romapāda, who raises her as his own. That is one source of the "Rāma's elder sister" tradition; it is not in Vālmīki, where she is Aṅga's princess and Ṛśyaśṛṅga is Romapāda's son-in-law (*jāmātā*, 9.19). [Purāṇic]
- **The Aśvamedha and the guest-list** - Daśaratha first performs the year-long **Aśvamedha** (horse sacrifice) on the Sarayū's northern bank, Ṛśyaśṛṅga presiding (Vālmīki, Bāla 12.3, 14.1). In preparing it, Vasiṣṭha sends Sumantra to summon the kings of the earth and names **Janaka of Mithilā first**, "knowing him to be a connection of long standing" (*pūrva-saṃbandhin*), ahead of the kings of Kāśī, Kekaya and Aṅga. It is the epic's earliest planting of the Mithilā tie (Vālmīki, Bāla 13.21–25; CE 1.12.18–19).
  - *Kausalyā and the horse:* in the rite itself Kausalyā circles the horse and strikes it with three knives, then spends a night beside it "desiring dharma" (Vālmīki, Bāla 14.33–34), the queen's part prescribed in the Vedic aśvamedha ritual.
- **The Putrīyā Iṣṭi** - With the horse sacrifice concluded, Ṛśyaśṛṅga conducts the **Putrakāmeṣṭi / Putrīyā Iṣṭi** ("son-desiring oblation") (Vālmīki, Bāla 15.1–3).
- **The gods' parallel petition** - At the same rite the Devas, oppressed by **Rāvaṇa**, beg Brahmā for rescue. Brahmā explains the loophole: Rāvaṇa asked to be unkillable by gods, gandharvas, yakṣas and rākṣasas, and **out of contempt did not name men**, so a man can kill him (Vālmīki, Bāla 15.13–14). Viṣṇu arrives, agrees to be born as a man and to rule the earth for eleven thousand years, and chooses Daśaratha as his father (Vālmīki, Bāla 15.21–31, 16.1–8).
- **The pāyasa from the fire** - A great radiant being, dark and red-robed, with beard and hair like a lion's mane, rises from the sacrificial fire bearing a golden vessel of divine **pāyasa** (milk-pudding) for the queens. He names himself a being sent by Prajāpati (*prājāpatya*); "Yajña-puruṣa" is the translators' and tradition's label (Vālmīki, Bāla 16.11–16).
  - *Distribution (Vālmīki):* Daśaratha gives half to Kausalyā and half of the remainder (a quarter) to Sumitrā. He gives half of what is left (an eighth) to Kaikeyī, and then, after a moment's thought, the last eighth again to Sumitrā. Hence Sumitrā bears **two** sons (Vālmīki, Bāla 16.27–29). Later retellings vary the order.
- **Brahmā's vānara commission** - Brahmā fathers **Jāmbavān** from his own yawn, then orders the gods, gandharvas and sages to beget vānara sons of matching might on apsarases, yakṣīs, nāga-women and she-bears — an army raised a full generation before Rāma will need it: **Indra→Vālin, Sūrya→Sugrīva, Bṛhaspati→Tāra, Kubera→Gandhamādana, Viśvakarman→Nala, Agni→Nīla, the Aśvins→Mainda and Dvivida, Varuṇa→Suṣeṇa, Parjanya→Śarabha, Vāyu→Hanumān** (Vālmīki, Bāla 17.7–16). → full father-by-father roster and the Mt. Ṛkṣavat settlement at [vānaras and ṛkṣas](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/vanaras-and-rikshas.md).

## Birth of Rāma and His Brothers (sarga 18)

- **The four princes born** - Six seasons after the sacrifice, "in the twelfth month", Caitra, on the ninth tithi:
  - **Rāma** — to **Kausalyā**, called *viṣṇor ardham*, "half of Viṣṇu" (Vālmīki, Bāla 18.8–11).
  - **Bharata** — to **Kaikeyī**, "a manifest quarter of Viṣṇu" (*sākṣād viṣṇoś caturbhāgaḥ*) (Vālmīki, Bāla 18.13).
  - **Lakṣmaṇa** and **Śatrughna** — to **Sumitrā**, together "endowed with half of Viṣṇu" (*viṣṇor ardha-samanvitau*), born on the following day (Vālmīki, Bāla 18.14–15). The text says Sumitrā bore two sons; that they are twins is the usual reading. Lakṣmaṇa attaches himself to Rāma from childhood and Śatrughna to Bharata (Vālmīki, Bāla 18.27–33).
  - *Note (the fractions):* the halves and quarters are Vālmīki's own wording, not a later scheme. Read literally they over-count (½ + ¼ + ½). The vulgate translator's notes therefore read the fractions as shares of the sacrificial pāyasa rather than of Viṣṇu, who cannot be divided, and record that whether Rāma is a full (*pūrṇa*), half or partial (*aṃśa*) avatāra has become a standing debate (notes on 18.11, 18.13). [commentarial] The CE keeps all three phrases (CE 1.17.6, 8, 9).
- **Astrological birth-chart** - The chart is in the vulgate text itself. Rāma is born under **Punarvasu** (the asterism whose deity is Aditi), with five planets exalted and Jupiter with the Moon rising in the **Karkaṭa (Cancer) lagna**. Bharata is born under **Puṣya** in the Mīna lagna, and Sumitrā's sons under **Āśleṣā** in Karkaṭa at sunrise (Vālmīki, Bāla 18.8–10, 18.15). Jyotiṣa and the Rāma-navamī observance build on these verses.
  - *Recension note:* the CE has none of this. Its birth sarga passes straight from the sacrifice's end to "Kausalyā bore Rāma" with no month, tithi, asterism or planets, and the naming follows on the eleventh day (CE 1.17.1–12). The horoscope is a vulgate reading. [recensional]
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās' [Rāmcaritmānas](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md), Bāla, chanda after dohā 191; also attributed to the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa):* the framing is heavily devotional. Rāma first appears to Kausalyā **four-armed**, bearing his own weapons (*nija āyudha bhujacārī*), and becomes an infant only at her prayer. The Mānas is itself narrated by Śiva to Pārvatī, and the descent is "to lift Earth's burden". [late text]
  - *Variant (later Vaiṣṇava identification):* some texts equate Lakṣmaṇa with **Śeṣa/Ananta** and Bharata and Śatrughna with Viṣṇu's discus and conch. This is not in the Bāla Kāṇḍa. [late text]

## Viśvāmitra Claims Rāma; the Road Out (sargas 18–23)

- **The sage's demand** - **Viśvāmitra** arrives at court and asks for Rāma by name, for the ten nights of his sacrifice (*daśarātram*), to guard it from the rākṣasas Mārīca and Subāhu. Lakṣmaṇa is not asked for; he is called along with Rāma only at the departure (Vālmīki, Bāla 18–19, esp. 19.17–18; 22.1).
- **Rāma not yet sixteen** - Daśaratha's refusal turns on the boy's age: *ūna-ṣoḍaśa-varṣo me rāmo rājīva-locanaḥ* — "my lotus-eyed Rāma is less than sixteen years old" — and he offers himself, his army, anything but Rāma (Vālmīki, Bāla 20.2; CE 1.19.2).
  - *Note (the age crux):* the epic's plain age-statements do not reconcile. Rāma is under sixteen here and marries within the same kāṇḍa. Sītā twice says she lived twelve years in the Ikṣvāku house before the exile (Araṇya 47.4; Sundara 33.17). Araṇya 47.10 gives Rāma twenty-five and Sītā eighteen at the exile's start. The corpus records all of them rather than harmonizing. [scholarly]
- **Vasiṣṭha's counsel and the astra genealogy** - Vasiṣṭha persuades Daśaratha that Viśvāmitra is invincible and Rāma safe with him. In doing so he gives the Rāmāyaṇa's own account of where divine missiles come from. The astras are the **sons of Kṛśāśva Prajāpati** by two daughters of Dakṣa: fifty born of **Jayā** and fifty of **Suprabhā**, the latter fifty named the **saṃhāras** ("withdrawers"). They were given to Viśvāmitra long before, in his kingly days. The verse is passive and **names no giver**; the commentaries supply Śiva, and this corpus follows them while keeping the parentage and the donor apart, the one being text and the other inference (Vālmīki, Bāla 21.13–17; CE 1.20.13–14). [scholarly] → canonical home, with the two rosters of sarga 27–28 and what the epic's personified astras imply: [The sons of Kṛśāśva — the astra-genealogy](../krishashva-astra-genealogy.md).
- **Departure with Lakṣmaṇa** - Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa follow the sage out of Ayodhyā, bows in hand. After a yojana and a half they halt on the Sarayū's southern bank, and next day reach the **Sarayū–Gaṅgā confluence** (Vālmīki, Bāla 22.1–6, 22.10, 23).
- **Balā and Atibalā** - At that first halt Viśvāmitra grants the two boys the mantras **Balā** and **Atibalā**, banishing hunger, thirst, fatigue and disease (Vālmīki, Bāla 22.10–16).
- **Origin of Kāmadeva's ashram (Kāma's burning)** - At the confluence hermitage Viśvāmitra tells the first of his road-legends. Here **Kāma** (Madana) assailed Śiva, who was practising austerities and going his way, newly married, with the Maruts. Śiva burned him with his wrathful eye (*raudreṇa cakṣuṣā*; "third eye" is the translators' gloss) and his limbs fell from his body. Hence Kāma is *Ananga*, "the bodiless," and the country where he let his body (*aṅga*) go is **Aṅga** (Vālmīki, Bāla 23.10–14).

## Tāṭakā, the Astras, Subāhu (sargas 24–30)

- **Malada and Karūṣa** - Crossing the Gaṅgā into a wasted country, Viśvāmitra explains its names. After slaying Vṛtra, **Indra** was fouled by brahmahatyā. When the gods bathed him here, his filth (*mala*) and hunger (*kārūṣa*) left his body and went into the ground, so he blessed the two provinces **Malada** and **Karūṣa** with plenty. The yakṣiṇī who now holds them has since laid them waste (Vālmīki, Bāla 24.18–27).
- **The Tāṭakā forest** - Viśvāmitra tells of the **yakṣiṇī Tāṭakā**. Brahmā gave her to the childless yakṣa **Suketu** as a daughter, with the strength of a thousand elephants, and she was married to **Sunda, son of Jambha**, and bore **Mārīca**. When **Agastya's curse killed Sunda**, Tāṭakā and Mārīca rushed at the sage to devour him. Agastya cursed Mārīca to rākṣasa-hood and Tāṭakā to a man-eater's hideous form (Vālmīki, Bāla 25.5–13).
- **Slaying of Tāṭakā** - Rāma scruples at killing a woman. Viśvāmitra rules on dharma's authority that a protector must act for his people "whether the deed be cruel or not" (25.17–19). He cites precedents: **Indra killed Mantharā, Virocana's daughter**, when she sought to destroy the earth, and **Viṣṇu killed Bhṛgu's wife, Śukra's mother** (Vālmīki, Bāla 25.20–21). Rāma at first means only to cripple her, not to kill (26.10–12). **Rāma severs both her arms** with arrows as she charges, and **Lakṣmaṇa cuts off her ears and the tip of her nose**. She fights on invisibly, hurling rocks by illusion, until Viśvāmitra warns that rākṣasas grow unassailable at dusk and Rāma must finish before nightfall. Rāma then strikes her through the chest and she falls dead (Vālmīki, Bāla 26.10–26). The gods rejoice and ask Viśvāmitra to give Rāma the sons of Kṛśāśva (26.27–30; CE 1.25.18).
  - *Note:* the mutilate-then-kill sequence is Vālmīki's own text, not a retelling's softening — it is the same escalation the epic will repeat on Śūrpaṇakhā, and it is what makes Rāma's first blood a ruling on dharma rather than a reflex.
- **The arsenal of divine weapons** - Pleased, Viśvāmitra confers on Rāma the Kṛśāśva missiles in two matched rosters — some fifty **astras** of striking (daṇḍa, dharma, kāla, brahmaśiras, the missiles of Śiva, Viṣṇu, Indra, Varuṇa, Agni, Vāyu and the rest) and their **saṃhāra** (recall) counterparts — which appear to Rāma as embodied beings with folded hands, pledging service at a thought (Vālmīki, Bāla 27–28).
- **Siddhāśrama** - They reach Viśvāmitra's hermitage, the **Siddhāśrama**, where Viṣṇu once practised austerities and where Kaśyapa and Aditi asked him to be born as their son, the dwarf **Vāmana** who took back the worlds from Bali. Viśvāmitra lives there out of devotion to Vāmana (Vālmīki, Bāla 29.2–22). He begins his sacrifice, and the princes guard it for six days and nights (Vālmīki, Bāla 30.3–5).
- **Mārīca hurled, Subāhu slain** - As the demons assault the rite, Rāma uses the **Mānava astra** to fling **Mārīca** a hundred yojanas into the ocean. He does not kill him; Mārīca returns in the Araṇya Kāṇḍa as the golden deer. Rāma kills **Subāhu** with the **Āgneya astra** and strikes down the rest with the **Vāyavya** (wind) missile (Vālmīki, Bāla 30.15–23).

## Viśvāmitra's Legends on the Road (sargas 31–47)

With the sacrifice completed, Viśvāmitra leads the princes north-east toward Janaka's bow-sacrifice at Mithilā, and at each halt narrates the origin-myth of the ground they are standing on. These are full upākhyānas (sub-legends), and they are **Viśvāmitra's own telling**. The account of his own life given later at Mithilā is not.

- **Kuśa's four cities and Kuśanābha's hundred daughters** - Camped on the **Śoṇa**, Viśvāmitra answers Rāma's question about the country with his own house's history: **Kuśa**, Brahmā-born, whose four sons found Kauśāmbī, Mahodaya, Dharmāraṇya and Girivraja; **Kuśanābha**'s hundred daughters by the apsarā Ghṛtācī, bent into hunchbacks by **Vāyu** when they refuse him and straightened by their marriage to **Brahmadatta** of Kāmpilya — the etiology of **Kanyākubja** ("the maidens' hunch," Kanauj); and the line down through **Gādhi** to Viśvāmitra himself, with his sister **Satyavatī** who becomes the river **Kauśikī** (Vālmīki, Bāla 32–34; Kauśikī at 34.11). → full treatment at [Kuśanābha's hundred daughters and Brahmadatta](../../03-lineages-vamsha/kushanabha-daughters-and-brahmadatta.md); the genealogy at [Gādhi and the caru-swap](../../03-lineages-vamsha/gadhi-and-the-caru-swap.md).
- **Birth of the Gaṅgā and Pārvatī / Umā** - The lineage of Himavān, daughters Gaṅgā and Umā, and the cosmic birth of **Kārttikeya (Skanda)** from Agni and the Kṛttikās to slay Tāraka (Vālmīki, Bāla 35–37). Interrupted in the hundredth year of her union with Rudra and robbed of the son, **Umā curses the wives of the gods to bear no children and the Earth to be of many forms and the wife of many** — the rule Brahmā cites back one sarga later as the reason the war-leader must be got on Gaṅgā and not on a deva's own wife (Vālmīki, Bāla 36.20–24, 37.5–6). → [Umā's double curse](../uma-double-curse.md).
- **Sons of Sagara** - King **Sagara** wins a boon from the sage **Bhṛgu** after austerities. His wife **Keśinī** of Vidarbha bears one son, **Asamañja**, and his wife **Sumati**, Garuḍa's sister, bears a gourd from which come **60,000 sons** (Vālmīki, Bāla 38.2–19). Asamañja grows up to throw the townsfolk's children into the Sarayū and laugh as they drown, and Sagara banishes him (Vālmīki, Bāla 38.20–22). When **Indra steals** the sacrificial horse, the 60,000 dig up the earth seeking it. They find it beside the meditating **Kapila**, whom the text calls **Vāsudeva**, a form of Viṣṇu, accuse him of the theft, and are burnt to ash (Vālmīki, Bāla 39–40; Kapila-Vāsudeva at 40.25). → see broader cosmology in [cosmogony / pralaya themes](../../00-time-and-cosmology/kalpa-and-pralaya.md).
- **Aṃśumān finds the ashes** - Asamañja's son **Aṃśumān**, sent after his uncles, follows their tunnel, circles and greets the elephants of the quarters (41.7–12), and finds the horse and the heaps of ash. Wanting water for their funeral offerings, he meets **Garuḍa (Suparṇa)**, the dead princes' maternal uncle. Garuḍa tells him that only the heavenly river can redeem the ashes, the errand that will pass through Dilīpa to Bhagīratha (Vālmīki, Bāla 41; CE 1.40.16).
- **Descent of Gaṅgā (Bhagīratha)** - **Bhagīratha**'s austerities at Gokarṇa bring Gaṅgā from heaven to redeem the ashes. Śiva breaks her fall in his hair (hence *Gaṅgādhara*), and she follows Bhagīratha's chariot down to Pātāla — the **Gaṅgāvataraṇa**. Brahmā then names her Bhāgīrathī, Bhagīratha's daughter (Vālmīki, Bāla 42–44).
  - **Jahnu swallows the river** - Mid-descent the flood drowns the sage-king **Jahnu**'s sacrificial ground; he drinks the whole of Gaṅgā up, and releases her from his ears at the sages' plea — so she counts as his daughter and bears the name **Jāhnavī** (Vālmīki, Bāla 43.34–38). → [Jahnu](../../03-lineages-vamsha/jahnu.md).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Āraṇyaka Parva, Tīrthayātrā; Ganguli 3.106–109):* here the sage **Lomaśa**, not Viśvāmitra, tells the Sagara cycle to Yudhiṣṭhira. In it Sagara wins the boon from **Śiva** on Kailāsa, not from Bhṛgu. The wives are named by their houses: the **Vidarbha** princess bears the gourd of 60,000 sons and the **Śibi** princess bears Asamañja. The story also serves to explain how the ocean, drunk dry by Agastya, was filled again by Gaṅgā.
- **Churning of the Ocean (Samudra Manthana)** - At Viśālā, Viśvāmitra tells the Bāla-Kāṇḍa version of the **devas and asuras churning the Milk-Ocean**, with Mandara as the churning-stick and Vāsuki as the rope (Vālmīki, Bāla 45). In the vulgate the first thing to rise is the **Hālāhala** poison, which Śiva drinks at the gods' plea. When Mandara sinks, Viṣṇu takes **tortoise** form to bear it. After a thousand years come **Dhanvantari** and the apsarases, then **Vāruṇī**, then Uccaiḥśravas, Kaustubha and the **Amṛta** (Vālmīki, Bāla 45.18–39). → cross-link: [Samudra Manthana deep-dive](../samudra-manthana.md).
  - *Sura and asura:* Vālmīki gives his own etymology. The sons of Aditi accepted **Vāruṇī**, Varuṇa's daughter, and so are *suras*. The sons of Diti refused her and so are *a-suras* (Vālmīki, Bāla 45.36–38; CE 1.44.21–23).
  - *Recension note:* the CE's churning is far shorter. It goes straight from the churning (CE 1.44.17) to Dhanvantari and the apsarases (44.18), Vāruṇī (44.21–23), Uccaiḥśravas, Kaustubha and amṛta (44.24), and the war (44.25–27). It has **no Hālāhala, no Śiva drinking the poison, no tortoise and no Mohinī**. [recensional]
  - **Mohinī in Vālmīki** - In the vulgate, when the amṛta appears and the two sides fall on each other, **Viṣṇu carries the nectar off "assuming the bewitching illusion of Mohinī"** (*māyām āsthāya mohinīm*). The Mohinī form is thus present in the vulgate Rāmāyaṇa, in a single half-verse (Vālmīki, Bāla 45.42), but absent from the CE text. Even the vulgate omits the *episode* the Purāṇas build around it: the serving of the two rows, the doubling-back and Rāhu's severed head. It does tell a war over the amṛta, before and after the theft (Vālmīki, Bāla 45.40–44).
- **Diti and the Maruts** - How Indra split Diti's foetus into the forty-nine **Maruts** while she slept impurely, aiming to abort the asura who would kill him: she dozed off with her feet where her head should be, and Indra entered her womb and cut the foetus into seven, each of which he cut again (Vālmīki, Bāla 46.16–18, 47.1–5). Viśvāmitra then gives the line of Viśālā's kings down to the reigning **Sumati**, who receives the party (Vālmīki, Bāla 47).

## The Redemption of Ahalyā (sargas 48–49)

The last stop before Mithilā, and the last of Viśvāmitra's road-legends.

- **Ahalyā's curse** - At Gautama's deserted hermitage on Mithilā's outskirts, Viśvāmitra tells how **Indra**, disguised as the sage **Gautama**, seduced his wife **Ahalyā**. Gautama returns as Indra leaves and curses him to lose his testicles, which fall at once (Vālmīki, Bāla 48.27–28). He curses Ahalyā to live for many thousands of years in the hermitage, unseen by all beings, living on air and lying in ashes, until Rāma comes (Vālmīki, Bāla 48.29–32).
  - *Indra's ram's testicles:* in sarga 49 Indra tells the gods he did the deed in their service, to spend Gautama's ascetic power. Agni and the gods then ask the Pitṛs to give Indra a **ram's (meṣa)** testicles, and they do (Vālmīki, Bāla 49.1–10; CE 1.48.6–10).
  - *In Vālmīki:* Ahalyā **knowingly** recognizes Indra through the disguise and consents "out of curiosity about the king of the gods" (*devarāja-kutūhalāt*, Bāla 48.19; CE 1.47.19). She is cursed to invisible ascetic penance, **not turned to stone**.
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās' Rāmcaritmānas, Bāla dohā 210; the same motif is widely attributed to the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa and the Purāṇas, and dominates popular tradition):* Ahalyā lies as a **stone** (*upala deha dhari*) and "longs for the dust of Rāma's lotus feet". She is restored when his foot touches her — the iconic "stone-to-woman" image, and the reason the popular memory of this episode is almost the inverse of the text's. The Mānas does not make her innocent, though: elsewhere it calls her the Ahalyā "who had done great wrong" (*kṛta agha bhūrī*, Bāla 223.3). [late text]
- **Rāma redeems her** - Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa enter the hermitage. Ahalyā's form becomes visible again at the curse's end, and **the princes touch her feet** in reverence (Vālmīki, Bāla 49.16–17; CE 1.48.17). She remembers Gautama's words and receives them with water and hospitality; flowers rain down, and Gautama returns and takes her back. It is the first overt sign of Rāma's redemptive power, and a gesture the popular retellings reverse into her touching the dust of his feet. → full dossier: [Ahalyā](../characters/ahalya.md).

## Mithilā: Arrival and Śatānanda's Narration (sargas 50–65)

- **Arrival at Janaka's yajña** - They reach **Mithilā** (Videha) for King **Janaka**'s sacrifice; Janaka comes out with his priest **Śatānanda** to welcome Viśvāmitra, and asks who the two youths are (Vālmīki, Bāla 50).
- **Śatānanda takes up the story** - Śatānanda — Gautama and **Ahalyā's son** — hears that his mother has been restored, and out of gratitude turns to Rāma and narrates, at length, the life of the sage who brought him: the whole of the Viśvāmitra saga that follows is **Śatānanda's telling at Mithilā**, not Viśvāmitra's on the road (Vālmīki, Bāla 51).
- **Viśvāmitra and the wish-cow** - The kṣatriya king **Viśvāmitra (Kauśika)**, son of Gādhi, covets Vasiṣṭha's wish-cow **Śabalā** (Vālmīki, Bāla 51–53). The cow brings forth Pahlavas, then Śakas and Yavanas, then Kāmbojas, Mlecchas and others, who destroy his army (Vālmīki, Bāla 54.18–23, 55.1–4). His hundred sons charge Vasiṣṭha and are **burnt to ash by the sage's mere "huṃ"** (*huṃkāreṇaiva*, 55.5–7; CE 1.54.6). Viśvāmitra then wins the divine missiles from Śiva by austerity, but Vasiṣṭha's **brahmadaṇḍa** (Brahmā-staff) swallows every one, the brahmāstra included (Vālmīki, Bāla 55.12–17, 56.1–23). The humiliated king begins millennia of tapas to become what force cannot make him.
  - *Note on the name:* "Nandinī" is the name of Vasiṣṭha's cow in the Mahābhārata's telling of the same quarrel (Ādi Parva, Caitraratha sub-parva; Ganguli 1.176), in Kālidāsa's *Raghuvaṃśa* (1.82) and in later retellings. Vālmīki's Bāla Kāṇḍa calls her Śabalā throughout. [variant — non-Vālmīki]
- **Triśaṅku's heaven** - Viśvāmitra raises the outcast king **Triśaṅku** bodily to heaven; when the gods cast him down, Viśvāmitra creates a counter-heaven (Triśaṅku's "southern" constellation) and halts him mid-sky (Vālmīki, Bāla 57–60).
- **Ambarīṣa and Śunaḥśepa** - King **Ambarīṣa**'s sacrificial animal is stolen, and he buys **Śunaḥśepa**, the middle son of the sage Ṛcīka, as the victim (Vālmīki, Bāla 61). At the lake of Puṣkara the boy throws himself into the lap of his **maternal uncle** Viśvāmitra (62.2–3). Viśvāmitra asks his own sons to take the boy's place. When they refuse, he curses them to be born among dog-eaters (62.8–17). He tells Śunaḥśepa to praise Agni when bound to the post and gives him two divine gāthās (62.19–20). Bound, the boy praises **Indra and Indra's younger brother (Upendra-Viṣṇu)**, and Indra grants him long life (Vālmīki, Bāla 62.25–26; CE 1.61.24).
  - *Variant (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.13–18, the Vedic source):* the story belongs to king **Hariścandra** and his son Rohita. Śunaḥśepa is the son of **Ajīgarta**, who sells him. Freed after praising a series of Vedic deities, he is adopted by Viśvāmitra as Devarāta. The Bāla Kāṇḍa has moved the tale to Ambarīṣa and made Ṛcīka the father. [Vedic]
- **Menakā and Rambhā** - The apsarases are sent to break his austerity. He succumbs to **Menakā**, who stays "five and five years" (Vālmīki, Bāla 63.8). He then curses **Rambhā** to stand as a stone for **ten thousand** years (*daśa varṣa-sahasrāṇi*, Bāla 64.12; CE 1.63.11; the "thousand" of most summaries drops a word). Finding that anger has again cost him his accumulated tapas, he finally conquers both desire and anger and wins the title **Brahmarṣi** from Brahmā and from Vasiṣṭha's own mouth (Vālmīki, Bāla 63–65; the acknowledgment at 65.25).
  - *Not in Vālmīki:* the Bāla Kāṇḍa does not say that Menakā bore **Śakuntalā**. That birth is told in the Mahābhārata's Ādi Parva (the *Śakuntalopākhyāna*) and in Kālidāsa. Here Menakā's ten years are simply a lapse in his tapas.

## The Bow of Śiva and the Winning of Sītā (sargas 66–67)

- **Sītā's earth-birth** - Janaka relates that **Sītā** ("furrow") rose from the ground before his plough while he was ploughing and clearing the sacrificial field. She is *ayonijā*, not womb-born, a daughter of the Earth (Bhūmi) raised as his own (Vālmīki, Bāla 66.13–15; CE 1.65.15).
  - *Variant (Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa & some Jaina versions, e.g. Saṅghadāsa's Vasudevahiṇḍī, Guṇabhadra's Uttarapurāṇa):* Sītā is the **daughter of Rāvaṇa and Mandodarī**, abandoned/buried because of a prophecy she would destroy his line, and recovered by Janaka. [variant — non-Vālmīki]
  - *Variant (Vedavatī identity):* the identification of Sītā with **Vedavatī**, the ascetic woman who cursed Rāvaṇa and entered the fire, is in **Vālmīki's own Uttara Kāṇḍa**: "she who was Vedavatī in the Kṛta age … is now called Sītā" (Uttara 17; CE 7.17.31). That book is generally held to be later than the core epic. Sītā as **Lakṣmī** to Rāma's Viṣṇu is spoken by Brahmā in the Yuddha Kāṇḍa: *sītā lakṣmīr bhavān viṣṇuḥ* (Yuddha 117.28; CE 6.105.25). Both identifications are textual rather than merely Purāṇic, but neither is made in the Bāla Kāṇḍa. [late text]
- **The Śiva-dhanus (Pināka): first account** - Janaka gives the bow's provenance: at the destruction of **Dakṣa's sacrifice**, Śiva — denied his share of the oblation — raised this bow in fury and threatened to take the gods' heads; appeased, he gave the bow over to them gladly, and the gods deposited it with Janaka's ancestor **Devarāta** for custodial keeping. It has stood in Mithilā since, and no king has budged it (Vālmīki, Bāla 66.8–12; *nyāsa-bhūtam*, "as a deposit", CE 1.65.13).
- **The vīrya-śulka and the year-long siege** - Janaka vows Sītā to whoever can string the bow — *vīrya-śulka*, "the bride-price of valour". In Bāla 66 this is a standing trial and not a suitor-choice assembly. The kings who fail take the refusal as insult and **besiege Mithilā**; when a full year has passed (*saṃvatsare pūrṇe*) the city's resources are exhausted. Janaka then wins a fourfold army from the gods by austerity and breaks them (Vālmīki, Bāla 66.15–26; the year at 66.22).
  - *Internal variant (Ayodhyā 118):* when Sītā tells the story to Anasūyā, Janaka resolves to hold a **svayaṃvara** (*svayaṃ varaṃ … kariṣyāmi*, Ayodhyā 118.38), the very word Bāla 66 avoids. [scholarly: the two tellings frame the contest differently]
- **Breaking the bow** - At Viśvāmitra's bidding the bow is brought in its iron, eight-wheeled chest, hauled by five thousand men (*nṛṇāṃ śatāni pañcāśat*). Rāma takes it by the middle as if in play, strings it effortlessly — and it **snaps in the middle with a thunderclap** that fells the onlookers. Sītā is won (Vālmīki, Bāla 67.3–18).
  - *Variant (internal Rāmāyaṇa "contradiction"):* In the Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa Sītā tells Anasūyā that **Varuṇa** gave Janaka an excellent bow, with two inexhaustible quivers, at a great sacrifice (Ayodhyā 118.39). Bāla Kāṇḍa names it Śiva's Pināka, deposited by the gods. The discrepancy is well noted. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās' Rāmcaritmānas):* expands a full **dhanuṣa-yajña / svayaṃvara** scene — many kings fail, Lakṣmaṇa's fiery taunts, Rāma's effortless feat; the most popular North-Indian telling. It adds the **jayamālā**: Sītā, prompted by her companions, garlands Rāma, a ceremony with no counterpart in Vālmīki (Bāla soraṭhā 264). [late text]
  - *Variant (folk, on Sītā's strength):* As a child Sītā once lifted the bow's box while sweeping, proving she alone "matched" it. [folk tradition]

## The Four-Fold Marriage (sargas 68–73)

- **Daśaratha summoned** - Janaka's envoys reach Ayodhyā after three nights on the road, horses spent, and report that Rāma has broken the bow (Vālmīki, Bāla 68.1–10). Daśaratha sets out the next day with Vasiṣṭha, Vāmadeva and his ministers (Vālmīki, Bāla 68–69).
- **The Ikṣvāku genealogy recited** - Vasiṣṭha answers with the Solar line from **Brahmā → Marīci → Kaśyapa → Vivasvat → Manu → Ikṣvāku**, down through thirty-four kings counting Ikṣvāku and Daśaratha. **This roster is the Rāmāyaṇa's own and diverges sharply from the Purāṇic Solar list**: **Kakutstha** appears *after* Bhagīratha, not near the head of the line; **Raghu** is Kakutstha's son; **Kalmāṣapāda** (here **Pravṛddha**) is Raghu's son; **Nahuṣa and Yayāti** — Lunar kings everywhere else — stand inside the Ikṣvāku succession; and **Hariścandra is absent altogether** (Vālmīki, Bāla 70.19–43). → compare the Purāṇic spine at [Sūrya- & Candra-vaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md). [scholarly: the two lists are not harmonizable and the corpus keeps both]
  - *Sagara's name:* the list stops to explain it. King Asita, driven out by the Haihayas, Tālajaṅghas and Śaśabindus, died in the Himālaya leaving two pregnant queens. One poisoned the other to destroy her child. The poisoned queen was blessed by the Bhārgava sage **Cyavana**, and her son was born "together with the poison" (*saha gareṇa*), hence **Sa-gara** (Vālmīki, Bāla 70.27–37). Which of the two queens is the **Kālindī** named at 70.33 is read both ways; the vulgate translator takes Kālindī as the poisoner. [disputed]
- **The Videha genealogy and the Sudhanvan war** - Janaka answers with the Nimi/Videha line. In the middle of it he names a war the retellings drop: **Sudhanvan** of Sāṃkāśya demanded both the bow and Sītā, besieged Mithilā, and was killed by Janaka. Janaka then installed his own younger brother **Kuśadhvaja** on the conquered throne of Sāṃkāśya. He closes by offering **Sītā to Rāma and Ūrmilā to Lakṣmaṇa** (Vālmīki, Bāla 71; the offer at 71.20–22). → [the Videha/Mithilā line of Janaka](../../03-lineages-vamsha/videha-mithila-line-janaka.md).
- **Kuśadhvaja's daughters** - Kuśadhvaja has already been fetched from Sāṃkāśya at Janaka's order (Vālmīki, Bāla 70.1–8). Now Viśvāmitra, seconded by Vasiṣṭha, proposes his two daughters for Bharata and Śatrughna, so that all four princes may marry together (Vālmīki, Bāla 72.1–5).
- **Yudhājit arrives** - On the day of Daśaratha's great gift of cows, the eve of the wedding, **Yudhājit**, Kaikeyī's brother and Bharata's maternal uncle, reaches Mithilā. He had come to Ayodhyā from Kekaya to see his nephew, learned that the court had gone to Mithilā, and followed. It is the arrival that will end with Bharata out of the capital at the worst possible moment (Vālmīki, Bāla 73.1–7).
- **The four couples** - Janaka himself gives all four brides (Vālmīki, Bāla 73.26–33):
  - **Rāma — Sītā** (Janaka's daughter). Janaka's words are "this is Sītā, my daughter, your partner in dharma; take her hand in yours" (*iyaṃ sītā mama sutā sahadharmacarī tava*) (Vālmīki, Bāla 73.26–27).
  - **Lakṣmaṇa — Ūrmilā** (Janaka's second daughter) (Vālmīki, Bāla 73.30).
  - **Bharata — Māṇḍavī** (Kuśadhvaja's daughter) (Vālmīki, Bāla 73.32).
  - **Śatrughna — Śrutakīrti** (Kuśadhvaja's daughter) (Vālmīki, Bāla 73.33).
  - *Variant (folk):* the "sleep of Ūrmilā" (*Ūrmilā-nidrā*), in which Ūrmilā sleeps through the fourteen years of exile in Lakṣmaṇa's place, belongs to later regional tellings. It is not in Vālmīki or the Bāla Kāṇḍa. [folk tradition]
- **Wedding rites** - Vasiṣṭha, with Viśvāmitra and Śatānanda, builds the altar and conducts the fire-weddings; the gods shower flowers and drums sound in heaven (Vālmīki, Bāla 73.20–28, 73.36–40).

## The Paraśurāma Encounter (sargas 74–76)

- **Omens and the wrathful sage** - As the wedding party returns to Ayodhyā, dire omens precede the arrival of **Paraśurāma (Rāma Jāmadagnya)**, the Bhārgava brahmin-warrior and previous (6th) [avatāra of Viṣṇu](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md), furious that Śiva's bow has been broken (Vālmīki, Bāla 74).
- **The challenge of the second bow** - Paraśurāma produces the great **bow of Viṣṇu**, twin to Śiva's bow, both "well made by Viśvakarmā" (75.11). He calls Śiva's bow the one that destroyed Tripura (75.12). Daśaratha begs him to spare his young sons and reminds him of his vow to Indra to lay down arms. Paraśurāma ignores the king and dares Rāma to string the bow and fight (Vālmīki, Bāla 75.1–10, 75.26–27). The Bāla Kāṇḍa never calls it *Śārṅga*; that name is the later standard usage read back onto this scene.
- **The bow's provenance: second account** - Paraśurāma gives a *different* account of how Śiva's bow reached Mithilā from the one Janaka gave. Brahmā, asked by the gods which of the two gods was the stronger, set Śiva and Viṣṇu against each other. Viṣṇu's *huṃkāra* slackened (*jṛmbhitam*) Śiva's bow and stunned him. The **indignant Rudra himself handed the bow, with its arrow, to Devarāta**, rather than the gods depositing a gift gladly given (Vālmīki, Bāla 75.14–21; CE 1.74.17). The bow of Viṣṇu, meanwhile, passed from Viṣṇu to the Bhārgava **Ṛcīka**, then to his son **Jamadagni**, and so to Paraśurāma after **Kārtavīrya Arjuna** killed his father, who had laid down arms (Vālmīki, Bāla 75.21–24). The two tellings of Śiva's bow sit unreconciled in the same kāṇḍa. [scholarly: an internal doublet the text does not resolve]
- **The transfer of power** - Rāma effortlessly strings the Vaiṣṇava bow and fits an arrow; since a Viṣṇu-arrow cannot be loosed in vain, he asks where to send it. Paraśurāma, his vigour gone, recognizes Rāma as Viṣṇu, "the slayer of Madhu". Having given the earth to Kaśyapa, he no longer spends the night on it, so he asks to keep his power of movement and to lose instead the **worlds he has won by tapas**. Rāma's arrow destroys those worlds, and Paraśurāma withdraws to Mount Mahendra, his kṣatriya-slaying mission ended and the avatāra-torch passed to Rāma (Vālmīki, Bāla 76.5–24; Kaśyapa at 76.14).
  - *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas):* Paraśurāma arrives **at Janakpur itself**, in the assembly just after the bow breaks and Sītā garlands Rāma, not on the road home (Bāla, after dohā 267). A long, famous comic-dramatic **Lakṣmaṇa–Paraśurāma war of words** dominates the scene; Rāma stays calm while Lakṣmaṇa mocks the axe-wielder. [late text]
  - *Variant (theological):* some traditions read this as one Viṣṇu-portion ceding to a fuller one; others as Paraśurāma (a *cira-jīvī*, immortal) merely yielding temporarily, since he reappears in the Mahābhārata era.

## Return to Ayodhyā (sarga 77)

- **The bow banked with Varuṇa** - As soon as Paraśurāma has gone, still on the road and before the party moves on to Ayodhyā, Rāma **gives the bow of Viṣṇu into the hand of Varuṇa** (Vālmīki, Bāla 77.1; CE 1.76.1). He carries it for a single scene. In the Daṇḍaka forest Agastya later presents Rāma with "a great divine bow of Viṣṇu, made by Viśvakarmā" (Araṇya 12.32). The Araṇya text does not say how Agastya came by it, so reading that gift as a **return** of the Varuṇa deposit is an inference, not something either passage states. [interpretive]
- **Bharata and Śatrughna leave for Kekaya** - Yudhājit asks for his nephew; with the leave of Daśaratha, Rāma and the queens, **Bharata departs for Kekaya with Śatrughna** — the absence on which the whole Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa turns (Vālmīki, Bāla 77).
- **Rāma in his father's service** - Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa take up the king's business and the people's welfare, devoted to their new wives, as the kāṇḍa closes — setting the stage for the [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](2-ayodhya-kanda.md) (Vālmīki, Bāla 77).
