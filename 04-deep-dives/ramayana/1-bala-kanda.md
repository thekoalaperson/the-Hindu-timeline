# Bāla Kāṇḍa — Book of Childhood

> Cosmic address: [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) ← but the Rāmāyaṇa itself unfolds in the [Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current Vaivasvata Manvantara. Rāma is the 7th [Daśāvatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) of [Viṣṇu](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md), of the [Sūryavaṃśa / Ikṣvāku line](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
> Parent: [Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive index](README.md) · Part of [04-deep-dives](../README.md). Primary source: Vālmīki [Rāmāyaṇa](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md).

The **Bāla Kāṇḍa** ("Book of Childhood/Youth") is the first of the seven kāṇḍas of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa — **77 sargas** in the vulgate / Southern recension, **76** in the Baroda Critical Edition; every sarga number cited on this page is **vulgate**, the convention this corpus uses throughout. It frames the entire epic, narrates Rāma's birth and boyhood, his tutelage under Viśvāmitra, the slaying of his first demons, the redemption of Ahalyā, the breaking of Śiva's bow, the four-fold marriage at Mithilā, and the confrontation with Paraśurāma. Its embedded legends have **two different narrators**: Viśvāmitra tells the tīrtha-origin cycle on the road (sargas 23–25, 31–49), while Viśvāmitra's own long autobiography (51–65) is told at Mithilā by **Śatānanda**, Janaka's house-priest and Ahalyā's son.

## The Frame Story (sargas 1–4)

- **Nārada's epitome** - The sage Vālmīki asks Nārada who in the world is the perfectly virtuous man; Nārada answers "Rāma of the Ikṣvākus" and recites a compressed sixteen-quality summary of the whole story (the *Saṃkṣipta Rāmāyaṇa*) (Vālmīki, Bāla 1).
- **The first śloka** - At the Tamasā river Vālmīki sees a hunter shoot one of a pair of mating krauñca (curlew) birds; his grief (*śoka*) bursts out as metrical verse (*śloka*) — the birth of the anuṣṭubh meter and of poetry itself (Vālmīki, Bāla 2).
  - *On its form:* the utterance is cast as an imprecation — *mā niṣāda pratiṣṭhāṃ tvam agamaḥ śāśvatīḥ samāḥ*, "Niṣāda, may you never attain standing through the endless years" — so commentators have long debated whether the first verse of Sanskrit poetry is formally a **śāpa** (curse). The text never applies the word, and some commentators reject the reading precisely on the ground that an epic must not open with a curse. [disputed]
- **Brahmā's commission** - Brahmā appears and commands Vālmīki to compose Rāma's life in this new meter, granting him divine insight into every event (Vālmīki, Bāla 2).
- **Vālmīki's vision of the whole poem** - Seated on darbha grass with water sipped and mind gathered, Vālmīki is granted yogic sight of Rāma's entire life "as a myrobalan fruit in his own palm"; the sarga then runs the contents of all seven kāṇḍas as a table of contents before a line of the poem has been sung (Vālmīki, Bāla 3).
- **Lava and Kuśa** - Vālmīki teaches the completed poem to Rāma's twin sons, who later sing it before Rāma — a frame closed only in the Uttara Kāṇḍa (Vālmīki, Bāla 4).

## The Childless King and the Putrakāmeṣṭi (sargas 5–17)

- **Daśaratha of Ayodhyā** - The aged Ikṣvāku king of Kosala, ruling from Ayodhyā on the Sarayū, has three chief queens — **Kausalyā, Kaikeyī, Sumitrā** — but no son to continue the [Sūryavaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) (Vālmīki, Bāla 5–6).
- **The Sanatkumāra prophecy** - Sumantra recalls an old prophecy of **Sanatkumāra**: Daśaratha's four sons will come through the sage **Ṛśyaśṛṅga**, son of Vibhāṇḍaka (Vālmīki, Bāla 9).
- **Ṛśyaśṛṅga brought** - The tale itself follows: how the celibate boy with a deer's horn, who had never seen a woman, was lured out of the forest by courtesans to break Aṅga's drought under king Romapāda, and was married to Daśaratha's daughter/foster-daughter **Śāntā** (Vālmīki, Bāla 10–11).
- **The Aśvamedha and the guest-list** - Daśaratha first performs the year-long **Aśvamedha** (horse sacrifice) on the Sarayū's northern bank, Ṛśyaśṛṅga presiding; Vasiṣṭha sends Sumantra to summon the kings of the earth and names **Janaka of Mithilā first of all**, as the king's oldest and best-loved friend — the epic's earliest planting of the Mithilā tie (Vālmīki, Bāla 13).
- **The Putrīyā Iṣṭi** - With the horse sacrifice concluded, Ṛśyaśṛṅga conducts the **Putrakāmeṣṭi / Putrīyā Iṣṭi** ("son-desiring oblation") (Vālmīki, Bāla 14).
- **The gods' parallel petition** - Simultaneously the Devas, oppressed by **Rāvaṇa** (boon-protected against gods and demons but not against men), beg Brahmā and Viṣṇu for rescue; **Viṣṇu agrees to be born as Daśaratha's sons** to slay Rāvaṇa as a mortal (Vālmīki, Bāla 14–15).
- **The pāyasa from the fire** - A radiant being (the *Yajña-puruṣa* / Prajāpati's messenger) rises from the sacrificial fire bearing a golden vessel of divine **pāyasa** (milk-pudding) for the queens (Vālmīki, Bāla 16).
  - *Distribution (Vālmīki):* Daśaratha gives half to Kausalyā, half of the remainder to Sumitrā, then a portion to Kaikeyī, and the last share again to Sumitrā — hence Sumitrā bears **two** sons. The exact halving order varies slightly across recensions and retellings.
- **Brahmā's vānara commission** - Brahmā fathers **Jāmbavān** from his own yawn, then orders the gods, gandharvas and sages to beget vānara sons of matching might on apsarases, yakṣīs, nāga-women and she-bears — an army raised a full generation before Rāma will need it: **Indra→Vālin, Sūrya→Sugrīva, Bṛhaspati→Tāra, Kubera→Gandhamādana, Viśvakarman→Nala, Agni→Nīla, the Aśvins→Mainda and Dvivida, Varuṇa→Suṣeṇa, Parjanya→Śarabha, Vāyu→Hanumān** (Vālmīki, Bāla 17). → full father-by-father roster and the Mt. Ṛkṣavat settlement at [vānaras and ṛkṣas](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/vanaras-and-rikshas.md).

## Birth of Rāma and His Brothers (sarga 18)

- **The four princes born** - In the month of Caitra, after twelve months' gestation, under auspicious stars:
  - **Rāma** — to **Kausalyā** (eldest; bears half of Viṣṇu's essence) (Vālmīki, Bāla 18).
  - **Bharata** — to **Kaikeyī** (Vālmīki, Bāla 18).
  - **Lakṣmaṇa** and **Śatrughna** — twins to **Sumitrā** (Lakṣmaṇa bonds to Rāma, Śatrughna to Bharata) (Vālmīki, Bāla 18).
- **Astrological birth-chart** - Rāma born under Punarvasu / Cancer with exalted planets — a chart later traditions (jyotiṣa, Rāma-navamī observance) elaborate in detail.
  - *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Tulsīdās' [Rāmcaritmānas](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)):* heavily devotional framing — Rāma is the Supreme Brahman appearing four-armed to Kausalyā before assuming the infant form; the births occur "to lift Earth's burden" announced by Śiva to Pārvatī.
  - *Variant (avatāra-arithmetic, common):* the four brothers embody portions of Viṣṇu — Rāma 1/2, Bharata 1/4, Lakṣmaṇa + Śatrughna 1/4 between them; some texts equate Lakṣmaṇa with **Śeṣa/Ananta** and the cakra/śaṅkha with the other brothers.

## Viśvāmitra Claims Rāma; the Road Out (sargas 18–23)

- **The sage's demand** - **Viśvāmitra** arrives at court demanding the boy Rāma — for ten nights, alone — to guard his sacrifice from the rākṣasas Mārīca and Subāhu (Vālmīki, Bāla 18–19).
- **Rāma not yet sixteen** - Daśaratha's refusal turns on the boy's age: *ūna-ṣoḍaśa-varṣo me rāmo rājīva-locanaḥ* — "my lotus-eyed Rāma is less than sixteen years old" — and he offers himself, his army, anything but Rāma (Vālmīki, Bāla 20.2).
  - *Note (the age crux):* this is one of only three plain age-statements in the epic, and they do not reconcile — Rāma is under sixteen here and marries within the same kāṇḍa; Sītā says she lived twelve years in Rāma's house before the exile (Sundara 33.17); and Araṇya 47.10–11 gives Rāma twenty-five and Sītā eighteen at the exile's start. The corpus records all three rather than harmonizing them. [scholarly]
- **Vasiṣṭha's counsel and the astra genealogy** - Vasiṣṭha persuades Daśaratha that Viśvāmitra is invincible and Rāma safe with him, and in doing so gives the Rāmāyaṇa's own account of where divine missiles come from: the astras are the **sons of Kṛśāśva Prajāpati** — fifty born of his wife **Jayā** and fifty of **Suprabhā** — given to Viśvāmitra long before, in his kingly days. The verse is passive and **names no giver**; the commentaries supply Śiva, and this corpus follows them while keeping the parentage and the donor apart, the one being text and the other inference (Vālmīki, Bāla 21.13–17). [scholarly] → canonical home, with the two rosters of sarga 27–28 and what the epic's personified astras imply: [The sons of Kṛśāśva — the astra-genealogy](../krishashva-astra-genealogy.md).
- **Departure with Lakṣmaṇa** - Rāma and the inseparable Lakṣmaṇa set out downstream along the Sarayū, halting the first night on its southern bank a yojana and a half from the city, and reaching the **Sarayū–Gaṅgā confluence** next (Vālmīki, Bāla 22–23).
- **Balā and Atibalā** - Viśvāmitra grants the two boys the mantras **Balā** and **Atibalā**, banishing hunger, thirst, fatigue, and disease (Vālmīki, Bāla 22).
- **Origin of Kāmadeva's ashram (Kāma's burning)** - At the confluence hermitage Viśvāmitra tells the first of his road-legends: here **Kāma** (Madana) loosed his shafts at Śiva returning from his wedding and was burnt to ash by the third eye; hence Kāma is *Ananga*, "the bodiless," and the country where his body fell is **Aṅga** (Vālmīki, Bāla 23).

## Tāṭakā, the Astras, Subāhu (sargas 24–30)

- **Malada and Karūṣa** - Crossing the Gaṅgā into a wasted country, Viśvāmitra explains its names: after slaying Vṛtra, **Indra** was fouled by brahmahatyā, and when the gods bathed him here his filth (*mala*) and hunger (*karuṣa*) left his body and entered the ground — so he blessed the two provinces **Malada** and **Karūṣa** with plenty. They have been laid waste since by the yakṣiṇī who now holds them (Vālmīki, Bāla 24).
- **The Tāṭakā forest** - Viśvāmitra tells of the **yakṣiṇī Tāṭakā** — daughter of the yakṣa Suketu, once beautiful, wife of Sunda, cursed (with her son Mārīca) to demon-hood by the sage Agastya after her family attacked him (Vālmīki, Bāla 24–25).
- **Slaying of Tāṭakā** - Rāma scruples at killing a woman, and Viśvāmitra rules on dharma's authority that a protector of evil-doers must be slain. The kill comes in stages: **Rāma severs both her arms** with arrows as she charges; **Lakṣmaṇa cuts off her ears and the tip of her nose**; she fights on invisibly, hurling rocks by illusion, until Viśvāmitra warns that rākṣasas grow unassailable at dusk and Rāma must finish before nightfall — whereupon he strikes her through the chest and she falls dead. The gods rejoice (Vālmīki, Bāla 26.17–26).
  - *Note:* the mutilate-then-kill sequence is Vālmīki's own text, not a retelling's softening — it is the same escalation the epic will repeat on Śūrpaṇakhā, and it is what makes Rāma's first blood a ruling on dharma rather than a reflex.
- **The arsenal of divine weapons** - Pleased, Viśvāmitra confers on Rāma the Kṛśāśva missiles in two matched rosters — some fifty **astras** of striking (daṇḍa, dharma, kāla, brahmaśiras, the missiles of Śiva, Viṣṇu, Indra, Varuṇa, Agni, Vāyu and the rest) and their **saṃhāra** (recall) counterparts — which appear to Rāma as embodied beings with folded hands, pledging service at a thought (Vālmīki, Bāla 27–28).
- **Siddhāśrama** - They reach Viśvāmitra's hermitage, the **Siddhāśrama** (anciently Viṣṇu's Vāmana penance-ground); Viśvāmitra begins his six-day sacrifice (Vālmīki, Bāla 29).
- **Mārīca hurled, Subāhu slain** - As demons assault the rite, Rāma uses the **Mānava astra** to fling **Mārīca** a hundred yojanas into the ocean (not killing him — he returns in the Araṇya Kāṇḍa as the golden deer), and the **Āgneya astra** to kill **Subāhu**; the rest are scattered (Vālmīki, Bāla 30).

## Viśvāmitra's Legends on the Road (sargas 31–47)

With the sacrifice completed, Viśvāmitra leads the princes north-east toward Janaka's bow-sacrifice at Mithilā, and at each halt narrates the origin-myth of the ground they are standing on. These are full upākhyānas (sub-legends), and they are **Viśvāmitra's own telling** — the autobiography that follows at Mithilā is not.

- **Kuśa's four cities and Kuśanābha's hundred daughters** - Camped on the **Śoṇa**, Viśvāmitra answers Rāma's question about the country with his own house's history: **Kuśa**, Brahmā-born, whose four sons found Kauśāmbī, Mahodaya, Dharmāraṇya and Girivraja; **Kuśanābha**'s hundred daughters by the apsarā Ghṛtācī, bent into hunchbacks by **Vāyu** when they refuse him and straightened by their marriage to **Brahmadatta** of Kāmpilya — the etiology of **Kanyākubja** ("the maidens' hunch," Kanauj); and the line down through **Gādhi** to Viśvāmitra himself, with his sister **Satyavatī** who becomes the river **Kauśikī** (Vālmīki, Bāla 32–34). → full treatment at [Kuśanābha's hundred daughters and Brahmadatta](../../03-lineages-vamsha/kushanabha-daughters-and-brahmadatta.md); the genealogy at [Gādhi and the caru-swap](../../03-lineages-vamsha/gadhi-and-the-caru-swap.md).
- **Birth of the Gaṅgā and Pārvatī / Umā** - The lineage of Himavān, daughters Gaṅgā and Umā, and the cosmic birth of **Kārttikeya (Skanda)** from Agni and the Kṛttikās to slay Tāraka (Vālmīki, Bāla 35–37). Interrupted in the hundredth year of her union with Rudra and robbed of the son, **Umā curses the wives of the gods to bear no children and the Earth to be the wife of many** — the rule Brahmā cites back one sarga later as the reason the war-leader must be got on Gaṅgā and not on a deva's own wife (Vālmīki, Bāla 36.19–24, 37.5–6). → [Umā's double curse](../uma-double-curse.md).
- **Sons of Sagara** - King **Sagara**'s 60,000 sons (born of Sumati) and one son Asamañjas (of Keśinī); the 60,000 dig up the earth seeking the sacrificial horse, wrongly accuse the meditating sage **Kapila**, and are burnt to ash by his glance (Vālmīki, Bāla 38–40). → see broader cosmology in [cosmogony / pralaya themes](../../00-time-and-cosmology/kalpa-and-pralaya.md).
- **Aṃśumān finds the ashes** - Asamañjas's son **Aṃśumān**, sent after his uncles, is guided by Garuḍa to Kapila's ground, recovers the horse, and learns that only the heavenly river can redeem the ashes — the errand that will pass through Dilīpa to Bhagīratha (Vālmīki, Bāla 41).
- **Descent of Gaṅgā (Bhagīratha)** - **Bhagīratha**'s austerities at Gokarṇa bring Gaṅgā from heaven to redeem the ashes; Śiva breaks her fall in his hair (hence *Gaṅgādhara*); she follows his chariot to Pātāla — the **Gaṅgāvataraṇa** (Vālmīki, Bāla 42–44).
  - **Jahnu swallows the river** - Mid-descent the flood drowns the sage-king **Jahnu**'s sacrificial ground; he drinks the whole of Gaṅgā up, and releases her from his ears at the sages' plea — so she counts as his daughter and bears the name **Jāhnavī** (Vālmīki, Bāla 43.34–38). → [Jahnu](../../03-lineages-vamsha/jahnu.md).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Āraṇyaka Parva):* Agastya, not Viśvāmitra, narrates the Gaṅgā/Sagara cycle to the Pāṇḍavas; details of Sagara's wives and the horse differ slightly.
- **Churning of the Ocean (Samudra Manthana)** - At Viśālā, Viśvāmitra tells the Bāla-Kāṇḍa version of the **devas and asuras churning the Milk-Ocean** with Mandara mountain and Vāsuki, the rise of Hālāhala poison (drunk by Śiva), Dhanvantari, the apsarases, and Amṛta (Vālmīki, Bāla 45). → cross-link: [Samudra Manthana deep-dive](../samudra-manthana.md).
  - **Mohinī in Vālmīki** - When the amṛta appears and the two sides fall on each other, it is **Viṣṇu who carries the nectar off "assuming the bewitching illusion of Mohinī"** (*māyām āsthāya mohinīm*) — the Mohinī form is present in the Rāmāyaṇa, in a single half-verse (Vālmīki, Bāla 45.42). What the compact Rāmāyaṇa version omits is the *episode* the Purāṇas build around it: the serving of the two rows, the doubling-back, Rāhu's severed head, and the war that follows.
- **Diti and the Maruts** - How Indra split Diti's foetus into the forty-nine **Maruts** while she slept impurely, aiming to abort the asura who would kill him (Vālmīki, Bāla 46–47).

## The Redemption of Ahalyā (sargas 48–49)

The last stop before Mithilā, and the last of Viśvāmitra's road-legends.

- **Ahalyā's curse** - At Gautama's deserted hermitage on Mithilā's outskirts, Viśvāmitra tells how **Indra**, disguised as the sage **Gautama**, seduced his wife **Ahalyā**; Gautama cursed Indra (loss of testicles, later replaced with a ram's) and Ahalyā to invisible penance in the ashes of the hermitage, living on air, to be released only by Rāma's coming (Vālmīki, Bāla 48).
  - *In Vālmīki:* Ahalyā **knowingly** recognizes Indra and yields out of curiosity at the king of the gods' desire; she is cursed to invisible ascetic penance, **not turned to stone**.
  - *Variant (Purāṇic / Adhyātma / Tulsīdās & most popular tradition):* Ahalyā is **innocent** (deceived) and is turned to **stone**, restored when the dust of Rāma's foot touches her — the iconic "stone-to-woman" image, and the reason the popular memory of this episode is almost the inverse of the text's. [late text]
- **Rāma redeems her** - Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa enter the hermitage and **touch Ahalyā's feet** in reverence; her form becomes visible again, Viśvāmitra and the princes receive her worship, and Gautama returns and takes her back — the first overt sign of Rāma's redemptive power, and a gesture the popular retellings reverse into her touching the dust of his (Vālmīki, Bāla 49.17). → full dossier: [Ahalyā](../characters/ahalya.md).

## Mithilā: Arrival and Śatānanda's Narration (sargas 50–65)

- **Arrival at Janaka's yajña** - They reach **Mithilā** (Videha) for King **Janaka**'s sacrifice; Janaka comes out with his priest **Śatānanda** to welcome Viśvāmitra, and asks who the two youths are (Vālmīki, Bāla 50).
- **Śatānanda takes up the story** - Śatānanda — Gautama and **Ahalyā's son** — hears that his mother has been restored, and out of gratitude turns to Rāma and narrates, at length, the life of the sage who brought him: the whole of the Viśvāmitra saga that follows is **Śatānanda's telling at Mithilā**, not Viśvāmitra's on the road (Vālmīki, Bāla 51).
- **Viśvāmitra and the wish-cow** - The kṣatriya king **Viśvāmitra (Kauśika)**, son of Gādhi, covets Vasiṣṭha's wish-cow **Śabalā/Nandinī**; the cow's body brings forth armies that destroy his own, his hundred sons are burnt by Vasiṣṭha's *brahmadaṇḍa*, and the humiliated king begins millennia of tapas to become what force cannot make him (Vālmīki, Bāla 51–56).
- **Triśaṅku's heaven** - Viśvāmitra raises the outcast king **Triśaṅku** bodily to heaven; when the gods cast him down, Viśvāmitra creates a counter-heaven (Triśaṅku's "southern" constellation) and halts him mid-sky (Vālmīki, Bāla 57–60).
- **Ambarīṣa and Śunaḥśepa** - The human-sacrifice victim **Śunaḥśepa**, bought for king **Ambarīṣa**'s rite, is saved by the mantras to Indra and Agni that Viśvāmitra — his maternal uncle — gives him (Vālmīki, Bāla 61–62).
- **Menakā and Rambhā** - The apsarases sent to break his austerity; he succumbs to **Menakā** for ten years (begetting **Śakuntalā**), then curses **Rambhā** to stone for **ten thousand** years (*daśa varṣa-sahasrāṇi*, Bāla 64; the "thousand" of most summaries drops a word) — and, finding that anger has again cost him his accumulated tapas, finally conquers both desire and anger to win the title **Brahmarṣi** from Brahmā and from Vasiṣṭha's own mouth (Vālmīki, Bāla 63–65).

## The Bow of Śiva and the Winning of Sītā (sargas 66–67)

- **Sītā's earth-birth** - Janaka relates that **Sītā** ("furrow") rose from the ground before his plough while he was consecrating the sacrificial field — *ayonijā*, not womb-born, a daughter of the Earth (Bhūmi) raised as his own (Vālmīki, Bāla 66.13–14).
  - *Variant (Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa & some Jaina versions, e.g. Saṅghadāsa's Vasudevahiṇḍī, Guṇabhadra's Uttarapurāṇa):* Sītā is the **daughter of Rāvaṇa and Mandodarī**, abandoned/buried because of a prophecy she would destroy his line, and recovered by Janaka. [variant — non-Vālmīki]
  - *Variant (Vedavatī identity):* Sītā is the rebirth of **Vedavatī**, the chaste woman who cursed Rāvaṇa; some link her to **Lakṣmī** incarnate as Rāma is Viṣṇu. [scholarly / Purāṇic]
- **The Śiva-dhanus (Pināka): first account** - Janaka gives the bow's provenance: at the destruction of **Dakṣa's sacrifice**, Śiva — denied his share of the oblation — raised this bow in fury and threatened to take the gods' heads; appeased, he gave the bow over to them gladly, and the gods deposited it with Janaka's ancestor **Devarāta** for custodial keeping. It has stood in Mithilā since, and no king has budged it (Vālmīki, Bāla 66.9–12).
- **The vīrya-śulka and the year-long siege** - Janaka vows Sītā to whoever can string the bow — *vīrya-śulka*, "the bride-price of valour," a standing trial and not a suitor-choice assembly. The kings who fail take the refusal as insult and **besiege Mithilā for a full year**, wasting its treasury, until Janaka wins an army by austerity and breaks them (Vālmīki, Bāla 66.20–26).
- **Breaking the bow** - At Viśvāmitra's bidding the eight-wheeled chest is dragged in by five thousand men; Rāma lifts the bow effortlessly, bends it to string — and it **snaps with a thunderclap** that fells the onlookers. Sītā is won (Vālmīki, Bāla 67).
  - *Variant (internal Rāmāyaṇa "contradiction"):* In the Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa Sītā recalls the bow as **Varuṇa's** bow / a bow given by Varuṇa, while Bāla Kāṇḍa names it Śiva's Pināka — a noted textual discrepancy. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās' Rāmcaritmānas):* expands a full **dhanuṣa-yajña / svayaṃvara** scene — many kings fail, Lakṣmaṇa's fiery taunts, Rāma's effortless feat; the most popular North-Indian telling. [late text]
  - *Variant (folk, on Sītā's strength):* As a child Sītā once lifted the bow's box while sweeping, proving she alone "matched" it. [folk tradition]

## The Four-Fold Marriage (sargas 68–73)

- **Daśaratha summoned** - Janaka's messengers ride three days to Ayodhyā; Daśaratha arrives with Vasiṣṭha, Vāmadeva and his ministers (Vālmīki, Bāla 68–69).
- **The Ikṣvāku genealogy recited** - Vasiṣṭha answers with the Solar line from **Brahmā → Marīci → Kaśyapa → Vivasvat → Manu → Ikṣvāku** and down through some thirty-four kings to Daśaratha. **This roster is the Rāmāyaṇa's own and diverges sharply from the Purāṇic Solar list**: **Kakutstha** appears *after* Bhagīratha, not near the head of the line; **Raghu** is Kakutstha's son; **Kalmāṣapāda** (here **Pravṛddha**) is Raghu's son; **Nahuṣa and Yayāti** — Lunar kings everywhere else — stand inside the Ikṣvāku succession; and **Hariścandra is absent altogether** (Vālmīki, Bāla 70). → compare the Purāṇic spine at [Sūrya- & Candra-vaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md). [scholarly: the two lists are not harmonizable and the corpus keeps both]
- **The Videha genealogy and the Sudhanvan war** - Janaka answers with the Nimi/Videha line, and in the middle of it names a war the retellings drop: **Sudhanvan** of Sāṃkāśya demanded both the bow and Sītā, besieged Mithilā, and was killed by Janaka — who then installed his own younger brother **Kuśadhvaja** on the conquered throne of Sāṃkāśya (Vālmīki, Bāla 71). → [the Videha/Mithilā line of Janaka](../../03-lineages-vamsha/videha-mithila-line-janaka.md).
- **Kuśadhvaja's daughters** - Kuśadhvaja is fetched from Sāṃkāśya and brings his two daughters, so that all four princes may marry on one day (Vālmīki, Bāla 72).
- **Yudhājit arrives** - **Yudhājit**, Kaikeyī's brother and Bharata's maternal uncle, reaches Ayodhyā from Kekaya, learns the court has gone to Mithilā, and follows — the arrival that will end with Bharata out of the capital at the worst possible moment (Vālmīki, Bāla 73).
- **The four couples** -
  - **Rāma — Sītā** (Janaka's daughter) (Vālmīki, Bāla 73).
  - **Lakṣmaṇa — Ūrmilā** (Janaka's second daughter) (Vālmīki, Bāla 73).
  - **Bharata — Māṇḍavī** (Kuśadhvaja's daughter) (Vālmīki, Bāla 73).
  - **Śatrughna — Śrutakīrti** (Kuśadhvaja's daughter) (Vālmīki, Bāla 73).
  - *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas / popular):* the pairing is foregrounded as a deliberate matching (Daśaratha noting Lakṣmaṇa's love for Ūrmilā); some folk versions add **Ūrmilā's "sleep" (Ūrmilā-nidrā)** boon foreshadowed here. [folk tradition]
- **Wedding rites** - Vasiṣṭha and Śatānanda conduct the fire-weddings; the gods shower flowers and the gandharvas sing (Vālmīki, Bāla 73).

## The Paraśurāma Encounter (sargas 74–76)

- **Omens and the wrathful sage** - As the wedding party returns to Ayodhyā, dire omens precede the arrival of **Paraśurāma (Rāma Jāmadagnya)**, the Bhārgava brahmin-warrior and previous (6th) [avatāra of Viṣṇu](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md), furious that Śiva's bow has been broken (Vālmīki, Bāla 74).
- **The challenge of the second bow** - Paraśurāma produces the great **bow of Viṣṇu** — twin to Śiva's Pināka, both forged by Viśvakarmā — and dares Rāma to string it and fight (Vālmīki, Bāla 75). The Bāla Kāṇḍa never calls it *Śārṅga*; that name is the later standard usage read back onto this scene.
- **The bow's provenance: second account** - Paraśurāma also gives a *different* account of how Śiva's bow reached Mithilā from the one Janaka gave. Brahmā, asked by the gods which of the two was the stronger, set Śiva and Viṣṇu at each other; Viṣṇu's *huṃkāra* slackened Śiva's bow and stunned him, and the **indignant Rudra himself handed the bow to Devarāta** — not the gods depositing a gift gladly given (Vālmīki, Bāla 75). The two tellings sit unreconciled in the same kāṇḍa. [scholarly: an internal doublet the text does not resolve]
- **The transfer of power** - Rāma effortlessly strings the Vaiṣṇava bow and fits an arrow; since a Viṣṇu-arrow cannot be loosed in vain, he asks where to send it. Paraśurāma realizes Rāma is Viṣṇu himself, offers his own accumulated **tapas/heavenly worlds** as the target, and withdraws to Mahendra mountain — his kṣatriya-slaying mission ended, the avatāra-torch passed to Rāma (Vālmīki, Bāla 75–76).
  - *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas):* a long, famous comic-dramatic **Lakṣmaṇa–Paraśurāma war of words** dominates the scene; Rāma stays calm while Lakṣmaṇa mocks the axe-wielder. [late text]
  - *Variant (theological):* some traditions read this as one Viṣṇu-portion ceding to a fuller one; others as Paraśurāma (a *cira-jīvī*, immortal) merely yielding temporarily, since he reappears in the Mahābhārata era.

## Return to Ayodhyā (sarga 77)

- **The bow banked with Varuṇa** - Reaching Ayodhyā, Rāma **gives the bow of Viṣṇu into the hand of Varuṇa** — he carries it for a single scene and never again. This is the detail that makes Agastya's presentation of "Viṣṇu's bow" in the Daṇḍaka forest (Araṇya 12) a **return** of the deposit rather than a first bestowal (Vālmīki, Bāla 77).
- **Bharata and Śatrughna leave for Kekaya** - Yudhājit asks for his nephew; with the leave of Daśaratha, Rāma and the queens, **Bharata departs for Kekaya with Śatrughna** — the absence on which the whole Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa turns (Vālmīki, Bāla 77).
- **Rāma in his father's service** - Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa take up the king's business and the people's welfare, devoted to their new wives, as the kāṇḍa closes — setting the stage for the [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](2-ayodhya-kanda.md) (Vālmīki, Bāla 77).
