# The Rāmāyaṇa — Overview & Recensions

> Cosmic address: [Brahma's current life](../../README.md) › [Vaivasvata Manvantara](../../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md) › [28th Mahāyuga](../../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md) › **[Tretā Yuga](../../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** — the events of the Rāmāyaṇa are traditionally set near the close of the Tretā Yuga of the current Mahāyuga.

The **Rāmāyaṇa** ("the journey/career of Rāma") is the *ādi-kāvya* — the "first poem" of classical Sanskrit literature — narrating the life of **Rāma**, prince of Ayodhyā, 7th avatāra of [Viṣṇu](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md). It is one of the two great Itihāsas (alongside the [Mahābhārata](../mahabharata/README.md)), and the most widely retold story-cycle in Asia — by tradition existing in **"three hundred Rāmāyaṇas"** (A. K. Ramanujan's phrase) across languages, religions, and cultures.

---

## The Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa — the base text

- **Authorship & tradition** - Ascribed to the sage **Vālmīki**, called the *ādi-kavi* ("first poet"). By tradition the *śloka* meter itself was born from his grief-curse (*śoka → śloka*) on seeing a hunter kill a mating krauñca bird; Brahmā then commissioned him to compose Rāma's story (Bāla Kāṇḍa 2). Tradition makes Vālmīki a **contemporary of Rāma** who shelters the exiled Sītā and teaches the epic to her twin sons Lava and Kuśa (Uttara Kāṇḍa).
- **Size** - ~**24,000 verses (ślokas)** in **7 books (kāṇḍas)**, ~480–500 *sargas* (chapters); traditionally said to total 24,000 to mirror the 24 syllables of the [Gāyatrī mantra](../../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md) (one syllable per 1,000 verses) [tradition].
- **Meter** - Predominantly *anuṣṭubh* (32-syllable śloka), with elaborate *upajāti/triṣṭubh* passages at sarga-ends.

### The 7 Kāṇḍas (deep-dive files)

- **[Bāla Kāṇḍa](1-bala-kanda.md)** - "Book of Childhood": Rāma's birth via the *putra-kāmeṣṭi* sacrifice, the boy-sages' protection of Viśvāmitra's yajña, the breaking of Śiva's bow, marriage to Sītā.
- **[Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](2-ayodhya-kanda.md)** - "Book of Ayodhyā": Kaikeyī's two boons, Daśaratha's death, Rāma's 14-year exile, Bharata's regency with Rāma's sandals.
- **[Araṇya Kāṇḍa](3-aranya-kanda.md)** - "Book of the Forest": Daṇḍaka forest, Śūrpaṇakhā, the golden deer Mārīca, and Rāvaṇa's abduction of Sītā.
- **[Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa](4-kishkindha-kanda.md)** - "Book of Kiṣkindhā": alliance with Sugrīva, slaying of Vālī, the vānara search for Sītā.
- **[Sundara Kāṇḍa](5-sundara-kanda.md)** - "Beautiful Book": Hanumān's leap to Laṅkā, finding Sītā in the Aśoka grove, burning of Laṅkā. (Recited as an auspicious self-contained unit.)
- **[Yuddha Kāṇḍa](6-yuddha-kanda.md)** - "Book of War" (a.k.a. Laṅkā Kāṇḍa): bridge to Laṅkā, the great war, slaying of Rāvaṇa, Sītā's *agni-parīkṣā*, coronation (*paṭṭābhiṣeka*).
- **[Uttara Kāṇḍa](7-uttara-kanda.md)** - "Final Book": Rāvaṇa's back-story, Sītā's banishment, birth of Lava-Kuśa, Sītā's return to the earth, Rāma's departure. *Widely regarded by scholars as a later addition* [scholarly].

> Note: the **Bāla** and **Uttara** kāṇḍas are considered by most academics to be **later interpolations** framing an older core (kāṇḍas 2–6) — it is chiefly in these two that Rāma's full divinity as Viṣṇu is made explicit [scholarly].

### Recensions of the Vālmīki text itself

The Sanskrit text survives in **thousands of manuscripts** (oldest ~11th c. CE) grouped into regional *pāṭhas*. The Baroda **Critical Edition** (Oriental Institute, 1960–1975, gen. eds. G. H. Bhatt & U. P. Shah) collated these.

- **Northern recension** - subdivided into the **Vāyavya/north-western** and **Gauḍīya (Bengali)** pāṭhas.
- **Southern recension (Dākṣiṇātya)** - generally regarded as more conservative/closer to the archetype; the basis of most popular printed editions.

## Dating — tradition vs. scholarship

- *Traditional (scriptural):* set in the **Tretā Yuga** of the current 28th Mahāyuga — i.e., hundreds of thousands to millions of years ago by the [yuga framework](../../00-time-and-cosmology/yugas-and-mahayuga.md); Vālmīki composes it contemporaneously with Rāma. (Dharma stands on **3 legs** in Tretā — see [Tretā Yuga](../../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md).)
- *Astronomical "scientific dating"* [disputed] - planetarium-software readings of verse sky-descriptions place Rāma's birth at **~5114 BCE** (Nilesh Oak, Pushkar Bhatnagar, et al.). Treated as fringe by mainstream philology.
- *Scholarly (philological):* the oral core composed **c. 750–500 BCE** (R. P. Goldman: archetype "unlikely much earlier than the beginning of the 7th c. BCE"); Brockington's layered model puts the first stage **5th–4th c. BCE**, with the text reaching roughly its present form (including Bāla & Uttara kāṇḍas) by **c. 3rd c. CE**.

## Catalog of recensions & retellings (variant tradition)

### Sanskrit retellings

- **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** - Embedded in the *Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa*, ascribed to Vyāsa; ~4,200 verses, 7 kāṇḍas. Recasts the story as **Advaita-Vedānta allegory**: Rāma is the supreme Brahman, the "real" Sītā is hidden with Agni and only a *māyā-Sītā* (shadow) is abducted. ~14th–15th c. A primary source for Tulsīdās. [late text]
- **Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa** - Attributed to Vālmīki [pseudepigraphic]; ~15th c. Focuses on Rāma's **later reign and miscellaneous/peripheral legends** (e.g., Rāvaṇa's earlier conquests, ritual lore); popular for vrata stories. [late text]
- **Yoga Vāsiṣṭha (Vāsiṣṭha Rāmāyaṇa / Mahā-Rāmāyaṇa)** - Attributed to Vālmīki; a vast (~29,000-verse) **philosophical dialogue** in which sage Vasiṣṭha instructs young Rāma in Advaita and the illusory nature of the world — narrative is a frame for [Vedānta](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md). [late text]
- **Agastya Rāmāyaṇa** & **Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa** - Supplementary Sanskrit traditions; the *Adbhuta* foregrounds a fierce **Śākta/Devī** form of Sītā who slays a thousand-headed Rāvaṇa. [late text] [Shakta variant]

### Regional Indian retellings

- **Iramāvatāram (Kamba-rāmāyaṇam)** - **Tamil**, by **Kamban**, c. 12th c.; ~10,000+ verses, 6 kāṇḍas (omits Uttara). A literary masterwork; elevates Rāma's divinity and reworks episodes (e.g., a more sympathetic Rāvaṇa). [scholarly]
- **Rāmacaritamānasa** - **Awadhi** (Hindi belt), by **Tulsīdās**, 1574 CE; 7 kāṇḍas in *caupāī-dohā*. The single most influential North-Indian *bhakti* retelling; draws on the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa — Rāma is wholly God; basis of the *Rāmlīlā* pageants. See [smṛti — epics](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md).
- **Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ (Śrīrām Pāñcālī)** - **Bengali**, by **Kṛttibās Ojhā**, 15th c.; the most beloved Bengali version, rich in folk and *bhakti* coloring; introduces episodes like Rāma's autumn (*akāl-bodhan*) worship of Durgā before battle. [folk tradition]
- **Raṅganātha Rāmāyaṇamu** - **Telugu**, by **Gona Buddhā Reddy**, c. 1300–1310 CE; first complete Telugu Rāmāyaṇa, in *dvipada* meter; includes distinctive folk episodes.
- **Adhyātma-rāmāyaṇam Kiḷippāṭṭu** - **Malayalam**, by **Thunchaththu Ezhuthachan**, 16th c.; a "parrot-song" rendering of the Sanskrit Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa; foundational to Malayalam literature.
- **Saptakāṇḍa Rāmāyaṇa** - **Assamese**, by **Mādhava Kandalī**, 14th c.; among the earliest complete vernacular Rāmāyaṇas in a modern Indian language.
- **Bhāvārtha Rāmāyaṇa** (**Marathi**, Ekanāth, 16th c.), **Torave Rāmāyaṇa** (**Kannada**, Kumāra Vālmīki/Narahari, 16th c.), and **Giridhar Rāmāyaṇa** (**Gujarati**) — further major regional renderings.

### Jain retellings (Rāma as *śalākāpuruṣa*, all characters Jains)

- **Paümacariya(ṃ)** - **Prākrit (Jain Mahārāṣṭrī)**, by **Vimalasūri**, often dated c. 3rd–5th c. CE (Jains claim earlier); the oldest Jain Rāma-narrative. Rationalizes the epic: **Rāvaṇa is a noble, learned Vidyādhara (not a demon)**, vānaras are a human clan with monkey-banners, and **Lakṣmaṇa (not Rāma) kills Rāvaṇa** — so Rāma, the non-violent hero, attains liberation. [Jain variant]
- **Padma-purāṇa (Rāviṣeṇa, 7th c.)** & **Uttara-purāṇa (Guṇabhadra)** - Sanskrit Jain recastings continuing this tradition.
- **Kumudendu Rāmāyaṇa** - **Kannada**, Jain, by **Kumudendu Muni**, 13th c. [Jain variant]

### Buddhist retelling

- **Dasaratha Jātaka** (No. 461) - **Pāli**, in the Jātaka collection; Rāma (Rāma-paṇḍita) is a **previous birth of the Buddha (bodhisatta)**. No Rāvaṇa or war: Rāma is exiled to avoid a stepmother's plot, returns after 12(?) years, rules righteously. In several versions **Sītā is Rāma's sister/half-sister** (later his queen), a notable inversion. [Buddhist variant]

### Southeast Asian retellings

- **Rāmakīen** - **Thai** national epic; Rāma = **Phra Ram**, Hanumān is a prominent amorous warrior; Rāvaṇa = **Tosakanth**. Basis of *khon* masked dance; the Thai kings take the name "Rama." [folk tradition]
- **Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa** - **Old Javanese**, c. 9th c. CE; oldest and one of the finest Southeast Asian versions, modeled partly on the Sanskrit *Bhaṭṭikāvya* rather than Vālmīki directly. [scholarly]
- **Reamker** - **Khmer (Cambodian)**; foregrounds Hanumān and the heroine Neang Seda; central to Khmer dance-drama.
- **Phra Lak Phra Lam** (**Lao**), **Yama Zatdaw** (**Burmese**), **Maharadia Lawana / Darangen** (**Maranao, Philippines**), and **Hikayat Seri Rama** (**Malay**) — further national variants. Several recast Rāma within local Buddhist or Islamic-influenced frames. [folk tradition]

## Why so many variants

- *Religious recasting:* Vaiṣṇava *bhakti* (Tulsīdās, Adhyātma) makes Rāma fully divine; Śākta (Adbhuta) centers Devī/Sītā; Jain & Buddhist versions **demythologize** and reassign the killing of Rāvaṇa or even invert Sītā's relationship to Rāma.
- *The "core vs. frame" question:* the explicit Viṣṇu-avatāra theology lives mainly in the (later) **Bāla** and **Uttara** kāṇḍas; the older core presents Rāma more as an ideal human king (*maryādā-puruṣottama*) [scholarly].
- *Telling-as-worship:* recitation of the **Sundara Kāṇḍa** or the whole *Rāmacaritamānasa* is itself a devotional act; this drove endless regional re-composition.
