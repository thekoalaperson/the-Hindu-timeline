# The Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa & Variant Rāmāyaṇas — Vedāntic and Regional Retellings

> Cosmic address: [Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga — the *story* is Tretā-Yuga scripture-time; almost all of these *texts* are **Kali-Yuga** (post-3102 BCE) re-tellings, mostly 3rd c. CE → 17th c. CE. Two clocks: scripture-time vs. authorial-time.

This file is the **comparative deep-dive node** that sits *parallel* to the Vālmīki kāṇḍa set ([Bāla](./1-bala-kanda.md) → [Uttara](./7-uttara-kanda.md)): where those trace the base narrative book-by-book, this one traces how the **same story-skeleton is theologically re-coded** — from Vālmīki's *maryādā-puruṣottama* (ideal human king) into the supreme Brahman (Adhyātma, Tulsīdās), the Devī/Śakti (Adbhuta), a Jain *śalākāpuruṣa*, or a previous birth of the Buddha. It builds directly on the recension notes in the [Rāmāyaṇa overview](./README.md). The governing principle is A. K. Ramanujan's "**Three Hundred Rāmāyaṇas**": there is no single Rāmāyaṇa, only a field of *tellings* (Skt. *kathā*), each a complete world. **Catalog-all-variants** policy applies throughout.

*Citation keys used below:* **AR** = Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, kāṇḍa.sarga.verse as numbered in the sanskritdocuments.org edition (other prints number differently); **VR CE** = Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Baroda Critical Edition (GRETIL); **MBh** = Mahābhārata, BORI Critical Edition (GRETIL); **KūrmaP** = Kūrma Purāṇa (GRETIL numbering); **RCM** = Rāmcaritmānas, kāṇḍa + dohā. ## 1. The Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa — the Advaita-Vedānta keystone

*(Full treatment: [recensions/adhyatma-ramayana.md](./recensions/adhyatma-ramayana.md). Summary + the two doctrines that radiate outward to the bhakti tradition.)*

- **Text & embedding** - The **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** ("Spiritual / Self-knowledge Rāmāyaṇa") is a Sanskrit re-telling embedded in the latter (*uttara-bhāga*) portion of the [Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa](../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/brahmanda-purana.md), traditionally ascribed to **Veda-Vyāsa**. It has **7 kāṇḍas and 65 sargas** (Bāla 7, Ayodhyā 9, Araṇya 10, Kiṣkindhā 9, Sundara 5, Yuddha 16, Uttara 9). The sanskritdocuments.org edition has about **4,250 verses**; other counts run from ~4,200 to ~4,500. It re-reads the whole epic as **Advaita-Vedānta allegory wrapped in intense Rāma-bhakti**. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa)
  - *Dating variant [scholarly] [late text]:* **13th–15th c. CE** (most often **14th–15th c.**) — firmly Kali-Yuga authorial-time.
  - *Attribution variant [disputed]:* some tradition credits the saint **Rāmānanda** / his Rāmānandī circle, consistent with its saguṇa-Rāma devotionalism.
- **Outer frame — Śiva → Pārvatī** - Told as an *Umā-Maheśvara saṃvāda*: Śiva, supreme *jñānin* and arch-devotee of Rāma, reveals the **secret inner meaning** of Rāma's life to Pārvatī. This replaces Vālmīki's frame, in which Nārada tells the story to Vālmīki and Lava and Kuśa recite it. Pārvatī asks the question the whole text answers: if Rāma is the Supreme and knows himself, why did he weep for Sītā; and if he does not know himself, how is he different from any other soul? (AR 1.1.12–15)
- **Core doctrine 1 — Rāma *is* Brahman** - Rāma is **Parabrahman, sat-cit-ānanda, the One-without-a-second**; his grief, exile, and search for Sītā are *līlā* (play) staged for the world's instruction, never real limitation. Sītā is his **Māyā-śakti / Prakṛti** — the power by which the formless One appears as the world. In her own words, "know me as *mūla-prakṛti*", the cause of creation, preservation and dissolution (AR 1.1.34). This is the metaphysical floor under all later Rāma-as-God bhakti. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, passim)
- **Core doctrine 2 — the Māyā-Sītā / Chāyā-Sītā ("shadow Sītā")** - Rāma knows of Rāvaṇa's plan. **Before the golden-deer episode** he tells Sītā privately that Rāvaṇa will come disguised as a mendicant. She is to leave a *chāyā* (shadow) of her own form in the hut, and stay **invisible inside the fire for a year**, until she regains him after Rāvaṇa's death. She does so, and it is the **māyā-Sītā** who sees the golden deer and asks for it (AR 3.7.1–6). Lakṣmaṇa is not told (AR 3.8.3). Rāma then grieves on purpose, "like an ordinary man", because a grieving husband's search is what brings him to Laṅkā (AR 3.8.3–6). So **the real Sītā is never touched by Rāvaṇa**. At the [agni-parīkṣā](./6-yuddha-kanda.md) Rāma speaks harshly to a Sītā he knows to be the māyā-form "made for a purpose", and she enters the fire. The lines she speaks there are almost word for word Vālmīki's own (AR 6.12.67–83; cf. VR CE 6.104.23–25). Agni then hands back the **true Sītā** "whom you placed in me in the forest". The reflection-form (*pratibimba-rūpiṇī*) has done its work and vanishes (AR 6.13.19–22). An apparent test of chastity becomes a reunion. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya & Yuddha kāṇḍas)
  - *Older attestation — Kūrma Purāṇa (a distinct variant):* here the initiative is Sītā's, not Rāma's. When Rāvaṇa approaches disguised as an ascetic, she takes refuge in the household fire (*āvasathya*) and recites a hymn to Agni (the *vahny-aṣṭaka*). Agni creates a *māyāmayī* Sītā for Rāvaṇa to carry off and takes the real one away. He later says he kept her with Pārvatī (Bhavānī). After the war the māyā-Sītā enters the fire "to convince the world" and is burnt, and Agni gives the true Sītā back to a startled Rāma (KūrmaP 2.33.112–140, GRETIL numbering; some editions number it 2.34). The Purāṇa tells it as an example of a *pativratā*'s power, inside a section on expiation. The Kūrma is generally dated well before the Adhyātma, which makes it the earliest known Sītā-substitution story [scholarly]. Whether the Adhyātma drew on it directly is not established here.
  - *Seed in Vālmīki:* the epic already has an **illusory Sītā**, but as a battlefield trick. Indrajit puts a *māyāmayī* Sītā in his chariot and cuts her down in front of Hanumān to demoralize the army (VR CE 6.68.5, 6.68.28). The real Sītā is abducted in Vālmīki, and no substitution happens there.
  - *Reach of the motif:* Tulsīdās takes it over (see §2). Later Purāṇic tellings give the shadow Sītā a life story of her own. The Devī-Bhāgavata and Brahmavaivarta accounts reportedly identify her with **Vedavatī** before and **Draupadī** after (Wikipedia, *Maya Sita*, citing Doniger and the Vijñānānanda translation). These two accounts were not checked against the Purāṇas here. [late text] [unverified here]
- **The inset *Rāma-Hṛdaya*** - This is the **opening sarga** itself, which its colophon calls *śrī-rāma-hṛdayaṃ nāma prathamaḥ sargaḥ*. It is set **after the coronation**. Rāma asks Sītā to teach the truth to Hanumān, who has done his work and wants only knowledge. **Sītā speaks first.** She says Rāma is *paraṃ brahma*, the One without a second, and she is the root-nature. Every deed told of him is really hers, superimposed on the changeless Self, from the birth through the "abduction of the māyā-Sītā" to the coronation (AR 1.1.25–43). **Then Rāma himself** teaches Hanumān the three forms of consciousness. Like space in a pot, space as a whole, and space reflected in water, they are the delimited, the whole, and the reflection. Knowing the identity taught by the *mahāvākya*s ends ignorance (AR 1.1.44–52). Śiva names the passage the *Rāma-Hṛdaya* and calls it the essence of all Vedānta (AR 1.1.53–56). Some scholars hold that it (with the Rāma-Gītā) began as an **independent Advaitic composition** later stitched into the frame. [scholarly]

### 1a. The Rāma-Gītā — the "lesser Gītā"

- **Location & form** - Uttara Kāṇḍa, **sarga 5 (AR 7.5.1–62)**, 62 verses. Rāma has just told Lakṣmaṇa old stories such as King Nṛga's curse. Lakṣmaṇa then asks to be taught how to cross the ocean of ignorance, and **Rāma instructs him** in Advaita (AR 7.5.1–6). In the closing verse Rāma calls it "this whole *vijñāna*, the one essence of the śruti, sung by me" (AR 7.5.62). (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa)
- **Why a "lesser Gītā"** - It is to the Rāmāyaṇa what the [Bhagavad-Gītā](../mahabharata/README.md) is to the Mahābhārata: God-incarnate teaching the highest wisdom to a beloved disciple **on the far side of the war**, not before it. In this text it comes after the coronation, and after the sarga in which Rāma and Sītā agree on her exile (AR 7.4). Its teaching is **Advaita** — *jīva = Brahman*, liberation by realizing one's true Self — with devotion to Rāma as the path that yields that knowledge (AR 7.5.61–62). Commented on by the Advaita tradition and recited as a stand-alone Vedāntic text. ([Rama Gita](http://stotrarathna.blogspot.com/2017/06/rama-gita-from-uthara-kanda-of.html); [text](https://sanskritdocuments.org/doc_giitaa/raamagitaa.html))
- **Contrast with the *other* Rāma-Gītā** - Do not confuse it with two other texts of the same name. One is the *Rāma-Gītā* of the **Guru-jñāna-vāsiṣṭha / Tattvasārāyaṇa**, a separate Advaitic dialogue between Rāma and **Hanumān**, popular mainly in South India, especially Tamil Nadu (Hindu Blog). The other is the Rāma-to-Hanumān discourse in the **Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa** (cantos 11–14), which also circulates as a *Rāma-Gītā* (sanskritdocuments.org lists it as "rāmagītā 2, adbhutarāmāyaṇāntargatam"). [disputed terminology]

### 1b. Other episodes peculiar to the Adhyātma

*(None of these turns is in the Vālmīki Critical Edition. Each one recasts a human failing as part of the divine plan.)*

- **The boatman who washes Rāma's feet** - At the Gaṅgā crossing **on the way to Mithilā**, just after Ahalyā is freed, a boatman will not take Rāma aboard until he has washed his feet. He jokes that dust which turned a stone into a woman might turn his wooden boat into one too, and then his household would be ruined (AR 1.6.2–5). In Vālmīki the river crossings have no such scene.
- **Sarasvatī enters Mantharā** - The gods send the goddess of speech (*vāṇī*) to block the coronation. She enters **Mantharā first, then Kaikeyī**, and is to return to heaven once the obstacle is in place (AR 2.2.44–46). Later Kaikeyī repents, and Rāma tells her that her words "came out of your mouth impelled by me", for the gods' work, so she bears no fault (AR 2.9.60–64).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna):* Brahmā sends a **gandharvī named Dundubhī**, who is born on earth as the hunchbacked Mantharā (MBh 3.260.9–10). See §3.
- **Rāvaṇa's chosen enmity** - Lying awake after Khara's death, Rāvaṇa reasons it out. If Rāma is the Supreme Lord, being killed by him wins Vaikuṇṭha; if not, Laṅkā is his to enjoy. So he chooses **hostility as the quicker road to the Lord** than devotion (AR 3.5.58–61). Later Agastya tells Rāma the story behind it. The women of Śvetadvīpa had handled Rāvaṇa like a toy. After that he decided to provoke Viṣṇu deliberately, abducted Sītā knowing who she was, and **guarded her "like a mother"** while he waited to be killed (AR 7.4.1–11).
- **Kālanemi** - Rāvaṇa sends the demon Kālanemi, disguised as a sage in a hermitage, to delay Hanumān on his herb errand. Hanumān sees through him and kills him with a blow of his fist (AR 6.6–6.7; death at 6.7.30–33). The episode is absent from the Vālmīki Critical Edition.
- **The pre-arranged exile of Sītā** - The gods want Rāma back in Vaikuṇṭha. They ask Sītā to go there first so that he will follow, and Sītā passes the request on to Rāma. He works out a plan. He will "use public talk as a pretext" and abandon her in the forest, "as though afraid of gossip". She will bear twins near Vālmīki's hermitage, return, swear an oath, and go to Vaikuṇṭha **through an opening in the earth**, and he will follow (AR 7.4.32–44). Only after this does Vijaya, one of the companions who entertain him with stories, report the citizens' gossip (AR 7.4.46–53). At the oath scene Rāma still says he abandoned her "out of fear of the people", and asks Vālmīki's pardon (AR 7.7.28–36).
  - *Contrast (Vālmīki):* the rumour comes first and Rāma's decision is a grieving king's own (VR Uttara Kāṇḍa; see [Uttara](./7-uttara-kanda.md)).

## 2. The vernacular bhakti Rāmāyaṇas

- **Rāmcaritmānas (Tulsīdās, 1574 CE)** - **Awadhi**, 7 kāṇḍas in *caupāī–dohā*; the most influential North-Indian *bhakti* telling and the textual basis of the **Rāmlīlā**. **Draws directly on the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** — inherits Rāma-as-God *and the māyā-Sītā doctrine*. While Lakṣmaṇa is out gathering roots, Rāma tells Sītā he is about to stage a "lovely human play" (*lalita nara-līlā*): *tumha pāvaka mahu karahu nivāsā, jau lagi karauṃ nisācara nāsā* ("dwell in the fire until I destroy the night-rangers"). She enters the fire and leaves *nija pratibiṃba* (her own reflection), and "even Lakṣmaṇa did not know this secret" (RCM Araṇya, dohā 23 and the caupāīs that follow). The Mānas sets no one-year term; the Adhyātma does. Saguṇa-bhakti is foregrounded over Advaita allegory. *Full file:* [recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md](./recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md). [late text]
- **Adhyātmarāmāyaṇam Kiḷippāṭṭu (Ezhuthachan, 16th c.)** - **Malayalam** "parrot-song" rendering of the Sanskrit Adhyātma — carries the Vedāntic frame and chāyā-Sītā into Kerala; foundational to modern Malayalam literature.
- **Iramāvatāram / Kamba-rāmāyaṇam (Kamban, c. 12th c.)** - **Tamil**, ~10,000+ verses in **6 kāṇḍas (omits Uttara)** — the Tamil **Uttara Kāṇḍam** was later supplied by **Ottakkūttar**. A supreme literary kāvya; elevates Rāma's divinity, deepens characterization, and is famous for a **more sympathetic, tragic Rāvaṇa**. *Full file:* [recensions/kamban-iramavataram.md](./recensions/kamban-iramavataram.md). [scholarly]
- **Raṅganātha Rāmāyaṇamu (Gona Buddhā Reddy, c. 1300–1310 CE)** - **Telugu**, first complete Telugu Rāmāyaṇa, ~**17,290 couplets in *dvipada* (two-footed) meter**; rich in distinctive folk episodes. A separate **Uttara-Raṅganātha** continuation supplies the seventh book.
- **Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ / Śrīrām Pāñcālī (Kṛttibās Ojhā, 15th c.)** - **Bengali**, the most beloved Bengali telling; folk- and bhakti-saturated. It **popularizes the *akāl-bodhan*** — Rāma's "untimely" autumn invocation of **Durgā** before the war, the mythic charter of autumn Durgā Pūjā. *Full file:* [recensions/krittivasi-ramayan.md](./recensions/krittivasi-ramayan.md). [folk]
  - *Older Sanskrit precedent (distinct):* in the **Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa**, Nārada finds Rāma grieving after the abduction. He tells him Sītā is alive and prescribes a nine-day vow to the Devī "in this very month of Āśvina", and he acts as Rāma's priest. The Devī appears and promises victory over Rāvaṇa (Devī-Bhāgavata 3.30, Vijñānānanda tr., wisdomlib). That chapter has no "untimely awakening". The *akāla* framing (Dakṣiṇāyana, when the gods sleep), the 108 blue lotuses and Rāma's offered eye belong to the Bengali telling. Which way the influence ran between the two is not established here.
- **Other major regional renderings** - **Saptakāṇḍa Rāmāyaṇa** (Assamese, Mādhava Kandalī, 14th c., under the Barāhī king Mahāmāṇikya — among the earliest complete vernacular Rāmāyaṇas). Kandalī's first and last books were lost and were supplied in the 16th c., the **Ādi** by **Mādhavadeva** and the **Uttara** by **Śaṅkaradeva**. Also **Bhāvārtha Rāmāyaṇa** (Marathi, Ekanāth, 16th c.), **Torave Rāmāyaṇa** (Kannada, Kumāra Vālmīki/Narahari, 16th c.), and **Giridhar Rāmāyaṇa** (Gujarati). [open-ended roster — many further vernacular tellings exist]

## 3. Other Sanskrit Rāmāyaṇas — Śākta, devotional, and philosophical

- **Rāmopākhyāna (Mahābhārata, Āraṇyaka Parva 3.257–275)** - Generally held to be the oldest retelling outside Vālmīki [scholarly], and it sits inside the other epic. After Draupadī's rescue from Jayadratha, Yudhiṣṭhira asks whether anyone was ever more unfortunate, and **Mārkaṇḍeya** answers with Rāma's story (MBh 3.257.1–10). It is a compact account with its own variants:
  - *Rāvaṇa's family:* Viśravas is half of Pulastya's body, born of his anger. Kubera gives him three *rākṣasī* attendants. **Puṣpotkaṭā** bears Rāvaṇa and Kumbhakarṇa, **Mālinī** bears Vibhīṣaṇa, and **Rākā** bears the twins Khara and Śūrpaṇakhā (MBh 3.259.1–8). In Vālmīki one mother, Kaikasī, bears Rāvaṇa, Kumbhakarṇa, Śūrpaṇakhā and Vibhīṣaṇa (VR CE 7.9.3–27).
  - *Mantharā:* Brahmā sends the gandharvī **Dundubhī**, who becomes the hunchback Mantharā (MBh 3.260.9–10).
  - *Avindhya:* Rāvaṇa has a wise old minister named **Avindhya** who is on Rāma's side. Through Trijaṭā he sends Sītā word that Rāma is allied with Sugrīva, and that Nalakūbara's curse stops Rāvaṇa from forcing any unwilling woman (MBh 3.264.53–59). Avindhya later hands Sītā back to Rāma (MBh 3.275.6–7).
  - *Who kills whom:* **Lakṣmaṇa kills Kumbhakarṇa** with the brahmāstra (MBh 3.271.10–17), whereas Rāma does so in Vālmīki (VR CE 6.55). Lakṣmaṇa kills Indrajit (MBh 3.273.20–24). Rāma kills Rāvaṇa with an arrow charged with the brahmāstra, which burns him so completely that not even ash remains (MBh 3.274.24–31).
  - *Sītā's vindication — no fire in the constituted text:* Rāma rejects her, "like an oblation licked by a dog" (MBh 3.275.10–13). She swears that the elements may take her life if she has sinned. **Vāyu, Agni and Varuṇa** speak from the sky to declare her blameless, then Brahmā speaks. Daśaratha, who has come down with the gods (MBh 3.275.17–19), speaks last (MBh 3.275.21–35). In the constituted text **she never enters a fire**. The fire-entry exists only in a passage the Critical Edition relegates to its apparatus (the passage after 3.275.16). It is close to the Adhyātma's wording at AR 6.12.76–83, and a starred line adds Yama as a fourth witness. [interpolation — CE apparatus]
- **Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa** - **Śākta** Sanskrit poem in **27 sargas** (a total of ~1,355 verses is sometimes given; not verified here), ascribed pseudepigraphically to Vālmīki, who narrates it to Bharadvāja. It skips most of the familiar plot. The whole war with the ten-headed Rāvaṇa is squeezed into about 20 verses (canto 16), and the poem is built around **Sītā as the supreme Śakti**. (Chhawchharia ed., 2010)
  - *Why the avatāra:* Nārada's curses on Viṣṇu and on Lakṣmī, set in the stories of King Ambarīṣa's daughter and of Hari-mitra (cantos 1–7).
  - *Sītā born to Mandodarī:* the sage **Gṛtsamada** had blessed a pot of milk with a kuśa blade, hoping Lakṣmī would be born as his daughter. Rāvaṇa happened to use the same pot to collect blood he had drawn from the sages, and left it with Mandodarī, warning her that it was deadlier than poison. She was distraught at his conduct and drank it to die, but conceived instead. She buried the embryo at **Kurukṣetra**, where Janaka's golden plough later turned up the girl (canto 8, vv. 19–38).
  - *Rāma as Viṣṇu:* he shows Paraśurāma his cosmic form (canto 9) and Hanumān his four-armed form, then teaches Hanumān Vedānta and bhakti (cantos 10–14; the "Adbhuta Rāma-Gītā").
  - *The thousand-headed Rāvaṇa:* at the victory court Sītā reveals that the **Sahasra-mukha Rāvaṇa** of Puṣkara is the ten-headed one's **elder brother** (canto 17). In the war that follows Rāma is struck down unconscious (cantos 18–22; 22.48–50). **Sītā assumes the form of Mahākālī** and destroys the demon with his sons and army (canto 23), inverting the hero/heroine roles. The gods calm her and Rāma revives (canto 24). She shows him her cosmic form and he praises her with a **thousand names** (canto 25), after which she returns to her gentle form (canto 26). [late text] [Shakta variant]
- **Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa** - Pseudepigraphically "Vālmīki," ~15th c., again framed as Śiva speaking to Pārvatī. It has **nine kāṇḍas**: Sāra, Yātrā, Yāga, Vilāsa, Janma, Vivāha, Rājya, Manohara and Pūrṇa. It focuses on Rāma's **later reign and peripheral/etiological legends** and is a quarry for *vrata* (vow) stories, stotras and kavacas.
  - *Rāvaṇa abducts Kausalyā (Sāra Kāṇḍa 1.35–69):* Brahmā has told Rāvaṇa that the son of Daśaratha and Kausalyā will kill him. Rāvaṇa wrecks Daśaratha's boat on the Sarayū, then abducts Kausalyā from the king of Kosala. He locks her in a chest and leaves it with a *timiṅgila* fish in the sea. Daśaratha and his minister Sumantra drift to the island where the fish has set the chest down. They find her, and the king marries her there by gandharva rite. When Rāvaṇa boasts that he has foiled Brahmā, Brahmā announces the wedding. Rāvaṇa opens the chest and finds three people in it, and on Brahmā's warning he sends them back to Ayodhyā.
  - *Other back-stories:* the text is also said to supply motives for **Kaikeyī and Mantharā** that the older epic leaves unexplained. This was not checked against the text here. [late text] [unverified here]
- **Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha (Vāsiṣṭha Rāmāyaṇa / Mahā-Rāmāyaṇa)** - A vast (~**29,000-verse**) philosophical Rāmāyaṇa-frame: a **young, world-weary Rāma** is instructed by **Vasiṣṭha** in radical Advaita — specifically *ajātavāda* (the doctrine of **non-origination**: the world never truly arose; it is mind-only appearance) — illustrated by hundreds of nested parables. Six **prakaraṇas**: (1) *Vairāgya* (dispassion), (2) *Mumukṣu-vyavahāra* (yearning for liberation), (3) *Utpatti* ("creation"), (4) *Sthiti* (sustenance), (5) *Upaśama* (dissolution/quieting), (6) *Nirvāṇa* (liberation). Syncretic — fuses Vedānta, Yoga, Sāṃkhya, Pratyabhijñā/Trika Śaivism, and Mahāyāna idealism; the narrative is a *pretext* for [Vedānta](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md). ([Yoga Vasishtha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga_Vasishtha)) [late text]
- **Agastya Rāmāyaṇa** - Supplementary Sanskrit tradition (Agastya as teacher/transmitter). [late text]
- **The "core vs. frame" hinge** - The Vedāntic/Śākta theology of all the above lives in the conceptual space opened by the (later) **Bāla** and **Uttara** kāṇḍas of Vālmīki, where Rāma's Viṣṇu-divinity is made explicit — the older core (kāṇḍas 2–6) keeps Rāma chiefly human. See [Rāmāyaṇa overview → core vs. frame](./README.md). [scholarly]
  - *Complication inside the core:* in the Critical Edition's constituted Yuddha Kāṇḍa, at the fire ordeal, Rāma says "I think of myself as a man, Rāma son of Daśaratha". Brahmā answers *bhavān nārāyaṇo devaḥ*, "you are the god Nārāyaṇa", and lists his Viṣṇu names (VR CE 6.105.10–14). Agni then returns Sītā and testifies to her purity (VR CE 6.106.3–9). Scholars of the historical-critical school (e.g. Brockington, Goldman) discuss whether such passages are late layers within kāṇḍa 6. Even so, the Adhyātma's "Rāma who only seems human" has a foothold in the Vālmīki text itself. [scholarly]

## 4. Jain Rāmāyaṇas — demythologizing, ahiṃsā, and reassigned violence

*(Full treatment: [recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md](./recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md).)*

- **Paümacariya(ṃ) (Vimalasūri)** - **Prākrit (Jain Māhārāṣṭrī)**, the oldest surviving Jain Rāma-narrative. Its own colophon dates it **530 years after Mahāvīra's nirvāṇa** (about the turn of the Common Era on the traditional reckoning); academic estimates mostly run c. 3rd–5th c. CE [disputed]. **Rationalizes** the epic: **Rāvaṇa is a noble, learned Vidyādhara**, *vānaras* are a human clan bearing monkey-banners, and — because Rāma is the non-violent hero destined for liberation — **Lakṣmaṇa, not Rāma, kills Rāvaṇa** (in Jain *śalākāpuruṣa* cosmology the *Vāsudeva* slays the *Prati-vāsudeva*). In this line Sītā is **Janaka's daughter**. ([Paumacariya](https://culturalsamvaad.com/rama-in-paumacariya-jaina-retelling-of-the-ramayana/); date: Pannalal Jain's introduction to Raviṣeṇa's *Padmapurāṇa*, Bhāratīya Jñānapīṭha) [Jain variant]
  - *Sītā's parentage variant:* in **Guṇabhadra's *Uttara-purāṇa*** and **Saṅghadāsa's** Śvetāmbara account, **Sītā is born to Rāvaṇa and Mandodarī** and exposed because of a prophecy that she will destroy the family, then raised by Janaka. This sharpens the moral critique of Rāvaṇa's desire. Compare the Śākta Adbhuta (§3), where Mandodarī is also Sītā's mother. [Jain variant]
- **Padma-purāṇa (Raviṣeṇa, 7th c.)** & **Uttara-purāṇa (Guṇabhadra, 9th c.)** - Sanskrit Jain recastings. Raviṣeṇa closely follows Vimalasūri for a Digambara audience. Guṇabhadra diverges, with Daśaratha at Vārāṇasī, Sītā as Rāvaṇa's daughter, and different genealogies. **Svayambhūdeva's *Paümacariu*** (Apabhraṃśa, later 8th c.) continues the Vimalasūri–Raviṣeṇa line.
- **Kumudendu Rāmāyaṇa (Kumudendu Muni, 13th c.)** - **Kannada**, Jain. [Jain variant]

## 5. Buddhist Rāmāyaṇa

- **Dasaratha Jātaka (No. 461)** - **Pāli**; Rāma (**Rāma-paṇḍita**, "Rāma the Wise") is a previous birth of the **Buddha (bodhisatta)**. The Buddha tells it in Jetavana to a landowner crushed by grief for his dead father. **No Rāvaṇa, no war, no abduction.** (Jātaka 461, Rouse tr. 1901)
  - *Setting & cast:* Daśaratha reigns at **Benares (Bārāṇasī)**, not Ayodhyā. His chief queen bears **Rāma-paṇḍita, Lakkhaṇa and a daughter, Sītā**, so Sītā is Rāma's **full sister** by the same mother. After that queen dies, the new queen bears Bharata and asks for the kingdom for him.
  - *Exile as protection:* Daśaratha refuses her and fears she will have his elder children murdered. So he himself sends them away "to some neighbouring kingdom, or to the woodland". Soothsayers say he has twelve years to live, so he tells them to return after twelve years. Sītā chooses to go with her brothers to the Himalaya.
  - *The moral centre:* the king dies of grief in the ninth year. When Bharata brings the news, Lakkhaṇa and Sītā faint, but Rāma stays calm and gives stanzas on impermanence: "as sure as for the ripened fruit there comes the fear of fall, so surely comes the fear of death to mortals one and all" (Pāli *phalānam iva pakkānaṃ niccaṃ papatanā bhayaṃ*).
  - *The sandals:* Rāma keeps his father's term and stays three more years. He gives Bharata his **straw slippers** to rule in his place. On the throne they **beat against each other when a case is wrongly judged**.
  - *Ending:* Rāma returns, **Sītā is made his queen-consort**, and he reigns for **16,000 years** (Pāli *dasa vassasahassāni saṭṭhivassasatāni ca*, "ten thousand years and sixty hundred"). The Buddha then identifies the characters. Śuddhodana was Daśaratha, Mahāmāyā the mother, Rāhula's mother Sītā, Ānanda Bharata, and the Buddha himself Rāma-paṇḍita. [Buddhist variant]
- **Southeast-Asian Buddhist/Islamic-framed tellings** - Rāmakīen (Thai), Reamker (Khmer), Phra Lak Phra Lam (Lao), Yama Zatdaw (Burmese), Hikayat Seri Rama (Malay), and others — catalogued in [recensions/southeast-asian-ramayanas.md](./recensions/southeast-asian-ramayanas.md). [folk]

## 6. Comparative crib — the same skeleton, re-coded

- **Who is Rāma?** - *Ideal human king* (Vālmīki core, though Brahmā calls him Nārāyaṇa at VR CE 6.105) → *supreme Brahman* (Adhyātma, Tulsīdās, Kamban) → *vehicle of the Goddess* (Adbhuta) → *Jain hero bound for mokṣa* (Paümacariya) → *bodhisatta* (Dasaratha Jātaka).
- **Is Sītā abducted?** - Yes (Vālmīki; Rāmopākhyāna) → **only her shadow is** (Kūrma Purāṇa, on Sītā's own appeal to Agni; Adhyātma and Tulsīdās, on Rāma's instruction) → she is the one who **destroys** the demon as Mahākālī (Adbhuta) → no abduction at all (Dasaratha Jātaka).
- **Who kills Rāvaṇa?** - **Rāma** (Vālmīki, Rāmopākhyāna, bhakti tellings) → **Lakṣmaṇa** (Jain) → **Sītā-as-Kālī** kills the *thousand-headed* Rāvaṇa (Adbhuta) → *no one / no Rāvaṇa* (Dasaratha Jātaka).
- **What is the agni-parīkṣā?** - A genuine test of chastity. Sītā enters the fire, and Agni brings her out and vouches for her (Vālmīki, VR CE 6.104–106) → **no fire at all**, only an oath and the testimony of Wind, Fire and Water (Rāmopākhyāna constituted text) → the **burning of the shadow and the re-emergence of the true Sītā** from the fire that has hidden her all along (Kūrma; Adhyātma/Tulsīdās chāyā-Sītā).
- **Who is Sītā's mother?** - The Earth, found in a furrow by Janaka (Vālmīki) → **Mandodarī**, by Rāvaṇa (Guṇabhadra's Jain *Uttara-purāṇa*) → **Mandodarī**, via a pot of sages' blood and a sage's blessed milk (Adbhuta) → Daśaratha's chief queen, making her Rāma's sister (Dasaratha Jātaka).
- **What is the genre?** - Narrative kāvya (Vālmīki, Kamban) → Vedānta *śāstra* in story-clothing (Adhyātma, Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha) → Purāṇic vrata-lore (Ānanda) → Śākta hymn-epic (Adbhuta) → didactic Jātaka / Jain *carita*.
