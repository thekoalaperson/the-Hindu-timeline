# Jain & Buddhist Ramayanas — Paumacariya and the Dasaratha Jataka

Deep-dive: [Ramayana](../README.md) · [Deep Dives](../../README.md) · [Master Index](../../../README.md)
The *heterodox* Rama traditions — the **Jain** retellings (Vimalasuri's *Paumacariya* and its lineage, plus the separate *Vasudevahindi*/*Uttarapurana* current) and the **Buddhist** tales (the Pali *Dasaratha Jataka* and two Chinese canonical stories) — which rationalize, re-motivate, and re-purpose the Rama story. The Jain texts subordinate Rama to a Jain cosmology of *shalakapurushas* and give Ravana's death to Lakshmana; the Pali tale has no abduction and no war and turns the story into a sermon on impermanence. These are not Hindu *recensions* but rival reworkings by rival traditions — kept here under "recensions" for ease of comparison. Nothing below is Valmiki unless it is explicitly cited as Valmiki.

> Two clocks: the *story* is set in (Jain) the age of the 20th Tirthankara Munisuvrata or (Buddhist) a past life of the Bodhisatta; the *texts* are academic-dated literary works (Jain c. 3rd/5th–13th c. CE; Pali *Dasaratha Jataka* verses of uncertain but probably early date, prose commentary c. 5th c. CE; Chinese translations 3rd and 5th c. CE). This file dates the **texts** and catalogs the **variant plots**, not the events.

## The Jain Rama: Cosmological Frame

- **Rama as a *shalakapurusha*** - In Jain universal history, 63 *shalakapurushas* ("illustrious persons") appear in each half-cycle of time; Rama, Lakshmana and Ravana form the eighth of the nine triads of **Baladeva**, **Vasudeva** (Narayana) and **Prativasudeva**. Vimalasuri has Gautama recite the full roster of the 63 before beginning Rama's life (Paumacariya 20; Chandra, ch. 2 summary).
  - **Rama = 8th Baladeva** - the elder half-brother, who does not slay the Prativasudeva and ends in liberation. He is not wholly non-violent in the Paumacariya: he shoots the shape-shifter Sahasagati dead with arrows (Paumacariya 47; Chandra, ch. 2).
  - **Lakshmana = 8th Vasudeva** - the martial half-brother who wields the discus (*chakra*), slays the Prativasudeva, becomes *ardhacakravartin* (lord of half of Bharatavarsha), and sinks into hell after death (Paumacariya 73, 91; Kulkarni 1990).
  - **Ravana = 8th Prativasudeva** - destined to be killed by his Vasudeva counterpart, Lakshmana — never by Rama.
  - *Variant (naming of the Baladevas):* Paumacariya 5.154 calls the 8th Baladeva **Padma** and the 9th (Krishna's brother) **Rama**; the Digambara *Tiloyapannatti* 4.517 lists Rama as 8th and Padma as 9th; the *Vasudevahindi* calls Dasaratha's son "Rama", not "Padma" (Chandra, ch. 1 §2B).
- **Rakshasas and Vanaras as Vidyadharas** - the "demons" and "monkeys" are recast as branches of the **Vidyadharas**, a human race wielding *vidyas* (magical lores). The Vanara dynasty is so called because its founder Amaraprabha adopted the monkey as the emblem of his state (Paumacariya 6), and Ravana's line descends from the Vidyadhara Meghavahana, first ruler of Lanka (Paumacariya 5) (Chandra, ch. 2). Ravana is a learned, Jina-worshipping king. See the divine/monstrous framing in [Yuddha Kanda](../6-yuddha-kanda.md) and [Uttara Kanda](../7-uttara-kanda.md).
- **Rationalist polemic** - Vimalasuri frames his work as a correction. King Shrenika doubts the Rama story current in the *loyasattha* ("popular scripture") and calls its authors liars; Gautama then tells him the "true" story (Paumacariya 2.105–117; 3.3). The absurdities Shrenika lists are: Rama killing a golden deer, Rama stealthily killing Vali, the vanaras being literal monkeys, monkeys building a bridge, monkeys beating stronger rakshasas, Indra being carried captive to Lanka, rakshasas eating flesh and drinking wine, and Kumbhakarna sleeping for six months and swallowing elephants and men (Paumacariya 2.105–117; 3.8–13; Chandra, ch. 6 §2, who traces each item to the Valmiki text). [scholarly]
  - **"Dasamukha" rationalized** - Ravana's ten heads are not on that list, but the text explains the name anyway. As a baby Ravana grabbed an heirloom necklace of nine great gems, which came down from the rakshasa lord Bhima through Meghavahana to his father Ratnashrava. When his mother Kekasi put it on him, his face was reflected in the nine gems, so he was named Dasamukha, "ten-faced" (Paumacariya 7.96; Chandra, ch. 4 §1).
  - **"Ravana" etymologized** - the name comes from the cry (*ravo kao*) that Dasamukha let out when the monk Vali pressed down the mountain he was trying to lift (Paumacariya 9.78; Chandra, ch. 4 §1).

## Jain Versions — Text by Text

- **Paumacariya (Vimalasuri)** - the **earliest extant Jain Ramayana** and the source of the main Jain tradition; *Paümacariya* = "Deeds of Padma" (Padma = Rama's Jain name). It is in **Maharashtri Prakrit**, **118 cantos**, **8,651 gathas** (about 10,000 *granthagra* units) (Chandra, ch. 6 §9.1; Kulkarni 1990).
  - *Dating [disputed]:* the closing *prasasti* says the work was finished 530 years (one manuscript reads 520) after Mahavira's *nirvana* (Paumacariya 118.103). That gives c. 4 CE on the traditional 527 BCE nirvana date, or c. 63 CE on Jacobi's 467 BCE. Jacobi himself put the text in the 3rd–4th c. CE, on the evidence of the word *dinara*, the peoples it names, and its astronomical terms. Chandra (1970) argues for c. 473 CE, on the view that the era was really Vikrama. The earliest sure mention of the work is Uddyotana's *Kuvalayamala* (778 CE) (Chandra, ch. 1 §2C; Kulkarni 1990). Jain tradition and academic dating diverge sharply here. [scholarly]
  - *Sect [disputed]:* Vimalasuri names no sect. His Naila lineage (Paumacariya 118.117–118) appears in Shvetambara lineage lists, but some doctrinal details agree with Digambara works and others with Shvetambara ones. Premi suggested the Yapaniya; Chandra declines to assign any sect (Chandra, ch. 1 §2B). [scholarly]
  - **Plot keystones** - Rakshasas/Vanaras are Vidyadharas; Ravana is a Jina-devotee bound by a vow not to take an unwilling woman; Rama's exile is voluntary; Sita has a twin brother; **Lakshmana kills Ravana** with Ravana's own discus; Lakshmana, not Rama, is crowned at Ayodhya; Sita is banished, passes a fire ordeal, becomes a nun and is reborn as a god; Lakshmana dies of shock and goes to hell; Rama renounces and attains liberation (episode guide below).
  - **Kaikeyi re-motivated** - she wins her boon by driving Dasaratha's war-chariot at her own *svayamvara* (Paumacariya 24). She spends it to keep Bharata in the world when both Dasaratha and Bharata resolve to become monks (Paumacariya 31). She later goes to the forest herself to beg Rama to return (Paumacariya 32), and in the end she renounces (Paumacariya 80–84). In Paumacariya 83.12 the printed text says she attains liberation, but 95.26 sends her to a celestial abode, and three manuscripts read *sammottam* for *siddhipayam* (Chandra, ch. 1 §2B; ch. 2). The Valmiki Kaikeyi's boons come from the Devasura war; contrast [Ayodhya Kanda](../2-ayodhya-kanda.md).
  - **Vali becomes a monk** - in the Paumacariya, Vali renounces long before Rama's time. Ravana demanded his sister Shriprabha and his submission; Vali crowned Sugriva rather than fight and became a monk (Paumacariya 9.24–50). When Ravana tried to lift the mountain on which Vali was meditating (Ashtapada/Kailasa), Vali pressed it down with his toe (Paumacariya 9.78). The Kishkindha crisis of Rama's day is a different one: the Vidyadhara **Sahasagati** takes Sugriva's form to win Sutara (Tara's counterpart), and Rama kills him (Paumacariya 47) (Chandra, ch. 2; ch. 4 §1). Contrast Rama shooting Vali from hiding in [Kishkindha Kanda](../4-kishkindha-kanda.md).
- **Padmacarita / Padma-purana (Ravisena)** - **Sanskrit**, **late 7th c. CE**. The poem says it was finished 1,203½ years after Mahavira's *nirvana*, conventionally converted to 676–678 CE (Padmacarita 123.181; Kulkarni 1990). It follows Vimalasuri canto for canto, without naming him (Chandra reads Ravisena's source "Anuttaravagmin" as probably a veiled reference to Vimalasuri), but enlarges him to 123 cantos and 18,023 verses (Padmacarita 123.186), mostly with fuller description (Chandra, ch. 6 §9.1). Ravisena is a Digambara. He gives Dasaratha a fourth queen, **Suprabha** (Padmacarita 22.176) (Chandra, ch. 3 §I.1). His version became the standard Jain Rama for later Digambara writers.
- **Paumacariu (Svayambhu / Svayambhudeva)** - **Apabhramsha**, in 90 *sandhis*. Estimates of his date vary: the 8th c. CE (*Jain Journal* 1999), 840–920 CE (Chandra, ch. 6 §9.2, following the Paumacariu edition), or anywhere between the 7th and 10th c. (*Studies in Jainism*) [disputed]. Chandra calls him a Yapaniya (Chandra, ch. 1 §2B). He left the poem unfinished, and his son **Tribhuvana** wrote the last seven or eight *sandhis*; the sources differ on the count (*Jain Journal* 1989 and 1999 and *Studies in Jainism*, via jainqq). Svayambhu says he wrote by the favour of Ravisena (Paumacariu 1.2.9; Chandra, ch. 6 §9.2). The story follows Vimalasuri and Ravisena, sometimes in shorter form (e.g. Paumacariu 84.2–22 for the previous births).
  - *Same parentage as Vimalasuri:* Rama is born of **Aparajita** and Lakshmana of **Sumitra**; Bharata and Shatrughna are both Kaikeyi's sons; and **Sita is born to Janaka's queen Videha** (Paumacariya 25, 26.75; Paumacariu 21.5.4). This is the Vimalasuri line's own account, not a Svayambhu innovation. It stands against the Vasudevahindi/Gunabhadra line below (Chandra, ch. 3 §II.2).
- **Vasudevahindi (Sanghadasagani)** - a **Shvetambara Prakrit** prose narrative whose Rama episode belongs to a **second Jain tradition**, independent of Vimalasuri. Chandra dates it earlier than, or roughly contemporary with, the Paumacariya [disputed] (Chandra, ch. 6 §6).
  - *Variant (Sita = Ravana's daughter):* Mandodari's newborn daughter is prophesied to destroy her family. She is put in a casket, carried by a minister to Janaka's land, and fixed to a ploughshare by a *vidya*. Janaka's queen **Dharini** adopts her (Vasudevahindi I p. 241; Chandra, ch. 3 §II.2). Chandra uses this to correct Bulcke's claim that Gunabhadra was the first to make Sita Ravana's daughter.
  - *Other traits:* Dasaratha's first queen is Kausalya; Kaikeyi gets two boons on two occasions; Manthara incites her; the exile lasts **twelve years**, as in the Dasaratha Jataka; Bharata takes Rama's sandals; Khara and Dushana are Shurpanakha's sons; Rama disfigures Shurpanakha; there is a golden deer; Jatayu is a Vidyadhara; **Rama kills Vali** openly; a bridge is built; Hanuman is Sugriva's minister; Rama himself is enthroned; there is no banishment of Sita (Chandra, ch. 3 §I.1 for Kausalya; ch. 6 §6 for the rest).
- **Uttarapurana (Gunabhadra)** - **Sanskrit**, a Digambara *purana*, the second part of the Jain *Mahapurana*. Its *prasasti* dates the completion to Shaka 820 (Pingala year), given there as 23 June 897 CE, at Bankapura under Lokaditya (*History of Rastrakutas of Malkhed and Jainism* via jainqq). Its Rama narrative is condensed and does **not** derive from Vimalasuri (Chandra, ch. 6 §6).
  - *Variant (Dasharatha's city):* Dasharatha first reigns at **Varanasi** and moves to Ayodhya when its king Sagara dies (Uttarapurana 67.148–165). Rama and Lakshmana are installed as king and heir of Varanasi (Chandra, ch. 3 §I.1; ch. 6 §6).
  - *Variant (parentage):* queen **Subala** bears Rama; **Kaikeyi**, here the second queen, bears Lakshmana; Bharata and Shatrughna are sons of an unnamed third queen (Uttarapurana 67.148–165; Chandra, ch. 3 §I.1; ch. 6 §6).
  - *Variant (Sita = Ravana's daughter):* in a previous life Sita's soul was the Vidyadhara princess **Manimati**. When Ravana tried to seize her *vidyas*, she resolved to be reborn as his daughter and cause his death (Uttarapurana 68.13–17). Born to Mandodari, she is shut in a casket with a letter, which Maricha buries near Mithila on Ravana's order, and she is found and raised by Janaka and his queen **Vasudha** (Uttarapurana 68.17–27; Chandra, ch. 3 §II.2; ch. 4 no. 54). Compare Valmiki's [Vedavati](../../characters/vedavati.md) curse (Valmiki 7.17).
  - *Variant (no exile):* there is no boon and no exile. Sita is given to Rama for protecting Janaka's sacrifice, with no bow and no *svayamvara*. **Narada** incites Ravana. A golden deer lures Rama away. There is no Jatayu. Hanuman is called **Anuman**. Sita is taken back without suspicion, with no fire ordeal and no banishment. Her sons are **Vijayarama** and **Ajitanjaya** (Chandra, ch. 6 §6).
  - *Variant (Vali's death):* **Lakshmana kills Vali**, after Vali first offers help and then challenges Rama (Chandra, ch. 6 §6). Compare Vali the renouncer in the Paumacariya.
  - *Variant (endings):* Rama lives 13,000 years (Uttarapurana 67.150). After Lakshmana's death he crowns Lakshmana's eldest son by **Prithvisundari** and becomes a monk with Sugriva, Anuman and Vibhishana. Rama and Anuman attain liberation on **Sammeta** (Uttarapurana 68.704–721; Chandra, ch. 3 §IX.5).
  - *Followers:* **Pushpadanta's** Apabhramsha *Mahapurana* (10th c.) follows Gunabhadra, e.g. on Sita's birth (Mahapurana 70.8–9) (Chandra, ch. 3 §II.2; ch. 6 §9.6).
- **Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra, Book VII (Hemacandra)** - **Sanskrit**, **12th c. CE**, Shvetambara; follows the Vimalasuri line (Chandra, ch. 6 §9.3; Johnson's translation).
  - *Afterlife made explicit:* **Ravana, Lakshmana and Shambuka** are in the **fourth hell**. Sita, now the god Sitendra, goes down to stop their fighting and tries to lift them out, but they slip through his hands like quicksilver (Hemacandra VII.10, "Sita visits Lakshmana in hell").
  - *Future births:* Ravana's soul will become a **Tirthankara**, with Sita's soul as his *ganadhara*; Lakshmana's soul will later become a cakravartin and then a Tirthankara as well (Hemacandra VII.10, "Future births of Ravana, Lakshmana, and Sita").
  - Hemacandra gives Rama 15,000 years and Lakshmana 12,000 years (Chandra, ch. 3 §IX.5).
- **Ramapurana (Bhattaraka Somasena)** - a late Digambara Sanskrit retelling that follows Vimalasuri but merges in the casket story of Sita's birth. It is the text that places **Rama's** and Hanuman's liberation on **Tungagiri** (Chandra, ch. 3 §II.2, §IX.5). [late text]
- **Ramachandra-charita-purana / "Pampa Ramayana" (Nagachandra, "Abhinava Pampa")** - **Kannada** *champu*, 12th c. CE (dated 1149 CE by shastriyakannada.org; Nagachandra's *Mallinathapurana* is dated 1105). It is the earliest extant Kannada Ramayana and draws on Vimalasuri and Ravisena. It presents Ravana as a tragic hero (Wikipedia, *Hoysala literature*; shastriyakannada.org).
- **Kumudendu Ramayana (Kumudendu)** - **Kannada**, **1275 CE**, said to be in *shatpadi* metre and influenced by Nagachandra (Wikipedia, *Hoysala literature*, the only source found). Do not confuse this poet with the Kumudendu traditionally credited with the *Siribhoovalaya*.
  - *Note:* the plot of Kumudendu's text is thinly documented in accessible sources. It is generally taken to belong to the Vimalasuri/Ravisena tradition. [open roster — variants under-documented]

## Paumacariya Episode Guide (Vimalasuri)

All episodes below are the Paumacariya's own, located by canto. The plot is from Chandra's chapter 2 summary unless another source is named.

- **Prophecy and decoy (23)** - Narada warns Dasaratha that Vibhishana plans to kill him and Janaka, because a son of Dasaratha and a daughter of Janaka will cause Ravana's death. The two kings wander in disguise; Vibhishana beheads an effigy of Dasaratha at Saketa.
- **Kaikeyi's svayamvara (24)** - Kaikeyi chooses Dasaratha, drives his chariot to victory over the rejected suitors, and earns her boon.
- **Births (25)** - Padma (Rama) is born to Aparajita, Lakshmana to Sumitra, and Bharata and Shatrughna to Kaikeyi.
- **Sita's twin Bhamandala (26–30)** - Videha bears twins. A god steals the boy, and the Vidyadhara king Candragati raises him as **Bhamandala**. Narada, insulted at Sita's palace, plants her portrait where Bhamandala sees it and falls in love. Candragati forces a bow test, which Rama wins. Bhamandala later remembers his previous birth and learns that Sita is his sister.
- **Exile (31–32)** - Dasaratha resolves to renounce; Kaikeyi claims the throne for Bharata; Bharata refuses to supplant his brother; Rama leaves of his own accord to free Bharata's conscience. No fixed term of exile is given (Chandra, ch. 6 §6). Rama crowns Bharata in the forest when Kaikeyi comes to recall him.
- **Exploits on the road (33–40)** - Rama and Lakshmana settle quarrels between kings. They restore Vajrakarna of Dashapura, free Balikhilya from the Mleccha chief Rudrabhuti, and disguise themselves as dancing girls to capture Ativirya. Lakshmana collects many betrothals (Vanamala, Kalyanamala, Jitapadma and others). Rama has Jina shrines built on "Ramagiri". Chandra reads several of these episodes as echoes of 2nd–5th c. CE politics (Chandra, ch. 1 §2C). [scholarly]
- **Jatayu (41)** - a sick vulture is healed by water used to wash two monks' feet and is entrusted to Sita.
- **Shambuka (43)** - Lakshmana finds the divine sword **Suryahasa** in a bamboo thicket and, testing it, unknowingly beheads **Shambuka**, son of Ravana's sister **Candranakha** (Shurpanakha's counterpart), who had been doing penance to win that sword. Candranakha then falls for Rama and Lakshmana, is refused, and stirs up her husband **Kharadushana** (one person here). Nobody is mutilated. Contrast Valmiki's Shambuka, a shudra ascetic beheaded by Rama in [Uttara Kanda](../7-uttara-kanda.md), and Shurpanakha's mutilation in [Aranya Kanda](../3-aranya-kanda.md).
- **Abduction (44–46)** - there is no golden deer. Ravana imitates Lakshmana's agreed lion-roar signal to draw Rama away, seizes Sita, and wounds Jatayu. Because of the vow he took before the omniscient **Anantavirya** (Paumacariya 14), he does not force her.
- **Kishkindha (47–48)** - Rama kills the sham Sugriva, **Sahasagati**. Lakshmana lifts the **Kotishila** rock, fulfilling Anantavirya's prophecy that whoever lifts it will slay Ravana.
- **Hanuman (49–54)** - Hanuman (Hanu, Shrishaila) is the son of Pavananjaya and **Anjanasundari** and Sugriva's son-in-law. He has many wives. He delivers Rama's ring and brings back Sita's jewel; Sita will leave only with Rama himself. He wrecks Ravana's garden, but **does not burn Lanka** (Chandra, ch. 6 §6). There is **no bridge**: the army subdues island kings on its way.
- **War (55–73)** - Vibhishana is expelled and joins Rama; Bhamandala brings an army. Ravana's spear *Amoghavijaya* fells Lakshmana, and the bath-water of the princess **Vishalya**, fetched with Kaikeyi's help, cures him (no herb mountain); Vishalya becomes his chief queen. Ravana masters the *Bahurupa vidya* in the shrine of Jina Shanti. In the final battle his severed heads and arms multiply. He hurls his discus at Lakshmana, but it obeys Lakshmana, who kills him with it on the 11th day of the dark half of Jyeshtha.
- **Aftermath in Lanka (74–77)** - Indrajit, Meghavahana, Kumbhakarna (Bhanukarna), Mandodari, Candranakha and others become monks and nuns. Rama declines the throne of Lanka. Rama and Lakshmana marry the princesses betrothed to them on the road.
- **Ayodhya (79–91)** - Bharata and Kaikeyi renounce. At Rama's suggestion **Lakshmana is crowned king of Ayodhya** (85) and conquers half of Bharatavarsha. Shatrughna takes Mathura from Madhu. Rama has **8,000 wives**, with Sita, Prabhavati, Ratinibha and Shridama as chief queens (Paumacariya 91.18); Lakshmana has **16,000**, with Vishalya as chief queen (Chandra, ch. 3 §IX.5). Contrast Valmiki's single wife for each brother.
- **Banishment (92–100)** - when the people murmur, Rama has his general **Kritantavadana** abandon the pregnant Sita in a forest under the pretext of a pilgrimage. King **Vajrajangha** of Pundarikapura shelters her as a brother. Her two sons **Lavana and Ankusha** grow up, fight Rama and Lakshmana, whose weapons fail against them, and are recognized.
- **Fire ordeal and renunciation (101–105)** - Sita returns to Saketa, rebukes Rama, and passes a fire ordeal in which the fire-pit becomes a pool of water. She then refuses to resume worldly life, becomes a nun, and is reborn as a **Pratindra in the Acyuta heaven**, a male god-rank; Hemacandra's version calls this god Sitendra and speaks of him as "he" (Chandra, ch. 2; ch. 6 §10.1 for the fire-pit; Hemacandra VII.10). Contrast [Uttara Kanda](../7-uttara-kanda.md), where Sita enters the earth.
- **Previous births (103, 106)** - the omniscient Sakalabhushana explains the karmic causes. Sita was once **Vegavati/Vedavati**, raped by a king Svayambhu (Shambhu) whose soul became Ravana, and cursed him to be the cause of his death. She had also once falsely accused a monk, and therefore was herself first slandered and then cleared (Chandra, ch. 4 no. 54). Compare the [Vedavati](../../characters/vedavati.md) story at Valmiki 7.17.
- **Lakshmana's death (106–110)** - Bhamandala is killed by lightning, and Hanuman renounces and attains liberation (108). Two gods test the brothers' love by staging lamentation for Rama's death; **Lakshmana dies of the shock** (Chandra, ch. 3 §IX.5). Contrast Valmiki, where Lakshmana is banished over Durvasa and Indra takes him bodily to heaven from the Sarayu (vulgate 7.106, Chandra's numbering; CE 7.96.16–17, GRETIL).
- **Rama's madness and liberation (111–118)** - Rama carries Lakshmana's corpse about until two gods, formerly Kritantavadana and Jatayu, bring him to his senses. He becomes a monk under Suvrata with Shatrughna, Sugriva, Vibhishana and others. He resists the Pratindra Sita's attempt to distract him, attains omniscience at the Kotishila site, and reaches *nirvana* (Paumacariya 117–118.89). The text gives him 17,000 years (118.86) (Chandra, ch. 3 §IX.5).

## Jain Plot Inversions vs. Valmiki (Quick Map)

- **Who kills Ravana** - **Lakshmana (the Vasudeva), not Rama**, in all three Jain currents (Paumacariya, Vasudevahindi, Uttarapurana). The Baladeva Rama never slays the Prativasudeva (Chandra, ch. 6 §6, "nucleus"). Contrast [Yuddha Kanda](../6-yuddha-kanda.md), where Rama himself slays Ravana.
- **Who kills Vali** - nobody (Paumacariya: Vali is a monk, and Rama kills the impostor Sahasagati); **Rama** (Vasudevahindi); **Lakshmana** (Uttarapurana). *Three-way variant within the tradition.*
- **Ravana's character** - a learned, ascetically accomplished Jina-worshipping Vidyadhara king with one fatal passion, restrained even then by his vow (Paumacariya 14, 44–46). He is sympathetic, not a monster.
- **Sita's origin** - either Janaka's own daughter by Videha, with a twin brother Bhamandala (Vimalasuri, Ravisena, Svayambhu, Hemacandra), **or** Ravana and Mandodari's exposed daughter, found by the plough (Vasudevahindi, Gunabhadra, Pushpadanta). *Variant within the tradition.*
- **The fire ordeal & banishment** - only the Vimalasuri line has them. There Sita passes the ordeal and becomes a Jain nun, and the story foregrounds renunciation over wifely vindication. The Vasudevahindi and the Uttarapurana have no banishment and no ordeal (Chandra, ch. 6 §6). Contrast the *agnipariksha* and exile in [Uttara Kanda](../7-uttara-kanda.md).
- **Endings** - **Rama → omniscience and *moksha*.** The place varies: the Kotishila site (Paumacariya 117–118), **Sammeta** (Uttarapurana 68.721), or **Tungagiri** (Somasena's Ramapurana). **Lakshmana → hell** in the Paumacariya (Kulkarni 1990); **Ravana, Lakshmana and Shambuka → the fourth hell** in Hemacandra. **Sita → the Acyuta heaven as a male god** (Paumacariya 105). The epic becomes a teaching on *ahimsa*, karma and renunciation.
- **Polygamy** - Rama has 8,000 wives and Lakshmana 16,000 in the Paumacariya (91.18) and the Uttarapurana (68.666ff) (Chandra, ch. 3 §IX.5). This runs against the Valmiki ideal of Rama's single wife.
- **Miracles removed vs. miracles replaced** - there is no monkey bridge and no flying leap. Instead the Vidyadharas fly in aerial cars and work through *vidyas*. Chandra's general editor, Nathmal Tatia, asks whether this is real rationalization or "sheer substitution of tweedledum for tweedledee" (Chandra, General Editor's Note). [scholarly]

## The Buddhist Rama: Dasaratha Jataka (No. 461)

- **Dasaratha Jataka, Jataka No. 461** - a Pali *jataka* (birth-story of the Bodhisatta) in the *Ekadasaka-nipata* ("Book of Elevens"), *Matuposaka-vagga*, no. 7. Only its **13 verses (*gatha*)** are canonical, in the Khuddaka Nikaya. The prose story around them belongs to the *Jatakatthavannana* commentary (SuttaCentral ja461, Pali text of the Mahasangiti edition; PTS Ja iv 123ff.). It is often counted among the oldest Rama narratives outside the Hindu tradition. [scholarly]
  - *Dating [disputed]:* the verses may be quite old, possibly pre-Christian era. The prose commentary is conventionally dated to the *Jatakatthavannana*, **c. 5th c. CE**. [scholarly]
- **Frame story** - at Jetavana, the Buddha visits a landowner so overcome by grief for his dead father that he neglects all his duties, and tells him this story of the past. At its end the man attains the first path (Ja 461 prose).
- **Setting & cast** - King **Dasaratha rules at Benares (Varanasi)**, not Ayodhya. His eldest queen, chief of 16,000 wives, bears **Rama-pandita** ("Rama the Wise"), **Lakkhana** and a daughter, **Sita**. She dies, and the king makes another queen chief consort; this queen bears **Bharata**. The king offers her a boon. When the boy is seven she asks for the kingdom for him, and he angrily refuses (Ja 461 prose).
- **No abduction, no war, no Ravana** - the conflict is *domestic and pre-emptive*. Fearing that the queen may have his elder sons murdered, Dasaratha sends them to a neighbouring kingdom or the forest. Soothsayers tell him he has twelve years to live, so he tells them to return after **twelve years**. Sita insists on going with her brothers. They live in a hermitage in the **Himalaya**, where Lakkhana and Sita gather fruit and Rama stays at the hut "in place of a father" to them. **There is no Ravana, no Hanuman, no bridge, no Lanka, and no battle whatsoever** (Ja 461 prose; DPPN).
- **Bharata's mission and the sandals** - Dasaratha, pining for his sons, dies in the **ninth year**. The queen orders the royal umbrella raised over Bharata, but the courtiers refuse, saying its lords are in the forest. Bharata goes with an army to bring Rama back and **tells him the true news of the father's death**. Rama refuses to return before the twelve years are up, that is, for three more years. He gives his **straw slippers** to rule in his place. For three years the slippers sit on the throne and clap against each other whenever a case is wrongly judged (Ja 461 prose). Rouse notes that the clapping is an addition to the Ramayana's sandal episode, which he locates at *Ramayana* ii.115 without naming an edition.
- **The parable on grief** - Rama-pandita breaks the news by having Lakkhana and Sita stand in a pond and speaking the first verse; they faint three times. When Bharata asks why Rama does not grieve, he answers in verses 3–12. Nothing that men cannot keep can be held by lamenting. Young and old, foolish and wise, rich and poor all end in death. Tears do not bring back the dead. The wise man scatters grief "like cotton in the wind" and goes on supporting his kin (Ja 461 vv. 1–12, Pali at SuttaCentral; Rouse tr.). The story is a **sermon on impermanence (*anicca*) and equanimity**, not an adventure epic.
- **The Sita-as-sister inversion** - **Sita is Rama's sister, not his wife.** When Rama returns to Benares, his brothers and the court make **Sita queen-consort** and consecrate the two of them together. The prose makes no comment on this (Ja 461 prose). It is the tale's most jarring departure from Valmiki. Its interpretation is left open here. [disputed]
- **The Bodhisatta identification** - in the closing *samodhana*, Suddhodana was Dasaratha, Mahamaya was the mother, **Rahula's mother (Rahulamata) was Sita**, **Ananda was Bharata**, **Sariputta was Lakkhana**, the Buddha's followers were the court, and the Buddha himself was Rama-pandita (Ja 461 prose; Ancient Buddhist Texts cast list; DPPN *Lakkhana*, J.iv.130). Rama reigns from the palace **Sucandaka**.
- **The legendary lifespan** - the final verse, said to be spoken by the Buddha after his awakening, reads *dasa vassasahassani satthi vassasatani ca*, "ten thousand years and sixty hundred", i.e. **16,000 years**, which the prose confirms. Rama is called *kambugivo*, with a neck folded like a conch-shell, a mark of good fortune (Ja 461 v. 13). This is a poetic figure, not chronology. [folk number]

### Verse Parallels with Valmiki

- **The ripe-fruit simile** - Jataka verse 5, *phalanam iva pakkanam niccam patanato bhayam / evam jatana maccanam niccam maranato bhayam* ("as ripe fruit always fears falling, so mortals once born always fear death"). It is close to Valmiki **CE 2.98.17** = vulgate **2.105.17**, *yatha phalanam pakvanam nanyatra patanad bhayam / evam narasya jatasya nanyatra maranad bhayam*. In Valmiki these are Rama's words consoling Bharata at Citrakuta after Dasaratha's death (GRETIL CE, `R_2,098.017`; valmikiramayan.net, Ayodhya 105.17). The setting is the same in both: Rama consoling his family over the father's death. The surrounding Valmiki verses (2.98.15–22) are also a sermon on mortality. Which text borrowed from which, or whether both drew on common gnomic stock, is not settled here. [scholarly]

## Other Buddhist Rama Narratives (Chinese Canon)

- **"King Ten-Chariots" — *Za baozang jing* (T203), story no. 1, 十奢王緣** - translated under the Northern Wei (5th c. CE) by Jijiaye (吉迦夜) and Tanyao. In an age when men lived 10,000 years, King 十奢 ("Ten-Chariots" = Dasharatha) rules Jambudvipa. His four queens bear **Rama** (羅摩), **Lakshmana** (羅漫), **Bharata** (婆羅陀) and a fourth son 滅怨惡 ("destroyer of enemies", i.e. Shatrughna). The king falls ill and installs Rama. The junior queen, who nursed him, claims an earlier promise and has Rama deposed. Lakshmana urges resistance, and Rama refuses to harm his family. The two brothers are sent to the mountains for **twelve years**. Bharata, away abroad, returns, rebukes his mother, and follows with an army. When Rama will not come back, Bharata places Rama's **leather sandals** (革屣) on the throne and bows to them morning and evening. After twelve years the brothers return, defer to each other, and Rama reigns. The moral is filial piety. **There is no Sita and no Ravana** (Taisho T04, no. 203, p. 447a16–c17).
- **The nameless king — *Liudu ji jing* (T152), story no. 46** - translated by Kang Senghui (Wu kingdom, 3rd c. CE). It is told under the perfection of forbearance (*kshanti*). An unnamed Bodhisatta-king abandons his kingdom to a grasping maternal uncle rather than spill his people's blood, and goes to the forest with his queen. A sea-dragon disguised as a meditating brahmin carries her off. A great bird blocks the dragon's path and loses its right wing fighting it, a Jatayu-like figure. The king helps a monkey-king whom *his* uncle has dispossessed, and the monkeys search for the queen. Indra, in the form of a mangy little monkey, has the host carry stones into the sea to make a **causeway** to the island. When the dragon's poison fog fells the army, the little monkey revives it with divine medicine put in their noses. The king shoots the dragon through the chest. Back home, the king says custom requires a wife who has been away so long to be sent back to her family; she calls on **the earth to split** as proof of her chastity, and it does. Identifications: the king is the Buddha, the queen is **Gopi** (俱夷), the uncle is **Devadatta**, Indra is **Maitreya** (Taisho T03, no. 152, fasc. 5, pp. 26c06–27b13). Unlike Ja 461, this story has the abduction, the vulture-figure, the monkey alliance, the bridge, and a chastity test, but no names.
- **Ravana in the Mahayana** - the *Lankavatara Sutra* opens with a chapter in which Ravana, lord of Lanka, invites and questions the Buddha (Shikshananda's translation, T672, ch. 1, 羅婆那王勸請品). It is a Ravana text, not a Rama retelling.

## Why These Matter

- **Demythologization or substitution** - both traditions alter the supernatural in Valmiki. Jains turn demons and monkeys into Vidyadhara humans and drop Rama's divinity, though they keep flying cars and magic lores. The Pali tale drops the war and the miracles entirely. The Chinese T152 story keeps the war and miracles but gives Indra and a dragon the roles of Hanuman and Ravana.
- **Doctrinal recapture** - each tradition makes Rama carry its own ethic. For the Jains it is *ahimsa*, renunciation and karma, so Rama never slays Ravana and ends in *moksha*. For the Buddhists it is *anicca* and equanimity (Ja 461), filial piety (T203), or forbearance (T152), with Rama as the Bodhisatta.
- **Counter-canon evidence** - these texts show the Rama story crossing sectarian boundaries in early India. Chandra, drawing on Bulcke, treats two features as an older stage of the story than Valmiki's: the twelve-year exile (Vasudevahindi, backed by Ja 461) and the absence of Sita's banishment and fire ordeal (Vasudevahindi, Uttarapurana, as in the Mahabharata *Ramopakhyana*) (Chandra, ch. 6 §6). The twelve-year exile also appears in T203. Other departures, such as the Benares setting and Sita as Ravana's daughter (a motif Bulcke traces in Kashmiri, Tibetan, Khotanese and Southeast Asian tellings; Chandra, ch. 3 §II.2), show how far the [Valmiki tradition](../README.md) was from the only version in circulation. [scholarly]
- **Comparative anchor** - read alongside the Hindu regional recensions [Kamban's Iramavataram](./kamban-iramavataram.md) and [Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md), and the [Southeast Asian Ramayanas](./southeast-asian-ramayanas.md), to see the full spectrum of Rama retellings, from divine *bhakti* to rationalist-heterodox.
