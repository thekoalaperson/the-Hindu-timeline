# Jain & Buddhist Ramayanas — Paumacariya and the Dasaratha Jataka

Deep-dive: [Ramayana](../README.md) · [Deep Dives](../../README.md) · [Master Index](../../../README.md)
The *heterodox* Rama traditions — the **Jain** retellings (Vimalasuri's *Paumacariya* and its lineage) and the **Buddhist** *Dasaratha Jataka* — which systematically rationalize, demythologize, and re-purpose Valmiki's epic. The Jain texts subordinate Rama to a Jain cosmology of *shalakapurushas* and reassign Ravana's death to Lakshmana; the Buddhist Pali tale strips the war entirely and turns the story into a parable on impermanence. These are not Hindu *recensions* but rival reworkings by rival traditions — kept here under "recensions" for ease of comparison.

> Two clocks: the *story* is set in (Jain) Rama's era or (Buddhist) a past life of the Bodhisatta; the *texts* are academic-dated literary works (Jain ~3rd–13th c. CE; *Dasaratha Jataka* core verses possibly pre-Christian era, prose commentary ~5th c. CE). This file dates the **texts** and catalogs the **variant plots**, not the events.

## The Jain Rama: Cosmological Frame

- **Rama as a *shalakapurusha*** - In Jain universal history, 63 *shalakapurushas* ("illustrious/torch-bearer persons") recur each half-cycle of time; Rama, Lakshmana and Ravana are slotted into the triadic set of 9 **Baladevas** (Balabhadras), 9 **Vasudevas**, and 9 **Prativasudevas** (Wikipedia, *Rama in Jainism*; herenow4u, *Jain Versions of Ramayana*).
  - **Rama = 8th Baladeva/Balabhadra** - the gentle, non-violent elder brother who does not kill; ultimately renounces and attains liberation (*moksha*).
  - **Lakshmana = 8th Vasudeva** - the martial half-brother who wields the discus (*chakra*) and is fated to slay the Prativasudeva — and to fall to hell for that violence.
  - **Ravana = 8th Prativasudeva** - the antagonist destined by the karmic structure to be killed by his Vasudeva counterpart, Lakshmana — never by Rama.
- **Rakshasas and Vanaras as Vidyadharas** - the "demons" and "monkeys" are demythologized into the **Vidyadharas**, a civilized, *vidya* (magical-science)-wielding human race; Ravana is their noble, devout-Jain king, and Hanuman and the vanaras are Vidyadhara nobles bearing monkey-banners/totems, not literal apes (Live History India, *Jain Ramayana*; wisdomlib, *Paumacariya critical study*). See contrast with the divine/monstrous framing in [Yuddha Kanda](../6-yuddha-kanda.md) and [Uttara Kanda](../7-uttara-kanda.md).
- **Rationalist polemic** - Vimalasuri explicitly frames his text as a *correction* of the "lies" (e.g., monkeys building bridges, demons eating humans, Ravana's ten literal heads) told by the Hindu poets; the Jain version claims to give the *true*, naturalistic account (wisdomlib, *Vimalasuri's Acquaintance with the Valmiki Ramayana*). [scholarly]
  - **"Dasamukha" rationalized** - Ravana is called *Dasamukha* ("ten-faced") not because he has ten heads but because a brilliant nine-gem pearl necklace given at birth reflected his face nine times, showing ten faces in all (Wikipedia, *Rama in Jainism*).

## Jain Versions — Text by Text

- **Paumacariya (Vimalasuri)** - the **earliest extant Jain Ramayana** and the fountainhead of the whole tradition; *Paümacariya* = "Deeds of Padma" (Padma = Rama's Jain name). Composed in **Maharashtri Prakrit**, ~10,000 verses in 118 *parvans* (wisdomlib; herenow4u).
  - *Dating [disputed]:* Vimalasuri's own colophon dates it 530 years after Mahavira's *nirvana* (~3rd c. CE by Jain reckoning); many scholars place the surviving text rather later, **c. 4th–5th c. CE**. Jain tradition vs. academic dating diverge sharply here. [scholarly]
  - **Sect** - generally associated with the **Shvetambara** tradition (later Digambara works recast its details).
  - **Plot keystones** - Rakshasas/Vanaras are Vidyadharas; Ravana is a pious Jain king and accomplished ascetic with a fatal flaw (lust for Sita); **Lakshmana kills Ravana**; Rama (Padma) renounces and attains *moksha*; Lakshmana and Ravana go to hell; Sita renounces as a nun and ascends to heaven.
  - **Kaikeyi sympathetic** - her boon is re-motivated; the harsh Hindu portrayal is softened.
  - **Vali becomes a monk** - in Paumacariya, after the Vali–Sugriva conflict **Vali renounces, becomes a *muni*, and attains nirvana** rather than being shot dead by Rama — a pointed reversal of [Kishkindha Kanda](../4-kishkindha-kanda.md) (herenow4u).
- **Padma-purana / Padmacarita (Ravisena)** - **Sanskrit**, ~**7th–8th c. CE** (often dated **678 CE**); a near-replica of Vimalasuri's Prakrit that recasts the story for a **Digambara** audience without acknowledging its debt to Vimalasuri, adding ornate descriptions and Digambara doctrinal coloring (herenow4u; Wikipedia). It became the standard authoritative Jain Rama for later Digambara writers.
- **Paumacariu (Svayambhu / Svayambhudeva)** - **Apabhramsha** verse, **later 8th c. CE**, by the lay poet (*shravaka*) Svayambhu; closely follows Vimalasuri and Ravisena (herenow4u).
  - *Variant (parentage):* Rama is son of **Aparajita**, Lakshmana son of **Sumitra**, and **Sita is the daughter of Janaka** — i.e., it keeps the orthodox Janaka-birth of Sita, unlike the Gunabhadra line below (herenow4u).
- **Uttara-purana (Gunabhadra)** - **Sanskrit**, **9th c. CE** (c. 879 CE), a Digambara *purana*; gives a strikingly **divergent, condensed** Rama narrative that does **not** derive from Vimalasuri (Wikipedia; herenow4u).
  - *Variant (Dasharatha's city):* Dasharatha reigns at **Varanasi (Benares)**, not Ayodhya.
  - *Variant (parentage):* queen **Subala** bears Rama; **Kaikeyi** bears Lakshmana.
  - *Variant (Sita = Ravana's daughter):* **Sita is born of Ravana and Mandodari** and then abandoned (exposed) because of a prophecy that she will cause the family's destruction — making Ravana's later abduction of her an unwitting near-incest. This is the most notorious Jain inversion. [late text]
  - *Variant (Vali's death):* **Lakshmana kills Vali** here (versus Vali-as-renouncer in Paumacariya).
- **Sanghadasa's version** - a condensed Shvetambara account in which **Sita is Mandodari's child**, exposed because of a destruction-prophecy — a parallel to the Gunabhadra "Sita as Ravana's daughter" motif (Wikipedia). [scholarly]
- **Pampa Ramayana / Ramachandra-charita-purana (Nagachandra)** - **Kannada**, **12th c. CE** (c. 1149 CE); the first major Jain Rama in Kannada, source-stream for the later Kumudendu (Wikipedia, *Hoysala literature*; *Versions of the Ramayana*).
- **Kumudendu Ramayana (Kumudendu)** - **Kannada**, **1275 CE**, in *shatpadi* (six-line) metre, by the Jain poet Kumudendu, influenced by Nagachandra's Pampa Ramayana; a Hoysala-era Jain retelling carrying the Vidyadhara/Lakshmana-kills-Ravana framework into medieval Kannada (Wikipedia, *Hoysala literature*; *Versions of the Ramayana*).
  - *Note:* detailed plot specifics of Kumudendu's text are thinly documented in accessible sources — its narrative is generally taken as within the Jain (Vimalasuri/Ravisena-descended) tradition. [open roster — variants under-documented]

## Jain Plot Inversions vs. Valmiki (Quick Map)

- **Who kills Ravana** - **Lakshmana (the Vasudeva), not Rama** — across the entire Jain tradition; Rama (Balabhadra) must remain non-killing. Contrast [Yuddha Kanda](../6-yuddha-kanda.md) where Rama himself slays Ravana.
- **Ravana's character** - a learned, devout, ascetically accomplished Jain Vidyadhara king with one fatal passion — sympathetic, not a cackling demon.
- **Sita's origin** - either Janaka's daughter (Vimalasuri/Svayambhu line) **or** the exposed daughter of Ravana & Mandodari (Gunabhadra/Sanghadasa line). *Variant within the tradition.*
- **The fire ordeal & banishment** - present but re-purposed; Sita ultimately becomes a Jain nun (*aryika*) and attains heaven, foregrounding renunciation over wifely vindication. Contrast the *agnipariksha* and exile in [Uttara Kanda](../7-uttara-kanda.md).
- **Endings** - **Rama → kevala-jnana (omniscience) and moksha** (traditionally at **Tungi Giri**); **Lakshmana and Ravana → hell** (for their violence/passion); **Sita → heaven**. The whole epic is reframed as a teaching on *ahimsa*, karma, and renunciation.
- **Polygamy detail** - some Jain accounts give Rama many wives (Sita as chief), against the Hindu *ekapatni-vrata* (one-wife vow) ideal. [scholarly]

## The Buddhist Rama: Dasaratha Jataka (No. 461)

- **Dasaratha Jataka, Jataka No. 461** - a Pali *jataka* (birth-story of the Bodhisatta) in the **Khuddaka Nikaya** of the Sutta Pitaka; one of the oldest *non-Hindu* Rama narratives and arguably among the oldest Rama stories in writing (Wikipedia, *Dasaratha Jataka*; ancient-buddhist-texts.net, *Ja 461*).
  - *Dating [disputed]:* the **verses (*gatha*)** may be quite old (pre-Christian era); the **prose framing/commentary** is conventionally dated to the *Jatakatthavannana* compilation, **c. 5th c. CE** (Buddhaghosa's era). [scholarly]
- **Setting & cast** - King **Dasaratha rules at Benares (Varanasi)**, not Ayodhya; by his chief queen he has **Rama-pandita** ("Rama the Wise"), **Lakkhana** (Lakshmana), and a **daughter Sita**. After the queen's death he takes a second queen who bears **Bharata** and demands the kingdom for her son (ancient-buddhist-texts.net; Wikipedia).
- **No abduction, no war, no Ravana** - the entire conflict is *domestic and pre-emptive*: to avert a succession war, Dasaratha sends his three eldest children into the forest for **twelve years**, to return after his death. **There is no Ravana, no Hanuman, no bridge, no Lanka, and no battle whatsoever** (Wikipedia; aimwell.org, *DPPN*).
- **The Sita-as-sister inversion** - **Sita is Rama's sister, not his wife.** In the forest Rama lives as an ascetic while Lakkhana and Sita provision him. On the father's death (the throne-grabbing widow having died early), Rama returns and rules — and, in the older Indian royal idiom of the text, **takes Sita as his queen-consort**, his consecrated chief queen, even as she is figured as his sister. This sibling-couple framing is the tale's most jarring departure from Valmiki (Wikipedia; aimwell.org). [folk/early text]
- **The parable on grief** - the doctrinal core: when news (initially false) of the father's death reaches the forest, Lakkhana and Sita are overcome but **Rama-pandita remains composed**, teaching that all that is born must die, that lamentation cannot restore the dead, and that the wise mourn not. The story is essentially a **sermon on impermanence (*anicca*) and equanimity**, not an adventure epic (ancient-buddhist-texts.net; Wikipedia).
- **The Bodhisatta identification** - in the closing *samodhana* (connection), the Buddha reveals: **Rama-pandita was the Bodhisatta** (the Buddha-to-be); **Lakkhana = Sariputta**, **Sita = the mother of Rahula (Bimba/Yasodhara)** or Uppalavanna in some readings, **the father Dasaratha = Suddhodana**, etc. (varies by edition). [variant identifications]
- **The legendary lifespan** - the verses close with Rama ruling for a fabulous span — **"sixty hundred years and ten thousand more,"** read as ~**16,000 years** of reign (some renderings give the inflated ~600,000+); a poetic flourish, not chronology (ancient-buddhist-texts.net). [folk number]

## Why These Matter

- **Demythologization** - both traditions strip the supernatural from Valmiki: Jains rationalize demons/monkeys into humans and remove Rama's divinity; Buddhists remove the war and miracles entirely.
- **Doctrinal recapture** - each makes Rama a vehicle for its own ethic: Jain *ahimsa* + renunciation + karma (hence Rama never kills and ends in *moksha*), Buddhist *anicca* + equanimity (hence the grief-parable, the Bodhisatta).
- **Counter-canon evidence** - they demonstrate the Rama story's circulation **across sectarian boundaries** in early India and preserve archaic variants (Benares setting; Sita as sister or as Ravana's daughter) that the orthodox [Valmiki tradition](../README.md) suppressed. [scholarly]
- **Comparative anchor** - read alongside the Hindu regional recensions [Kamban's Iramavataram](./kamban-iramavataram.md) and [Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) to see the full spectrum of Rama retellings, from divine-bhakti to rationalist-heterodox.
