The **Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ**, properly the **Śrīrām Pā̃cālī** ("Rāma sung-narrative"), is the **Bengali retelling** of the Rāma story attributed to **Kṛttibās Ojhā** (conventionally c. 1381–1461 CE; see dating below). It is usually called the **first Bengali rendering of Vālmīki's Sanskrit Rāmāyaṇa**. It is *not* a literal translation but a **folk-bhakti reworking** that recasts the epic in the idiom, emotion, social texture and ritual life of medieval Bengal. Dinesh Chandra Sen called it "by far the most popular book in Bengal" and "the Bible of the people of the Gangetic Valley" (Sen 1911, p. 170). It is the **charter-text of Bengali Durgā Pūjā** through its *akāl-bodhan* episode. It survives in **over 1,500 manuscripts**, most of them from the last quarter of the 18th century or later, and scholars hold the original text of Kṛttibās to be unrecoverable (Benoît 2007, p. 168).

**How this page cites the text.** Because no critical edition exists, episode citations below name a **printed vulgate**: *Kṛttivās Rāmāyaṇ*, ed. Pūrṇacandra De (Calcutta: Chakravarty Chatterjee, 1949), read in its archive.org OCR and cited as "**Dey 1949**, kāṇḍa, chapter, page". The 108-lotus count was also checked in two other printings (Gurudas Chattopadhyay, 1313 BS ≈ 1906; DLI copy dated 1940). These are 20th-century descendants of the 19th-century printed text, not Kṛttibās's own words.

## Author, date & patronage

- **Kṛttibās Ojhā** - medieval Bengali poet, of a **Kulīn Brāhmaṇ** family of the **Mukhaṭī** (Mukherjee) line at **Phulia village, Nadia district** (West Bengal). His own verse autobiography (*ātma-paricaya*), printed at the head of the vulgate, names his father **Banamālī**, mother **Mālinī**, grandfather **Murāri Ojhā**, and the ancestor **Narasiṃha Ojhā** who settled at Phulia after a disturbance in East Bengal. Kṛttibās was one of six brothers, with a half-sister by a stepmother (Dey 1949, prefatory autobiography; trans. Sen 1920, pp. 153–156).
  - *Variant (Banglapedia):* birthplace alternatively given as **Phulia near Premtali, Rajshahi district** (now Bangladesh) rather than Nadia — [disputed].
  - *The name:* Kṛttivāsa ("clad in a hide") is an epithet of Śiva. In the autobiography, Murāri named the child on the eve of leaving on pilgrimage to the south. Wikipedia names the shrine as **Candaneśvar** in Odisha (Sen 1920, p. 156; Wikipedia).
- **Education** - by the autobiography, when he had finished his eleventh year and entered his twelfth, he crossed the **Baḍa Gaṅgā (Padmā)** to study in **North Bengal** (Varendra); Banglapedia agrees. He then sought a post as royal paṇḍita at the court of Gauḍa (Sen 1920, pp. 156–157; Banglapedia).
  - *Variant (Wikipedia):* sent to **Nabadwip** at age 11. The autobiography itself says north across the Padmā.
- **Royal patronage** - the autobiography describes the audience at Gauḍa in the month of Māgha. He sent in five Sanskrit verses and passed **nine gates**, then recited seven ślokas before the king. The king had him **garlanded** (*puṣpamāl*), **Kedāra Khān** poured **sandal-water** on his head, and the king gave a **silk wrap** (*pāṭer pāchaḍā*). When offered more, Kṛttibās refused gifts ("I take nothing from anyone… glory alone is what I seek"), and the king asked him to compose the Rāmāyaṇa (*rāmāyaṇa racite karilā anurodh*) (Dey 1949, autobiography; trans. Sen 1920, pp. 157–160).
  - *Variant (which Gauḍa monarch) [disputed]:* Kṛttibās never names the king. The candidates are:
    - **Rājā Gaṇeśa** (r. c. 1415–18): favoured by Sen and H. E. Stapleton in *Dacca Review* (1912–13), because the court seems Hindu-led with no Muslim officer named (Sen 1920, pp. 161–167).
    - **Sultan Jalāluddīn** (r. c. 1418–31): Banglapedia's alternative, spelled there "Jalaluddin Mahmud Shah".
    - **Ruknuddīn Bārbak Shāh**: argued by the historian **R. C. Majumdar** (Wikipedia, "Krittibas Ojha"). The old page called this a "popular tradition"; that was a misattribution.
    - **Kaṃsanārāyaṇa of Tāhirpur**: Sen's own 1911 proposal, which he withdrew in 1920 because the rāja's pedigree places him about 150 years too late (Sen 1920, p. 161).
- **Dating — two clocks** - *Authorial time:* the conventional "c. 1381–1461" comes from Sukumar Sen and Banglapedia, but the birth year is unsettled [disputed]:
  - Sen (1920) counted generations from an ancestor at Ballāla Sena's court and arrived at the late 14th century. Stapleton put the birth "not later than 1380".
  - Jogeśacandra Rāy calculated from the autobiography's "Sunday, Śrīpañcamī, the end of Māgha" and found only two possible dates, Śaka 1269 and Śaka 1354 (Sen prints these as 1337 and 1432 CE), preferring the second. Sen notes that the reading *pūrṇa Māgha* is doubtful, so "the date remains unsettled" (Sen 1920, pp. 163–167).
  - Benoît puts Kṛttibās in the last third of the 15th century. He adds that the link between the poet and the text now under his name is probably "purely nominal" (Benoît 2007, p. 168).
  - Wikipedia's article on the poem calls him "fourteenth-century".
  - Sen also records that Kṛttibās died childless and was in poor health by the end of the Araṇya-kāṇḍa (Sen 1920, p. 168).
  - *Scripture time:* the narrated events sit near the close of [Tretā Yuga](../../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga. Kṛttibās retells the story; he does not move its cosmic setting.
  - *Priority [disputed]:* the poem is often called the oldest Indo-Aryan vernacular Rāmāyaṇa, but **Mādhava Kandalī's Assamese Rāmāyaṇa** (mid-14th c.) contests that claim (Benoît 2007, p. 168).

## Language, form & meter

- **Middle Bengali vernacular** - composed in the **pā̃cālī** (sung-recitation) genre of Middle Bengali devotional literature, designed to be **chanted/sung aloud** to ordinary listeners. The poet's autobiography calls it a *gīt* (song) composed "*lok bujhābār tare*", "to make people understand" (Dey 1949, autobiography). This parallels Tulsīdās's Awadhī [Rāmcaritmānas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) and Kambaṉ's Tamil [Irāmāvatāram](./kamban-iramavataram.md) in the wider vernacular-bhakti wave [scholarly].
- **Pāyār meter** - the backbone is the **pāyār** couplet, each line of **fourteen syllables (akṣaras)**, rhyming in pairs. It is the standard narrative meter of Bengali kāvya and gives an easy, oral, memorizable flow (Banglapedia). Some passages switch to the **tripadī** stanza, for example Brahmā's counsel in Laṅkā ch. 94, Rāma's hymn in ch. 99 and the Goddess's reply in ch. 102. Other hymns in the same episode, such as ch. 97, stay in pāyār (Dey 1949, pp. 516–523).
- **"Lively and familiar" register** - Benoît says Kṛttibās turns Vālmīki's hieratic epic into a narrative that is "sung, lively and familiar", meant to instruct and entertain common Bengali audiences rather than to preserve courtly grandeur (Benoît 2007, pp. 167–168).

## Structure — seven kāṇḍas (Bengali names)

- **Seven books, following Vālmīki's frame** - **Ādi-kāṇḍa**, **Ajodhyā-kāṇḍa**, **Araṇya-kāṇḍa**, **Kiṣkindhā-kāṇḍa**, **Sundar-kāṇḍa**, **Laṅkā-kāṇḍa** (Vālmīki's Yuddha), and **Uttar-kāṇḍa**. This is the same seven-kāṇḍa architecture as [Vālmīki](../README.md), but heavily re-narrated rather than translated. The autobiography itself speaks of composing "seven kāṇḍas" (*sāt kāṇḍa*).
- **Text-critical caution** - over **1,500 manuscripts** survive, rarely earlier than the last quarter of the 18th century. They differ a great deal, notably by region of Bengal, and the oldest fragments are at least two centuries later than the poet's estimated date. Attempts at scholarly editions have been "without convincing result", and Benoît concludes "it is impossible to form an idea of the original text of Kṛttibās" (Benoît 2007, p. 168). Sukumar Sen judged that by the end of the 17th century the text "retained nothing of the original except the name of the poet and perhaps a few sporadic couplets" (quoted in Benoît 2007, n. 1). The printed *vulgate* is a layered, evolving tradition, not a fixed authorial work [scholarly] [disputed].

## Folk-bhakti coloring — what makes it Bengali

- **Domestication of the epic** - Rāma, Sītā and the court are re-imagined with Bengali household customs, kinship sentiment, women's rituals (*strī-ācār*), folk speech and local color. The divine figures become intimate family figures, which is the keynote of the poem's popularity.
- **Bhakti devotionalism** - the poem foregrounds loving surrender and the moral exemplarity of Rāma over martial grandeur. This places it in the Bengali **Vaiṣṇava-bhakti** ferment that would crest with Caitanya. It also absorbed Śākta (Goddess) and Śaiva colouring. Sen notes Rāvaṇa and his clan cast as Śiva-devotees, Kauśalyā as a worshipper of Śiva and Pārvatī, and Vibhīṣaṇa's defection sanctioned by **Kubera** and by **Śiva** (Sen 1920, pp. 120–121; Dey 1949, Sundar-kāṇḍa chs. 40–41 titles).
- **The redeemed Rākṣasa** - Sen's central reading is that the Laṅkā-kāṇḍa "as we get it to-day" turns the war into a field of devotion. Rākṣasa warriors such as Taraṇīsen, Vīrabāhu and Atikāya fight Rāma while adoring him. Even Rāvaṇa is pitied when Caṇḍī, "to whom he had clung throughout as a child to its mother", finally forsakes him. Sen ties this to the Caitanya-era stories of converted sinners such as Jagāi and Mādhāi (Sen 1920, pp. 146–149, 100–101) [scholarly].
  - *Authorship of these passages [scholarly] [disputed]:* Sen held the Rākṣasa-bhakti passages to be probably **later interpolations**. Some Kṛttivāsa manuscripts from the **Tipperah (Tripura) district** in the Asiatic Society of Bengal lack them. He suspected **Śaṅkara Kavicandra** of Lego (Burdwan, 16th c.), a Bhāgavata translator who also wrote a Rāmāyaṇa. He left open whether Kavicandra or Kṛttibās wrote the Taraṇīsen and Vīrabāhu episodes (Sen 1920, pp. 99–101, 149).
- **Original/folk episodes absent from Vālmīki** - the printed text contains beloved sub-tales not found in the Sanskrit epic [folk]. Its 1949 editor lists the main "products of Kṛttibās's imagination" as **Taraṇīsen, Vīrabāhu, Hanumān holding the Sun under his arm, Mahīrāvaṇa, Ahīrāvaṇa, and the lotus stolen during the Devī-pūjā** (Dey 1949, editor's introduction).
  - *Akāl-bodhan* — Rāma's untimely autumn worship of **Durgā** before Rāvaṇa's death (see below); the signature Bengali addition.
  - *Mahīrāvaṇa and Ahīrāvaṇa* (Laṅkā chs. 79–87, Dey 1949, pp. ~490–505) [folk] [late text]:
    - **Mahīrāvaṇa** is Rāvaṇa's **son**, born in the netherworld (*mahī*) during Rāvaṇa's conquests, and lord of **Pātāla**. Vibhīṣaṇa, warning the camp, calls him Mandodarī's son. He is a worshipper of **Kālī**, and his house goddess is named **Yogādyā** in the vulgate.
    - Summoned after Lakṣmaṇa's recovery, he tries to enter Rāma's guarded fort disguised in turn as Daśaratha, Bharata, Kauśalyā and Janaka. He finally gets in as **Vibhīṣaṇa**, casts a sleep-spell, and carries Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa down to Pātāla to be sacrificed to Kālī.
    - **Hanumān** follows, enters the shrine as a **fly** and whispers to the image. The goddess herself teaches him the trick. The brothers say they "do not know how to bow", Mahī prostrates to show them, and Hanumān takes the sword from the image and **cuts him in two** (Dey 1949, Laṅkā ch. 86: *lāph diyā mahīre karila duikhān*; full translation in Sen 1920, pp. 255–282).
    - **Ahīrāvaṇa** is a different figure: **Mahīrāvaṇa's son**. Hanumān's kick makes the fighting widowed queen, ten months pregnant, deliver on the spot, and the newborn, eight-armed and four-headed, attacks at once. Hanumān dashes him against a rock (Dey 1949, Laṅkā ch. 87: *mahīrāvaṇer beṭā se ahirāvaṇ*).
    - *Correction:* the older form of this page made Mahī/Ahi Rāvaṇa a "brother/ally" whose "life resides in bees". The Kṛttivāsī vulgate has neither detail. Any bee-life motif belongs to other tellings and is not attested here.
    - *Status:* Sen calls the *Mahīrāvaṇer Pālā* "a folk-tale pure and simple" and "a later addition to the epic", with roots in the Tantric Kālī-worship also seen in the Dharma-maṅgala poems (Sen 1920, pp. 253–254, 118).
    - *Variant (Balarāma Bandyopādhyāya's Rāmāyaṇa preface):* after Mahī's death Hanumān carried the goddess Yugādyā (Yogādyā) to **Kṣīragrām** in Bengal, where she is still worshipped (Sen 1920, p. 118) [regional].
  - *Taraṇīsen* (Laṅkā ch. 53, Dey 1949, p. 433) [folk]:
    - He is the son of **Vibhīṣaṇa** and **Saramā**, and a secret Rāma-bhakta who goes to battle with **Rāma's name written on his body and chariot**. He seeks death at Rāma's hand as a passage to Goloka.
    - Vibhīṣaṇa whispers to Rāma that only the **brahmāstra** can kill him, a boon from Brahmā. Rāma looses it and cuts him in two, and the severed head goes on saying "Rāma, Rāma" (*taraṇīr kāṭā-muṇḍa "rām rām" bale*).
    - Only then does Vibhīṣaṇa reveal that the dead warrior is his son. Rāma weeps and reproaches him: had he known, he would not have fought the boy.
    - Taraṇīsen and Vīrabāhu do not occur in Tulsīdās (Sen 1920, p. 106).
  - *Vīrabāhu* (Laṅkā ch. 54, Dey 1949, p. 440) — another **son of Rāvaṇa**, killed with **Dhūmrākṣa** and **Bhasmalocana**. Rāvaṇa himself lists him with Indrajit among his fallen sons (Sen 1920, p. 258). Dhūmrākṣa is a Vālmīki figure. Sen counts Vīrabāhu, with Taraṇīsen and Mahīrāvaṇa, among the characters that are "altogether new" (Sen 1920, p. 181) [folk].
  - *Hanumān and the Sun* (Laṅkā ch. 73, p. 480) — Rāvaṇa orders the Sun to rise at midnight so that the herb-errand for Lakṣmaṇa will fail. Hanumān holds the Sun under his arm until the work is done, and frees him in ch. 78. Nearby are the killing of **Kālanemi** (ch. 72), whose daydream of carving up half of Laṅkā is a famous comic soliloquy (Sen 1920, pp. 181–182), and Hanumān's **testing of Bharata** (ch. 75) [folk].
  - *Bhagīratha's birth* (Ādi ch. 12, "*gaṅgār utpatti o bhagīrather janma*", Dey 1949):
    - **King Dilīpa** dies childless while searching for the Gaṅgā. **Śiva** comes to his **two widows** and tells them to make love with each other, and one of them conceives. The child is born a boneless lump of flesh. On Vasiṣṭha's advice they leave him on the road, where the crooked-limbed sage **Aṣṭāvakra** blesses him whole.
    - He is named *Bhagīratha* because he was "born of two *bhaga*s" (*bhage-bhage janma-hetu bhagīrath-nām*).
    - **Ruth Vanita** has drawn attention to it as a rare premodern account of two women conceiving a child. An English rendering by Kumkum Roy appears in *Same-Sex Love in India* (2000) [folk] [disputed].
    - *Textual status:* Wikipedia, citing Mandakranta Bose, says the episode is in most of the textual tradition but absent from Harekrishna Mukhopadhyaya's edition (Sahitya Samsad, 1957). Nalinikanta Bhaṭṭaśālī's 1936 Ādikāṇḍa, from a single manuscript, has another version.
    - Like everything in the poem, it cannot be proven to be Kṛttibās's own. The earlier claim that it is "almost certainly a later interpolation" was not supported by any source and has been removed.
    - *Vālmīki contrast:* Bhagīratha is born in the ordinary way to the living Dilīpa, who crowns him before dying of illness (VR 1.42.7–10; CE 1.41).
    - Cross-ref the [Ikṣvāku/Sūryavaṃśa lineage](../../../03-lineages-vamsha).
- **Lava–Kuśa / Uttar-kāṇḍa** - the Bengali tradition keeps and elaborates the **twin sons Lava and Kuśa**, Sītā's exile to Vālmīki's hermitage, and Sītā's return to the earth.
  - The printed text adds a full **war of the twins against Ayodhyā**. Lava and Kuśa tie up the sacrificial horse, then lay low **Śatrughna**, then **Bharata and Lakṣmaṇa**, and finally defeat **Rāma**, who faints. Sītā prepares to enter fire, and Vālmīki revives the fallen before the twins sing the Rāmāyaṇa and Sītā enters Pātāla (Dey 1949, Uttara chs. 57–67).
  - The 1949 editor points to the **Jaimini-Bhārata** (Jaiminīya Aśvamedha), chs. 30–36, as the source of the battle (Dey 1949, editor's introduction). Sen notes that it "does not form a part of the Sanskrit Uttara Kāṇḍa" (Sen 1920, pp. 68–69).
  - *Vālmīki contrast:* in the Critical Edition the twins only recite. They refuse Rāma's gold (CE 7.85.14), Rāma recognises them (7.86.2, 7.88.4), and Sītā's oath opens the earth (7.88.10–14). There is no battle.

## Episodes reworked from Vālmīki — a short roster

Each item gives the Kṛttivāsī form (Dey 1949) and then the Vālmīki form, kept separate.

- **Vālmīki as Ratnākara** — Ādi chs. 2–3: the robber **Ratnākara** is cleansed by repeating Rāma's name and is named Vālmīki by Brahmā. Sen notes the story is not in the epic and that it seems indigenous to Bengal (Sen 1920, p. 125). The nameless Sanskrit parallel is in the [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](./adhyatma-ramayana.md) (AR 2.6.64–86). See also [Vālmīki–Ratnākara](../../characters/valmiki-ratnakara-conversion.md).
- **Kaikeyī's two boons, earned separately** — Ādi chs. 32–34 (pp. 55–56):
  - After Daśaratha kills **Śambara** for Indra, Kaikeyī heals his weapon-wounds with her healing skill, and he grants her one boon.
  - Later a festering sore grows under his **nail**. Padmākara, son of Dhanvantari, offers two cures: eat snail broth, or have someone suck the nail. Kaikeyī sucks it, earning the second boon.
  - She defers both boons ("two boons, at two times, stay with you").
  - *Vālmīki:* she twice carries the wounded, unconscious king out of the Śambara battle, and both boons are given together (VR 2.9.11–17). (This bears out, in outline, what [Kaikeyī](../../characters/kaikeyi.md) reported from a popular summary; that summary's "mantra" detail is not what Dey 1949 shows.)
- **Tārā's curse** — Kiṣkindhā ch. 13: the widowed Tārā curses Rāma. He will win Sītā back but not keep her; he will weep for her; and since he killed the monkey-king without fault, "Rāma, in another birth he will kill you" (*māribe tomāre rām, sei janmāntare*). *Vālmīki:* Tārā laments and asks Rāma to kill her with the same arrow, but pronounces no curse (VR 4.24; the translator notes this sarga is absent from the critical editions). See [Tārā](../../characters/tara-vanara-queen.md).
- **Rāvaṇa's death-arrow** — Laṅkā ch. 104 (p. 524). Brahmā made one arrow as Rāvaṇa's only death, and it is kept hidden by **Mandodarī**. **Hanumān**, disguised (Sen: as a Brahmin astrologer), coaxes it out of her (Dey 1949, Laṅkā ch. 104; Sen 1920, p. 51). The chapter itself records variants: "in another opinion" the arrow must pierce the navel, or Śiva protects Rāvaṇa in battle (*āra ek rūp kathā kahe matāntare*). Just before this (ch. 103), Hanumān, as a fly, spoils **Bṛhaspati's Caṇḍī-recitation** for Rāvaṇa's safety by licking away two letters (*dvi-akṣar*) of a verse (ch. 103 title: *hanumān-kartṛk caṇḍīr śloka lop-karaṇ*). *Vālmīki:* Mātali reminds Rāma of the Brahmā-made arrow Agastya had given him, and it splits Rāvaṇa's heart (VR 6.108.1–18; CE 6.97.1–17).
- **Rāvaṇa teaches statecraft** — Laṅkā ch. 106 (p. 528): Rāma goes to the dying Rāvaṇa to learn *rāja-nīti*. Not in Vālmīki; Sen notes that this deathbed advice is "not to be found in the Sanskrit epic" (Sen 1920, p. 124).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's fourteen-year vow** — Uttara chs. 2–3. Agastya reveals, to Rāma's disbelief, that Lakṣmaṇa never slept, never ate and never looked at Sītā's face for fourteen years. The proofs given are these:
  - He recognized only her **anklets** (Kṛttibās VII.2.32–35).
  - He drove away sleep with his arrows, and sleep returned only at the coronation, when he dropped Rāma's parasol.
  - The uneaten fruit, brought back in a quiver, is seven days short, and he accounts for each of those days.
  - The episode is analysed in Benoît 2007, pp. 178–180. *Vālmīki:* the anklet verse ("I know not the armlets… I know the anklets, from bowing daily at her feet") is in the vulgate (VR 4.6.22). It is **not in the Critical Edition**, whose Kiṣkindhā 6 has no such verse.
- **Other local colour** (chapter titles, Dey 1949): Śani's gaze on Daśaratha and his friendship with Jaṭāyu (Ādi 27–28); the boy Daśaratha shoots is named **Sindhu**, son of the blind sage Andhaka (Ādi 30–31); Sītā offers piṇḍa to Daśaratha at **Gayā** and curses the Brahmin, the tulasī and the Phalgu river while blessing the banyan (Ajodhyā 21–23); Rāma curses the **cakravāka** pair (Araṇya 25); Hanumān tears open his chest to show Rāma's name in his bones (Laṅkā 128) [folk].

## The Akāl-bodhan — Rāma's autumn worship of Durgā (the Durgā Pūjā charter)

Text: Dey 1949, Laṅkā-kāṇḍa chs. 94–102, pp. 516–523, read against the 1906 and 1940 printings for the lotus count.

- **What "akāl-bodhan" means** - *akāl-bodhan* is the **"untimely awakening/invocation"** of the Goddess.
  - *The text's own reason:* Rāma objects that **spring is the proper season** for her worship (*basanta śuddha-samay*), so this pūjā is *akāl*. The prescribed day for the awakening, the kṛṣṇa-navamī, has already passed, and so has the pratipad on which King **Suratha**'s rite begins. Brahmā therefore prescribes awakening her by the **ṣaṣṭhī-kalpa** (Dey 1949, Laṅkā ch. 94).
  - *Ritual gloss (popular, attributed):* the goddess's canonical worship is the **vernal Bāsantī Pūjā**, while autumn falls in **Dakṣiṇāyana**, the gods' "night" when deities sleep, hence "out of season" (hṛdayāravinde). The Dakṣiṇāyana argument is not stated in the chapter read here.
- **The crisis** - in battle, the Goddess shelters Rāvaṇa (Rāma: "*rakṣila rāvaṇe maheśvarī*"), and Rāma and his army weep. **Indra** petitions **Brahmā** (Dey 1949, Laṅkā ch. 93–94). In Sen's paraphrase, Rāma alone sees the dark-blue Mother beside Rāvaṇa, encouraging him (Sen 1920, pp. 116–117).
- **Brahmā's awakening, then Rāma's rite** - the chapter title is "Brahmā's untimely awakening of the Devī for Rāvaṇa's death". **Brahmā himself first awakens Caṇḍī in autumn and worships her with the gods**, then comes to Rāma and tells him to do the same (*akāle bodhan kari, pūja devī maheśvarī*; chs. 93–94).
  - Rāma reads the **Caṇḍī**, performs the awakening at dusk on **Ṣaṣṭhī** with the *bilva-adhivāsa*, **fashions a clay image himself** (*āpani gaḍilā rām pratimā mṛnmayī*), and binds the **navapatrikā**. He then worships through **Saptamī, Aṣṭamī and Navamī** (ch. 95).
  - This is the liturgical frame of modern Bengali Durgā Pūjā.
- **The 108 blue lotuses (nīl-padma / nīlotpala)** - on Navamī the Goddess accepts the worship but stays hidden, and Rāma despairs. **Vibhīṣaṇa** advises offering **108 blue lotuses** (*aṣṭottara-śata nīlotpal kar dān*). He tells Hanumān that they grow only at **Devīdaha**, a ten-year journey, and **Hanumān** fetches them at wind-speed (chs. 96–97).
  - Rāma makes a formal vow (*saṅkalpa*) to offer all 108, and **the Goddess steals one** (ch. 98 title: *devī-kartṛk ekṭi padma-haraṇ*). Hanumān insists the count was 108 (*śatāṣṭa āche gaṇan*) and that Devīdaha holds no more.
  - The 1906 and 1940 printings agree on 108.
  - *Variant count:* Sen's 1920 English paraphrase says "a blue-lotus from out of 101 flowers" (Sen 1920, p. 115). No manuscript is cited for this, and all three printings checked read 108.
- **The eye of Rāma** - after further hymns (chs. 99–100), Rāma reasons that "everyone calls me **kamala-locana** (lotus-eyed)" and resolves to give **one eye** to complete the vow (*kamal-locan more bale sarva jane / ek cakṣu diba āmi saṅkalpa-pūraṇe*). He takes an arrow from his quiver to pluck it out. **Kātyāyanī** seizes his hand: "your vow is fulfilled; I do not want the eye" (ch. 101).
  - (Earlier versions of this page said "Padmalocana… since childhood". The vulgate has *kamala-locana*, "as all people say", with no childhood reference.)
- **Boon, Daśamī and immersion** - the Goddess praises Rāma as the supreme Brahman. She calls Sītā the supreme Prakṛti, says Rāvaṇa was once his **door-keeper in Vaikuṇṭha** cursed into enmity, and says Rāma made this untimely worship of the **ten-armed** Goddess known on earth "so that people may know". Then she vanishes.
  - Rāma performs the **Daśamī** rites, **immerses** the Goddess (*daśamīte pūjā kari, visarjiyā maheśvarī*), and goes to battle (ch. 102).
  - Rāvaṇa dies only after chs. 103–105 (the spoiled Caṇḍī-recitation and the stolen death-arrow).
  - *Festival tradition:* Bengali and pan-Indian practice links Rāma's victory to **Vijayā Daśamī / Dussehra**. In the chapter read here, Daśamī is the day of immersion and setting out, not of Rāvaṇa's fall.
- **Cultural charter** - this episode is **a Bengali development, not in Vālmīki**. It fused the existing Bengali **Śākta autumn Goddess-worship** with the pan-Indian Rāma narrative and gave **Śāradīya Durgā Pūjā** epic sanction and prestige beyond regional cult status. The Goddess's closing speech states the charter in the text itself. See [Durgā Pūjā (Bengal)](../../../05-concepts-and-dharma/durga-puja-bengal.md), [Festivals & Vratas](../../../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md) and [Regional Temple Festivals](../../../05-concepts-and-dharma/regional-temple-festivals.md).
  - *Vālmīki's own pre-battle rite is solar, not Śākta:* **Agastya** teaches Rāma the **Āditya-hṛdaya** hymn to the Sun before the final duel (VR 6.105). Even that hymn is **absent from the Critical Edition's constituted text**.
  - *Variant lineage of the motif:* the Rāma-worships-the-Goddess theme appears in Sanskrit Śākta texts in different shapes [late text]. Kṛttibās's tradition is the channel that **popularized** it in Bengal, whether or not it invented every detail [scholarly] [disputed]. The forms are kept separate here:
    - *Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa 3.30:* **Nārada**, not Brahmā, advises the *Navarātra* vow. Rāma performs it in **Āśvina** on a hilltop, before the bridge is built, fasting nine days. The Devī appears on a lion on the night of **Aṣṭamī** and foretells victory. She tells him to worship her again in spring after killing Rāvaṇa, and he performs the Vijayā rite on the tenth day. There is **no Brahmā-bodhana, no lotus and no eye** (DBh 3.30.1–61, Vijnanananda trans.).
    - *Mahābhāgavata (Devī) Purāṇa, chs. 38–48* (an Upapurāṇa that R. C. Hazra places in Bengal, c. 10th–11th c.): frightened by Kumbhakarṇa, Rāma is led by **Brahmā** to a **bilva tree on the sea-shore**. Brahmā awakens the Devī in the tree on **kṛṣṇa-navamī**, image-worship runs **śukla-saptamī to navamī**, the image is **immersed on Daśamī**, and Rāma kills Rāvaṇa with a missile the Devī gives him. Hazra's summary mentions no lotus-theft or eye-offering. Hazra also finds the Bṛhaddharma Purāṇa borrowing heavily from this text (Hazra, *Studies in the Upapurāṇas*, via wisdomlib). This is the closest Sanskrit model of the Bengali calendar.
    - *Kālikā Purāṇa:* Sen states that the Caṇḍī-pūjā by Rāma "first appeared in the Sanskrit Kālikā Purāṇa" and was then developed in the Bengali Rāmāyaṇas (Sen 1920, p. 115). The Kālikā and Bṛhaddharma passages were not opened for this page.
    - *The lotus-theft and eye [disputed]:* the 1949 editor counts the lotus-theft among Kṛttibās's own inventions. Wikipedia's "Akalabodhana" article instead attributes the 108-lotus and eye story to "the Bengali version of Bhagavata Purana", citing Pañcānan Tarkaratna's *Śrīmahābhāgavatam*. These two claims are recorded, not reconciled. The pan-Indian Vaiṣṇava mirror is Viṣṇu's lotus-eye offering for the Sudarśana: see [Viṣṇu's Lotus-Eye](../../vishnu-lotus-eye-sudarshana.md).
  - *Open roster [flagged]:* the precise division between Kṛttibās's own composition and later scribal expansion of the akāl-bodhan passage is genuinely unsettled. Manuscripts vary, and the printed vulgate may amplify the original.

## Manuscript & print history

- **Manuscript corpus** - **1,500+ surviving manuscripts**, rarely earlier than the last quarter of the **18th century** and varying by region (Benoît 2007, p. 168). They are held in West Bengal universities (Calcutta, Visva-Bharati, Rabindra Bharati, Jadavpur, Burdwan, North Bengal), in **Assam** (Silchar), in **Bangladesh** (Dhaka, Jahangirnagar), in the **UK** (British Library, SOAS) and in **France** (BnF) (Wikipedia). The number testifies to immense circulation but frustrates recovery of an "original".
- **First printed edition (1802–03)** - issued in **five volumes** from the **Serampore Mission Press** (the Baptist mission press of Carey, Marshman and Ward), among the earliest printed Bengali books. Banglapedia judges the first Serampore edition the best. (Banglapedia and Benoît date it 1802–03; Wikipedia gives the *editio princeps* as 1803.)
- **The standard vulgate (1830–34)** - a revised two-volume edition by **Jaygopāl Tarkālaṅkār** (Banglapedia: 1830–34; Wikipedia: published 1834). It became the **standard popular text**, and most 20th-century editions build on it. Benoît adds that current editions "have hardly changed since the 1840s" and bear the corrections of 19th-century paṇḍits (Benoît 2007, p. 168).
- **Absorbed poems** - the vulgate also carries other poets' work. Sen shows that the celebrated **Aṅgader Rāybār** (Aṅgada's embassy, Laṅkā ch. 15 in Dey 1949) is **Kavicandra's** 16th-century poem. It entered Kṛttivāsa's text through the cheap **Baṭtalā** prints, while old manuscripts of Kṛttivāsa tell the episode differently. Sen urged that Kavicandra's name be restored in the colophon (Sen 1920, pp. 216–217).
- **Later editions** - Nalinikanta Bhaṭṭaśālī's Ādikāṇḍa (Dacca University, 1936) follows a single variant manuscript, and Harekrishna Mukhopadhyaya's edition (Sahitya Samsad, 1957) differs in content (Wikipedia).

## Influence & legacy

- **Mass devotional culture** - for Bengali, Assamese and eastern Indian Hindus the work made Rāma's story domestically accessible and **shaped household piety, women's rituals, recitation and festival life** for centuries. Sen describes *maṅgala* singers and *kathaka* storytellers drawing on it. He also pictures the village grocer reading it aloud in the idle afternoon, and the widow chanting it to a circle of women (Sen 1920, pp. 146, 168). It is woven into **Durgā Pūjā** through the akāl-bodhan.
  - *Resistance [scholarly]:* the 18th-century rāja **Kṛṣṇacandra Rāy** of Nadia is reported to have tried to ban its reading as part of a push to Sanskritise Bengali religion (Sandipan Sen 2013–14, via Wikipedia).
- **Literary downstream** - later poets paid it homage:
  - Mukundarāma (16th c.) bows "to Kṛttivāsa, the first of those who popularised the Rāmāyaṇa", and later Bengali Rāmāyaṇa poets open with praise of him (Sen 1920, pp. 152–153).
  - **Michael Madhusudan Dutt** built his *Meghnādbadh Kābya* (1861) in dialogue with this tradition. His sonnet **"Kṛttivās"** (*Caturdaśapadī Kavitāvalī*, no. 5) compares the poet to Hanumān: as Hanumān crossed the sea to pour Sītā's news into Rāma's ear, so Kṛttibās sings Rāma's name across Bengal, having pleased "father-poet Vālmīki".
  - Figures from **Tagore** onward engaged it as the bedrock vernacular classic (Wikipedia).
  - *Influence on Tulsīdās [scholarly] [disputed]:* Sen hears Kṛttivāsa's rhythm in some lines of the Rāmcaritmānas and notes that Śiva's wedding appears in both. Wikipedia's stronger claim of direct inspiration is unsourced.
  - Sen also reports that Oriya poets later copied bhakti passages from the Bengali Rāmāyaṇas (Sen 1920, pp. 136–137, 149).
- **Comparative place** - stands alongside [Tulsīdās's Awadhī Rāmcaritmānas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md), [Kambaṉ's Tamil Irāmāvatāram](./kamban-iramavataram.md), the Vedāntic [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](./adhyatma-ramayana.md), and the heterodox [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](./jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md) as a major **regional recension** that re-creates rather than reproduces [Vālmīki](../README.md). Each of these "two-clock" texts retells Tretā-Yuga events in its own Kali-Yuga vernacular.
