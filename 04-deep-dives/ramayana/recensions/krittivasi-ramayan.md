# Krittivasi Ramayan — the Bengali Sriram Panchali

> Cosmic address: [Tretā Yuga](../../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current 28th Mahāyuga — the *story* is set near the close of Tretā; the *text* is a **15th-century CE** (Kali Yuga) Bengali retelling. The **Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ** — properly the **Śrīrām Pā̃cālī** ("Rāma sung-narrative") — is the **Bengali-vernacular retelling** of the Rāma story by **Kṛttibās Ojhā** (c. 1381–1461 CE), the **first translator/adapter of Vālmīki's Sanskrit Rāmāyaṇa into Bengali**. It is *not* a literal translation but a **folk-bhakti reworking** that recasts the epic in the idiom, emotion, social texture, and ritual life of medieval Bengal. By common verdict "by far the most popular book in Bengal" and "the Bible of the people of the Gangetic Valley," it is the single most beloved literary work of Bengali Hindu households — and the **charter-text of Bengali Durgā Pūjā** through its *akāl-bodhan* episode. Survives in **over 1,500 manuscripts** (mostly 18th c. onward); the original Kṛttibās text is unrecoverable beneath centuries of accretion.

## Author, date & patronage

- **Kṛttibās Ojhā** - medieval Bengali poet (c. 1381–1461 CE), born into a **Kulīn Brāhmaṇ** family at **Phulia village, Nadia district** (West Bengal). Father **Banamālī Ojhā**, grandfather **Murāri Ojhā** — a lineage versed in religious texts (Banglapedia; Wikipedia).
  - *Variant (Banglapedia):* birthplace alternatively given as **Phulia near Premtali, Rajshahi district** (now Bangladesh) rather than Nadia — [disputed].
- **Education** - sent at age ~11–12 to **Nabadwip** (or, in another account, to **Varendra/Barendra in North Bengal**) for higher study under religious scholars, then sought a post as royal paṇḍita at the court of Gauḍa (Banglapedia; Wikipedia).
- **Royal patronage** - traditionally honoured by the **King of Gauḍa** in person with a **garland, sandal-water, and a silk scarf (chador)**, the king delighted by his verses and commissioning the Rāmāyaṇa.
  - *Variant (which Gauḍa monarch) [disputed]:* candidates include **Rājā Gaṇeśa** (r. c. 1415–18) or **Sultan Jalāluddīn Muḥammad Shāh** (r. c. 1418–31); popular tradition also names **Ruknuddīn Bārbak Shāh**. The patron's identity is unsettled and bears on the exact dating.
- **Dating — two clocks** - *Authorial time:* 15th c. CE (Kali Yuga). *Scripture time:* the narrated events sit near the close of [Tretā Yuga](../../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga — Kṛttibās retells, he does not relocate, the cosmic setting.

## Language, form & meter

- **Middle Bengali vernacular** - composed in the **pā̃cālī** (sung-recitation) genre of Middle Bengali devotional literature, designed to be **chanted/sung aloud** to ordinary listeners — a deliberate democratizing move away from the Brāhmaṇical Sanskrit monopoly, paralleling Tulsīdās's Awadhī [Rāmcaritmānas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) and Kambaṉ's Tamil [Irāmāvatāram](./kamban-iramavataram.md) in the wider vernacular-bhakti wave [scholarly].
- **Pāyār meter** - the backbone is the **pāyār** couplet — each line of **fourteen syllables (akṣaras)**, rhyming in pairs — the standard narrative meter of Bengali kāvya, lending an easy, oral, memorizable flow (Banglapedia).
- **"Lively and familiar" register** - Kṛttibās reorients Vālmīki's hieratic Sanskrit into something "sung, lively and familiar," meant to **instruct and entertain** common Bengali audiences rather than to preserve courtly grandeur — domesticating the epic into Bengali village and household life.

## Structure — seven kāṇḍas (Bengali names)

- **Seven books, following Vālmīki's frame** - **Ādi-kāṇḍa**, **Ajodhyā-kāṇḍa**, **Araṇya-kāṇḍa**, **Kiṣkindhā-kāṇḍa**, **Sundar-kāṇḍa**, **Laṅkā-kāṇḍa**, and **Uttar-kāṇḍa** — the same seven-kāṇḍa architecture as [Vālmīki](../README.md), but heavily re-narrated rather than translated.
- **Text-critical caution** - because **1,500+ manuscripts** survive (overwhelmingly 18th c. and later) with massive scribal accretion, scholars hold it "impossible to formulate an impression of the original text of Kṛttibās"; the printed *vulgate* is a layered, evolving "living" tradition rather than a fixed authorial work [scholarly] [disputed].

## Folk-bhakti coloring — what makes it Bengali

- **Domestication of the epic** - Rāma, Sītā, and the court are re-imagined with Bengali household customs, kinship sentiment, women's rituals (*strī-ācār*), folk speech, and local color — the divine recast as intimate family figures, the keynote of its enduring popularity.
- **Bhakti devotionalism** - the poem foregrounds loving surrender and the moral exemplarity of Rāma over martial epic grandeur, situating it within the Bengali **Vaiṣṇava-bhakti** ferment that would soon crest with Caitanya; later it cross-fertilized with Bengali Śākta (Goddess) devotion through the Durgā material.
- **Original/folk episodes absent from Vālmīki** - Kṛttibās (and accreting tradition) inserts beloved sub-tales not in the Sanskrit critical text [folk]:
  - *Akāl-bodhan* — Rāma's untimely autumn worship of **Durgā** before the war (see below); the signature Bengali addition.
  - *Mahīrāvaṇa / Ahirāvaṇa episode* — a sorcerer-king (brother/ally of Rāvaṇa) of **Pātāla** abducts the sleeping Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa; **Hanumān** descends to the netherworld, learns the demon's life resides in bees, and rescues them — one of the most popular folk interpolations in eastern Rāmāyaṇas [folk] [late text].
  - *Tāraṇīsen* — son of **Vibhīṣaṇa**, a devout Rāma-bhakta who fights for Rāvaṇa's side out of duty while chanting Rāma's name; slain by Rāma, a poignant martyrdom motif [folk].
  - *Bīrbāhu* and other added warrior-sons of Rāvaṇa, expanded war-vignettes, and emotional set-pieces (Sītā's sorrows, women's laments) tuned to Bengali pathos [folk].
  - *Bhagīratha's birth* — a striking accretion depicting **Bhagīratha** (who later brings the Gaṅgā to earth) born of **two co-widows of King Dilīpa** by divine blessing — read by modern scholars as a rare premodern portrayal of same-sex/parthenogenic generation; almost certainly a later interpolation, not original Kṛttibās [folk] [late text] [disputed]. Cross-ref the [Ikṣvāku/Sūryavaṃśa lineage](../../../03-lineages-vamsha).
- **Lava–Kuśa / Uttar-kāṇḍa** - the Bengali tradition keeps and elaborates the **twin sons Lava and Kuśa**, Sītā's exile to Vālmīki's hermitage, the horse-sacrifice confrontation, and Sītā's return to the earth — sustaining the Uttar-kāṇḍa's bittersweet close.

## The Akāl-bodhan — Rāma's autumn worship of Durgā (the Durgā Pūjā charter)

- **What "akāl-bodhan" means** - *akāl-bodhan* = the **"untimely awakening/invocation"** of the Goddess. Durgā's *canonical* worship is **vernal (Basantī Pūjā)** during **Uttarāyaṇa** (the gods' "day"); Rāma instead awakens her in **autumn (Śāradīya), in Dakṣiṇāyana** — the gods' "night" when deities sleep — hence "out of season," a bold ritual improvisation forced by war (Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ; hṛdayāravinde).
- **The crisis** - on the battlefield **Śakti/Caṇḍikā herself shields Rāvaṇa**, and Rāma's army loses hope. **Indra** petitions **Brahmā**, who reveals the secret remedy: to win, Rāma must propitiate **Caṇḍikā/Durgā** directly with an autumn worship (Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ).
- **The rite** - on Brahmā's instruction Rāma begins worship on **Śukla Ṣaṣṭhī**, continuing through **Saptamī, Aṣṭamī, Navamī**, to culminate in victory on **Vijayā Daśamī** — the exact liturgical calendar of modern Bengali Durgā Pūjā / Navarātri.
- **The 108 blue lotuses (nīl-padma / nīl-kamal)** - three days of worship yield no boon; **Vibhīṣaṇa** advises offering **108 celestial blue lotuses** on Navamī. **Hanumān** fetches them, but **Durgā tests Rāma's devotion by hiding one lotus**, leaving the offering one short.
- **The eye of Rāma (Padmalocana)** - rather than break his vow, Rāma — called **"Padmalocana" ("lotus-eyed")** since childhood — prepares to **pluck out his own eye** to complete the count of 108 "lotuses." Durgā, satisfied, **stays his hand**, declares his *saṅkalpa* fulfilled, and grants victory, promising to stand with him on **Vijayā Daśamī** (Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ; hṛdayāravinde).
- **Cultural charter** - this episode — **a Kṛttibās/Bengali innovation, not in Vālmīki** — fused the pre-existing Bengali **Śākta autumn Goddess-worship** with the pan-Indian Rāma narrative, giving **Śāradīya Durgā Pūjā** scriptural-mythic sanction and national-epic prestige rather than mere regional cult status. The Daśamī of Rāma's victory is itself **Vijayā Daśamī / Dussehra**. See [Festivals & Vratas](../../../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md) and [Regional Temple Festivals](../../../05-concepts-and-dharma/regional-temple-festivals.md).
  - *Variant lineage of the motif:* the autumn-Goddess-and-Rāma theme also appears in the Śākta **Devī-Bhāgavata** and **Kālikā / Bṛhaddharma Purāṇa** strands [late text]; Kṛttibās is the channel that **popularized** it for Bengal, whether or not he invented every detail [scholarly] [disputed].
  - *Open roster [flagged]:* the precise division between Kṛttibās's own composition and later scribal expansion of the akāl-bodhan passage is genuinely unsettled — manuscripts vary, and the printed vulgate may amplify the original.

## Manuscript & print history

- **Manuscript corpus** - **1,500+ surviving manuscripts**, the bulk from the **18th century onward**, scattered across West Bengal universities, **Assam, Bangladesh, the UK, and France** — testifying to immense circulation but frustrating recovery of an "original."
- **First printed edition (1802–03)** - issued in **five volumes** from the **Serampore Mission Press** (the Baptist mission press of Carey, Marshman, Ward) — among the earliest printed Bengali books; the **first Serampore edition is judged the best** (Banglapedia; Wikipedia).
- **The standard vulgate (1830–34)** - revised/edited two-volume edition by **Jaygopāl Tarkālaṅkār**, which became the **standard popular text** read for the next two centuries — i.e., most "Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ" copies in Bengali homes descend from this 19th-c. recension, not directly from Kṛttibās.

## Influence & legacy

- **Mass devotional culture** - for Bengali, Assamese, and eastern Indian Hindus the work made Rāma's story domestically accessible and **shaped household piety, women's rituals, recitation, and festival life** for centuries; recited at **Rām-navamī** and woven into **Durgā Pūjā** through akāl-bodhan.
- **Literary downstream** - influenced Bengali Vaiṣṇava-bhakti sensibility and later Bengali letters; the modern epic poet **Michael Madhusudan Dutt** built his *Meghnādbadh Kābya* in dialogue with this Rāmāyaṇa tradition, and figures from **Tagore** onward engaged it as the bedrock vernacular classic.
- **Comparative place** - stands alongside [Tulsīdās's Awadhī Rāmcaritmānas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md), [Kambaṉ's Tamil Irāmāvatāram](./kamban-iramavataram.md), the Vedāntic [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](./adhyatma-ramayana.md), and the heterodox [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](./jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md) as a major **regional recension** that re-creates rather than reproduces [Vālmīki](../README.md) — each "two-clock" text retelling Tretā-Yuga events in its own Kali-Yuga vernacular.
