# Ramcharitmanas (Tulsidas) — the Awadhi Bhakti Ramayana

> Cosmic address: [Tretā Yuga](../../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current 28th Mahāyuga — the *story* is set near the close of Tretā; the *text* is a **1574 CE** (Kali Yuga) Awadhi retelling. Two clocks: scripture-time vs. authorial-time.

> See also: [North Bhakti Sants](../../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/north-bhakti-sants.md) · [Sundara Kāṇḍa (Vālmīki)](../5-sundara-kanda.md) · [Daśāvatāra (Rāma)](../../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md)

The **Rāmcaritmānas** ("the Mānasa-lake of Rāma's deeds") is the Awadhi-vernacular retelling of the Rāma story by **Gosvāmī Tulsīdās** (c. 1532–1623), begun **1574 CE** and the single most influential devotional text of the North Indian Hindi belt. It is *not* a translation of Vālmīki but a **saguna-bhakti** reworking drawing chiefly on the [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md), recasting Rāma as the supreme Brahman-with-attributes (Parabrahma) accessible through loving devotion. It is the scriptural basis of **Rāmlīlā** and was inscribed on **UNESCO's Memory of the World** regional register in **2024**.

## Author, date & language

- **Gosvāmī Tulsīdās** - North Indian Vaiṣṇava poet-saint (c. 1532–1623 CE), counted among the **[Nirguṇa/Saguṇa bhakti sants](../../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/north-bhakti-sants.md)** of the Rāma-bhakti stream; by hagiography a reincarnation/spiritual heir of Vālmīki. Associated with Varanasi (Tulsi Ghat), Ayodhya, and Chitrakoot.
- **Composition begun 1574 CE** - Vikram Saṃvat 1631, on **Rāmanavamī** (ninth day of Caitra, Rāma's birthday) in **Ayodhyā**; tradition holds it took ~2 years 7 months, with portions composed across Ayodhyā, Vārāṇasī (Kāśī), and Citrakūṭ (Tulsidas's own colophons / hagiography).
- **Awadhi vernacular** - deliberately written in the eastern-Hindi *Awadhī* dialect (not Sanskrit) to make the path of devotion accessible to the common people, in conscious contrast to the Brahminical Sanskrit monopoly — a defining bhakti-egalitarian move [scholarly].
  - *Note:* a minority of Tulsidas's other works (e.g. *Vinaya-patrikā*, *Kavitāvalī*) use Braj Bhāṣā; the *Mānas* itself is predominantly Awadhi.

## Structure — seven kāṇḍas, the meter, and the scale

- **Seven kāṇḍas (books/sopāna)** - Bāl, Ayodhyā, Araṇya, Kiṣkindhā, Sundar, Laṅkā (≈ Vālmīki's Yuddha), and Uttar. The *Mānas* uses **Laṅkā Kāṇḍ** for the war-book where Vālmīki has *Yuddha Kāṇḍa*.
- **~1,073 stanzas / ~12,800 lines** - the canonical count is **1,073 stanzas** comprising roughly 12,800 lines; verse-units (including all dohā/caupāī/chand/śloka/sorṭhā) tally on the order of ~2,200–2,250 by some enumerations (Wikipedia; traditional editions).
- **Bāl Kāṇḍ + Ayodhyā Kāṇḍ ≈ more than half the work** - the childhood/boyhood and exile-departure books together exceed 50% of the total bulk, reflecting Tulsidas's devotional emphasis on Rāma's birth, marriage to Sītā, and the pathos of the exile over the war narrative.
- **Caupāī–dohā meter** - the backbone is the **caupāī** (four-line quatrain) clustered in groups, punctuated by a closing **dohā** (rhyming couplet) — and occasionally a **sorṭhā** (inverted dohā) — that crystallizes the doctrinal/emotional point of the preceding caupāīs.
  - *Variant meters:* each kāṇḍ opens with Sanskrit **ślokas** (maṅgalācaraṇa) and the text interleaves lyrical **chand** passages; Tulsidas is credited with handling many meters (Sanskrit and Prakrit) across the work [scholarly].
- **Maṅgalācaraṇa (auspicious invocation)** - every kāṇḍ begins with formal Sanskrit invocations to deities (Sarasvatī, Gaṇeśa, Śiva, Pārvatī, Rāma, Sītā, the guru), per classical convention to ensure unobstructed completion.

## The "seven steps to Lake Mānasarovar" framing

- **Title metaphor** - *Rām-carit-mānas* = "the Mānasa-lake of Rāma's deeds." Tulsidas frames the whole poem as a sacred lake (the legendary **Mānasarovar**) of Rāma's story, into which the devotee descends to be purified in body and soul.
- **Seven kāṇḍas = seven ghāṭs/steps (sopāna)** - the seven books are figured as **seven flights of steps (sopāna)** leading down into the holy lake — hence the kāṇḍas are styled *sopāna* ("staircase"), each descent washing away a layer of ignorance toward divine realization.
- **Lake symbolism extended** - within the conceit Tulsidas elaborates the lake's features allegorically (its four ghāṭs as the four dialogues/expounders, the water as Rāma's glory, lotuses, swans, etc.), making the architecture of the poem itself a meditative geography.

## Saguṇa theology & the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa base

- **Primary source = Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** - the chief doctrinal/narrative template is the late, Vedāntic **[Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)** (a portion of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa) [late text], which presents Rāma as the supreme Brahman and Sītā as Yogamāyā; Tulsidas inherits its theology while adding bhakti warmth.
  - *Other sources cataloged:* Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (narrative spine), the **Hanumannāṭaka**, Purāṇic Rāma material, and the **māyā-Sītā / chāyā-Sītā** motif (the real Sītā hidden with Agni, an illusory double abducted by Rāvaṇa) drawn from Adhyātma/Kūrma-Purāṇa traditions.
- **Saguṇa Brahman bhakti** - Rāma is *Parabrahma* with form and attributes (saguṇa), worshipped through loving surrender (*śaraṇāgati*) and the **Rām-nām** (the divine Name, held even greater than the named) — placing the work squarely in the saguṇa wing of the [North bhakti movement](../../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/north-bhakti-sants.md).
- **Reconciliation of nirguṇa & saguṇa** - Tulsidas explicitly harmonizes the formless absolute (nirguṇa) and the personal Rāma (saguṇa), arguing the two are one — a deliberate bridge across the bhakti spectrum [scholarly].

## The three nested frame-dialogues (narrators)

- **Śiva → Pārvatī** - the outermost frame: **Śiva (as Mahādeva)** narrates Rāma's story to **Pārvatī**, who, doubtful about Rāma's divinity (the test/illusion episode of Satī taking Sītā's form), is instructed in true devotion. Establishes the Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava harmony central to Tulsidas.
- **Yājñavalkya → Bharadvāja** - the sage **Yājñavalkya** expounds the tale to **Bharadvāja** at Prayāg during the Māgha Melā — the framing that opens the recitation in Bāl Kāṇḍ.
- **Kākabhuśuṇḍi → Garuḍa** - the immortal crow-sage **Kākabhuśuṇḍi** recounts Rāma's glory to **Garuḍa** (Viṣṇu's eagle-mount), who had been confused by Rāma's human-seeming bondage in the war; the most philosophically rich frame, prominent in Uttar Kāṇḍ.
  - *Variant reading:* commentators also see an implicit fourth conversation — **Tulsidas ↔ Rāma / the devotee ↔ the Lord** — running beneath the three explicit frames.

## Notable internal units & devotional uses

- **Sundar Kāṇḍ** - the fifth book (Hanumān's leap to Laṅkā, meeting Sītā, burning of Laṅkā). In popular Hindu practice the *Mānas* **Sundar Kāṇḍ is recited as a stand-alone unit** for protection, obstacle-removal, and Hanumān's grace — the single most ritually recited kāṇḍ. Compare the Vālmīki [Sundara Kāṇḍa](../5-sundara-kanda.md).
- **Hanumān Cālīsā** - the 40-verse (cālīs = 40) Awadhi hymn to Hanumān is **traditionally attributed to Tulsidas** and is the most-recited Hindu devotional poem in the world; though a *separate* composition (not part of the *Mānas*'s seven kāṇḍas), it is part of the Tulsidas Hanumān-bhakti corpus and routinely printed/recited alongside the *Mānas* [folk/traditional attribution].
- **Dohāvalī, set-piece prayers** - famous embedded stūtis (e.g. the *Rāma-stuti*, the *Rudrāṣṭakam* in Sanskrit, Sītā's prayers) are extracted for daily worship.

## Laṅkā Kāṇḍ — the war-book divergences from Vālmīki

The *Mānas*'s war-book carries the most-quoted images of the whole Rāma story in the Hindi belt, and they are Tulsīdās's rather than Vālmīki's. Because they are the versions everyone knows, they are routinely cited as "the Rāmāyaṇa" — so each is set here against what the Sanskrit epic actually says.

| Image | Rāmcaritmānas (Laṅkā Kāṇḍ) | Vālmīki |
|---|---|---|
| The Setu | stones float across the sea | **nothing floats** — Nala's boon is Viśvakarman-equal *craft* and Sāgara bears the causeway (Yuddha 22) |
| Rāvaṇa's death-wound | the arrow dries the **nectar in his navel** | the Brahmā-arrow **tears off his heart**; no amṛta, no navel (Yuddha 108) |
| Sītā and the fire | a **chāyā-Sītā** is abducted; the real Sītā is hidden in Agni and returns from it | the abducted Sītā is Sītā, and the *agni-parīkṣā* is a real ordeal of the real woman (Yuddha 116–118) |
| Aṅgada's embassy | he **plants his foot** in the court and no rākṣasa can move it | he delivers the message, then leaps to the palace roof and tramples it down (Yuddha 41) |
| Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā | never narrated — but **Mandodarī taunts Rāvaṇa** with it | absent entirely |

- **The floating stones** - Tulsīdās gives the miracle as the effect of Rāma's *pratāpa* (majesty), summed in the Laṅkā Kāṇḍ dohā *"śrī raghubīra pratāpa te sindhu tare pāṣāna"* — "by the might of Raghubīr, stones floated across the ocean." The further image of **Rāma's name written on each stone** is a popular elaboration built on that line rather than a verse of the *Mānas*; the squirrel who carries pebbles is folk, later still. [folk]
- **The navel-nectar** - Vibhīṣaṇa discloses that Rāvaṇa's life is held in a vessel of nectar at his navel; Rāma's arrows drink the navel dry while the rest shear off the ten heads and twenty arms. This is the death every North Indian audience knows — and the reason the corpus flags the Vālmīki heart-shot separately in the [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](../6-yuddha-kanda.md) file.
- **The chāyā-Sītā** - Inherited from the [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md): in the *Mānas*'s Araṇya Kāṇḍ, Rāma privately tells Sītā to enter the fire and leave a shadow-double in her place, so that what Rāvaṇa carries off is never Sītā at all; the Laṅkā Kāṇḍ ordeal is then the *return* of the true Sītā from Agni rather than a test of her. The doctrine dissolves the ordeal's whole moral problem — which is why Tulsī can keep the scene and still make it joyful. [late text]
- **Aṅgada's foot** - The court set-piece the *Rāmlīlā* stages every year: Aṅgada plants his foot and challenges the assembly to shift it, warrior after warrior fails, and when Rāvaṇa himself rises Aṅgada answers that it is Rāma's feet he should be grasping, not his. Vālmīki's Aṅgada wrecks the palace roof instead. [folk / devotional]
- **The Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā, and the Mānas's own oddity about it** - The protective line drawn around Sītā's hut is **not in Vālmīki and not in the** *Mānas*'s **Araṇya Kāṇḍ either** — Tulsīdās never narrates it. Yet in the Laṅkā Kāṇḍ, **Mandodarī**, deflating her husband's boasts of valour, throws at him that he could not so much as cross a small line drawn by Lakṣmaṇa. The taunt presupposes an episode the poem has not told, which makes the *Mānas* (Laṅkā Kāṇḍ 35.1) a witness that the rekhā was already current in performance and oral tradition by 1574 even though no Sanskrit text carries it. Versions that actually *narrate* the drawing of the line are later still — the Rāmlīlā stage and vernacular retellings such as Rādheśyām Kathāvācak's Hindi *Rāmāyaṇ*, where Sītā crosses it absent-mindedly in the duty of hospitality to a guest. [oral tradition]

## Cultural & ritual afterlife

- **Rāmlīlā** - the *Mānas* is the **textual basis of Rāmlīlā**, the multi-day dramatic enactment of the Rāma story across North India; the **Rāmnagar Rāmlīlā** of Vārāṇasī (a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage tradition) stages the poem over ~31 days, with the dialogue and song drawn directly from Tulsidas.
- **Mānas-recitation traditions** - *akhaṇḍ pāṭh* (continuous recitation), *navāhna pāṭh* (nine-day), Mānas-kathā expounders (*kathā-vācak*), and household daily reading make it functionally a "fifth Veda" for the Hindi belt [folk].
- **Pan-Asian reach** - read and adapted across the Indian diaspora and Southeast Asia; the Rāma story-cycle it carries circulates in Thailand, Cambodia, Indonesia, Sri Lanka and the Caribbean/Fiji/Mauritius diasporas.

## UNESCO Memory of the World (2024)

- **Inscription** - the **Rāmcaritmānas** was inscribed on **UNESCO's Memory of the World Committee for Asia and the Pacific (MOWCAP) Regional Register** at the **10th General Meeting, 7–8 May 2024, Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia**.
- **Joint Indian nominations** - inscribed alongside the **Pañcatantra** and **Sahṛdayāloka-Locana** (Ānandavardhana/Abhinavagupta) — the first time India had **three** nominations accepted in a single cycle.
- **Manuscript heritage** - the nomination drew on early/rare *Mānas* manuscripts (including, per reports, an 18th-century Arabic-script manuscript witness), recognizing the text's documentary and civilizational value.

## Relation to other Rāmāyaṇas

- **Vs. Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa** - Vālmīki = Sanskrit *ādi-kāvya*, narrative-heroic, set in Tretā; *Mānas* = Awadhi bhakti-devotional, theologically saguṇa, composed in Kali Yuga (1574 CE). The *Mānas* compresses/omits much war detail and amplifies devotion, marriage, and Bharata's love.
- **One of the "three hundred Rāmāyaṇas"** - the *Mānas* is the dominant North-Indian member of the vast Rāma retelling-family (cf. Tamil **Kamba Rāmāyaṇam**, Bengali **Kṛttivāsī**, Telugu, Odia, etc.); see the [Rāmāyaṇa overview](../README.md) for the recension landscape. *(This recensions/ directory is an open-ended roster — additional regional recensions remain to be cataloged.)*
