# Ramcharitmanas (Tulsidas) — the Awadhi Bhakti Ramayana

> Cosmic address: [Tretā Yuga](../../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current 28th Mahāyuga — the *story* is set near the close of Tretā; the *text* is a **1574 CE** (Kali Yuga) Awadhi retelling. Two clocks: scripture-time vs. authorial-time.

The **Rāmcaritmānas** ("the Mānasa-lake of Rāma's deeds") is the Awadhi retelling of the Rāma story by **Gosvāmī Tulsīdās** (trad. c. 1532–1623). By its own dating verse it was begun in **Vikram Saṃvat 1631 (1574 CE)**, and it is the single most influential devotional text of the North Indian Hindi belt. It is *not* a translation of Vālmīki. It is a **saguṇa-bhakti** reworking that takes much of its theology and several episodes from the [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](./adhyatma-ramayana.md). It presents Rāma as the supreme Brahman with form and attributes (Parabrahma), reached through loving devotion. Tulsīdās names his own sources: the Purāṇas, Vedas and Āgamas, "what is told in the Rāmāyaṇa, and something from elsewhere too" (*nānāpurāṇanigamāgamasammataṃ yad rāmāyaṇe nigaditaṃ kvacid anyato 'pi*, Bāl, opening śloka 7). It is the textual basis of most **Rāmlīlā**, and its illustrated manuscripts were inscribed on **UNESCO's Memory of the World** Asia-Pacific regional register in **2024**.

> **How verses are cited here.** Numbers follow the Gita Press text as reproduced on ramcharit.in. Each run of caupāīs is numbered by the dohā or soraṭhā that closes it. So **Bāl 34.2** is the second caupāī before dohā 34, and **dohā 34** is that closing couplet. When two closing dohās are paired they are *ka/kha*, and "śl." marks the Sanskrit ślokas at the head of a kāṇḍa. Other editions, such as the Tulsī Pīṭh critical edition, number some passages differently.

## Author, date & language

- **Gosvāmī Tulsīdās** - North Indian Vaiṣṇava poet-saint. He is the leading voice of the **saguṇa** Rāma-bhakti wing of the [north-Indian bhakti movement](../../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/north-bhakti-sants.md), as opposed to nirguṇa sants such as Kabīr. He is associated with Vārāṇasī (Asī/Tulsī Ghāṭ), Ayodhyā and Citrakūṭ. His life, dates and other works have their own page: [Tulsīdās](../../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/tulsidas.md).
  - *Dates:* this corpus's [Tulsīdās](../../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/tulsidas.md) page gives the traditional span as **1532–1623 CE**. Wikipedia, *Tulsidas*, instead leads with a birth in **1511 CE** (VS 1568, the year Grierson gives), and notes the popular **1497 CE**, which implies a life of 126 years. A tradition-couplet places his death at **Asī Ghāṭ in Śrāvaṇa VS 1680 (1623 CE)** (Wikipedia, *Tulsidas*). [traditional] [disputed]
  - *Vālmīki reborn:* Nābhādās, a contemporary, describes him as an incarnation of Vālmīki in a six-line stanza of his *Bhaktamāl* (composed between 1583 and 1639, per Wikipedia). The *Bhaviṣya Purāṇa* (Pratisarga-parvan 4.20, per Wikipedia) has Śiva foretell the same to Pārvatī. [hagiography] [late text]
- **Composition begun 1574 CE** - the poem dates itself: *saṃbata soraha sai ekatīsā … naumī bhauma bāra madhu māsā, avadhapurīṃ yaha carita prakāsā*. That gives VS 1631, the ninth day of Caitra (*madhu-māsa*), a **Tuesday** (*bhauma-bāra*), at **Ayodhyā**, on the day of Rāma's birth (Bāl 34.2–3). Wikipedia gives "Sunday", which conflicts with the text.
  - *Hagiography, not the text:* the poem itself names only Ayodhyā. Wikipedia, *Tulsidas*, adds from the traditional biographies a writing time of **two years, seven months and twenty-six days** and completion on **Vivāha-pañcamī** of VS 1634 (1577 CE). The best-known of those biographies, the *Mūla Gosāīṃ Carita*, is widely regarded by scholars as spurious (Wikipedia). The older claim on this page that the work was spread over Ayodhyā, Vārāṇasī and Citrakūṭ has no source located. [hagiography] [disputed] [unverified]
- **Awadhi vernacular** - the narrative is in **Awadhī**, the eastern-Hindi vernacular. Sanskrit ślokas stand at the head of each kāṇḍa and at the close.
  - *The poet's stated reason:* he writes "for his own inner joy" (*svāntaḥsukhāya tulasī raghunāthagāthā bhāṣānibandham atimañjulam ātanoti*, Bāl śl. 7). He apologises that his "speech is the vernacular and my wit is simple" (*bhāṣā bhaniti bhori mati morī*, Bāl 9.2). The closing śloka of the Uttar Kāṇḍ says he set in the vernacular what Śambhu had first composed, "to still the darkness within" (*svāntas-tamaḥ-śāntaye bhāṣābaddham idaṃ cakāra tulasīdāsaḥ*).
  - *Modern reading:* scholars read the choice of Awadhi over Sanskrit as a bhakti move. On this reading it opened devotion to people with no Sanskrit and stood against the Sanskrit-learned establishment. The text itself frames the choice as humility and inner need. [scholarly]
  - *Note:* several of Tulsīdās's other works are in Braj Bhāṣā (*Vinaya-patrikā*, *Kavitāvalī*, *Gītāvalī*, *Kṛṣṇa-gītāvalī*). *Jānakī-maṅgal*, *Pārvatī-maṅgal* and *Barvai Rāmāyaṇ* are in Awadhi (Wikipedia, *Tulsidas*). The *Mānas* itself is Awadhi.

## Structure — seven kāṇḍas, the meter, and the scale

- **Seven kāṇḍas (books / *sopāna*)** - Bāl, Ayodhyā, Araṇya, Kiṣkindhā, Sundar, Laṅkā (≈ Vālmīki's Yuddha) and Uttar. The text's own colophons only number the books as "steps": *iti śrīmadrāmacaritamānase sakalakalikaluṣavidhvaṃsane ṣaṣṭhaḥ sopānaḥ samāptaḥ*, "thus ends the sixth step of the Rāmacaritamānasa, destroyer of all the stains of Kali". In the Gita Press text the kāṇḍa names stand only in headings and in bracketed end-notes such as "(लंकाकाण्ड समाप्त)".
  - *Variant (the sixth book's name):* Gita Press prints **Laṅkākāṇḍ**. The Tulsī Pīṭh critical edition of Jagadguru Rāmbhadrācārya calls it **Yuddhakāṇḍa** (ramcharit.in, *Metres in the Ramcharitmanas*).
- **Scale, and how it is counted** - the Gita Press dohā-numbering gives **1,074** numbered dohā-groups: Bāl 361, Ayodhyā 326, Araṇya 46, Kiṣkindhā 30, Sundar 60, Laṅkā 121 and Uttar 130 (counted from the ramcharit.in kāṇḍa texts). Wikipedia's figure is "1,073 stanzas, ~12,800 lines".
  - *Verse tally (Tulsī Pīṭh edition):* **10,902 verses** in 18 metres. That is 9,388 caupāī, 1,172 dohā, 87 soraṭhā, 47 Sanskrit ślokas and 208 chand (Harigītikā, Caupaiyā, Tribhaṅgī, Tomara). The dohās by book are 359, 314, 51, 31, 62, 148 and 207 (ramcharit.in, *Metres*). The two series do not line up book by book, because a group may close with a soraṭhā instead of a dohā, or with two or more dohās (*ka/kha*).
- **Bāl Kāṇḍ + Ayodhyā Kāṇḍ ≈ two-thirds of the work** - the two books hold 687 of the 1,074 numbered groups (about 64%). Rāma's birth, the marriage and the pathos of the exile get far more room than the war. The Laṅkā Kāṇḍ has 121 groups, and the three middle books (Araṇya, Kiṣkindhā, Sundar) have 136 between them.
- **Caupāī–dohā meter** - the backbone is the **caupāī**, a *mātrika* metre counted in instants (mātrās). Each foot has 16 mātrās with a pause after the eighth, and each printed line pairs two rhyming feet (*ardhālī*). Gita Press numbers every two lines as one caupāī. A run of caupāīs is closed either by a **dohā**, four feet of 13–11–13–11 mātrās rhyming on the even feet, or by a **soraṭhā**, the mirror pattern 11–13–11–13. In the Ayodhyā Kāṇḍ a soraṭhā falls after every 25th group (2.25, 2.50, 2.75 …) (ramcharit.in, *Metres*).
  - *Other metres:* lyrical **chand** passages, most often Harigītikā, are interleaved. Ahalyā's release is the only place the Tribhaṅgī is used (Bāl 211, four verses). The Sanskrit ślokas include Anuṣṭubh, Śārdūlavikrīḍita, Pramāṇikā (Atri's hymn *namāmi bhakta-vatsalaṃ*, Araṇya 4) and Bhujaṅgaprayāta (the *Rudrāṣṭaka*, Uttar 108) (ramcharit.in, *Metres*).
- **Maṅgalācaraṇa (auspicious invocation)** - every kāṇḍa opens with Sanskrit ślokas, and the fullest set heads the Bāl Kāṇḍ:
  - Vāṇī and Vināyaka (śl. 1);
  - Bhavānī and Śaṅkara as faith and trust (śl. 2);
  - the guru in Śaṅkara's form (śl. 3);
  - Vālmīki and Hanumān, "lord of poets and lord of monkeys" (*kavīśvara-kapīśvarau*, śl. 4);
  - Sītā (śl. 5);
  - Rāma as Hari, in whose māyā the world appears as a snake appears in a rope (śl. 6);
  - the sources-and-purpose verse (śl. 7).

  Awadhi soraṭhās to Gaṇeśa, Viṣṇu, Śiva and the guru follow. The other kāṇḍas open with one to three ślokas, mostly addressed to Rāma, Śiva or Hanumān.

## The "seven steps to Lake Mānasarovar" framing

- **Title, with two meanings** - the poem explains its own name. Śiva composed this story of Rāma and kept it in his own *mānasa* (mind). When the time came he told it to Śivā (Pārvatī), and "so Hara, looking into his heart, gladly named it *Rāmacaritamānasa*" (*raci mahesa nija mānasa rākhā … tāteṃ rāmacaritamānasa bara, dhareu nāma hiyaṃ heri haraṣi hara*, Bāl 35.6). The second meaning is the Himalayan lake **Mānasarovar**, which Tulsīdās turns into an extended allegory of the poem (Bāl 35–43).
- **Seven kāṇḍas = seven flights of steps** - "the seven books are the seven lovely flights of steps" (*sapta prabandha subhaga sopānā*, Bāl 37.1). That is why the colophons call each book a *sopāna*.
- **Lake symbolism extended** - the poem's "four beautiful dialogues" are the lake's **four ghāṭs** (*suṭhi suṃdara saṃbāda bara … ghāṭa manohara cāri*, dohā 36). Rāma's unbounded greatness is the depth of its water (37.1). Rāma's and Sītā's glory is the nectar-like water, the similes are its ripples, and the caupāīs are its thick lotus-leaves (37.2). The architecture of the poem becomes a meditative geography.

## Saguṇa theology & the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa base

- **Primary theological source = Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** - the chief doctrinal template is the Vedāntic **[Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](./adhyatma-ramayana.md)**. It is a late-medieval Sanskrit text, most often dated to the 14th–15th century, that presents itself as part of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa [late text]. It makes Rāma the supreme Brahman and Sītā his māyā-power. Tulsīdās adds bhakti warmth to what he takes from it:
  - the **Śiva–Pārvatī frame**;
  - the **chāyā-Sītā** (AR 3.7.1–4, in the *Mānas* at Araṇya 24.1–3);
  - Śabarī's **nine-fold bhakti** (AR 3.10, in the *Mānas* at Araṇya 35–36);
  - the **Rāmeśvara liṅga** installed at the causeway (AR 6.4.1–3, in the *Mānas* at Laṅkā 2.2);
  - the **Kālanemi** episode (AR 6.6–7, in the *Mānas* at Laṅkā 56–57).
  - *Other sources:* Vālmīki is the narrative spine and is honoured in śl. 4 as *kavīśvara*. The **Hanumannāṭaka** and Jayadeva's **Prasannarāghava** are also counted among the sources (Wikipedia, *Tulsidas*). Purāṇic material supplies Jaya–Vijaya and Nārada's curse. For the **māyā-Sītā**, the [Kūrma Purāṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/kurma-purana.md) is an older witness to the motif than the Adhyātma; see the [Adhyātma page](./adhyatma-ramayana.md).
- **Saguṇa Brahman bhakti** - Rāma is *Parabrahma* with form and attributes (saguṇa), worshipped through loving surrender (*śaraṇāgati*) and through the **Rām-nām**, the divine Name. Tulsīdās says outright that the Name is greater than the one named: "in my view the Name is greater than both", that is, than the formless and the embodied Brahman (*moreṃ mata baḍa nāmu duhū teṃ*, Bāl 23.1). He repeats that "the Name is greater than Brahman and than Rāma" (23.3), and the dohā closes the argument: "by my own reckoning I say the Name is greater than Rāma" (*kahauṃ nāmu baḍa rāma teṃ nija bicāra anusāra*, dohā 23). This places the work squarely in the saguṇa wing of the [north bhakti movement](../../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/north-bhakti-sants.md).
- **Reconciliation of nirguṇa & saguṇa** - the text states the bridge itself: "the unqualified and the qualified are both forms of Brahman" (*aguna saguna dui brahma sarūpā*, Bāl 23.1). Śiva repeats it to Pārvatī: "between saguṇa and nirguṇa there is no difference … the formless, unseen, unborn one becomes saguṇa under the sway of the devotee's love" (*sagunahi agunahi nahiṃ kachu bhedā … bhagata prema basa saguna so hoī*, Bāl 116.1). Scholars read this as a deliberate bridge across the bhakti spectrum. [scholarly]
- **Many Rāmāyaṇas, many kalpas** - the poem expects readers to know other tellings and tells them not to be surprised by its own. "Rāma has descended in many ways, and the Rāmāyaṇas are a hundred crore and endless" (*nānā bhāṃti rāma avatārā, rāmāyana sata koṭi apārā*, Bāl 33.3). The great sages have sung Hari's deeds in many ways "according to the difference of kalpas" (*kalapabheda haricarita suhāe*, 33.4). The *Mānas* thus builds in its own licence to differ from Vālmīki.

## The three nested frame-dialogues (narrators)

- **The chain of transmission (the text's own account)** - Śambhu composed the story and told it to Umā. He then gave it to Kākabhuśuṇḍi, knowing him to be a devotee fit to receive it. Yājñavalkya received it from Bhuśuṇḍi and sang it to Bharadvāja (*soi siva kāgabhusuṃḍihi dīnhā … tehi sana jāgabalika puni pāvā, tinha puni bharadvāja prati gāvā*, Bāl 30ka.2–3). Tulsīdās says he himself heard it from his guru at **Sūkarkhet** (Varāha-kṣetra) as a child, and understood little of it then (dohā 30ka–kha).
- **Śiva → Pārvatī** - the outermost divine frame, which begins in a **previous life** of Pārvatī.
  - As **Satī**, she doubted that the grieving prince searching the forest for his wife could be Brahman. She took **Sītā's form** to test him (dohā 52), and Rāma saw through her. Śiva later recalls the act: "Satī took the guise of Sītā" (*satīṃ kīnha sītā kara beṣā*, Bāl 56.4).
  - Śiva renounced her as his wife in his heart, and she gave up her body at Dakṣa's sacrifice (Bāl 48ka–64).
  - Reborn as **Pārvatī**, she won Śiva by austerity and married him (Bāl 65–103).
  - Only then does she ask him to remove her doubt about Rāma (Bāl 107–111), and his answer is the Rāma story.

  The frame establishes the Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava harmony central to Tulsīdās.
- **Yājñavalkya → Bharadvāja** - the sage **Yājñavalkya** expounds the tale to **Bharadvāja** at Prayāg. All the sages come there every year in **Māgha**, "when the sun enters Capricorn" (*māgha makaragata rabi jaba hoī*, Bāl 44.2). After the bathing, Bharadvāja asks his question. Yājñavalkya answers by relaying Śiva's narration to Umā (Bāl 44–47). This frame opens the recitation in the Bāl Kāṇḍ.
- **Kākabhuśuṇḍi → Garuḍa** - the immortal crow-sage **Kākabhuśuṇḍi** recounts Rāma's glory to **Garuḍa** (Viṣṇu's eagle-mount). In the war Nārada had sent Garuḍa to free Rāma from Indrajit's **serpent-noose** (*nāgapāśa*) (Uttar 58.2). Afterwards Garuḍa was troubled that the one whose Name frees people from worldly bondage could himself be bound by a petty demon's noose (dohā 58). He took the doubt to Nārada, then to Brahmā (Uttar 60.1), who sent him to Śiva. Śiva sent him to Bhuśuṇḍi, who lives on a **Nīla mountain far to the north of Sumeru** (Uttar 56.4). Bhuśuṇḍi's telling, his autobiography and his answers to Garuḍa's **seven questions** (Uttar 121ka) fill most of the Uttar Kāṇḍ. This is the most philosophically rich of the frames.
  - *Bhuśuṇḍi's past lives (Uttar 96–113):*
    - In an earlier Kali Yuga he was born a **śūdra in Ayodhyā** (Uttar 97ka). He became a proud devotee of Śiva who despised Viṣṇu.
    - When he insulted his brahmin guru in Śiva's temple, Śiva cursed him to become a serpent (*sarpa hohi*, Uttar 107ka.4). The guru sang the *Rudrāṣṭaka* to soften the curse (Uttar 108).
    - Many births later, born a brahmin, he asked the sage **Lomaśa** for teaching. Lomaśa taught the formless (nirguṇa) Brahman, and he kept arguing for worship of the Lord with form (saguṇa) instead (Uttar 111ka.4–7, 112ka.6). Lomaśa's curse made him a crow, "a caṇḍāla among birds" (*sapadi hohi pacchī caṃḍālā*, Uttar 112ka). That crow-body became his lasting form of devotion (Uttar 110kha–113).
- **The fourth ghāṭ** - the poem's own count of dialogues is **four**, not three (dohā 36, above), though the dohā does not name them. The Gita Press gloss lists Bhuśuṇḍi–Garuḍa, Śiva–Pārvatī, Yājñavalkya–Bharadvāja and, as the fourth, **Tulsīdās's own telling to the saints**. The poet's frequent asides to his own mind (*mana*) belong to that fourth frame.
  - *Variant reading (devotional commentary):* some commentators extend the fourth frame into a conversation between the devotee and the Lord, running beneath the three divine ones. The text itself specifies only the number four.

## Notable internal units & devotional uses

- **Sundar Kāṇḍ** - the fifth book: Hanumān's leap to Laṅkā, his meeting with Sītā, the burning of Laṅkā, Vibhīṣaṇa's surrender and the ocean's submission. In popular Hindu practice the *Mānas* **Sundar Kāṇḍ is recited as a stand-alone unit** for protection, removal of obstacles and Hanumān's grace. It is the single most ritually recited kāṇḍa. Compare the Vālmīki [Sundara Kāṇḍa](../5-sundara-kanda.md).
  - *Mānas-specific in this book:* Rāvaṇa **kicks Vibhīṣaṇa** out of the court (*asa kahi kīnhesi carana prahārā*, Sundar 41.3). In Vālmīki, Rāvaṇa only rebukes him harshly and Vibhīṣaṇa leaves (Yuddha 16).
  - *The ocean's offer:* Rāma prays to the ocean for three days without answer and then draws his bow. The ocean then tells him that the brothers Nala and Nīla received a sage's blessing as children, so that heavy rocks they touch "will float on the sea by your glory" (*tinha keṃ parasa kieṃ giri bhāre, tarihahiṃ jaladhi pratāpa tumhāre*, Sundar 60.1).
  - *A disputed line:* in the same speech the ocean says *ḍhola gavāṃra sūdra pasu nārī, sakala tāḍanā ke adhikārī* (Sundar 59.3). It is the most argued-over line of the poem. Readers dispute both the sense of *tāḍanā* and whether the opinion belongs to the speaker or to the poet. [disputed]
- **Hanumān Cālīsā** - the 40-caupāī Awadhi hymn to Hanumān, with opening and closing dohās. It is **traditionally attributed to Tulsīdās** on the strength of its signature line, and some authors doubt the attribution (Wikipedia, *Tulsidas*). It is a *separate* composition, not part of the *Mānas*'s seven kāṇḍas, but it is routinely printed and recited alongside the *Mānas*. [traditional attribution] [disputed]
- **Set-piece stutis extracted for worship** - several embedded hymns are recited on their own:
  - *bhae pragaṭa kṛpālā dīnadayālā*, the chand at Rāma's birth (Bāl 192);
  - Ahalyā's hymn *parasata pada pāvana*, in Tribhaṅgī (Bāl 211);
  - Sītā's prayer to Gaurī in the garden, *jaya jaya giribararāja kisorī* (Bāl 235.3);
  - Atri's *namāmi bhakta-vatsalaṃ* (Araṇya 4);
  - the Sanskrit **Rudrāṣṭaka**, *namāmīśam īśāna nirvāṇarūpaṃ*, which Bhuśuṇḍi's guru sings to Śiva (Uttar 108; its colophon reads *rudrāṣṭakam idaṃ proktaṃ vipreṇa haratoṣaye*).
  - *Not from the Mānas:* the popular *śrī rāmacandra kṛpālu bhaju mana* is a hymn attributed to Tulsīdās and often associated with the **Vinaya-patrikā** (Wikipedia, *Shri Ramachandra Kripalu*). It does not occur in the seven kāṇḍas; the full text was searched. The **Dohāvalī** is likewise a separate Tulsīdās collection of couplets, not a section of the *Mānas*.
- **Navāhna and māsa-pārāyaṇa** - printed editions mark rest-points (*viśrāma*) that divide the poem for recitation over nine days (*navāhna*) or over a month (*māsa-pārāyaṇa*).

## Laṅkā Kāṇḍ — the war-book divergences from Vālmīki

The *Mānas*'s war-book carries the most-quoted images of the whole Rāma story in the Hindi belt, and they are Tulsīdās's rather than Vālmīki's. Because they are the versions everyone knows, they are routinely cited as "the Rāmāyaṇa" — so each is set here against what the Sanskrit epic actually says.

| Image | Rāmcaritmānas (Laṅkā Kāṇḍ) | Vālmīki |
|---|---|---|
| The Setu | stones **float**, by Nala and Nīla's touch through a sage's childhood boon and by Rāma's *pratāpa* (Sundar 60.1; Laṅkā dohā 3) | **no floating miracle**: Nala, Viśvakarman's son, has a boon from his father and is his equal, and Sāgara promises "I will bear it" (*tam ahaṃ dhārayiṣyāmi*, Yuddha 22.44–45) |
| Rāmeśvaram | Rāma **installs a Śiva-liṅga** at the shore as the causeway is begun (Laṅkā 2.2) | no installation; on the flight home Rāma only points out where Mahādeva once showed him grace, near the "Setubandha" tīrtha (Yuddha 123.19–20, vulgate) |
| Rāvaṇa's death-wound | of 31 arrows, one **dries the nectar-pool in his navel** and the rest take heads and arms (Laṅkā dohā 102, 103.1) | the Brahmā-weapon **tears open his heart** (*bibheda hṛdayaṃ*, Yuddha 108.18); no amṛta, no navel |
| Sītā and the fire | a **chāyā-Sītā** is abducted; the real Sītā is hidden in Agni and returns from it (Araṇya 24; Laṅkā 108–109) | the abducted Sītā is Sītā, and the *agni-parīkṣā* is a real ordeal of the real woman (Yuddha 115–118) |
| Aṅgada's embassy | he **plants his foot** in the court and no rākṣasa can move it (Laṅkā 34.4–35.2) | he delivers the message, then leaps onto the palace roof, which crumbles under him (Yuddha 41.89–91) |
| Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā | never narrated — but **Mandodarī taunts Rāvaṇa** with it (Laṅkā 36.1) | absent entirely |

- **The floating stones** - Tulsīdās gives the miracle twice. The ocean credits it to the Nala–Nīla boon acting through Rāma's glory (Sundar 60.1). The Laṅkā Kāṇḍ dohā sums it up as *"śrī raghubīra pratāpa te sindhu tare pāṣāna"*, "by the might of Raghubīr the stones crossed the ocean" (Laṅkā dohā 3). The couplet's second line calls those who leave Rāma to worship another lord dull-witted. The further image of **Rāma's name written on each stone** is a popular elaboration built on that line rather than a verse of the *Mānas*; the squirrel who carries pebbles is folk, later still. [folk]
- **Rāmeśvaram** - as the causeway is begun, Rāma declares "here I will establish Śambhu" (*karihauṃ ihāṃ saṃbhu thāpanā*, Laṅkā 2.2). He then promises that those who see Rāmeśvara will reach his own realm at death (*je rāmesvara darasanu karihahiṃ*, Laṅkā 3.1). The *Adhyātma* has the same installation (AR 6.4.1–3), and the Vālmīki vulgate has none. In the plot itself it is the *Mānas*'s Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava harmony. [late text]
- **The navel-nectar** - Vibhīṣaṇa discloses that "in his navel dwells a pool of nectar; on its strength Rāvaṇa lives" (*nābhikuṃḍa piyūṣa basa yākeṃ*, Laṅkā 102.3). Rāma draws the bow to his ear and looses **thirty-one** arrows (dohā 102). One dries up the navel-pool, and the others shear off the ten heads and twenty arms (*sāyaka eka nābhi sara soṣā, apara lage bhuja sira*, Laṅkā 103.1). This is the death every North Indian audience knows — and the reason the corpus flags the Vālmīki heart-shot separately in the [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](../6-yuddha-kanda.md) file.
- **The chāyā-Sītā** - inherited from the [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](./adhyatma-ramayana.md).
  - *Araṇya Kāṇḍ:* while Lakṣmaṇa is away gathering roots, Rāma tells Sītā privately that he is about to play a human part. "Dwell in the fire until I destroy the night-roamers" (*tumha pāvaka mahuṃ karahu nivāsā*). She enters the fire and leaves her reflection (*nija pratibiṃba*) in her place, "of the same nature and form", and not even Lakṣmaṇa knows (Araṇya 24.1–3). What Rāvaṇa carries off is therefore never Sītā at all.
  - *Laṅkā Kāṇḍ:* Rāma speaks "a few harsh words" on purpose (dohā 108), because he means to bring out the Sītā he had first placed in the fire (*sītā prathama anala mahuṃ rākhī, pragaṭa kīnhi caha*, Laṅkā 108.7). In the fire "the reflection and the worldly stain burned away" (*pratibiṃba aru laukika kalaṃka pracaṃḍa pāvaka mahuṃ jare*). Agni then takes form and hands the true Sītā back (Laṅkā 109, chand 1–2). The ordeal is a *return* of the true Sītā, not a test of her.

  The doctrine dissolves the ordeal's whole moral problem — which is why Tulsī can keep the scene and still make it joyful. [late text]
- **Aṅgada's foot** - the court set-piece the Rāmlīlā stages every year. It is Tulsīdās's own text, not a stage addition. Aṅgada makes his vow before the assembly and plants his foot (*sabhā mājha pana kari pada ropā*, Laṅkā 34.4). If anyone can shift it, Rāma will turn back and Sītā is lost (34.5). Warrior after warrior fails (dohā 34ka–kha). When Rāvaṇa himself rises and bends to grasp it, Aṅgada answers: "grasping my feet will not save you; why not go and grasp Rāma's feet, fool?" (*mama pada gaheṃ na tora ubārā, gahasi na rāma carana saṭha jāī*, Laṅkā 35.1–2). Vālmīki's Aṅgada wrecks the palace roof instead.
- **The Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā, and the Mānas's own oddity about it** - the protective line drawn around Sītā's hut is **not in Vālmīki and not in the** *Mānas*'s **Araṇya Kāṇḍ either**.
  - *Araṇya Kāṇḍ:* Lakṣmaṇa simply leaves when Sītā's wounding words shake his mind, "prompted by Hari" (*marama bacana jaba sītā bolā, hari prerita lachimana mana ḍolā*, Araṇya 28.3). No line is drawn.
  - *Laṅkā Kāṇḍ:* after Aṅgada's embassy, **Mandodarī** deflates her husband's boasts of valour. "Rāma's younger brother drew a thin line, and you could not cross even that — such is your manhood" (*rāmānuja laghu rekha khacāī, sou nahiṃ nāghehu asi manusāī*, **Laṅkā 36.1**).

  The taunt presupposes an episode the poem has not told. The *Mānas* is therefore a witness that the rekhā was already current in performance and oral tradition by the 1570s, even though no Sanskrit text carries it. Versions that actually *narrate* the drawing of the line are later still. They include the Rāmlīlā stage and vernacular retellings such as Rādheśyām Kathāvācak's Hindi *Rāmāyaṇ*, where Sītā crosses it absent-mindedly in the duty of hospitality to a guest (Wikipedia, *Lakshmana rekha*). [oral tradition]
- **Kālanemi and Bharata's arrow** - two more Laṅkā-Kāṇḍ episodes take a Mānas-specific shape.
  - *Kālanemi:* Rāvaṇa sends the rākṣasa **Kālanemi**, disguised as a sage, to delay Hanumān on his way to the herb-mountain (Laṅkā 56–57). The episode comes from the Adhyātma line (AR 6.6–7), not from Vālmīki.
  - *Bharata's arrow:* flying back over Ayodhyā with the mountain, Hanumān is taken for a rākṣasa. **Bharata** brings him down with a headless arrow (*binu phara sāyaka*, dohā 58), learns the news, and offers to send him on to Laṅkā riding his arrow (*caḍhu mama sāyaka saila sametā*, Laṅkā 60ka.3). Vālmīki has no such meeting.

## Episodes the Mānas adds or reshapes (Bāl to Uttar)

Each entry gives the *Mānas* reading with its verse, then Vālmīki's as a separate variant. Neither is treated as a correction of the other.

- **Why Rāma descends — several causes in several kalpas (Bāl 121–188)** - Śiva gives Pārvatī more than one reason for the avatāra, each belonging to a different kalpa (cf. *kalapabheda*, Bāl 33.4).
  - *Jaya and Vijaya:* Hari's two doorkeepers are cursed to be born as demons (Bāl 122–123).
  - *Jalandhara:* in another kalpa the demon **Jalandhara** becomes Rāvaṇa, after Viṣṇu deceives Jalandhara's chaste wife (*tahāṃ jalaṃdhara rāvana bhayaū*, Bāl 124ka.1).
  - *Nārada's curse:* **Nārada**, infatuated with the princess Viśvamohinī (Bāl 130.2), is given a monkey's face by Viṣṇu. He curses Viṣṇu to take a human body, to suffer separation from his wife, and to be helped by monkeys (Bāl 137.4; episode Bāl 124ka–139).
  - *Manu and Śatarūpā:* **Manu and Śatarūpā** win by austerity the boon "you will be king of Avadh, and then I will be your son" (*hoihahu avadha bhuāla taba maiṃ hoba tumhāra suta*, dohā 151). They are reborn as Daśaratha and Kauśalyā (Bāl 142–152).
  - *Pratāpabhānu:* the righteous king **Pratāpabhānu** of Kekaya is tricked by a vanquished enemy disguised as an ascetic and cursed by brahmins. He is reborn as **Rāvaṇa**, his brother Arimardana as **Kumbhakarṇa**, and his minister Dharmaruci as **Vibhīṣaṇa** (Bāl 153–176.2).
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* the gods at Daśaratha's sacrifice ask for a way to end Rāvaṇa, whom Brahmā's boon protects from gods and demons. Viṣṇu agrees to be born as a man (Bāla 15–16). The doorkeeper, Jalandhara, Nārada and Pratāpabhānu backstories are absent.
- **Śiva's wedding (Bāl 65–103)** - Pārvatī's austerities, the burning of Kāma, the comic wedding procession of ghosts and goblins and the marriage itself (Bāl 101.3) are told at length *before* Rāma is born. They are the frame's prerequisite, since Pārvatī can ask her question only once she is Śiva's wife.
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* the Bāla touches this material only in Viśvāmitra's place-legends: Śiva burned Kāma at the hermitage where the party rests (Bāla 23), and Himavat gave Umā to Rudra (Bāla 35). There is no wedding narrative and no frame.
- **Ahalyā as stone (Bāl 210–211)** - Rāma sees a rock (*silā*, Bāl 210.6). Viśvāmitra explains that Gautama's wife, under her husband's curse, "bears a body of stone" and longs for the dust of Rāma's feet (*gautama nāri śrāpa basa upala deha dhari dhīra*, dohā 210). At the touch of his foot she rises (*parasata pada pāvana*, Bāl 211 chand).
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* Gautama's curse makes her "invisible to all beings", living on air and lying in ashes (*adṛśyā sarvabhūtānām … bhasmaśāyinī*, Bāla 48.30). Rāma's arrival ends the curse, and it is the brothers who **clasp her feet** (*rāghavau tu tatas tasyāḥ pādau jagṛhatuḥ*, Bāla 49.17). The stone and the foot-touch are later; both are already in the [Adhyātma](./adhyatma-ramayana.md) (AR 1.5.27–36). See [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../1-bala-kanda.md).
- **The garden meeting (*puṣpa-vāṭikā*) and Sītā's Gaurī-pūjā (Bāl 227–236)** - Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa go into Janaka's garden to pick flowers for their guru. At that moment Sītā arrives, sent by her mother to worship Girijā (*tehi avasara sītā tahaṃ āī, girijā pūjana janani paṭhāī*, Bāl 228.1). The two see each other and fall in love before the bow-contest, and Sītā prays to Gaurī for him as her husband (Bāl 235–236).
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* there is no prior meeting. Janaka shows the bow to Viśvāmitra's party and offers Sītā to whoever can string it, and Rāma breaks it (Bāla 66–67).
- **The bow-contest as a public svayaṃvara, and Paraśurāma at the court (Bāl 240–285)** - Kings "from every continent" have come on hearing Janaka's vow (*dīpa dīpa ke bhūpati nānā*, Bāl 251.4), and all of them fail. As the bow breaks, **Paraśurāma** storms into the same assembly (*tehiṃ avasara suni siva dhanu bhaṃgā, āyasu bhṛgukula kamala pataṃgā*, Bāl 268.1). A long, sharp exchange follows, with Lakṣmaṇa's sarcasm set against Rāma's humility (Bāl 268–284). Paraśurāma then recognises Rāma's power (dohā 284) and leaves for the forest (Bāl 285.4).
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* Paraśurāma confronts the wedding party **on the road back to Ayodhyā**, after the marriage, and there is no Lakṣmaṇa dialogue (Bāla 74–76).
- **The boatman Kevaṭ (Ayodhyā 100–101)** - at the Gaṅgā the boatman will not bring his boat. He says he knows Rāma's "secret" (*māgī nāva na kevaṭu ānā, kahai tumhāra maramu maiṃ jānā*, Ayodhyā 100.2): the dust of those feet turned a stone into a woman, and his wooden boat might go the same way (100.3). He washes the feet, drinks the water with his family (dohā 101), and refuses the fare.
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* Guha, king of the Niṣādas, has a sturdy boat with a helmsman brought, and there is no foot-washing (Ayodhyā 52.6–7).
  - *Variant (Adhyātma):* a boatman washes Rāma's feet for the same fear, but at the Gaṅgā crossing on the way to Mithilā (AR 1.6.2–5; see the [Adhyātma page](./adhyatma-ramayana.md)).
- **Janaka comes to Citrakūṭ (Ayodhyā 270–316)** - after Bharata reaches Citrakūṭ, Janaka's envoys arrive (Ayodhyā 270.2), and then **Janaka himself comes with the Mithilā court** (*giribaru dīkha janakapati jabahīṃ*, Ayodhyā 275.1). The two royal households confer before Rāma gives Bharata his sandals (*prabhu kari kṛpā pāṃvarīṃ dīnhīṃ*, Ayodhyā 316.2). Janaka has no part in Vālmīki's Citrakūṭ meeting.
- **Śabarī and the nine-fold bhakti (Araṇya 34–36)** - Śabarī falls at the brothers' feet and brings them "the most delicious roots, tubers and fruits". Rāma eats them with love, praising them again and again (*prema sahita prabhu khāe bārambāra bakhāni*, dohā 34). He then teaches her the **nine kinds of devotion** (*navadhā bhagati*), beginning with the company of saints (Araṇya 35.4 ff.).
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* Śabarī has "gathered forest food of many kinds" for Rāma (*mayā tu vividhaṃ vanyaṃ saṃcitam*, Araṇya 74.17). She shows him Mataṅga's hermitage and enters the fire to go to heaven, and there is no teaching.
  - *Folk:* the **tasted berries** (*jūṭhe ber*), the fruit she bites to test before offering, are in neither text. [folk]
- **The Uttar Kāṇḍ — no exile of Sītā** - after the coronation the *Mānas* describes **Rāmarājya**, where "no affliction of body, fate or the elements" touches anyone (*daihika daivika bhautika tāpā, rāma rāja nahiṃ kāhuhi byāpā*, Uttar 21.1). Of the next generation it gives a single couplet: "Sītā bore two lovely sons, Lava and Kuśa, sung of in Veda and Purāṇa", and each of the brothers had two sons (*dui suta sundara sītāṃ jāe, lava kusa beda purānanha gāe*, Uttar 25.3–4). The poem then turns to the Bhuśuṇḍi–Garuḍa dialogue, a long portrait of the Kali Yuga (Uttar 97–102) and the doctrine of bhakti.
  - *Absent:* Sītā's banishment, the washerman's gossip, Vālmīki's hermitage, Śambūka, the horse-sacrifice narrative, Lava and Kuśa singing the epic, Sītā's return into the earth and Rāma's departure into the Sarayū. The horse-sacrifice survives only as one line, "the Lord performed crores of horse-sacrifices" (*koṭinha bājimedha prabhu kīnhe*, Uttar 24.1). A search of the Uttar text finds no Śambūka and no washerman, and its only mention of Vālmīki is Bhuśuṇḍi's recap of the forest years (*bālamīka prabhu milana bakhānā*, Uttar 65.2). Compare the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../7-uttara-kanda.md) and [Uttara-Rāmāyaṇa / Lava–Kuśa](../../uttara-ramayana-lava-kusha.md) pages.
  - *Interpretation:* the omission is commonly read as Tulsīdās declining to show his Lord in an act that devotion cannot bear. It also fits the chāyā-Sītā doctrine, under which the Sītā held in Laṅkā was never the real one. [scholarly]

## Cultural & ritual afterlife

- **Rāmlīlā** - the *Mānas* is the **textual basis of Rāmlīlā**, the multi-day dramatic enactment of the Rāma story across North India. UNESCO inscribed **"Ramlila, the traditional performance of the Ramayana"** on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage in **2008**, after proclaiming it in 2005. The inscription covers the Rāmlīlā tradition as a whole, not one town's staging. It says most Rāmlīlās recount episodes from the Rāmcaritmānas. It lists the Rāmlīlās of **Ayodhyā, Rāmnagar and Benares, Vṛndāvan, Almora, Sattna and Madhubani** as the most representative. Most performances last ten to twelve days; Rāmnagar's "may last an entire month" (UNESCO ICH).
- **Mānas-recitation traditions** - *akhaṇḍ pāṭh* (continuous recitation), *navāhna pāṭh* (nine-day), *māsa-pārāyaṇa* (month-long), Mānas-kathā expounders (*kathā-vācak*), and household daily reading make it functionally a "fifth Veda" for the Hindi belt [folk].
- **Diaspora reach** - the *Mānas* travelled with indentured labourers from the Awadhi- and Bhojpuri-speaking districts to the Caribbean (Trinidad, Guyana, Suriname), Fiji and Mauritius, and it is read throughout the wider Indian diaspora.
  - *Not the Mānas:* the Rāma traditions of Thailand, Cambodia, Laos, Indonesia, Myanmar and Malaysia (Ramakien, Reamker, the Old Javanese Kakawin and others) descend from older, independent transmissions, several of them centuries before Tulsīdās. They are not derived from the *Mānas*; see [Southeast Asian Rāmāyaṇas](./southeast-asian-ramayanas.md).

## UNESCO Memory of the World (2024)

- **Inscription** - **"The Illustrated Manuscripts of Rāmacaritamānasa of Tulasīdāsa"** was inscribed on the **Memory of the World Committee for Asia and the Pacific (MOWCAP) Regional Register** at the committee's **10th General Meeting, 7–8 May 2024, in Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia** (UNESCO).
- **Joint Indian nominations** - it was inscribed alongside **"The 15th-Century Manuscript of the Pañcatantra Fables"** and **"The Manuscript of the Sahṛdayāloka-Locana"**, which is Ānandavardhana's *Dhvanyāloka* with Abhinavagupta's *Locana*. UNESCO says this is the first time India has had three nominations inscribed in a single cycle. The UNESCO article also quotes the head of the Kala Nidhi division of the Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts (IGNCA).
- **Manuscript heritage** - the MOWCAP register entry gives the holder of the inscribed manuscripts as the **National Museum, New Delhi**, and notes **over 200 miniature paintings** in them. What is inscribed is these illustrated copies as documents, not the poem as such.
  - *Unconfirmed:* an exam-coaching summary says the nomination included an 18th-century manuscript "in Arabic". Neither the UNESCO article nor the MOWCAP entry mentions one. [unverified]

## Relation to other Rāmāyaṇas

- **Vs. Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa** - Vālmīki is the Sanskrit *ādi-kāvya*, narrative and heroic, set in Tretā. The *Mānas* is Awadhi, devotional and theologically saguṇa, composed in Kali Yuga (1574 CE). It compresses the war and the forest books and amplifies the birth, the marriage, Bharata's love and the doctrine of the Name. It also stops before the Uttara's tragedies. The episode-by-episode divergences are set out in the two sections above.
- **Vs. the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** - the Adhyātma is the closest parent, sharing the Śiva–Pārvatī frame, the chāyā-Sītā, the Rāmeśvara liṅga, Kālanemi and the nine-fold bhakti. The Adhyātma also has Ahalyā on a stone and a boatman's foot-washing, though on the way to Mithilā rather than at the exile crossing. The [Adhyātma page](./adhyatma-ramayana.md) does not record the Bhuśuṇḍi–Garuḍa frame, Pratāpabhānu, Janaka at Citrakūṭ, or the rekhā taunt among that text's episodes.
- **One of the "three hundred Rāmāyaṇas"** - the *Mānas* is the dominant North-Indian member of the vast family of Rāma retellings (cf. Tamil [Kamba Rāmāyaṇam](./kamban-iramavataram.md), Bengali [Kṛttivāsī](./krittivasi-ramayan.md), Telugu, Odia, etc.). The poem itself says the Rāmāyaṇas number "a hundred crore" (Bāl 33.3). See the [Rāmāyaṇa overview](../README.md) for the recension landscape. *(This recensions/ directory is an open-ended roster — additional regional recensions remain to be cataloged.)*
