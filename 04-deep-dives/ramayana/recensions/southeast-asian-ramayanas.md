# Southeast Asian Ramayanas — Ramakien, Kakawin, Reamker & beyond

Deep-dive: [Ramayana](../README.md) · [Deep Dives](../../README.md) · [Master Index](../../../README.md)
The Rama-cycle as it traveled, localized, and was reborn across mainland and maritime Southeast Asia — from the 9th-c. Old Javanese **Kakawin** to the Thai **Ramakien**, Khmer **Reamker**, Lao **Phra Lak Phra Lam**, Burmese **Yama Zatdaw**, Malay **Hikayat Seri Rama**, and the Maranao prose tale **Maharadia Lawana**. These are not Hindu *recensions* of [Valmiki](../README.md) but full re-tellings absorbed into Theravada-Buddhist and (in the maritime zone) Islamic literary worlds, kept here for comparison. Cross-link the parent overview's [Southeast Asian retellings](../README.md#southeast-asian-retellings) section.

> **Two clocks.** The *story* still nominally sits at the close of the [Treta Yuga](../../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md), but these are *texts* with their own academic datings (Kakawin c. 856–930 CE; Ramakien of Rama I 1798; Reamker 16th–18th c. after older lost layers). This file dates the **texts** and catalogs the **variant plots/characters**, not the cosmic events. The transmission vector is not direct Valmiki but a web of Sanskrit *kavya* (esp. the [Bhattikavya](#sources)), South Indian — especially Tamil — tellings (Thai and Malay name-forms such as *Kalaikot*, *Lastiyan*, *Baradan*, *Mulabalam* are Tamil forms; see [Kamban's Iramavataram](kamban-iramavataram.md)), the Pali *Dasaratha Jataka* (see [Jain & Buddhist Ramayanas](jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md)), and centuries of oral/performance tradition, the shadow-play above all. (Singaravelu 1968; Singaravelu 1982) [scholarly]

## Shared dynamics of localization

- **Topographic transplant** - Ayodhya, Lanka, and the forest exile are re-mapped onto local geography and royal capitals: the Thai capital **Ayutthaya** is named for Ayodhya and **Lopburi** (Lavapuri) is tied to Rama's son Lava; the Lao telling sets Rama's city at **Chanthaburi Sisattanak (Vientiane)** on the Mekong, with Ravana's first city at Inthapathanakhon. Thai kings took Rama's name long before the Chakri dynasty — Ramkhamhaeng of Sukhothai (inscription of 1292), Ramadhibodi founder of Ayutthaya (1350) — and the Bangkok monarchs are styled "Rama I–X". (Singaravelu 1982; Sahai) [folk tradition]
- **Religious reframing** - On the Theravada mainland (Thai, Lao, Khmer, Burmese), Rama is folded into Buddhist ethics; the Lao *Phra Lak Phra Lam* is explicitly cast as a *Jataka* in which the Buddha identifies himself as Rama, echoing the *Dasaratha Jataka*; karma and merit displace Hindu *bhakti*/avatara theology. In the Islamized maritime zone (Malay, Maranao), the frame becomes Islamic — Allah, the Prophet Adam, the angel Gabriel, and Sultanate court culture. (Sahai; Francisco 1969) [scholarly]
- **Rama's divinity: kept in name, thinned in character** - The Thai and Khmer texts keep the label — Phra Ram is Phra Narai reborn, Preah Ream an incarnation of Vishnu — as Valmiki's frame does (Vishnu agrees to be born as Dasaratha's son, VR CE 1.14.17–19, 1.15.3; Brahma's hymn names Rama as Vishnu, VR CE 6.105.5–14). The Malay case depends on the recension: Wikipedia's character list calls Seri Rama an *awatara* of Wisnu, but Sen's review of Aveling's translation of the Laud/Shellabear text finds him "not an incarnation" but an ordinary man with MahaBisnu and the Prophet Adam among his ancestors, and Singaravelu notes no mention of Vishnu's incarnation at the Malay birth-sacrifice (1968, p. 157). What changes is the portrayal: Rama becomes a human, sometimes rash king, and in the Lao Jataka framing the Hindu identity gives way to the bodhisatta. Several versions let the *antagonist* (Thotsakan, Maharaja Wana) be partly sympathetic. (Singaravelu 1982; Sahai; Boloji review; Wikipedia, "Hikayat Seri Rama") [scholarly]
- **The Hanuman expansion** - The most widespread SE Asian innovation: the Hanuman of [Sundara Kanda](../5-sundara-kanda.md), who has no wife or son in Valmiki and whose mind stays unmoved when he searches Ravana's sleeping harem (VR CE 5.9.38), becomes in Thai, Khmer and Lao tellings an amorous, scene-stealing warrior with wives, sons, and comic-romantic episodes; the Lao and Malay tellings also make him Rama's own son. (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 64–65, 69; Sahai, pp. 71, 73) (The later Indian image of a strictly celibate Hanuman is post-Valmiki devotional tradition.) [folk]
  - *Exception (Burmese):* in the Burmese *Yama Pyazat Daw-gyi* (*Thiri Rama*) Hanuman is celibate and decorous, which Dawn F. Rooney takes to follow Valmiki more closely than the Ramakien does. (Rooney 2022, pp. 333–334, as cited on Wikipedia) [scholarly]
- **Performance-first transmission** - These epics live primarily as *masked dance, shadow play, and puppetry* (Thai *khon* & *nang yai*; Khmer *lkhon khol*; Javanese/Balinese *wayang kulit*; Malay *wayang kulit Kelantan*; Burmese *yokthe* marionette & dance), so plot survives through stagecraft more than fixed manuscript. Thai scholars hold that the Rama story reached the Menam valley together with the shadow-play by way of the Malay Peninsula. (Singaravelu 1982) [folk]
- **New episodes** - A floating stock of non-Valmiki episodes recurs region-wide, esp. **Hanuman and the mermaid princess** (Suphanna-Maccha / Sovann Maccha) who tries to sabotage the causeway to Lanka and bears Hanuman a son. In Valmiki the causeway is built by Nala, son of Vishvakarman, with no saboteur (VR CE 6.15.8–20). [folk]
  - *Variant (Malay, Hikayat Seri Rama):* Ravana's son Gangga Mahasura orders the fish to wreck the causeway and Hanuman stuns them with his tail; separately, a fish swallows Hanuman's seed during his leap to Lanka and bears a son, Hanuman Tuganggah. (Singaravelu 1982, p. 65 nn.) [folk variant]
  - *Variant (Khmer):* the mermaid episode is absent from the standard written Reamker, where Rama compels the ocean god instead; it lives in the royal ballet. (Wikipedia, "Reamker") [folk variant]

## Thai — Ramakien (รามเกียรติ์)

- **Ramakien ("Glory of Rama")** - The Thai national epic; *Ramakirti* = "fame/glory of Rama." Older Ayutthaya-era versions were lost in the 1767 Burmese sack of Ayutthaya, and King Taksin's Thonburi court produced a dramatic version. The standard text was **composed under King Rama I (r. 1782–1809)** of the Chakri dynasty from surviving oral and written sources: a long poem in Thai *klon* verse, 52,086 verses, not divided into kandas. Later versions: **Rama II** (1815, for *khon* and *lakhon*), **Rama IV** and **Rama VI** (1910). (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 55–57) [late text]
  - *Dating variant:* Singaravelu gives **1798** for Rama I's text; Wikipedia gives both 1797 and "between 1799 and 1807". Not reconciled here. [disputed]
  - *Sources of the text:* Thai scholars derive it mainly from oral and shadow-play sources with Valmiki and Tamil, Bengali and Hindi versions also used (Singaravelu 1982, p. 57); Rama VI identified Valmiki, the *Vishnu Purana* and the *Hanuman Nataka* (Wikipedia, citing L. Ghosh 2017). [scholarly]
- **Prelude: Thotsakan's former life** - The poem opens with Phra Narai as the boar slaying Hiranyak, then tells of **Nonthok**, Phra Isuan's servant, granted a diamond finger that kills whatever it points at; when he abuses it, Narai dances before him as a beautiful woman, Nonthok copies her gestures, points the finger at himself and dies, to be reborn as **Thotsakan**. Singaravelu compares the Skanda Purana's Bhasmasura-type tale. Not in Valmiki. (Singaravelu 1982, p. 58) [folk variant]
- **Phra Ram** - Rama as a Thai prince; the reincarnation of **Phra Narai (Vishnu/Narayana)**, with Phra Lak, Phra Phrot and Phra Satrud as Narai's serpent, discus and mace (a pattern Singaravelu traces to Kamban). He acts as a human king in a cosmos where **Phra Isuan (Shiva)** "plays a commanding role" at every crisis, and he is capable of rash anger — ordering Sida's death over a drawn portrait, then repenting. (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 59, 68) [folk]
- **Thotsakan / Tosakanth** - Ravana, the ten-faced, twenty-armed king of Longka (from *Dashakantha*, "ten-necked"); son of Lastiyan (Pulastya, a Tamil name-form). Drawn as a complex, partly sympathetic figure whose abduction of Sida is "shrouded in the noble motive of love". His **soul is kept outside his body in a receptacle held by his teacher Khobutacan**; Hanuman and Ongkot (Angada) pretend to defect and obtain it, and Hanuman crushes it as Phra Ram's arrow strikes Thotsakan's chest. In Valmiki, Rama's arrow simply pierces Ravana's heart (VR CE 6.97.17). He sets Mount Kailasa upright again — the reverse of Valmiki's Ravana heaving it up (VR CE 7.16.17–20). (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 58–59, 66, 69; Wikipedia, "Ramakien", for the ten faces and twenty arms) [folk]
- **Nang Montho** - Mandodari, here **created from a frog** by four sages and given to Thotsakan by Phra Isuan as a reward; Thotsakan's first wife is Kala Akhi, daughter of Kala Nakha of the underworld. (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 58–59) [folk variant]
- **Nang Sida** - Sita, the incarnation of Narai's consort Laksami, and in the Ramakien **Thotsakan's own daughter**. A demon in crow form (Kakanasun) snatches part of the consecrated rice from Thotsarot's sacrifice; Nang Montho eats it and bears a girl. **Phiphek** (Vibhishana), an astrologer, declares the child inauspicious and fated to destroy the rakshasa race, and she is sealed in a casket and floated on the river. King **Chonok** (Janaka) finds her, buries her within lotus petals, and recovers her with a plough sixteen years later — the Ramakien fuses the Ravana's-daughter motif with Valmiki's furrow birth (VR CE 1.65.14–15). Rama's war is therefore a father losing a daughter he cast away. (Singaravelu 1968, pp. 158–159, 171–172; wisdomlib, Ramakien characters) [folk variant]
  - **The wider "Sita as Ravana's daughter" strand** - Not a Thai eccentricity: the Malay **Hikayat Seri Rama**, the Lao **Phra Lak Phra Lam**, and (per Przyluski) the Khmer and Tibetan and Khotanese tellings all make Sita the antagonist's daughter, though the mechanism differs in each — see the Malay and Lao sections and the comparative note below. The Jain *Vasudevahindi* (Sanghadasa) carries the motif in India. [folk variant]
  - **The late banishment, re-motivated** - The demoness **Adun**, disguised as a beautiful maid, persuades Sida to draw Thotsakan on a slate; Phra Ram orders Phra Lak to take her to the forest and kill her, and Lak spares her and shows Ram a doe's heart. This replaces the citizens' gossip that Bhadra reports to Rama in the [Uttara Kanda](../7-uttara-kanda.md) (VR CE 7.42.7–20) with a villain's plot: the citizens are acquitted, but Rama's own rashness is not. (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 67–68) [folk variant]
  - **Sons and ending** - Sida shelters with the sage **Wachamarik** (Valmiki) and bears **Phra Monkut**; when the sage thinks the child lost, he draws Monkut on a magic slate and puts it in the sacred fire, creating **Phra Loph** (compare the *Kathasaritsagara*, where Valmiki creates Kusha from grass). Sida refuses to return, is tricked by a false report of Ram's death, and goes down into the underworld; **Phra Isuan reunites the couple**. In Valmiki, Sita enters the earth and does not return (VR CE 7.88.10–14). (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 67–68) [folk variant]
- **Other Ramakien departures** - Ram and Sida fall in love at first sight before the bow contest (as in Kamban); Kucci (Manthara) bears a grudge from the child Ram's pellet-shots at her hump; Thotsakan flings Sida's ring at Sadayu (Jatayu); Phali catches Ram's arrow and plunges it into himself to honour Isuan's decree; the underworld king **Maiyarap** (Mahiravana) abducts Ram and is killed by Hanuman — a parallel to the Bengali *Mahiravana* episode (see [Krittivasi Ramayan](krittivasi-ramayan.md)); Ram destroys the causeway with his Phralayawat arrow after the war. (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 63–67) [folk variant]
- **Hanuman** - The most beloved figure: born when Phra Isuan's weapons are placed, via the wind god, in the mouth of **Sawaha**, daughter of Kala Acana — not Valmiki's Anjana, wife of the monkey Kesari, by Vayu (VR CE 4.65.8, 27). He is "neither celibate nor saintly": he wins over Thotsakan's fish-daughter **Suphanna-Maccha**, who bears **Macchanu**, and **Benyakai**, Phiphek's daughter, who floats on the river near Phra Ram's camp disguised as a dead Sida and later bears him **Asuraphad**. Phra Ram builds him the city of Nophburi. Central to *khon*. (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 61–62, 64–65, 67, 69) [folk]
- **Khon (โขน)** - Royal masked dance-drama whose repertoire is essentially the Ramakien; inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2018. Also performed as *nang yai* (large shadow puppets). The Ramakien is painted in mural galleries around **Wat Phra Kaew** (the Emerald Buddha temple), Bangkok, and carved on mid-19th-c. stone panels at Wat Phra Chetuphon. (UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list, "Khon, masked dance drama in Thailand"; Wikipedia, "Ramakien", citing Rooney 2022) [folk]

## Old Javanese — Kakawin Ramayana

- **Kakawin Ramayana** - The **oldest** surviving kakawin and the only work to survive from the Central Javanese period; an Old Javanese *kakawin* (court poem in Indian meters) of **26 cantos (sarga) and about 2,783 four-line stanzas**, nearly twice the Bhattikavya's 1,607 stanzas in 22 cantos. (Henry, citing Robson 1980 and Hooykaas 1955) [scholarly]
  - *Dating:* composed in **Central Java between 856 and about 930 CE** (Stuart Robson), with the last section likely added c. 900–930, under the **Mataram/Medang** kingdom. Contemporary with the great Hindu temple **Prambanan**, whose reliefs depict the Rama story. [scholarly]
  - **Sanskrit prototype** - Its model is **not Valmiki directly** but the Sanskrit *mahakavya* **Bhattikavya** (a.k.a. *Ravanavadha*) of Bhatti, a grammar-illustrating poem dated around the beginning of the 7th c. CE. The link was first noticed by H. B. Sarkar (1934) and worked out by C. Hooykaas (1955–58). The correspondence is graded: close through **canto 8**, tenuous by **canto 13 (= Bhattikavya 12)**, and from **canto 17 to the end (26)** the Javanese poem is independent of the Sanskrit except in general subject matter. The two even share a near-identical verse at canto 2.19. (Henry) [scholarly]
  - **Javanese expansions** - The battle in Lanka, including Rama's capture and escape, takes five cantos (XVII–XXI, 649 stanzas) against one Bhattikavya canto of 113; the descriptions of Mount Suvela (XVI, XXIV) and of the countryside on the return to Ayodhya (XXV, 117 stanzas against 13) are where, in Robson's words, "the Javanese world comes to the fore". Poerbatjaraka and Juynboll doubted these descriptive passages as interpolations; Hooykaas found no conclusive evidence of interpolation. (Henry) [disputed]
  - **Ending** - The poem closes with the enthroned Rama as a banyan (*waringin*) dripping the sap of immortality on the world (XXVI.48); its recitation is declared auspicious and able to release those born under an unlucky conjunction from their fate (XXVI.49–50), a protective function close to that of wayang. Robson examines the hypothesis that the poet's unnamed royal patron is also being praised as Rama. (Henry, citing Robson 1980) [scholarly]
  - **"Yogesvara" attribution** - Sometimes ascribed to a poet **Yogesvara**; authorship is effectively anonymous and the Yogesvara name is rejected by most scholars for lack of evidence. [disputed]
  - **Clown-servants are a later wayang layer** - The **Punakawan** (Semar and his sons Gareng, Petruk, Bagong) belong to later Javanese and Balinese *wayang*, for which the kakawin became a foundational source. Popular accounts, including Wikipedia, say they appear in the kakawin's second half; the scholarly comparison consulted here (Henry, after Hooykaas and Robson) does not mention them. The late-12th- or early-13th-c. kakawin *Ghatotkacasraya* is often cited as the first text with clown-servants, but E. Wieringa ("Mpu Panuluh's puzzling *Panakawans*," *BSOAS* 63.2, 2000) questions whether they appear there at all. [disputed]
- **Balinese continuation** - In Bali (which retained Hindu practice), the Rama story persists in living ritual *wayang* and dance. The **Kecak** "monkey chant" staging the Lanka battle was developed in the 1930s from the chorus of the *sanghyang* trance ritual by the dancer **Wayan Limbak** and the German painter **Walter Spies**, partly for foreign audiences — a modern form, not an ancient one, though the dancer-scholar I Wayan Dibia argues the Balinese were already developing it when Spies arrived. (Wikipedia, "Kecak") [folk]

## Khmer — Reamker

- **Reamker ("Glory/Honor of Rama")** - The Khmer (Cambodian) national epic; *Ream* = Rama, *ker* = *kerti* "glory". The 7th-c. **Veal Kantel inscription (K.359)** already records Ramayana and Mahabharata manuscripts offered to a temple with daily recitation; Angkorian reliefs at **Banteay Srei** (10th c.) and **Angkor Wat** (12th c.) depict Ramayana scenes, and the *Lbaeuk Angkor Wat* poem (1620) describes those reliefs. No Khmer literary Rama text before the 15th c. survives. (Wikipedia, "Reamker", citing Iyengar 2005 and Marrison 1989) [scholarly]
  - **Two surviving parts** - The written Reamker is the work of at least three unknown authors. **Part I (16th–17th c.)** runs from the Bala and Ayodhya material up to Ravana assembling his last army, and then breaks off: **Ravana's death, Sita's rescue and fire-ordeal, and the return to Ayodhya are missing**. **Part II (18th c.)** treats the later history — Sita's second rejection and exile, the birth of the two sons, the reunion, and Sita's descent into the earth. Fuller narratives survive orally (e.g. the reciter Ta Chak, recorded 1969). (Wikipedia, "Reamker", citing Marrison 1989) [late text]
- **Buddhist reinterpretation** - Reframes the Hindu story through a **Theravada Buddhist** worldview: heavy emphasis on **karma, merit, moral accountability, and compassion** rather than divine destiny; it reinterprets rather than rejects its Hindu roots. [scholarly]
- **Preah Ream** - Rama, understood as an incarnation of Vishnu but drawn with mortal strengths and weaknesses; dynamic secondary figures take the spotlight. [folk]
- **Krong Reap** - Ravana, the ten-headed king of Langka, who abducts Neang Seda. In the popular plot his severed parts regenerate until a defecting general reveals the one fatal spot. (Wikipedia, "Reamker", plot summary; not checked against the edited text) [folk]
- **Neang Seda** - Sita, the devoted heroine; Ream wins her by shooting arrows through a spinning wheel. Her chastity-ordeal and estrangement from Ream are foregrounded as moral drama; in the popular plot the late estrangement is again set off by a **portrait of Ravana** she draws at a servant's request, which wounds Ream from under the mattress. (Wikipedia, "Reamker", plot summary) [folk]
  - *Variant (Przyluski 1939, as cited by Singaravelu 1968, p. 149):* in the Cambodian version, as in the Thai, Sita is born as Ravana's daughter and adopted by the King of Mithila. [folk variant]
- **Hanuman** - The **white monkey general**: clever, courageous, mischievous, amorous — often stealing the show from Preah Ream. The Khmer audience's favourite episode is **Hanuman and the mermaid Sovann Maccha**, who steals the stones of the causeway to Langka and is won over by him — but it belongs to the royal ballet and folk tradition, not the standard written Reamker (see Shared dynamics above). [folk]
- **Lkhon Khol / Robam** - The Reamker is the core of Khmer **masked dance-drama (lkhon khol)** — *Lkhon Khol Wat Svay Andet* was inscribed on UNESCO's Urgent Safeguarding List in 2018 — and of classical *robam* dance, and is painted along the gallery walls of the **Royal Palace / Silver Pagoda**, Phnom Penh. (UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list, "Lkhon Khol Wat Svay Andet") [folk]

## Lao — Phra Lak Phra Lam

- **Phra Lak Phra Lam ("Honored Lakshmana, Honored Rama")** - The Lao national Rama epic, a vernacular Lao prose work recited in monasteries during the Buddhist Lent, blending Hindu narrative, Theravada Buddhist doctrine, and local folklore. Sachchidanand Sahai's critical edition (Vientiane, 1973) rests on late palm-leaf copies: the base manuscript from Ban Naxon was copied in 1934, a Ban Hom manuscript in 1891, and another in 1933. Both the 1934 and 1891 copies reproduce a final colophon naming one **Phutthaphochan** (perhaps a corruption of Buddhaghosacharya) as the writer in Chulasakarat 1212, 22 May 1850 CE, which Sahai takes as the date of composition. He reads allusions to post-1713 Laos in the text, calls that internal evidence "extremely speculative and tentative", and concludes the 1850 text was developed from an older Rama-story nucleus, oral or written. Wikipedia says versions were recorded on palm leaf between the 15th and early 19th c. (citing Richman 1991). (Sahai, pp. 67, 75–83) [late text]
  - **Why Lak comes first** - Two explanations are recorded: the order was chosen for euphony, since Phra Ram is the hero; or, as veteran Luang Prabang dancers say, Phra Lak is named first out of deference to his *voluntary* service, where Ram's actions were obligatory to his kingship. (Sahai, p. 73, for the rhythmic effect; Wikipedia, "Phra Lak Phra Ram") [folk]
- **Rama as Bodhisatta** - In Theravada Laos the story is treated explicitly as a **Jataka** (*Phra Lam Sadok* in Sahai; *Phra Ram Xadok* in Wikipedia): at its close the Buddha declares that he himself was Rama and his cousin **Devadatta was Ravana**. Its Hindu identity is effectively dissolved, though Indra, Shiva and Brahma remain as characters, Indra the most prominent. (Sahai, pp. 74, 84; Wikipedia, "Phra Lak Phra Ram") [folk variant]
- **Raphphanasuan (Hapmanasouane)** - Ravana is **Phra Lam's cousin**, son of King Virulaha of Inthapathanakhon. In a former life he was a conceited Maha Brahma born deformed as **Thao Lun Lu**, remoulded in Indra's heaven after answering Indra's riddles on Buddhist doctrine; Indra foretells that he can be killed by no weapon but the arrow **Keo Vasiraphet** lying on the ocean bed. In the first part of the story he abducts **Nang Chantha**, daughter of his uncle Thattarattha (Dasaratha) — breaking the Lao taboo on marrying an elder cousin and the custom of paying a dowry. (Sahai, pp. 68–69) [folk variant]
- **Nang Sida** - In the second part Raphphanasuan violates **Indra's wife Nang Susada**, who is reborn as **his own daughter Sida**; cast adrift by her father as an infant for attempting his life, she reaches a sage's hermitage and is adopted. Raphphanasuan fails to lift the sage's bow, is fobbed off with a look-alike, **Nang Suddho**, and later takes Sida by the golden-deer stratagem. This transforms Valmiki's Vedavati, who vows to be born again for Ravana's destruction (VR CE 7.17.25–31), into a rebirth *as his daughter*. (Sahai, p. 70) [folk variant]
- **Hullaman (Hanuman), Rama's own son** - While Phra Lam lives as a monkey for three years after eating a *Nikhot* fruit, the sage's daughter **Nang Phengsi** is likewise turned monkey, and their union produces the monkey son **Hullaman**. Hullaman retrieves Keo Vasiraphet from the ocean bed for Raphphanasuan's death. He is also a romantic hero: when the four daughters of the Naga king destroy the bridge to Lanka, they unite with its builders, Hullaman among them, and each bears a son. After the war Hullaman eats a Nikhot fruit, becomes a man, and is made collector of market taxes in Vientiane. (Sahai, pp. 70–73) [folk variant]
- **After the war** - Phra Lam condemns Sida for drawing a portrait of Raphphanasuan at the palace maidens' request; Phra Lak brings back a sword dipped in a dog's blood as proof of execution, while the horse **Manikap** carries her to her foster father's hermitage. There she bears **Phra But**; the sage carves a wooden image of him that he brings to life as **Phra Hup**. The family is reunited in purely Lao circumstances, after the boy refuses to sell *hmak teng* fruit to Hullaman's servants. (Sahai, pp. 72–73) [folk variant]
- **Localization** - Set in the Mekong landscape; the Naga of the river advises Thattarattha to move his capital from Phan Phao to the opposite bank, founding Sisattanak (Vientiane). Recited by monks and performed in court/temple dance and shadow theatre. (Sahai, p. 68) [folk]
- **Sister texts** - Besides the Phra Lak Phra Lam, Laos preserves the **Pommachak** (Tai Lue, from Muang Sing), the **Lanka Noi**, and the **Hleuang Khvay Thoraphi** (Luang Prabang palace), whose bull Thoraphi Sahai traces to Valmiki's buffalo-demon **Dundubhi** (VR CE 4.11.7–26). (Sahai, pp. 67 n., 74) [folk variant]

## Burmese — Yama Zatdaw

- **Yama Zatdaw (ရာမဇာတ်တော်) / Yamayana** - The unofficial national epic of Myanmar; *Yama* = Rama, *zatdaw* = an acted play or a jataka-style "birth-story". Bagan-period sculptures of Rama at the Nathlaung Kyaung temple and terracotta plaques at the Hpetleik pagodas (illustrating the *Dasaratha Jataka* among the Jatakas) are the earliest evidence; the earliest literary reference is a 1527 *pyo* by Shin Aggathamadi. U Aung Phyo's ***Rama Thagyin***, believed to have been composed in 1775, runs from the Bala to the Yuddha Kanda; court works followed — U Toe's *Yama yakan* and *Thida yakan* (1784), the *Yama pyazat* ballet (1789), *Kalay Yama wuthtu* (1800). Konbaung campaigns against Ayutthaya (sacked 1767) brought Ramakien elements into the Burmese stage tradition. (Wikipedia, "Yama Zatdaw") [late text]
- **Buddhist Jataka frame** - The "zat" label places the story in the Jataka genre and the tale carries didactic Buddhist moral weight; whether Yama is explicitly identified as the bodhisatta in the Burmese texts is not established by the sources consulted here. [disputed]
- **A Valmiki-like Hanuman** - The Burmese *Yama Pyazat Daw-gyi* keeps Hanuman celibate and decorous, against the Thai Ramakien's amorous Hanuman. (Rooney 2022, as cited on Wikipedia) [scholarly]
- **Mon retelling** - The Mon *Loik Samoing Ram*, written in 1834 by the monk Uttama, says in its preface that it follows the Burmese version, but it also shows Thai, Javanese and Malay connections and episodes of its own. (Wikipedia, "Yama Zatdaw") [late text]
- **Performance forms** - Distinctively staged through **yokthe** (Burmese marionette/string-puppet theatre), *zat pwe* dance-drama, and a highly acrobatic, ornately costumed Burmese dance idiom; episodes also appear in temple murals and lacquerware. [folk]

## Malay — Hikayat Seri Rama

- **Hikayat Seri Rama ("The Story of Sri Rama")** - The Malay prose adaptation in the **hikayat** genre (*hikayat*, from Arabic, = "story/tale"), written in its present form some time between the 13th and 17th c. It survives in several recensions; the two most cited are the Bodleian manuscript presented by Archbishop William Laud in **1633** (reportedly brought from Aceh by an English captain in 1612), edited by W. G. Shellabear (1915), and the text published by Roorda van Eysinga (1843). Its name-forms (*Baradan*, *Bibusanam*, *Patala Maharayan*) show Tamil influence. (Wikipedia, "Hikayat Seri Rama", for the dating and recensions; Boloji review of Aveling's translation, for the manuscripts; Singaravelu 1968, pp. 158, 168–169) [late text]
- **Islamic reframing** - Rawana, banished by his father for misconduct to **Bukit Serendib** (Sri Lanka), does penance for twelve years hanging head-down (over a fire, in the related *Hikayat Maharaja Rawana*); **Allah** sends **the Prophet Adam** to ask what he wants and grants him rule over four realms — the earth, the heavens, the seas, and the underworld. Dasaratha is the Prophet Adam's great-grandson. The underlying narrative remains the Rama cycle, with Siwa, Wisnu and other Hindu deities still present in some versions. (Francisco 1969, p. 211; Boloji review; Wikipedia, "Hikayat Seri Rama") [folk variant]
- **Sita's parentage — two accounts of the same episode** - The heroine is raised as Rawana's daughter, but the two scholarly summaries describe different mechanisms. Both draw on the Shellabear (Laud) text; whether the mechanisms are complementary parts of one plot or belong to different recensions was not resolved here, so they are kept apart: [folk variant]
  - *Account 1 (Aveling's translation, as reviewed by Sen; Francisco):* Rawana demands Dasaratha's queen **Mandu-dari** (found in a bamboo clump); she makes a green frog from the dirt (*daki*) of her body and turns it into her double, **Mandu-daki**, who goes to Rawana. Dasaratha secretly sleeps with Mandu-daki, so **Sita is Dasaratha's daughter, and Rama's half-sister**, though raised in Langkapuri. Rawana's brother **Bibusanam** (Vibhishana), an astrologer, foretells that his life and death depend on the child; Rawana puts her in an iron box and throws it into the sea; Mandu-daki wraps her in *chindai* cloth with two jewels; **Maharisi Kali** (Maharishi Kala in Sen's review; Janaka) of Darwata Purwa and Queen Minuram Dewi find her and name her Sita. (Boloji review; Francisco 1969, p. 198)
  - *Account 2 (Singaravelu's comparative summary):* a relative of Rawana, **Gagak Swara**, takes the form of a crow, snatches one of the six consecrated rice-balls from Dasaratha's sacrifice, and gives it to Rawana, who eats it; Rawana's wife then bears the girl, who is cast into the waters on the fear that she will kill her father; Maharisi Kali finds the casket during his morning worship. (Singaravelu 1968, pp. 157, 171–172)
- **Hanuman, son of Rama and Sita** - Seri Rama and Sita Dewi plunge into an enchanted pool, become monkeys and make love before bathing back into human form in another pool; the monkey embryo is massaged from Sita's womb and deposited in the mouth of **Dewi Anjati**, who bears **Hanuman** — with a human face, earrings, and a monkey's body. Rama later recognises him as his son by those earrings. (Francisco 1969, pp. 198–199, 213; Singaravelu 1982, p. 64 n.; Boloji review, for the face and earrings) [folk variant]
  - *Variant (Aveling's translation, as reviewed by Sen):* the nursemaid Baya Bata makes Sita spew out a jewel, and it is this jewel that is placed in Anjati Dewi's mouth. (Boloji review) [folk variant]
- **Other departures** - Sita drops her ring into Jatayu's beak as a sign of her abduction; Hanuman, not Lakshmana, builds the pyre for her fire-ordeal; **Patala Maharayan** abducts Rama (the Mahiravana episode); later, Sita draws Rawana on a fan at the request of Kikewi Dewi (Dasaratha's daughter by Balyadari, so Rama's half-sister, in Singaravelu; Bibasenam's younger sister in Sen's review), and in exile bears **Tabalawi**, while Maharisi Kali makes a second boy, **Gusi**, from grass. Laksamana's role grows until he rivals Rama, and Hanuman becomes commander-in-chief. (Singaravelu 1982, pp. 63–68 nn.; Singaravelu 1968, pp. 157, 172; Boloji review; Wikipedia, "Hikayat Seri Rama") [folk variant]
- **Rawana does not die** - Unlike every other version compared by Singaravelu, the Hikayat leaves Maharaja Rawana defeated but alive; in Aveling's text Rama stabs him repeatedly and he does not die, and the death is never narrated. (Singaravelu 1968, p. 168; Boloji review) [folk variant]
- **Moral inversion in the shadow-play** - *Variant (wayang kulit Kelantan, Hikayat Maharaja Wana):* the shadow-play version secularizes the tale and shifts its focus to **Maharaja Wana (Rawana)**, who is drawn more sympathetically than Seri Rama, seen there as arrogant and vain — a reversal of the Valmiki polarity that belongs to this performance tradition, not to the literary Hikayat. (Wikipedia, "Hikayat Seri Rama", citing E. Ding 2016; cf. Sweeney 1972) [folk variant]
- **Wayang transmission** - The Hikayat Maharaja Wana is the Malay **wayang kulit** (shadow-puppet) adaptation of the story, esp. in Kelantan; puppeteers favour the marriage of Seri Rama, the abduction, the battles at Langkapuri, and the rescue, and absorb Javanese clown-servant figures and local colour. (Wikipedia, "Hikayat Seri Rama") [folk]

## Maranao (Philippines) — Maharadia Lawana & the Darangen

- **Maharadia Lawana ("Maharaja Ravana")** - A **Maranao** (Lake Lanao, Mindanao, Philippines) **prose tale** telling a much-reduced, indigenized Rama story, named for the antagonist. The text, in romanized Maranao, was "recorded by Mamitua Saber and Severino Velasco; related by Bai Pamoki" of Taraka; **Juan R. Francisco** came upon it in 1968 and translated it with his Maranao teacher **Nagasura T. Madale**, publishing text and translation in 1969. Francisco judged it "not exactly a version of the Ramayana" but a tale of the same abduction and recovery of Sita, and dated its arrival in its present setting to between the mid-17th and early 19th c., after the Islamization of Java, Sumatra and Malaya, while allowing that the story may have pre-Islamic beginnings. (Francisco 1969, pp. 187–188, 217–218) [folk]
  - **Islamic & local elements** - Lawana, an eight-headed prince (seven-headed later in the text) exiled for his slanderous tongue to **Pulu Nagara**, climbs a tree over a fire he has built and cries that "the world is chained"; the angel **Diabarail** (Gabriel) reports to the Lord, who grants that nothing can kill him except a blade sharpened on a **whetstone set on naga wood in his palace** at Pulu Bandiarmasir. Sultans, an imam and Maranao customs frame the plot. (Francisco 1969, pp. 210–211) [folk variant]
  - **Indigenized cast and plot** - **Radia Mangandiri** and **Radia Mangawarna** (Rama and Lakshmana), sons of the Sultan of Agama Niog, win **Tuan Potre Malaila Ganding / Malano Tihaia** (Sita) of Pulu Nabandai by kicking a *sipa* rattan ball into her pent-house, not by stringing a bow. Lawana, as a deer with golden horns, lures both brothers away and carries her off. Rama's Hanuman-figure is **Laksamana**, a **monkey son of Radia Mangandiri**, born after Mangandiri dreams a carabao gores out one of his testicles and **Potre Langawi** swallows it, thinking it a jewel. Laksamana sharpens the brothers' *kampilan* on the palace whetstone, Mangawarna fells Lawana, and the party returns across the sea on crocodiles; there is **no fire-ordeal**. (Francisco 1969, pp. 198, 203–210) [folk]
- **Darangen** - A separate, much larger Maranao **epic** — 17 cycles, some 72,000 lines — centred on the hero **Bantugan** of Bembaran; proclaimed a UNESCO Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2005 (inscribed 2008). It preserves pre-Islamic Maranao beliefs and custom from before the 14th-c. conversion to Islam. The Maharadia Lawana is not one of its cycles, but Francisco notes that the Darangen's most often told episode — the **abduction and recovery of Princess Lawanen** — is a parallel, which helps explain the prose tale's popularity. (Wikipedia, "Darangen"; Francisco 1969, p. 188) [folk]
  - *Disputed claim:* some writers infer a pre-Islamic Rama-story stratum in the southern Philippines from the Darangen's antiquity. Francisco dates the Maharadia Lawana's arrival in its present form to the mid-17th to early 19th c., but he says its Islamic terms "do not invalidate" early pre-Islamic beginnings, so the inference stays open. (Francisco 1969, pp. 217–218) [disputed]

## Valmiki anchors for comparison

The Southeast Asian variants above are measured against these Valmiki passages, cited by the Baroda Critical Edition (VR CE) as given in the GRETIL e-text, with the vulgate numbering where it differs. None of the regional episodes is read back into Valmiki.

- **Sita's birth** - She rose from Janaka's plough while he was ploughing and clearing a field (a ritual field, in the valmikiramayan.net gloss), "not born of a womb" (*ayonija*) (VR CE 1.65.14–15 = vulgate 1.66.13–15).
- **Sita as Ravana's destined destroyer** - Vedavati, molested by Ravana, enters the fire vowing to be born again for his death, and is reborn from a furrow as Sita (VR CE 7.17.25–31, Uttara Kanda). This is Valmiki's own "Sita as Ravana's doom" motif; she is never his daughter.
- **Hanuman** - Son of Vayu by Anjana, wife of the monkey Kesari (VR CE 4.65.8, 4.65.16–17, 4.65.27–28); unmoved by the sight of Ravana's sleeping women (VR CE 5.9.38).
- **The causeway** - Built by Nala, son of Vishvakarman, after Rama's arrows have forced the ocean to yield (VR CE 6.14–6.15.20).
- **Ravana's death** - Rama's Brahma-weapon arrow pierces his heart (VR CE 6.97.17).
- **The banishment** - Bhadra reports the citizens' talk that Rama has taken back a wife held in Ravana's lap (VR CE 7.42.7–20); Sita's final ordeal ends with the Earth goddess taking her into the underworld (VR CE 7.88.10–14).
- **Rama's divinity** - Vishnu agrees to be born as Dasaratha's son (VR CE 1.14.17–19, 1.15.3); Brahma's hymn, spoken as Sita enters the fire, names Rama as Vishnu (VR CE 6.105.5–14).

## Comparative notes & open rosters

- **Whose name is on the title** - Most of the region's titles celebrate Rama's "glory" (*Ramakien*, *Reamker* = Skt. *Ramakirti*) or simply name him (*Hikayat Seri Rama*, *Yama Zatdaw*); the Lao *Phra Lak Phra Lam* fronts Lakshmana; only the Maranao *Maharadia Lawana* and the Kelantan shadow-play *Hikayat Maharaja Wana* are titled after Ravana — the two tellings where the antagonist's position is most enlarged.
- **Conditional invulnerability, not one motif** - Each tradition gives its Ravana a different single point of failure, none of them in Valmiki: the Thai Thotsakan's **soul in a receptacle** held by his teacher Khobutacan; the Lao Raphphanasuan's single fatal arrow on the ocean bed; the Maranao Lawana's **palace whetstone**; the Khmer Krong Reap's one fatal spot in the popular plot. Only the Thai is an external-soul motif in the strict folkloric sense. [folk]
- **The heroine as the antagonist's daughter** - Thai, Lao and Malay (and, per Przyluski, Khmer, Tibetan and Khotanese) tellings make Sita the child of Ravana, cast away in infancy as a danger to him (on a prophecy of his ruin in the Thai and Malay, for attempting his life in the Lao), though the mechanism varies: the Thai Mantho eats stolen sacrificial rice; the Lao Sida is Indra's violated wife reborn; in the Malay telling a crow steals sacrificial rice for Rawana, and one account makes her secretly Dasaratha's child by a frog-born double of his queen. The motif turns the abduction into an unwitting incest-taboo and the war into a father destroyed by the daughter he cast away. Przyluski (1939, as summarized by Singaravelu 1968) read the *Dasaratha Jataka*'s sister-wife Sita as earliest, the Ravana's-daughter form as a second stage, and Valmiki's furrow birth as a third. In India the motif appears in the Jain *Vasudevahindi* of Sanghadasa (and Gunabhadra's *Uttara-purana*), and the Sanskrit *Adbhuta Ramayana* makes Mandodari Sita's mother through a pot of sages' blood (see [Adhyatma & Variant Ramayanas](../adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md)). Valmiki's nearest analogue is Vedavati's vow (VR CE 7.17.25–31). [folk variant]
- **The portrait of Ravana** - A drawing of Ravana that Sita is induced to make replaces Valmiki's citizens' gossip (VR CE 7.42.7–20) in Thai (Adun, a slate), Lao (palace maidens), Khmer (a servant; in the popular plot the drawing wounds Rama) and Malay (Kikewi Dewi, a fan) tellings; Singaravelu notes the same motif in the Bengali *Ramayana* of Chandravati, where Kaikeyi's daughter Kukua asks for the drawing. [folk variant]
- **Rama's monkey son** - The Malay Hanuman, the Lao Hullaman and the Maranao Laksamana are all sons of the Rama-figure — by Sita, by Nang Phengsi, and by Potre Langawi respectively — where Valmiki's Hanuman is Vayu's son by Anjana (VR CE 4.65.8, 4.65.16–17, 4.65.27–28). [folk variant]
- **Open-ended roster** - Manuscript traditions, regional sub-versions (Balinese vs. Javanese; Kelantanese Malay; multiple Lao palm-leaf redactions; Cham, Mon, and Tai-Khmun retellings), and the full inventory of interpolated Hanuman episodes are **not exhaustively cataloged here** — these are living, multi-recension oral/performance traditions. [folk]
