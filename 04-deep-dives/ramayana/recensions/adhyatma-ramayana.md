# Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa — the Advaita-Vedānta Rāmāyaṇa

> Cosmic address: [Tretā Yuga](../../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga — the *story* is set near the close of Tretā; the *text* is a **c. 14th–15th century CE** (Kali Yuga) Sanskrit recasting embedded in the [Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/brahmanda-purana.md). Two clocks: scripture-time vs. authorial-time.

> See also: [Rāmcaritmānas (Tulsīdās)](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) · [Ātman, Brahman & the Six Darśanas](../../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md) · [Vālmīki Uttara Kāṇḍa](../7-uttara-kanda.md)

The **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** ("the *Spiritual* / *Self-knowledge* Rāmāyaṇa") is a Sanskrit re-telling of the Rāma story (~**4,200 verses**, 7 kāṇḍas, **65 sargas**) that re-reads the entire epic through the lens of **Advaita Vedānta** while wrapping that non-dualism inside intense **Rāma-bhakti**. It is the single most important *theological* bridge between Vālmīki's narrative epic and the great vernacular bhakti Rāmāyaṇas — the **direct doctrinal source** for Tulsīdās's [Rāmcaritmānas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) and Ezhuthachan's Malayalam *Adhyātmarāmāyaṇam Kiḷippāṭṭu*. Its two signature contributions are the **identification of Rāma with the supreme Brahman** and the **māyā-Sītā / "shadow Sītā" doctrine** that protects Sītā's chastity by metaphysical sleight-of-hand.

## Text, dating & attribution [two clocks]

- **Authorial-time: c. 14th–15th century CE** - the scholarly consensus places the text in late-medieval North India; the broader range cited is **13th–15th century CE** [scholarly]. The Rāma-*story* it narrates remains Tretā-Yuga scripture-time; the *composition* is firmly Kali-Yuga.
- **Scriptural attribution to Vyāsa** - by traditional ascription the text is the work of **Veda-Vyāsa**, embedded in the *Uttara-bhāga* (latter portion) of the [Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/brahmanda-purana.md), where it occupies **over a third (~35%)** of the Purāṇa's chapters (Wikipedia; tradition).
  - *Variant attribution (Bhakti tradition):* some Hindu tradition credits the Rāma-bhakti saint **Rāmānanda** (founder of the Rāmānandī sampradāya) or his circle with the work [disputed] — consistent with its strong saguṇa-Rāma devotionalism.
- **Embedding in the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa** - the work is presented as a *Purāṇic* dialogue rather than a free-standing kāvya, which is part of why later poets could treat it as authoritative *śāstra* on the inner meaning of the Rāma-līlā. See [Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/brahmanda-purana.md). [late text]
- **Independent cores later incorporated** - scholars hold that the **Rāma-Gītā** and the **Rāma-Hṛdaya** were originally **independent Advaitic compositions** later stitched into the Adhyātma frame, accounting for their self-contained philosophical density (scholarly view).
- **Verse-count variants** - enumerations differ: **~4,200 verses** (Hindupedia), **~4,500 verses** (Wikipedia), across **7 kāṇḍas / 65 sargas**. The sixth book is titled **Laṅkā Kāṇḍa** in some editions, **Yuddha Kāṇḍa** in others.

## Frame & structure — a dialogue inside a dialogue

- **Outer frame: Śiva narrates to Pārvatī** - unlike Vālmīki's frame (Vālmīki composing, Lava-Kuśa reciting), the Adhyātma is told as a **dialogue between Śiva and Pārvatī (Umā-Maheśvara saṃvāda)** — Śiva, the supreme jñānin and arch-devotee of Rāma, expounds the *secret inner meaning* of Rāma's life to his consort. This frame itself signals "esoteric, knowledge-bearing" intent. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, opening)
- **Seven kāṇḍas, paralleling Vālmīki** - Bāla, Ayodhyā, Araṇya, Kiṣkindhā, Sundara, Laṅkā/Yuddha, Uttara — but each episode is glossed for its Vedāntic allegory rather than narrated for plot.
  - **Bāla Kāṇḍa** - Rāma's *avatāra*-descent and childhood, framed as the formless Brahman voluntarily assuming form (saguṇa) out of compassion.
  - **Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa** - exile; opens (after Bāla) the devotional-philosophical hymnody.
  - **Araṇya Kāṇḍa** - the **golden-deer / abduction** sequence — locus of the māyā-Sītā substitution (see below).
  - **Kiṣkindhā / Sundara / Laṅkā Kāṇḍas** - Bālī's slaying, Hanumān's leap and the burning of Laṅkā, the war and Rāvaṇa's death; consistently read as Brahman dispelling ignorance (avidyā = the rākṣasas).
  - **Uttara Kāṇḍa** - epilogue and the **Rāma-Gītā** (see below); the philosophical climax.

## Core doctrine 1 — Rāma *is* Brahman

- **Rāma = the supreme Brahman (Parabrahman)** - the text's repeated thesis: Rāma is **the Absolute — sat-cit-ānanda, the One-without-a-second, the cause without a cause** — who only *appears* to act, suffer, and seek Sītā. His human emotions are *līlā*, a play for the world's instruction, never real limitation. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, passim)
- **Saguṇa mapped onto nirguṇa** - every attribute (saguṇa) of the embodied Rāma is systematically read back into the attributeless (nirguṇa) Brahman: the bow is the praṇava (Oṃ), the rākṣasas are the vāsanās/avidyā, the bridge to Laṅkā is the path of knowledge, etc. This is the **Advaitic-allegorical method** that defines the work. [scholarly]
- **Sītā = Māyā-śakti / Prakṛti** - Sītā is not merely Rāma's queen but his **Māyā-śakti** (power of cosmic illusion) or **Prakṛti** — inseparable from Brahman as heat is from fire. Their union is the Vedāntic union of Brahman and its śakti; their "separation" during the abduction is the cosmic appearance of duality. This pairing is the metaphysical engine that makes the shadow-Sītā doctrine *necessary*.
- **Synthesis with Vālmīki's "human" Rāma** - where [Vālmīki's Uttara Kāṇḍa](../7-uttara-kanda.md) and the older epic stress Rāma as the ideal *man* (maryādā-puruṣottama) who weeps and doubts, the Adhyātma insists every such moment is conscious divine theatre — a decisive shift from heroic to **theological** Rāma that the vernacular bhakti poets inherited.

## Core doctrine 2 — the Māyā-Sītā / "Shadow Sītā"

- **The chastity problem it solves** - if the real Sītā spent a year in Rāvaṇa's Aśoka-grove, her purity is open to doubt and the **Agni-parīkṣā (fire ordeal)** looks like cruelty. The Adhyātma resolves this by ensuring **the real Sītā is never abducted at all**. [folk/theological innovation]
- **The substitution (Araṇya Kāṇḍa)** - **before the golden-deer episode**, Rāma — who foreknows Rāvaṇa's plot — secretly instructs Sītā to enter the sacred **fire (Agni)** for safekeeping. Agni takes the **true Sītā** into protective custody and leaves a **māyā-Sītā / chāyā-Sītā ("shadow Sītā")**, an exact illusory double, in the hermitage. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa)
- **It is the shadow who is abducted** - Rāvaṇa carries off only the **illusory** Sītā; the captive in Laṅkā throughout is the māyā-form, never the real consort.
- **The fire ordeal as *recovery*, not test** - at the war's end the **shadow Sītā enters the fire**, and the **true Sītā emerges** from it — so the famous Agni-parīkṣā is re-read not as a trial of Sītā's virtue but as the **stage-managed return of the genuine Sītā**, "pre-planned and enacted at Rāma's own bidding" (Hindupedia). The "test" is theatre for the world.
- **Precedents & parallels** - the chāyā-figure motif echoes the **Chāyā / Saṃjñā** doublet of Sūrya's wives and the **Māyā-Sītā** already found in the *Kūrma Purāṇa* and *Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa*; the Adhyātma gives it its most influential, fully integrated form. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* the older [Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa](../README.md) has **no** shadow Sītā — the real Sītā is abducted and the Agni-parīkṣā is a genuine (if troubling) ordeal of chastity.
  - *Variant (reception in Tulsīdās):* Tulsīdās **adopts** the māyā-Sītā device in the [Rāmcaritmānas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md), carrying it into the Hindi belt's popular imagination.

## The two philosophical cores

- **Rāma-Hṛdaya ("the Heart of Rāma")** - the **opening chapter (Bāla Kāṇḍa, sarga 1)**; a compact credal hymn in which the inner secret — Rāma as Brahman, Sītā as Māyā, the devotee's identity with the Self — is stated up front. Often recited devotionally as a self-contained stotra. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa)
- **Rāma-Gītā ("the Song of Rāma")** - in the **Uttara Kāṇḍa (the 5th sarga of the seventh book)**; Rāma instructs Lakṣmaṇa in pure **Advaita Vedānta** — the nature of the Self (ātman), the unreality of the world-appearance, the three paths of **karma, jñāna and bhakti**, and liberation through Self-knowledge. Self-consciously modelled on the **Bhagavad-Gītā** (Rāma : Lakṣmaṇa :: Kṛṣṇa : Arjuna) and treated as an independent Vedāntic text in its own right. [scholarly]
  - *Note:* a separate, longer South-Indian **Rāma-Gītā** of the *Guru-Jñāna-Vāsiṣṭha-Tantra* tradition (Dakṣiṇāmūrti) also exists; the Adhyātma's Rāma-Gītā is the one referenced here. [disputed naming]

## The Advaitic-bhakti synthesis

- **Jñāna *and* bhakti, not either/or** - the work's lasting achievement is harmonising **non-dual knowledge (jñāna)** with **personal loving devotion (bhakti)** to a saguṇa deity: Rāma is simultaneously the impersonal Brahman *and* the supremely lovable Lord. Devotion becomes a *path to*, and an *expression of*, the realisation of non-duality. [scholarly]
- **Three yogas integrated** - the text teaches **karma, jñāna and bhakti** as complementary, with knowledge/devotion/detachment (jñāna-bhakti-vairāgya) as the fruit — a template later bhakti theologians leaned on.
- **Hymnody throughout** - numerous **stutis/stotras** in praise of Rāma (by Brahmā, Śiva, Ahalyā, the gods) punctuate the narrative, modelling the devotee's response to the Brahman-made-personal. These hymns are widely recited independently.
- **Doctrinal kinship** - the Brahman/Māyā framework links directly to classical Advaita; see [Ātman, Brahman & the Six Darśanas](../../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md) for the Śaṅkara-school metaphysics the text presupposes.

## Influence — the fountainhead of bhakti Rāmāyaṇas

- **Tulsīdās's Rāmcaritmānas (1574 CE, Awadhi)** - the Adhyātma is Tulsīdās's **principal immediate source**; he inherits the **Rāma-as-Parabrahman** theology, the **māyā-Sītā** device, and the jñāna-bhakti synthesis, re-clothing them in vernacular Awadhi for mass devotion. See [Rāmcaritmānas (Tulsīdās)](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md). [scholarly]
- **Ezhuthachan's *Adhyātmarāmāyaṇam Kiḷippāṭṭu* (Malayalam, early 17th c.)** - **Thunchaththu Rāmānujan Ezhuthachan**, "father of Malayalam literature," renders the Adhyātma into Malayalam in the **kiḷippāṭṭu ("parrot-song")** genre — the story sung by a **parrot (kiḷi)** narrator — and pioneers a Sanskritised **Maṇipravāḷam** literary register. It became Kerala's most beloved Rāmāyaṇa, recited through the **Rāmāyaṇa-māsam (Karkkiṭakam month)**. [scholarly]
- **Oriya, Bengali & other vernacular versions** - the Adhyātma's allegory seeded further regional Rāmāyaṇas in **Oriya** and **Bengali**, spreading the Advaitic-Rāma reading across eastern India. [open-ended roster — full vernacular catalogue not enumerated here]
- **Liturgical afterlife** - the **Rāma-Hṛdaya**, **Rāma-Gītā**, and the embedded stotras circulate as stand-alone devotional/recitational texts to the present day, independent of the full epic.
