# Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa — the Advaita-Vedānta Rāmāyaṇa

> Cosmic address: [Tretā Yuga](../../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga — the *story* is set near the close of Tretā; the *text* is a **late-medieval (most often dated 14th–15th century CE)** Kali-Yuga Sanskrit recasting that presents itself as part of the [Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/brahmanda-purana.md). Two clocks: scripture-time vs. authorial-time.

> See also: [Rāmcaritmānas (Tulsīdās)](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) · [Adhyātma & Variant Rāmāyaṇas (comparative)](../adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md) · [Ātman, Brahman & the Six Darśanas](../../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md) · [Vālmīki Uttara Kāṇḍa](../7-uttara-kanda.md)

The **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** ("the *Spiritual* / *Self-knowledge* Rāmāyaṇa") is a Sanskrit re-telling of the Rāma story in 7 kāṇḍas and **65 sargas** (about **4,250 verses** in the edition cited here). It re-reads the whole epic through **Advaita Vedānta** and wraps that non-dualism inside intense **Rāma-bhakti**. It is the main *theological* bridge between Vālmīki's narrative epic and the great vernacular bhakti Rāmāyaṇas, and the source text for Ezhuthachan's Malayalam *Adhyātmarāmāyaṇam Kiḷippāṭṭu* and a major source for Tulsīdās's [Rāmcaritmānas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md). Its two signature moves are the **identification of Rāma with the supreme Brahman** and the **māyā-Sītā / "shadow Sītā"**, which keeps the real Sītā out of Rāvaṇa's hands altogether.

*Citation key:* **AR** = Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, kāṇḍa.sarga.verse as numbered in the sanskritdocuments.org edition (other prints number differently); **VR** = Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, vulgate numbering as on valmikiramayan.net unless marked CE (Baroda Critical Edition). This matches the key used in the [comparative page](../adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).

## Text, dating & attribution [two clocks]

- **Authorial-time: late medieval, most often 14th–15th century CE** - modern estimates run **13th–15th century CE** (Wikipedia), with **c. 14th century** in Wikipedia's *Maya Sita* article (citing Williams) and **14th–15th century** in other scholarship (Wikipedia, *Adhyatma Ramayanam Kilippattu*) [scholarly]. The same *Maya Sita* passage, again citing Williams, says the text originated in **Vārāṇasī in North India**. The Rāma-*story* it narrates remains Tretā-Yuga scripture-time; the *composition* is firmly Kali-Yuga.
  - *Latest possible date:* Tulsīdās (Rāmcaritmānas begun 1574 CE) and Ezhuthachan (16th or early 17th c.) both build on it, so it existed by the 16th century. A mailing-list report says Appayya Dīkṣita (1520–1593) quotes it by name in his *Rāmāyaṇa-tātparya-sāra-saṅgraha* stotra; the verses were not identified there. [unverified here]
- **Scriptural attribution to Vyāsa** - tradition ascribes the text to **Veda-Vyāsa** as part of the [Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/brahmanda-purana.md). The attached *Māhātmya* ends "in the Uttara-khaṇḍa of the Śrī-Brahmāṇḍa-purāṇa" (*iti śrībrahmāṇḍapurāṇe uttarakhaṇḍe*, AR Māhātmya, colophon). Every kāṇḍa colophon calls the work only "the Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, a dialogue of Umā and Maheśvara" (*adhyātmarāmāyaṇe umāmaheśvarasaṃvāde*). Wikipedia says it makes up **over 35% of the chapters** of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa. That figure was not checked against a printed Purāṇa here. [unverified here]
  - *Variant attribution (Bhakti tradition):* some Hindu tradition credits the Rāma-bhakti saint **Rāmānanda** (see [North-Indian bhakti sants](../../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/north-bhakti-sants.md)) or his circle with the work (Wikipedia) [disputed]. This would fit its strong saguṇa-Rāma devotion.
- **Embedding in the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa** - the work presents itself as a *Purāṇic* dialogue, not a free-standing kāvya. That is part of why later poets could treat it as authoritative *śāstra* on the inner meaning of the Rāma-līlā. See [Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/brahmanda-purana.md). [late text]
- **Independent cores later incorporated** - some scholars hold that the **Rāma-Gītā** and the **Rāma-Hṛdaya** were originally **independent works** later incorporated into the Adhyātma frame (Hindupedia) [scholarly].
- **Where the text seems to end** - the Yuddha Kāṇḍa closes with a *phala-śruti* for "this wondrous Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa" as a whole, ending with Śiva having told his beloved "the whole hidden truth of Śrī Rāma" (AR 6.16.45–49). The Uttara Kāṇḍa then reopens with Pārvatī asking what Rāma did next and how he left the world (AR 7.1.2–4). This is an observation about the text's shape, not a published dating argument.
- **Verse-count variants** - the sanskritdocuments.org edition numbers about **4,250 verses**: Bāla 391, Ayodhyā 730, Araṇya 516, Kiṣkindhā 560, Sundara 327, Yuddha 1,115, Uttara 611. Its sargas run **7 / 9 / 10 / 9 / 5 / 16 / 9 = 65**. Other counts: **~4,200** (Hindupedia), **~4,500** (Wikipedia). Every colophon in this edition names the sixth book **Yuddha Kāṇḍa**, but Hindupedia says it is "sometimes designated as Lañkākānda", and Wikipedia's summary calls it **Laṅkā Kāṇḍa**. Tulsīdās also calls his sixth book Laṅkā Kāṇḍa.

## Frame & structure — a dialogue inside a dialogue

- **Outermost frame (Māhātmya): Nārada asks Brahmā** - in the attached *Māhātmya*, **Sūta** tells how **Nārada** visited **Brahmā** in Satyaloka and asked for the one secret that would save people in the Kali age (AR Māhātmya 2–9).
- **Main frame: Śiva narrates to Pārvatī** - unlike Vālmīki's frame (Nārada tells the story to Vālmīki, Lava and Kuśa recite it), the Adhyātma is a **dialogue between Śiva and Pārvatī (Umā-Maheśvara saṃvāda)**. On Kailāsa, Pārvatī asks the question the whole work answers. If Rāma is the Supreme, why did he lament for Sītā? And if he did not know himself, how is he worth worshipping above any other soul? (AR 1.1.6–15). Śiva answers that ignorance cannot touch Rāma any more than darkness can touch the sun (AR 1.1.16–24). Most sargas open *śrīmahādeva uvāca*, "Śrī Mahādeva said".
- **Inner frame: Sītā and Rāma teach Hanumān** - Śiva's first proof is a story within the story, the Rāma-Hṛdaya (AR 1.1.25–52; see below).
- **Seven kāṇḍas, paralleling Vālmīki** - Bāla, Ayodhyā, Araṇya, Kiṣkindhā, Sundara, Yuddha, Uttara. The narrative mostly follows Vālmīki, but it is far shorter. Again and again it stops for a hymn (*stuti*) or a Vedāntic teaching.
  - **Bāla Kāṇḍa (7 sargas)** - the Rāma-Hṛdaya (1.1). Then the gods' petition and Rāma's birth: he appears **four-armed** before Kausalyā, she hymns him, and at her request he becomes a crying infant (1.3.13–35). Then Ahalyā (1.5), the boatman and the bow at Mithilā (1.6), and Paraśurāma (1.7).
  - **Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa (9 sargas)** - Nārada's visit (2.1), Sarasvatī entering Mantharā (2.2), Vāmadeva's sermon to the grieving citizens (2.5.9–25), Lakṣmaṇa's teaching to Guha and Vālmīki's autobiography (2.6), and Kaikeyī's repentance at Citrakūṭa (2.9.59–64).
  - **Araṇya Kāṇḍa (10 sargas)** - Rāma's Vedāntic teaching to Lakṣmaṇa at Pañcavaṭī (3.4), Rāvaṇa's chosen enmity (3.5.58–61), the **māyā-Sītā substitution** (3.7.1–4), and Śabarī's nine-fold bhakti (3.10).
  - **Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa (9 sargas)** - Vālin's death and Rāma's consolation of Tārā (4.2–3), Rāma's teaching of ritual worship (*kriyā-mārga*) to Lakṣmaṇa (4.4.8 ff.), and Svayamprabhā (4.6).
  - **Sundara Kāṇḍa (5 sargas)** - Hanumān's leap, the guardian Laṅkinī (5.1), Sītā in the Aśoka grove (5.2–3), and Hanumān's sermon to Rāvaṇa urging him to seek liberation (5.4.14 ff.).
  - **Yuddha Kāṇḍa (16 sargas)** - Rāmeśvara installed before the bridge (6.4.1–3), Kālanemi (6.6–7), Rāvaṇa's death and absorption into Rāma (6.11), the fire ordeal and the return of the true Sītā (6.12–13), the coronation (6.15), and the closing *phala-śruti* (6.16.45–49).
  - **Uttara Kāṇḍa (9 sargas)** - Agastya's account of Rāvaṇa's origins (7.1–4), the pre-arranged exile of Sītā (7.4), the **Rāma-Gītā** (7.5), and the departures of Sītā, Lakṣmaṇa and finally Rāma into the Sarayū (7.7–9).

## Core doctrine 1 — Rāma *is* Brahman

- **Rāma = the supreme Brahman (Parabrahman)** - the text's repeated thesis. Sītā's first words to Hanumān are "know Rāma as the supreme Brahman, existence-consciousness-bliss, without a second" (*rāmaṃ viddhi paraṃ brahma saccidānandam advayam*, AR 1.1.32). He only *appears* to act, suffer and seek Sītā: "Rāma does not go, does not stay, does not grieve, does not desire" (AR 1.1.43). (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, passim)
- **How the text handles his human moments** - it does not allegorize the story point by point. Three devices recur instead:
  - *Śiva's running gloss* - Śiva keeps explaining that Rāma acts "like an ordinary man" on purpose. He chases the deer knowing it is Mārīca, "to please Sītā", since a self-fulfilled God has no use for "a deer or a woman" (AR 3.7.12–15). He grieves for her deliberately, since only a grieving husband's search will lead him to Laṅkā (AR 3.8.3–6).
  - *Recognition hymns* - character after character recognizes him as Viṣṇu or Brahman and praises him. Examples are Kausalyā (1.3), Ahalyā (1.5), Nārada (2.1), Vālmīki (2.6), Jaṭāyu (3.8), Svayamprabhā (4.6), the gods led by Brahmā and then Indra (6.13), Śiva (6.15), and Brahmā again (7.9). Agni, returning Sītā, also names Rāma as Hari (6.13.20–21), though his words are a speech, not a hymn.
  - *Doctrinal correspondences* - Nārada pairs Rāma and Sītā as Viṣṇu and Lakṣmī, Śiva and Śivā, Brahmā and Vāṇī, the Sun and his light (AR 2.1.10–14). Vāmadeva tells the citizens that Rāma is Ādi-Nārāyaṇa, the earlier avatāras included, Sītā is Yogamāyā and Lakṣmaṇa is Śeṣa (AR 2.5.10–20).
  - *Note:* point-by-point allegorical glosses sometimes attached to this text (the bow as praṇava, the rākṣasas as vāsanās, the bridge as the path of knowledge) are not found in the AR e-text cited here. Where they occur, they belong to later interpretation. More generally, Wikipedia (citing R. C. Prasad) describes the work as mapping Rāma's saguṇa attributes onto nirguṇa Brahman and reading his life as symbol [scholarly].
- **Sītā = Mūla-prakṛti / Māyā** - Sītā describes herself as **mūla-prakṛti**, "root-nature, the cause of creation, preservation and dissolution". She creates "by his mere proximity" (*tasya sannidhimātreṇa*, AR 1.1.34–35). She then claims that all of Rāma's deeds, from his birth to the coronation and including "the abduction of the māyā-Sītā", are really hers, superimposed on the changeless Rāma by the ignorant (AR 1.1.35–42). That pairing of Brahman with its power is what makes the shadow-Sītā device possible.
- **Even enemies are liberated** - since Rāma is Brahman, death at his hands or constant thought of him frees anyone. Mārīca's light enters Rāma, and the gods marvel that a sinner who harmed sages could win this (AR 3.7.19–24). Rāvaṇa's light enters Rāma before all the gods, and Nārada explains that hatred kept Rāma in Rāvaṇa's mind day and night (AR 6.11.78–86). *Variant (Vālmīki):* neither demon is liberated; their deaths are the deaths of enemies.
- **Synthesis with Vālmīki's "human" Rāma** - [Vālmīki's Uttara Kāṇḍa](../7-uttara-kanda.md) and the older epic stress Rāma as the ideal *man* (maryādā-puruṣottama) who weeps and doubts. The Adhyātma treats each such moment as conscious divine theatre. This shift from a heroic to a **theological** Rāma is what the vernacular bhakti poets inherited.

## Core doctrine 2 — the Māyā-Sītā / "Shadow Sītā"

- **The chastity problem it solves** - if the real Sītā spent the captivity in Rāvaṇa's Aśoka grove, her purity could be questioned and the **Agni-parīkṣā (fire ordeal)** looks cruel. The Adhyātma resolves this by ensuring **the real Sītā is never abducted at all**. [theological innovation; earlier precedent in the Kūrma Purāṇa, below]
- **The substitution (AR 3.7.1–4)** - after Rāvaṇa has enlisted Mārīca, and **before the golden deer appears**, Rāma "knowing all of Rāvaṇa's scheme" speaks to Sītā **privately**. Rāvaṇa will come disguised as a mendicant. She is to set up a **shadow (*chāyā*) of her own form** in the hut, stay **invisible in the fire for a year** at his command, and regain him after Rāvaṇa's death (AR 3.7.1–3). She sets the **māyā-Sītā** outside and vanishes into the fire herself (AR 3.7.4).
  - *Who acts:* in the AR the instruction is Rāma's and **Sītā herself** sets up the double and enters the fire. Agni's only roles are to keep her and give her back. Later Agni tells Rāma, "you placed her in me in the forest" and "made the illusory daughter of Janaka" (AR 6.13.20–21). In the Kūrma Purāṇa, by contrast, Agni creates the double (see below).
  - *Who knows:* Lakṣmaṇa is not told (AR 3.8.3). It is the **māyā-Sītā** who sees the golden deer and begs for it (AR 3.7.5–6), and who accuses Lakṣmaṇa (AR 3.7.28–32).
- **It is the shadow who is abducted** - Rāvaṇa carries off only the **illusory** Sītā; the captive in Laṅkā throughout is the māyā-form. In Uttara 7.4, Agastya adds that Rāvaṇa carried her off knowing Rāma to be the Supreme Self, and **guarded her "like a mother"** while he waited to be killed by Viṣṇu (AR 7.4.9–11).
- **The fire ordeal as *recovery*, not test** - Rāma has the māyā-Sītā brought to him, intending "to give up the māyā-Sītā and take back Jānakī, who was in the fire" (AR 6.12.67). Knowing her to be "the māyā-Sītā made for a purpose", he speaks harsh words to her (AR 6.12.75–76). She asks Lakṣmaṇa to light the pyre "for Rāma's trust and the people's conviction" and enters it (AR 6.12.77–83). **Agni** then rises with the **true Sītā** and returns her. The reflection-form (*pratibimba-rūpiṇī*) "has done what it was made for" and vanishes (AR 6.13.19–22). Hindupedia puts it this way: "the whole drama is pre-planned and enacted at the bidding of Rāma himself."
- **Precedents & parallels** - the AR is **not the first** Sītā-substitution story.
  - *Older attestation — Kūrma Purāṇa:* when Rāvaṇa approaches, **Sītā takes refuge in Agni**, praying to him at the household fire. Agni appears from it, **himself creates a māyā-Sītā** for Rāvaṇa to carry off, and takes the real Sītā away (KūrmaP 2.33.122–127). After the war the double is burnt and Agni returns the true Sītā. The Purāṇa tells it as an example of a *pativratā*'s power (KūrmaP 2.33.112–140, GRETIL numbering; some editions 2.34). The Kūrma is generally dated well before the AR (Wikipedia, *Maya Sita*: c. 550–850 CE) [scholarly]. See [Kūrma Purāṇa](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/kurma-purana.md).
  - *Other Purāṇic versions:* in the [Devī-Bhāgavata](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/devi-bhagavata-upapurana.md) telling, Agni comes to Rāma disguised as a brahmin to take Sītā into safekeeping. The shadow Sītā is later identified with **Vedavatī** before and **Draupadī** after (Wikipedia, *Maya Sita*, citing Vijñānānanda's translation and Mani's *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia*). The article calls the [Brahmavaivarta](../../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/brahmavaivarta-purana.md) account "similar" (citing Doniger). It dates the Brahmavaivarta **801–1100 CE** and counts it, with the Kūrma, among texts where the motif "was found earlier" than the AR. It dates the Devī-Bhāgavata only broadly, to the **6th–14th century**, so its order relative to the AR is uncertain. Neither Purāṇa was checked directly here. [late text] [unverified here]
  - *Seed in Vālmīki:* the epic has an **illusory Sītā** only as a battlefield trick, when Indrajit cuts down a *māyāmayī* Sītā in front of Hanumān (VR CE 6.68). See the [comparative page](../adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* the older [Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa](../README.md) has **no** shadow Sītā. The real Sītā is abducted, and the Agni-parīkṣā is a genuine (if troubling) ordeal of chastity.
  - *Variant (reception in Tulsīdās):* Tulsīdās **adopts** the device. His Rāma tells Sītā to "dwell in the fire until I destroy the night-roamers", and she leaves her *pratibimba* behind (RCM Araṇya, the chaupāīs following dohā 23, per ramcharit.in). This carried the māyā-Sītā into the Hindi belt's popular imagination; see [Rāmcaritmānas](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md).

## Episodes the Adhyātma adds or reshapes

*(Vālmīki comparisons use the vulgate unless marked. Several of these "Adhyātma" scenes are popularly assumed to be Vālmīki's.)*

- **Ahalyā on a stone (AR 1.5.27–36)** - Gautama curses Ahalyā to stay **on a stone (*śilā*) in his hermitage**, fasting, invisible and meditating on Rāma. Rāma **touches the stone with his foot** and she appears (AR 1.5.27–36), then hymns him (AR 1.5.43 ff.). Viśvāmitra later describes her as having been "in the form of a stone" (*śilārūpā*, AR 1.6.14). [Both phrasings appear in the AR itself.]
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* Ahalyā is not a stone. She lives for thousands of years in the hermitage, invisible to all beings, living on air and lying in ashes, and is freed by receiving Rāma as a guest (VR 1.48.29–32). See [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../1-bala-kanda.md).
- **The boatman washes Rāma's feet (AR 1.6.2–5)** - at the Gaṅgā crossing **on the way to Mithilā**, a boatman insists on washing Rāma's feet first. He fears the dust that turned a stone into a woman will turn his boat into one too.
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās):* the scene moves to the **exile** crossing, with the boatman Kevaṭ (RCM Ayodhyā 100–102; see [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](../2-ayodhya-kanda.md)).
- **Nārada's visit (AR 2.1)** - on the eve of the coronation Nārada reminds Rāma that he came to kill Rāvaṇa. Rāma promises to leave for the forest the next day (AR 2.1.35–39).
- **Sarasvatī enters Mantharā (AR 2.2.44–46)** - the gods send Vāṇī to block the coronation. She enters **Mantharā first, then Kaikeyī**. At Citrakūṭa Kaikeyī repents, and Rāma tells her "it was I who impelled the words that left your mouth", so she bears no fault (AR 2.9.59–64).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna):* Brahmā sends a gandharvī, Dundubhī, to be born as Mantharā (MBh 3.260.9–10; see the [comparative page](../adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md)).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's teaching to Guha (AR 2.6.1–16)** - when Guha blames Kaikeyī for Rāma sleeping on grass, Lakṣmaṇa answers that "one's own past karma is the cause of joy and sorrow", and gives him a short Vedāntic teaching.
- **Vālmīki's autobiography (AR 2.6.64–86)** - receiving Rāma at Citrakūṭa, Vālmīki tells his own past. A brahmin by birth, he grew up among the **Kirātas** and lived as a robber. Seven sages told him to ask whether his family would share his sins, and they would not. The sages then gave him Rāma's name **reversed, "*marā*"**, to repeat. He repeated it motionless until an **anthill (*valmīka*)** grew over him, and on coming out he was named Vālmīki. [Not in Vālmīki's own epic; this is the familiar "robber-poet" legend in Sanskrit form.]
- **Rāma's teaching at Pañcavaṭī (AR 3.4)** - asked by Lakṣmaṇa, Rāma explains māyā, the Self and the supreme Self, and teaches that "jīva and Paramātman are synonyms, with no difference between them".
- **Śabarī's nine-fold devotion (AR 3.10.20–31)** - Rāma tells Śabarī that "gender, caste, name and stage of life are no cause in my worship; devotion alone is the cause" (AR 3.10.20). He lists **nine means of bhakti (*navavidhā bhakti*)**. They are (1) the company of the good, (2) talk of him, (3) praise of his qualities, (4) explaining his words, (5) serving the teacher as him, (6) virtue and restraint with steady worship, (7) worship through his mantra, (8) honouring his devotees, seeing him in all beings and detachment, and (9) enquiry into his reality (AR 3.10.23–27). The text's ordinals are slightly ambiguous at the fifth and sixth. Śabarī then directs him to Sugrīva and enters the fire to reach liberation (AR 3.10.35–41). [The popular motif of Śabarī's pre-tasted berries is not in the AR text.]
- **Tārā consoled with Self-knowledge (AR 4.3.12–21)** - Tārā begs Rāma to kill her with the arrow that killed Vālin. Rāma asks whether she grieves for the body (inert) or for the soul (unborn and undying), and teaches that "the mind alone is saṃsāra".
- **Kriyā-yoga (AR 4.4.8 ff.)** - in the rains at Pravarṣaṇa, Lakṣmaṇa asks for the ritual path. Rāma teaches image worship: mantra from a guru, the heart, fire, image or śālagrāma as places of worship, Vedic and Tantric mantras, and *nyāsa*. The AR thus finds room for temple ritual alongside knowledge.
- **Svayamprabhā (AR 4.6.40–84)** - the yoginī of the cave tells the monkeys about Hemā and her own penance and sends them out. Then she goes to Rāma, hymns him as beyond māyā, and at his word goes to **Badarī**, where she gives up her body and reaches the highest state (AR 4.6.83–84).
- **Rāmeśvara before the bridge (AR 6.4.1–3)** - before the causeway is begun, Rāma installs and worships **Śiva as Rāmeśvara** and declares the merit of seeing him at the setu.
- **Kālanemi and Dhānyamālī (AR 6.6.36 – 6.7.33)** - Rāvaṇa sends Kālanemi, disguised as a sage, to delay Hanumān on the herb errand. In the sage's pond a crocodile seizes Hanumān. He kills it, and it turns out to be the cursed apsaras **Dhānyamālī**, who warns him about the false sage (AR 6.7.22–28). Hanumān gives Kālanemi a fist for his "guru's fee" and kills him (AR 6.7.29–33). [Absent from the Vālmīki Critical Edition.]
- **Rāvaṇa's chosen enmity (AR 3.5.58–61; 7.4.1–11)** - lying awake after Khara's death, Rāvaṇa reasons it out. If Rāma is the Supreme, death at his hands wins Vaikuṇṭha; if not, Laṅkā is his to enjoy. So he will approach Hari "by hostility, since the Lord is not quickly pleased by devotion" (AR 3.5.58–61). Agastya later traces this resolve to Rāvaṇa's humiliation by the women of Śvetadvīpa (AR 7.4.1–11).
- **The pre-arranged exile of Sītā (AR 7.4.35–44)** - the gods ask Sītā to go ahead to Vaikuṇṭha. She tells Rāma, and he plans to abandon her "using public talk as a pretext, as though afraid of gossip". She will bear twins near Vālmīki's hermitage and leave through an opening in the earth. Only afterwards is the citizens' gossip reported to him (AR 7.4.46 ff.).
  - *Contrast (Vālmīki):* the rumour comes first, and the decision is the grieving king's own (see [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../7-uttara-kanda.md)).
- **The departures (AR 7.8–9)** - Durvāsas forces Lakṣmaṇa to break Rāma's order. Rāma releases him, and Lakṣmaṇa goes to the Sarayū and leaves his body by yogic withdrawal of breath (AR 7.8.64–68). Rāma then touches the Sarayū's water, Brahmā hails him as the supreme Self, and he becomes four-armed Viṣṇu again, while his brothers become Śeṣa and the discus and conch (AR 7.9.52–58).

## The two philosophical cores

- **Rāma-Hṛdaya ("the Heart of Rāma")** - the **opening sarga** (AR 1.1), whose colophon names it *śrī-rāma-hṛdayaṃ nāma prathamaḥ sargaḥ*. It is **not a free-standing hymn but a dialogue** set **after the coronation**. Rāma asks Sītā to teach the truth to Hanumān, who "has done his work, desires nothing and seeks knowledge" (AR 1.1.29–30).
  - *Sītā speaks first* (AR 1.1.32–43): Rāma is Brahman, she is mūla-prakṛti, and every deed of the story is hers, superimposed on him.
  - *Then Rāma teaches* (AR 1.1.44–52): consciousness is threefold, like space. There is space as a whole, the space in a pool, and space reflected in the water. In the same way there is the whole, consciousness limited by the intellect, and its reflection (*ābhāsa*). The agency of the intellect with its reflection is wrongly superimposed on the changeless witness (AR 1.1.47), and the reflection itself is false (AR 1.1.48). Knowing the identity taught by "*tat tvam asi*" destroys ignorance and its effects (AR 1.1.45–50). Without devotion to him, knowledge and liberation are not won "even in a hundred births" (AR 1.1.51).
  - *Phala-śruti* (AR 1.1.53–56): Śiva calls it "the essence of all Vedānta". It is often recited on its own as a stotra.
- **Rāma-Gītā ("the Song of Rāma")** - Uttara Kāṇḍa **sarga 5 (AR 7.5, 62 verses)**. After stories such as Nṛga's curse, Lakṣmaṇa asks how to cross the ocean of ignorance, and Rāma teaches him (AR 7.5.2–6). (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa)
  - *Its argument is strict Advaita, not a blend of three paths.* (The three paths are named elsewhere, in Rāma's teaching to Kausalyā, AR 7.7.59; see below.) First one does the duties of one's varṇa and āśrama to purify the mind, then seeks a true guru (AR 7.5.7). Action keeps the wheel of rebirth turning. Ignorance is the root, and **knowledge alone** removes it; action cannot (AR 7.5.8–10). Rāma states the objection that knowledge needs action as its helper (AR 7.5.11–13) and rejects it. "The wise should give up action entirely; because it conflicts with knowledge, there can be no combination" (*vidyāvirodhān na samuccayo bhavet*, AR 7.5.16), citing the Taittirīya and Vājasaneyi śruti (AR 7.5.21). Ritual is enjoined only while one still identifies with the body (AR 7.5.17).
  - *Method:* the *mahāvākya* "tat tvam asi" (AR 7.5.24–27), discrimination of Self from body, and a Māṇḍūkya-style meditation on **Oṃ**. A = viśva, U = taijasa, M = prājña, each dissolved into the next and finally into pure consciousness (AR 7.5.48–51).
  - *Devotional close:* "until one sees everything as myself, one should be intent on worshipping me". Rāma is seen in the heart of the faithful devotee (AR 7.5.58), and whoever serves him, without qualities or with them, purifies the three worlds (AR 7.5.61).
  - *Comparison:* modern readers compare it with the Bhagavad-Gītā (Rāma : Lakṣmaṇa :: Kṛṣṇa : Arjuna), though it comes **after** the war rather than before it. It circulates as an independent Vedāntic text. [scholarly]
  - *Note:* two other texts are also called Rāma-Gītā. One is the Rāma-to-**Hanumān** dialogue of the *Guru-jñāna-vāsiṣṭha / Tattvasārāyaṇa*, popular mainly in South India (Hindu Blog). The other is the Rāma-to-Hanumān discourse of the **Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa** (sargas 11–14 in Wikipedia's summary, which does not itself call it a Rāma-Gītā; the name follows the [comparative page](../adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md)) [unverified here]. The Adhyātma's Rāma-Gītā is the one meant here. [disputed naming]

## The Advaitic-bhakti synthesis

- **Jñāna *and* bhakti, not either/or** - the work's lasting achievement is to harmonize **non-dual knowledge (jñāna)** with **loving devotion (bhakti)** to a personal deity. Rāma is at once the impersonal Brahman *and* the supremely lovable Lord. In the text's own terms bhakti is the **gate to knowledge**. Pārvatī calls devotion to Rāma "the well-known boat across saṃsāra" (AR 1.1.10–11). Rāma tells Śabarī that "as soon as devotion arises, the experience of my reality follows, and liberation in that very life" (AR 3.10.29–31). [scholarly framing; textual basis as cited]
- **Knowledge, devotion, detachment — and ritual kept below knowledge** - Pārvatī's opening request is for "knowledge with realization, joined with devotion and detachment" (*jñānaṃ savijñānam atha anubhakti-vairāgya-yuktam*, AR 1.1.9). Ritual has a place: varṇāśrama duties purify the mind (AR 7.5.7), and Rāma teaches image worship as *kriyā-mārga* (AR 4.4.8 ff.). In the Uttara Kāṇḍa Rāma also tells Kausalyā that he has taught "three paths to liberation: karma-yoga, jñāna-yoga and bhakti-yoga", then sorts devotion by the three guṇas and describes a nirguṇa bhakti beyond them (AR 7.7.59–66). Hindupedia summarizes this as teaching "the three yogas of karma, jñāna and bhakti". The Rāma-Gītā, though, **refuses to combine** action with knowledge as a means of liberation (AR 7.5.14–22). So the text names all three paths, while its Rāma-Gītā keeps action below knowledge. Bhakti theologians drew on this template.
- **Hymnody throughout** - many **stutis** to Rāma punctuate the narrative. Among the speakers are Kausalyā, Ahalyā, Nārada, Vālmīki, Jaṭāyu, Svayamprabhā, Indra, Brahmā and Śiva. Hindupedia's list also names Vibhīṣaṇa. They model the devotee's response to Brahman made personal, and several circulate on their own. sanskritdocuments.org, for example, prints Jaṭāyu's hymn (AR 3.8) as a separate stotra, in a variant wording.
- **Doctrinal kinship** - the Brahman/Māyā framework and the reflection theory of the jīva (*ābhāsa*, AR 1.1.46–48) tie the work to classical Advaita. See [Ātman, Brahman & the Six Darśanas](../../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md) for the Śaṅkara-school metaphysics it presupposes.

## Influence — the fountainhead of bhakti Rāmāyaṇas

- **Tulsīdās's Rāmcaritmānas (begun 1574 CE, Awadhi)** - influenced by the Adhyātma (Wikipedia). It shares the **Rāma-as-Parabrahman** theology, the **māyā-Sītā** device (RCM Araṇya, after dohā 23), **Sarasvatī turning Mantharā's mind**, and the **foot-washing boatman** (moved to the exile crossing). It re-clothes these in vernacular Awadhi for mass devotion. See [Rāmcaritmānas (Tulsīdās)](./ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md). [scholarly]
- **Ezhuthachan's *Adhyātmarāmāyaṇam Kiḷippāṭṭu* (Malayalam)** - **Thunchaththu Rāmānujan Ezhuthachan**, "father of modern Malayalam literature", renders the Adhyātma (not Vālmīki) into Malayalam. He uses the **kiḷippāṭṭu ("parrot-song")** genre, in which a **parrot (kiḷi)** recites the story to the poet. The language is "nearly-modern Malayalam", and Ezhuthachan is credited with mainstreaming the **Grantha-based Malayalam script** in place of Vaṭṭeḻuttu (Wikipedia). It became Kerala's most beloved Rāmāyaṇa and is recited through **Karkkiṭakam, the "Rāmāyaṇa month"**. [scholarly]
  - *Dating variants:* the poem is placed in the **early 17th century** (Wikipedia, *Adhyatma Ramayanam Kilippattu*). Ezhuthachan himself is placed in the 16th century (Pollock), the 17th (Burnell), or **1495–1575** (Ulloor), per Wikipedia, *Thunchaththu Ezhuthachan*. [disputed]
- **Other vernacular versions and translations** - Oriya and Bengali versions are named in general sources without titles (Wikipedia). Other named works:
  - Telugu: **Munipalle Subrahmaṇya Kavi**'s 18th-century *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*, 104 kīrtanas in six kāṇḍas ending with the Yuddha, with Rāma as Parabrahman and Sītā as Ādi-Māyā (Narthaki).
  - Nepali: **Bhānubhakta Ācārya**'s Rāmāyaṇa, from the Sanskrit (Wikipedia).
  - Kannada: Beladakere Sūryanārāyaṇa Śāstrī's translation (Mysore, 1948, per Wikipedia).
  - Hindi: the Gita Press translation.
  - English: Rai Bahadur Lala Baij Nath (Panini Office, Allahabad, 1913; *Sacred Books of the Hindus*, extra vol. 1).
  - [open-ended roster — full vernacular catalogue not enumerated here]
- **Liturgical afterlife** - the **Rāma-Hṛdaya**, **Rāma-Gītā** and the embedded stotras circulate as stand-alone devotional texts to the present day, independent of the full epic.
