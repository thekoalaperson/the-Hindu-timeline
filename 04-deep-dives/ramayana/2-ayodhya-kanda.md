# Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa — Book of Ayodhya

> Cosmic address: [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) ← but the Rāmāyaṇa itself unfolds in the [Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current Vaivasvata Manvantara. Rāma is the 7th [Daśāvatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) of [Viṣṇu](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md), of the [Sūryavaṃśa / Ikṣvāku line](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
> Parent: [Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive index](README.md) · Part of [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Follows [Bāla Kāṇḍa](1-bala-kanda.md). Primary source: Vālmīki [Rāmāyaṇa](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md).

The **Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa** ("Book of Ayodhyā") is the second of the seven kāṇḍas of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa — **119 sargas** in the vulgate / Southern recension, the numbering used for every citation on this page; the Baroda Critical Edition constitutes the same book in **111**, and the two do not correspond sarga for sarga. It is the great human-drama and *dharma-saṃkaṭa* (dilemma-of-duty) book: in the span of two days the joy of Rāma's announced coronation collapses into his fourteen-year exile, Daśaratha dies of grief, and Bharata, refusing the ill-gotten throne, installs Rāma's sandals as sovereign and rules as regent from Nandigrāma. The kāṇḍa contains no demon-combat — its battlefield is the conscience.

## The Coronation Announced (sargas 1–7)

- **Rāma's qualities and Daśaratha's resolve** - The aged Daśaratha, beloved of his people, resolves to install **Rāma** as *yuvarāja* (crown prince/heir-apparent) while he himself still lives; an assembly of citizens and feudatories acclaims the choice (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 1–2).
- **The omens of haste** - After the assembly Daśaratha confers with his ministers and fixes the consecration for the next day, when the moon will stand in Puṣya (4.1–2), having already set Vasiṣṭha and Vāmadeva to prepare the rite (3). To Rāma he explains the hurry: bad dreams, meteors falling with a roar by day, and the astrologers' warning that the Sun, Mars and Rāhu are pressing on his birth-star, the sign of a king's death or disaster (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 4.17–19).
- **Bharata and Śatrughna absent** - Crucially, **Bharata and Śatrughna are away** at the home of Bharata's maternal uncle Yudhājit (Kekaya), and Daśaratha says plainly why he wants the rite held now: *"while Bharata is away from the city — that, in my judgement, is the fit time for your consecration."* He adds that Bharata is righteous but that the minds of men are unsteady and may turn. The suspicion the retellings put in the courtiers' mouths is the king's own stated reasoning (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 4.25–27; cf. 1, 67).
- **Rāma instructed in vows** - Vasiṣṭha goes to Rāma and has him and Sītā keep a fast; that night Rāma offers ghee to Nārāyaṇa and sleeps with Sītā on a bed of kuśa grass in the shrine of Viṣṇu, while the city is decked for festival (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 5.9–11; 6.1–4).

## Mantharā and Kaikeyī (sargas 7–13)

- **Mantharā sees the festivities** - **Mantharā**, the hunchbacked maidservant Kaikeyī brought from Kekaya, learns of the coronation from a nurse and rushes to her mistress in fury (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 7–8).
  - *Variant — Raṅganātha Rāmāyaṇamu (Telugu, Dvipada metre, 13th c.):* Mantharā is given a grudge of her own. As a boy Rāma, playing with ball and stick, struck her on the knee when she threw his ball away, and she has waited since for revenge (summarised in Wikipedia, *Manthara*). Vālmīki gives her no personal motive. [regional] → see [Mantharā](../characters/manthara.md).
- **The poisoning of Kaikeyī's mind** - Mantharā argues that with Rāma crowned, Kausalyā will dominate and Kaikeyī will be reduced to a servant. Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa guard each other like the Aśvins, so Rāma will spare Lakṣmaṇa but **will surely harm Bharata**; Bharata had better go to the forest straight from his uncle's house. She brushes aside Kaikeyī's first, generous reaction, when Kaikeyī rewarded the news with a jewel (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 7; 8.31–36).
- **The plan: invoke the old boons** - Mantharā reminds Kaikeyī of two boons Daśaratha once promised her, and coaches the demands: **(1) Bharata crowned, (2) Rāma exiled for fourteen years**, long enough for Bharata to take root in the people's affection (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 9.20–21; claimed at 11).
  - *Where Mantharā's knowledge comes from:* the text closes the loop itself — "I do not know what happened there; **you yourself told me** this story long ago" (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 9.18–19). The weapon is one Kaikeyī handed her maid in an unguarded confidence.
- **The "anger chamber" (krodhāgāra)** - Kaikeyī strips off her ornaments, dons soiled garments, and casts herself on the floor of the *kopa-bhavana* / *krodhāgāra* (sulking-house) to await the king (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 9–10).

### The two boons: the battlefield backstory (variants)

The boons stem from a past war in which Kaikeyī saved Daśaratha's life. The texts disagree on the mechanism:

- *Vālmīki (Ayodhyā 9.11–17):* in the war of the gods and asuras, Daśaratha took Kaikeyī with him and went south toward Daṇḍaka to help Indra against the demon of the city called Vaijayanta, Timidhvaja, known as **Śambara**, the great asura of the hundred māyās (9.11–13). Cut to pieces by the demons' weapons, he was carried unconscious out of the battle by Kaikeyī, and protected by her a second time when he was wounded there (9.15–16). Pleased, he gave her two boons, and she chose to keep them for later (9.17). The text tells of two rescues and two boons but does not pair one boon with each rescue. The same battle is in the CE (2.9.9–12).
- *Variant — chariot pin (later tradition):* when the bolt of one wheel slips out mid-battle, Kaikeyī keeps the wheel on by thrusting her thumb into the bolt-hole, and Daśaratha rewards her with two boons. Wikipedia's *Kaikeyi* sources this to Vettam Mani's *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia* (p. 364) and names no primary text. [later tradition; primary source not identified]
- *Variant — attributed to Kṛttivāsa (Bengali):* a popular retelling said to follow the Bengali text has Kaikeyī heal Daśaratha's wounds from the Śambara war with the *saṃjīvanī* mantra (first boon), and later suck the pus from an infected nail when the physicians say nothing else will cure it (second boon). The retelling (hindu-blog.com, 2007) cites no passage, and it has not yet been checked against the Bengali text. [regional; unverified]

## Daśaratha Trapped by His Word (sargas 10–13)

- **The king's grief and pleading** - Finding Kaikeyī prostrate, the doting Daśaratha swears by Rāma to grant whatever she asks; she binds him to the oath before naming her price (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 10–11).
- **The demands spoken** - Kaikeyī claims her two boons: **Bharata to be consecrated**, and **Rāma to wear bark and matted hair and dwell in Daṇḍaka forest fourteen years** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 11).
- **Daśaratha's collapse** - The king faints, rages, begs, and reasons through the night, and Kaikeyī will not move (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 12–13). As the night runs out she presses him with examples of promises kept at any price: King **Śaibya**, who gave his own body to the hawk, **Alarka**, who plucked out his eyes for a brahmin who asked for them, and the ocean, which keeps to its shore (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 14.3–6). Neither Śaibya nor Alarka is an Ikṣvāku, and Hariścandra is not named.
- **The Truth-of-the-Raghus** - Daśaratha is destroyed not by Kaikeyī's power but by his own sworn word: he cannot revoke a granted boon (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 12–13). The famous formula for it, *raghukula rīti sadā cali āī, prāna jāhuṁ baru bacanu na jāī* ("this has always been the way of Raghu's house: let life go, but not the word given"), belongs to Tulsīdās. In his poem Daśaratha says it to Kaikeyī before she names her boons (*Rāmcaritmānas*, Ayodhyā 28.2).
- **The first repudiation** - In the same exchange, as the night gives way to sunrise (14.15, 14.19), the king renounces Kaikeyī. He gives up the hand he took at the nuptial fire "and your son with you", and rules that neither she nor Bharata may offer him the funeral water: Rāma must do it (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 14.14–17). This night-time wording, with the son included, is what Rāma later asks his father to withdraw (Yuddha 119.26).

## Rāma Accepts Exile (sargas 14–19)

- **Sumantra summons Rāma** - At dawn the charioteer-minister **Sumantra** finds the king broken; Kaikeyī herself sends for Rāma (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 14–16).
- **Kaikeyī delivers the sentence** - Because Daśaratha cannot speak, **Kaikeyī announces** the boons; Rāma accepts instantly, untroubled, for a father's word is dharma (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 18–19).
- **Rāma's equanimity** - Rāma shows no anger toward Kaikeyī or his father. His one grief, he says, is that the king did not tell him himself (19.6), and he promises to leave for Daṇḍaka that very day, once he has taken leave of his mother and comforted Sītā (19.25). This serene obedience is the kāṇḍa's ethical centrepiece (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 19).

## The Three Companions Insist (sargas 20–31)

- **Kausalyā's lament** - Rāma's mother **Kausalyā** grieves and at first would forbid him; Rāma reconciles her to his duty and asks her blessing (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 20–21, 24).
- **Kausalyā's benediction-rite** - Reconciled, she performs a full *svastyayana* over her son before he goes. She sips water, calls on the gods, seasons, quarters, mountains, oceans and rivers to guard him in the forest, and has a brahmin pour oblations of ghee, white garlands, fuel-sticks and white mustard into the fire. The brahmins pronounce blessings over honey, curds, rice and ghee (25.26–30). She wishes him the luck of Indra when Vṛtra fell, the blessing Vinatā gave Garuḍa going for the nectar, and the fortune of Viṣṇu's three strides, then ties the herb *viśalyakaraṇī* on his wrist as an amulet (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 25.32–38; CE 2.22.14). It is the longest blessing any mother gives in the epic.
- **Lakṣmaṇa's fury** - **Lakṣmaṇa** rages, urging armed resistance and seizure of the throne; Rāma calms him, insisting on submission to dharma and to their father's truth (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 21–23).
- **Sītā insists on accompanying him** - **Sītā** refuses to stay, declaring a wife's place is at her husband's side; she answers Rāma's warnings of forest hardship with vows of devotion and threatens to die if left behind (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 26–30).
  - *The forest foretold:* Sītā tells Rāma that in her father's house, brahmins who read bodily marks predicted she would live in a forest, and that the prediction is now hers to fulfil (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 29.8–11; CE 2.26.5–7).
  - *The taunt:* when he still refuses, she asks what her father thought he was getting in a son-in-law who is "a woman in the form of a man" (*striyaṃ puruṣavigraham*, Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 30.3; CE 2.27.2). It is among the harshest things anyone says to Rāma in the book, and it is at the end of this sarga that he relents.
- **Lakṣmaṇa granted leave to follow** - Lakṣmaṇa begs and is permitted to attend Rāma and Sītā as servant-protector in exile (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 31).
- **The Vāruṇa weapons taken to the forest** - Rāma sends Lakṣmaṇa to fetch the **weapons Varuṇa gave at Janaka's great sacrifice**: two dread bows, impenetrable armour, quivers that never empty, and two gold-hilted swords, kept garlanded and honoured in the house of the preceptor, the Ikṣvākus' guru **Vasiṣṭha** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 31.27–31; CE 2.28.11–14). Two men going out to live on roots and fruit arm themselves first with divine weaponry. The exile is a banishment in name and a campaign in its equipment.
- **Giving away wealth** - Before leaving, Rāma distributes his possessions to brahmins, dependents, and the poor (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 32). The one story told at length is that of **Trijaṭa**, a poor brahmin of the Garga clan who scratches a living from the soil. Rāma tells him in jest that he may have as many cows as his thrown staff can reach. Trijaṭa hurls it across the Sarayū into a herd of thousands, and Rāma gives him every cow up to the far bank, apologising for the joke (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 32.29–43). The CE keeps the jest, the throw, the apology and Trijaṭa taking the herd (CE 2.29.21–26), but not the verses where the staff lands across the Sarayū and the cows are given (VR 32.37–38).

## Departure from Ayodhyā (sargas 32–42)

- **Bark garments** - Kaikeyī produces bark/tree-fibre robes (*valkala / cīra*); Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa don them. Sītā, a princess of Mithilā, cannot work out how to wear hers, and Rāma ties it on for her over her silks (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 37.1–20).
  - *Vasiṣṭha's objection (same sarga, not a variant):* Vasiṣṭha stops her and rebukes Kaikeyī. He argues first that a wife is her husband's very self (*ātmā hi dārāḥ*), so Sītā should rule the earth in Rāma's place, and that if she goes the city will go with her. He then rules that the boon asked exile for **Rāma alone**, so Sītā should go adorned (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 37.21–36). Sītā does not change her mind (37.37). It is Daśaratha who then has the treasurer bring clothes and jewels enough for the fourteen years, and she sets out adorned (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 38; 39.14–18).
- **The city's revolt of grief** - The citizens of Ayodhyā wail and resolve to follow Rāma into the forest, abandoning the city to Kaikeyī (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 33, 40–41, 45–47).
- **Sumantra's reproach: Kaikeyī's mother** - Sumantra tells Kaikeyī she is her mother's daughter ("honey does not ooze from a neem tree"). The Kekaya king had a boon to understand the speech of all creatures. He laughed at something an ant said, and when his queen demanded the reason even at the cost of his life, he took the boon-giver's advice and put her away (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 35.17–28). The tale has no counterpart in the Critical Edition text on GRETIL. [recensional]
- **Treasury and army refused; the Asamañja precedent** - Daśaratha orders the granary, the treasury and an army to go with Rāma. Kaikeyī objects that Bharata will not take a kingdom drained like wine with its spirit drunk off, and cites the Ikṣvāku precedent of Sagara exiling his eldest son Asamañja (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 36.7–16; CE 2.32.5–11). The old minister **Siddhārtha** turns the precedent against her: Asamañja threw children into the Sarayū and was banished for it, but what has Rāma done? (36.18–30). Daśaratha then resolves to go to the forest himself (36).
- **Sumantra drives them out** - Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa mount Sumantra's chariot; the people and the king run after it. Rāma tells Sumantra to drive faster and, if the king scolds him, to say he did not hear the order to stop (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 39–40, esp. 40.41–48).
- **Sumitrā's charge to Lakṣmaṇa** - Alone among the mothers, **Sumitrā** sends her son away without a word of protest. She tells him that a younger brother should be ruled by his elder, and that giving, sacrifice and laying down one's life in battle are the old way of his house, and gives the line that defines him: *"Know Rāma to be Daśaratha, know Sītā to be me, know the forest to be Ayodhyā — go, my child, and be happy"* (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 40.5–9; CE 2.35.5–7).
- **Daśaratha repudiates Kaikeyī** - When Kaikeyī tries to help him up, the king formally casts her off a second time: *"Do not touch my limbs. I do not wish to look at you. You are neither wife nor kin of mine"*. He renounces, in this world and the next, the marriage-contract he made taking her hand around the fire, and refuses his own funeral: if Bharata is glad of the kingdom, let no death-offering from Bharata reach him (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 42.4–9; the first renunciation is 14.14–17). Long after, at Yuddha 119.25–27, Rāma asks his father's shade to let the curse "I abandon you with your son" not touch Kaikeyī and Bharata, and Daśaratha agrees.

## The Forest Road: Tamasā to the Ganges (sargas 43–53)

- **First night at the Tamasā** - The three camp on the **Tamasā** river; Rāma slips away before dawn to elude the citizens still following (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 45–46).
- **Through Kosala to the Vedaśrutī, Gomatī, Syandikā** - They cross the southern Kosala countryside and several rivers, Rāma pointing out the land he is leaving (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 49).
- **Guha of Śṛṅgaverapura** - At the Ganges they are received by **Guha**, chief of the Niṣādas (a tribal hunter-king) and Rāma's friend, who offers hospitality and lodging (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 50).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's vigil with Guha** - That night Guha offers to stand guard so that Lakṣmaṇa can sleep. Lakṣmaṇa refuses to lie down while his brother and Sītā sleep on the ground, and stays up talking with Guha, grieving for his father and his mothers (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 51).
- **Matted hair (jaṭā)** - On the morning of the crossing Rāma asks Guha for the **milky latex of a banyan (vaṭa-kṣīra / nyagrodha)** and mats his own and Lakṣmaṇa's hair with it into ascetic locks (*jaṭā*); bark-clad and matted, the two now look like sages (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 52.67–70; CE 2.46.55–56).
- **Sītā's vow to the Gaṅgā** - As they push off, Sītā prays to the goddess with folded hands and names her thanksgiving for a safe return: a hundred thousand cows, fine cloth and food to the brahmins (52.87–88), **a thousand jars of surā (spirituous liquor) and meat with cooked rice** (52.89), and worship of every shrine along the banks (52.90). At the next crossing she vows a thousand cows and a hundred jars of surā to the **Yamunā** (55.19–20). **Neither liquor vow is in the Critical Edition.** The CE's Gaṅgā prayer ends with the cows, cloth and food (CE 2.46.67–73). The CE has no prayer to the Yamunā at all, only Sītā's folded hands and her wish at the fig tree to see Kausalyā and Sumitrā again (CE 2.49.11–12). The friction with later vegetarian and teetotal readings of Sītā is a vulgate (Southern) reading; the corpus records the text, not the reception. [recensional]
- **Crossing the Ganges; Sumantra turned back** - With Guha's boat they cross the Ganges; Rāma sends the weeping **Sumantra back to Ayodhyā**, refusing to keep the chariot and charging him to console Daśaratha (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 52).
- **Rāma's night under the tree** - On the far bank, alone with Lakṣmaṇa for the first time, it is **Rāma** who breaks down. He fears that Kaikeyī may take the old king's life now that she has what she wanted. He asks what father would abandon such a son for a woman, and fears she will persecute Kausalyā and Sumitrā. He urges Lakṣmaṇa to go back in the morning and protect them. Lakṣmaṇa refuses, and Rāma lets him stay (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 53, esp. 53.6–17).

## Bharadvāja and Chitrakūṭa (sargas 54–56)

- **Sage Bharadvāja's hermitage** - At the Gaṅgā–Yamunā confluence (Prayāga) they reach **Bharadvāja**, who advises them to settle at **Chitrakūṭa** for its beauty and seclusion (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 54–55).
- **Crossing the Yamunā; the prayer to the fig tree** - They build a raft and cross the Yamunā; on the far side Sītā salutes the great banyan called **Śyāma**, asking that her husband complete his vow and that she see Kausalyā and Sumitrā again (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 55.16–25; CE 2.49.9–12).
- **Vālmīki met on the road** - Before they settle on Citrakūṭa the three come with folded palms to the hermitage of the sage **Vālmīki** himself and salute him, and he welcomes them (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 56.16–17). The poet walks into his own poem here, and will take Sītā in at the end of it, but **only in the vulgate**. The Critical Edition goes straight from the arrival on Citrakūṭa to the building of the hut (CE 2.50.11–14) and names no Vālmīki anywhere in its Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa. [recensional]
- **The leaf hut at Chitrakūṭa** - Lakṣmaṇa builds a leaf-and-timber hermitage (*parṇaśālā*) of strong timber and thatch (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 56.19–21). The river the text names here is the **Mālyavatī**, not the Mandākinī (56.38; CE 2.50.21). The popular name for the Citrakūṭa river enters the narrative only later, from Bharadvāja's directions and the idyll sargas onward (2.92–95), and Vālmīki never reconciles the two names. [scholarly: an unresolved doublet in the text's own geography]
- **The house-warming with a black antelope** - Before entering, Rāma has Lakṣmaṇa kill a **black antelope** and roast it, and he offers it to the deities of the dwelling with the prescribed mantras. This is a *vāstu-śānti*, the ordinary kṣatriya house-entry rite, performed on a hut of leaves (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 56.22–32; CE 2.50.15–19).

## Sumantra's Return and Daśaratha's Death (sargas 57–77)

- **Sumantra reports** - The charioteer returns to Ayodhyā and relays Rāma's words; the city and Daśaratha are crushed anew (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 57–59).
- **The horses that would not move** - Sumantra reports that on the road back the horses shed hot tears and would not go on, an omen of doom (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 59.1).
- **Daśaratha confesses the killing of the ascetic's son** - In Kausalyā's chamber the dying king recalls his youthful crime. As a young prince hunting by sound (*śabda-vedhī*) on the bank of the **Sarayū** in the rains, he loosed at what he took for an elephant drinking, and struck a boy filling a water-pot for his blind ascetic parents (63.21–25). Dying, the boy stops the king's self-reproach with a point of law: *"I am no brahmin — do not let that fear trouble you; I was born of a **śūdrā** mother by a **vaiśya** father"*, so no brahmahatyā attaches (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 63.52–53; CE 2.57.36). The blind father, told of it, curses Daśaratha to **die likewise of grief for a son**, and he and his wife then mount the funeral pyre (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 64.53–58).
  - *Naming variant:* **Vālmīki leaves the boy and his parents unnamed throughout**; he is only "the ascetic's son". The name **Śravaṇa (Śravaṇa-kumāra)** belongs to later Purāṇic and folk retellings. [folk tradition]
- **Death of Daśaratha** - Crying out to Rāma, to Kausalyā and the wretched Sumitrā, and calling Kaikeyī his enemy and the disgrace of his house, Daśaratha dies of grief some time after midnight. The curse is fulfilled (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 64.76–79).
- **The body preserved** - With no son present to perform the rites, the ministers lay the king's body in a vat of oil (*taila-droṇī*) to await Bharata (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 66.13–16; CE 2.60.11–13).

## Bharata Recalled and His Refusal (sargas 67–83)

- **The hymn on kinglessness (arājaka)** - With the king dead and no heir on the ground, the assembled brahmins and ministers press Vasiṣṭha with the epic's set-piece on **anarchy**. In a land without a king the thundering clouds bring no rain, no one sows even a handful of seed, the son does not obey the father nor the wife the husband, no one's property is his own, and men devour one another like fish; *"a kingless land is like a river without water, a garden without grass, cattle without a herdsman"* (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 67, esp. 67.9–10, 67.29, 67.31). It is the political argument the whole kāṇḍa is written against, and Rāma will refuse it anyway.
- **Messengers to Kekaya** - With Mārkaṇḍeya and the other sages concurring, Vasiṣṭha sends the envoys Siddhārtha, Jayanta, Vijaya, Aśoka and Nandana on fast horses. They are to tell **Bharata** only that he must come urgently, and nothing of the exile or the death (68.5–8). The sarga tracks their ride: along the Mālinī between Aparatāla and Pralamba, across the Gaṅgā at Hastināpura, west through Kurujāṅgala into Pāñcāla, over the Śaradaṇḍā, past a wish-granting tree into the city of Kuliṅgā, through Abhikāla and across the Ikṣumatī, through the Bāhlīka country toward Mt. Sudāman, past Viṣṇupada and the Vipāśā, to Girivraja (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 68.12–21). The catalogue of **Bharata's return route** runs at 2.71, and together the two are the epic's most detailed piece of north-Indian road geography.
- **Bharata's evil dreams** - That night Bharata dreams of his father, soiled and dishevelled, falling from a mountain-top into a pool of cow-dung, drinking oil from his palms and laughing, then plunging into the oil. He sees the ocean dried up and the moon fallen. He sees his father on an iron seat in black clothes, mocked by dark women, then garlanded in red and driving south in a chariot drawn by donkeys while a red-clad demoness drags him away laughing. A man seen riding a donkey-chariot in a dream, he says, will soon see the smoke of his own pyre (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 69.8–18; CE 2.63.7–15). The red-clad demoness (69.16) is vulgate only; the CE dream has no such figure. The same dream-language of oil, red garlands, donkeys and the southward road returns in Trijaṭā's dream of Rāvaṇa's fall in the [Sundara Kāṇḍa](5-sundara-kanda.md).
- **The truth revealed** - In Ayodhyā Bharata learns from Kaikeyī herself of his father's death, Rāma's exile, and **his own mother's** authorship of it; she tells him of the two boons and urges him to be crowned (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 72).
- **Bharata denounces Kaikeyī** - Bharata recoils in horror, condemns Kaikeyī, and refuses the kingdom she won for him, vowing to bring Rāma back (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 73–74); he then performs Daśaratha's funeral rites on the Sarayū and the twelfth- and thirteenth-day ceremonies (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 76–77).
- **Bharata's self-curse litany** - Before Kausalyā, who half-suspects him, Bharata swears himself clear with a long imprecation-catalogue — if this was done with his knowledge or consent, may he take on the guilt of the man who kills a brahmin, who abandons his master, who kills a cow, who deserts his wife, who sleeps at sunrise, who sells his learning, who lies to a friend, who eats alone, who drinks liquor. This is plain vulgate narrative, not a recension's addition: it is the mechanism by which Bharata's innocence is established in law rather than merely asserted (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 75).
- **Śatrughna and Mantharā** - Śatrughna, enraged, seizes the hunchback **Mantharā** and drags her across the floor, her ornaments scattering, and rounds on Kaikeyī, who takes refuge with Bharata. Bharata stops him: women are not to be killed. But for fear of Rāma's displeasure, he says, he would have killed Kaikeyī himself, and if Rāma heard that Mantharā had been killed he would never speak to either of them again (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 78.16–24).

## Bharata Marches to Chitrakūṭa (sargas 80–100)

- **The road built ahead of the army** - Refusing the crown, Bharata orders a road made for the march (79.13). Before a soldier moves, surveyors, road-makers, tree-fellers and well-diggers level the ground, bridge the streams, dam ponds, sink wells, surface the route and plant it with trees and flowers, and pitch camps along it. The road runs **as far as the Gaṅgā** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 80, esp. 80.21), so the march to fetch a barefoot ascetic goes out along a royal road. The sarga is one of the epic's few detailed pieces of civil engineering.
- **The army to the forest** - Bharata sets out with the queens, ministers, Vasiṣṭha, and a great host — not to enjoy rule but to **bring Rāma back** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 82–83).
- **Guha's armed stand at Śṛṅgaverapura** - Guha does not merely suspect Bharata. Seeing the Kovidāra banner, he **musters his Niṣāda kinsmen in armour on the bank** and puts five hundred boats on alert, each manned by a hundred young fishermen. The army will cross only if Bharata means Rāma well (84.1–9; CE 2.78.7). He then goes to Bharata with **honey, fish and meat**, and offers the army roots, fruits and fresh and dried meat (84.10–18). He takes Bharata's measure that night (85), then tells him how Rāma matted his hair and crossed (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 86).
- **The crossing of the Gaṅgā** - Guha gathers **five hundred boats** from every quarter, crewed by his own fisher-kin, and ferries the host over in relays; horses go in the boats, elephants swim across "like winged mountains" under their mahouts, and the rest cross on rafts, on earthen pots, and by arm (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 89.10–20; CE 2.83.9–19).
- **Bharadvāja's feast** - At Prayāga the sage, after first asking whether Bharata means Rāma harm (90), invokes Viśvakarman and the gods and by ascetic power conjures a **divine banquet** for Bharata's whole army: rivers of wine and of milk-rice, apsarases, food without end. At dawn the rivers, musicians and women go back the way they came (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 91, esp. 91.15, 91.41, 91.82–83; CE 2.85).
- **Bharadvāja absolves Kaikeyī** - Taking leave the next day, Bharata introduces the queens to the sage and presents his own mother as the angry, ignoble cause of the house's ruin (92.18–27). The sage, who knows the whole matter by ascetic sight, answers that **no fault attaches to Kaikeyī**: the exile will end in happiness, and its good will fall to gods, asuras and sages. It is the first of the few absolutions Kaikeyī receives in Vālmīki (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 92.29–30).
- **The Citrakūṭa idyll** - Two sargas of the exiles' life before it is interrupted. Rāma shows Sītā the hill's mineral-streaked crags, its flowering trees, its deer and peacocks, and then the **Mandākinī** — swans and geese on its coloured sand-bars, petals turning on the water, sages at the fords — telling her that with this before him he does not miss the kingdom, and asking her to take the mountain for Ayodhyā, the river for the Sarayū, and the forest creatures for the citizens. They bathe together and pass the day on the bank: the only untroubled happiness the three are given in the whole epic (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 94–95).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's rage from the treetop** - Seeing the dust and hearing the drums of an approaching army, Lakṣmaṇa climbs a flowering sāla and sights Bharata's Kovidāra banner. He comes down blazing: Bharata comes to kill them; he will kill Bharata first, and Kaikeyī and her hunchback with him, and with Bharata dead Rāma will rule the whole earth (96.11–30, esp. 96.24–26). **Rāma rebukes him**: what would he do with a kingdom bought at the price of a brother? Bharata comes in love, not in arms (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 96–97).
- **The brothers' reunion** - At Citrakūṭa, Bharata and Śatrughna fall at Rāma's feet; Rāma, learning of their father's death, faints, and bathes and offers the water-oblation in the Mandākinī (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 99–103).
- **The forest funeral** - The śrāddha Rāma can afford is made of what the forest has: the pulp of the **iṅgudī** mixed with jujube, laid on a mat of kuśa grass as the *piṇḍa*, offered with the line that measures how far the house has fallen — *what a man eats himself, of that his gods partake* (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 103.29–30; CE 2.95.29–30). When the queens come down to the river, Kausalyā finds the iṅgudī cake on the southward-pointing darbha and grieves that a king who ruled the earth should eat that (104.8–12; CE 2.96.5–11).

## The Great Debate and the Sandals (sargas 100–112)

- **The kaccit catechism** - Before a word about the throne, Rāma cross-examines Bharata on the running of the kingdom across some seventy verses, most of the questions opening *kaccit* ("I trust that…"). Does he keep from oversleeping, and spend the last part of the night thinking on policy (100.17)? Does he take counsel neither alone nor with too many, and keep his decisions secret (100.18)? Are the soldiers' rations and wages paid on time, since delay turns servants against their master (100.32–33)? Does he watch the enemy's eighteen and his own fifteen offices through three spies unknown to each other (100.36)? Does he keep away from *lokāyatika* (materialist) brahmins, "skilled in harm, childish, thinking themselves learned" (100.38–39; CE 2.94.31–32), a warning that makes Jābāli's speech eight sargas later pointed? Does he cherish those who live by farming and herding (100.47)? Are disputes between rich and poor judged without bias (100.58)? And does he shun the fourteen faults of kings, atheism first among them (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 100.65–67)? The passage is the Rāmāyaṇa's compact manual of *rājadharma*, a close parallel to Nārada's *kaccit*-catechism of Yudhiṣṭhira in the Mahābhārata (Sabhā Parvan 5).
- **Bharata begs Rāma to return** - Bharata pleads with Rāma to take the throne, declaring himself unwilling to rule; Kausalyā and the sages add their voices (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 101–106).
  - *Note on Kaikeyī:* the popular memory of a **repentant Kaikeyī** at Citrakūṭa is not in Vālmīki. She travels with the party and is present, but her last direct speech in the kāṇḍa is her account to Bharata at 2.72. After it she only takes refuge behind Bharata from Śatrughna (78.20), and is introduced by him to Bharadvāja as the root of the ruin (92.24–27). What Vālmīki gives instead is a series of men who absolve her. Bharadvāja is first (92.29–30). Rāma calls her demand "proper" (111.29), tells Bharata to treat her as his mother (112.19), and at parting charges him to protect her and not be angry with her (112.27–28, vulgate only; the CE keeps 112.19 as CE 2.104.18, and 111.29 as CE 2.103.28). Last, Daśaratha's shade lifts the curse at Rāma's request (Yuddha 119.25–27). The remorse belongs to Tulsīdās: at Citrakūṭa, seeing how guileless the brothers and Sītā are, Kaikeyī repents and begs the earth to open and death to take her (*Rāmcaritmānas*, Ayodhyā 252.3), and Rāma sends her home with her shame and grief dispelled (319). → see [Kaikeyī](../characters/kaikeyi.md). [late text]
- **Rāma refuses for dharma** - Rāma insists he must honour their father's word and complete the full fourteen years; a long discourse on duty, truth, the inevitability of death, and a son's obligation follows (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 101, 105).
- **The rājya-śulka: the kingdom was already promised** - Rāma then gives the argument that reframes the entire quarrel. Long ago, when Daśaratha married Kaikeyī, he pledged the kingdom to her father as *rājya-śulka*, a **bride-price of the throne** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 107.3; CE 2.99.2). The two boons came later, in the war of the gods and demons (107.4–6). Kaikeyī's boons did not steal the kingdom: they called in a debt the king had contracted before Rāma was born. It is the strongest textual footing any sympathetic reading of Kaikeyī has, and Rāma is the one who states it.
- **Jābāli's heterodox argument** - The brahmin **Jābāli** advances a near-materialist (Cārvāka-like) case. A man has no kinsman and is kin to none; he is born alone and dies alone. A father is only the seed of a body. The ancestral offering wastes food on the dead. The injunctions to sacrifice and give were written by clever men to make others give. Therefore let Rāma take the kingdom that is here, and let what lies beyond the senses alone (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 108.3–17). Rāma rejects it fiercely. If he acted so, he says, the world would follow its king into licence; truth is the god of the world and the root of all things, and nothing is higher (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 109.9–13).
  - *Note (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 109.37–39):* Jābāli afterward says he spoke as a non-believer only to persuade Rāma to return, and is no atheist. Commentators on Hindu epistemology have discussed the passage at length, and it is the epic's fullest hearing of the nāstika position from the mouth of a man of the court. In the CE Jābāli does not recant in his own voice: Vasiṣṭha says for him that he spoke only to bring Rāma back (CE 2.101.31). [scholarly]
  - *Interpolation (109.34):* the vulgate gives Rāma a verse that likens the nāstika to a thief and names *buddha* and *tathāgata* (*yathā hi coraḥ sa tathā hi buddhas tathāgataṃ nāstikam atra viddhi*). It is widely read as a late anti-Buddhist addition and cited in dating debates. It has no counterpart in the Critical Edition (no *tathāgata* in CE 2.101, Rāma's reply), and some translators, valmikiramayan.net among them, gloss *buddha* here as a mere rationalist rather than the Buddha. [interpolation]
- **Vasiṣṭha's cosmogony and the law of the eldest son** - Vasiṣṭha argues the point from the beginning of the world. All was water; **Brahmā** arose self-existent within it, took the form of a **boar** and lifted the earth out; from him came Marīci → Kaśyapa → Vivasvat → **Manu** → **Ikṣvāku**, first king of Ayodhyā. From that first king the house has kept one rule: *"In the whole line of Ikṣvāku the eldest son alone becomes king; while the eldest lives, no younger son is made king"* — and Vasiṣṭha charges Rāma not to be the one who breaks an eternal custom of his own race (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 110, esp. 110.35–36). Rāma still declines. The Varāha account here is the Rāmāyaṇa's own creation-myth, and it assigns the boar to **Brahmā**, not to Viṣṇu as the Purāṇas do. → compare [Varāha in the Daśāvatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md). [scholarly: an older stratum than the Purāṇic Varāha]
- **Bharata's hunger strike** - With Rāma unmoved, Bharata has kuśa grass spread before the hut and lies down to fast without food or light until Rāma yields, as a ruined brahmin lies at a debtor's door. Rāma tells him the method is a brahmin's, not a kṣatriya's, and the townsmen side with Rāma (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 111.13–22; CE 2.103.12–20).
- **Bharata's counter-offer: he will serve the exile instead** - Cornered, Bharata makes the proposal the retellings drop: that the sentence be **transferred**. If one of them must stay in the forest to keep their father's word, he will keep the fourteen years there in his brother's place (111.25–26; CE 2.103.25). He had vowed as much in Ayodhyā (79.8) and at the iṅgudī tree where Rāma slept (88.27–28). Rāma refuses: their father's disposal cannot be broken by either of them, a substitute in the forest is unacceptable, and what Kaikeyī asked was proper (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 111.28–29; CE 2.103.27–28).
- **The pādukā (sandals) enthroned** - Bharata, defeated in argument, asks Rāma to step onto his **gold-adorned sandals (pādukā)**, for they will secure the welfare of all. Rāma steps onto them and gives them to Bharata (112.21–22; CE 2.104.20–21). Bharata vows to wear matted hair and bark for fourteen years, rule only as the sandals' deputy, and **enter fire if he does not see Rāma on the day the fourteenth year is complete**. He carries the sandals off on the head of a great elephant (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 112.23–29). Bharata's whole vow, fire-vow included (112.23–25), and Rāma's charge about Kaikeyī (112.27–28) are vulgate only: the CE passes straight from the gift to the elephant (CE 2.104.21–22). [recensional]
  - *Tulsīdās emphasis (Rāmacaritamānasa, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa):* the pādukā episode is rendered with intense bhakti; the sandals are venerated as Rāma's living presence, a paradigm of *prapatti* (surrender) and ideal servant-rule (*rāma-rājya* in trust).

## Bharata's Regency at Nandigrāma (sargas 112–119)

- **The return to Ayodhyā** - Bharata carries the pādukā back, stopping to tell Bharadvāja what passed (113). Entering a desolate city and an empty palace (114), he refuses to rule from the capital (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 113–115).
- **Rule from Nandigrāma** - Bharata installs the sandals on the throne, places the royal canopy and chowries over them, and governs from the village of **Nandigrāma** outside Ayodhyā, himself wearing bark and matted hair, eating ascetic fare — sharing Rāma's exile in spirit (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 115).
- **The sages abandon Citrakūṭa — and Khara is named** - The ascetics of the hill come to Rāma and leave. A demon called **Khara**, they tell him, *Rāvaṇa's younger brother*, has uprooted the ascetics of Janasthāna and will not tolerate Rāma either; the harassment began when Rāma came to live there (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 116.11–13; CE 2.108.10–12). In the vulgate, Rāvaṇa's name has appeared in this kāṇḍa only once before, in the narrator's opening, as the demon the gods wanted killed (2.1.7). The CE lacks that verse, so in the CE this is the book's only mention of him. Either way the war arrives here as local news from frightened neighbours, two kāṇḍas before it is fought.
- **Rāma leaves Citrakūṭa** - With the sages gone, Rāma finds no pleasure in the place. It is where Bharata and his mothers came to him, and the memory haunts him, and the dried dung of Bharata's horses and elephants has fouled the ground. He moves on and comes to the hermitage of **Atri and Anasūyā** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 117.1–5).
- **Anasūyā honors Sītā** - The chaste sage-wife **Anasūyā** instructs Sītā in wifely dharma (*pātivratya*) and gifts her divine unfading garments, ornaments, and cosmetics (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 117–118). → the fuller treatment is at [Atri and Anasūyā](../atri-anasuya.md).
- **Sītā tells her own story** - Asked by Anasūyā how she was married, Sītā answers in her own voice and at length. She tells of Janaka ploughing the sacrificial ground and her rising from the furrow, covered in dust; of the voice from the sky, and the king's anxiety at a daughter not born of a womb; of the bow and the kings who could not lift it, Rāma's coming with Viśvāmitra, and the breaking. Rāma would not accept her until his father consented, and Ūrmilā was given to Lakṣmaṇa (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 118.27–54). It is the epic's **only first-person narration of her birth from the furrow**, and the events of the Bāla Kāṇḍa reach us a second time in the voice of the woman they were about.
  - *Doublet — whose bow?* In Sītā's telling, Janaka's great bow and its two inexhaustible quivers were a gift from **Varuṇa** at a great sacrifice (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 118.39; also in the CE, 2.110.37). The Bāla Kāṇḍa makes it **Rudra's** bow, used at Dakṣa's sacrifice and left in trust with Janaka's ancestor Devarāta (Bāla 66.8–12). Both recensions keep both provenances. [internal doublet]
- **Toward Daṇḍaka** - The kāṇḍa ends as Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa enter the deep **Daṇḍaka forest**, opening the demon-haunted action of the Āraṇya Kāṇḍa (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 119). → continues in the [Āraṇya Kāṇḍa](3-aranya-kanda.md).

## Major Variants Across Traditions

- *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Sanskrit, base text):* the human-tragedy framing above; Rāma's perfect obedience and Bharata's spotless renunciation are the moral core.
- *Tulsīdās, [Rāmacaritamānasa](recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) (Avadhi, 16th c.) [late text]:* heavy bhakti colouring. The gods send Sarasvatī to turn Mantharā's mind, so the plot becomes a divine necessity rather than a maid's malice (Ayodhyā 11–12). The famous couplet on Raghu-kula truth (*raghukula rīti sadā cali āī…*, 28.2). The boatman **Kevaṭ**, who will not ferry Rāma until he has washed his feet, in case the dust that turned a stone into Ahalyā turns his wooden boat into a woman too (Ayodhyā 100–102); in Vālmīki, Guha simply has a boat brought (52). The worship of the pādukā. Kaikeyī's remorse at Citrakūṭa (252.3).
- *Kamban, [Irāmāvatāram](recensions/kamban-iramavataram.md) (Tamil) [late text]:* recasts the emotional psychology of Kaikeyī, Daśaratha, and Bharata with distinctive lyric intensity; Bharata's grief and devotion expanded.
- *[Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](recensions/adhyatma-ramayana.md) (within Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa) [late text]:* Vedāntic/advaitic frame — Rāma is the supreme Brahman knowingly enacting a līlā; Sītā is often Māyā; the exile is a cosmic drama rather than mere misfortune.
- *[Krittivāsi Rāmāyaṇ](recensions/krittivasi-ramayan.md) (Bengali) [late text]:* devotional/regional retelling; alternate boon-backstory (the pus-sucking story above, [unverified] against the Bengali text) and emotive expansions of the queens' grief.
- *Buddhist [Dasaratha Jātaka](recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md) (Jātaka no. 461) [scholarly]:* a strikingly different early version. King Dasaratha of **Benares** has two sons, Rāma-paṇḍita and Lakkhaṇa, and a daughter, **Sītā**, by his first queen. When the second queen keeps demanding the kingdom for her son Bharata, the king sends his elder children away *to protect them* from her plotting. Told he has twelve years to live, he tells them to return then; Sītā goes with her brothers to the Himālaya. The king dies in the ninth year, and Bharata comes to fetch Rāma, who neither weeps nor grieves and teaches impermanence. Keeping his father's term, Rāma stays three more years and gives Bharata his **straw slippers**, which rule in his place, striking together when a case is wrongly judged. On his return Sītā, his sister, is made his queen. The frame identifies Rāma with the Buddha and Sītā with Rāhula's mother (tr. W. H. D. Rouse, 1895). Often cited in dating and transmission debates.
- *Kaikeyī's rehabilitation [scholarly]:* sympathetic readings have more footing in Vālmīki than is usually allowed: the throne pledged as her bride-price (107.3), Bharadvāja's absolution (92.29–30), Rāma's "what Kaikeyī asked was proper" (111.29), and Daśaratha's withdrawal of the curse (Yuddha 119.25–27). The reading of her as an instrument of the gods is voiced most famously in Tulsīdās's Sarasvatī frame (*Rāmcaritmānas*, Ayodhyā 11–12). → see [Kaikeyī](../characters/kaikeyi.md).

## Themes and Doctrinal Hooks

- **Dharma-saṃkaṭa** - The kāṇḍa is the epic's great study of conflicting duties — filial obedience vs. kingship vs. truth — central to Hindu ethics; links to [puruṣārthas](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/) and the dharma debates of the [Bhagavad Gītā](../). 
- **Satya (truthfulness) as supreme** - Daśaratha dies rather than break his word; the Ikṣvāku truth-ideal echoes the [Sūryavaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) ancestor-kings.
- **Karma and the curse** - Daśaratha's death realizes the blind ascetic's curse, laid on him for the son he killed by sound on the Sarayū — a textbook illustration of karmic ripening across time.
- **Rājadharma** - The *kaccit* catechism (2.100) and the *arājaka* hymn (2.67) sit on either side of Rāma's refusal, and between them state the epic's whole political theory: the kingdom cannot be left without a king, and it cannot be taken by a man who would break his father's word to have it.
- **Ideal servant-rule (pādukā / rāma-rājya in trust)** - Bharata's regency models renunciatory, custodial kingship — a recurring Hindu political ideal.
