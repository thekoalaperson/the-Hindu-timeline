# Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa — Book of Ayodhya

> Cosmic address: [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) ← but the Rāmāyaṇa itself unfolds in the [Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) of the current Vaivasvata Manvantara. Rāma is the 7th [Daśāvatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) of [Viṣṇu](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md), of the [Sūryavaṃśa / Ikṣvāku line](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
> Parent: [Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive index](README.md) · Part of [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Follows [Bāla Kāṇḍa](1-bala-kanda.md). Primary source: Vālmīki [Rāmāyaṇa](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md).

The **Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa** ("Book of Ayodhyā") is the second of the seven kāṇḍas of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa — **119 sargas** in the vulgate / Southern recension, the numbering used for every citation on this page; the Baroda Critical Edition constitutes the same book in **111**, and the two do not correspond sarga for sarga. It is the great human-drama and *dharma-saṃkaṭa* (dilemma-of-duty) book: in the span of two days the joy of Rāma's announced coronation collapses into his fourteen-year exile, Daśaratha dies of grief, and Bharata, refusing the ill-gotten throne, installs Rāma's sandals as sovereign and rules as regent from Nandigrāma. The kāṇḍa contains no demon-combat — its battlefield is the conscience.

## The Coronation Announced (sargas 1–7)

- **Rāma's qualities and Daśaratha's resolve** - The aged Daśaratha, beloved of his people, resolves to install **Rāma** as *yuvarāja* (crown prince/heir-apparent) while he himself still lives; an assembly of citizens and feudatories acclaims the choice (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 1–2).
- **The omens of haste** - Daśaratha urges Vasiṣṭha to fix the consecration for the very next morning under Puṣya nakṣatra, sensing inauspicious portents (meteors, bad dreams) and his own waning years (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 3–4).
- **Bharata and Śatrughna absent** - Crucially, **Bharata and Śatrughna are away** at the home of Bharata's maternal uncle Yudhājit (Kekaya), and Daśaratha says plainly why he wants the rite held now: *"while Bharata is away from the city — that, in my judgement, is the fit time for your consecration."* He adds that Bharata is righteous but that the minds of men are unsteady and may turn. The suspicion the retellings put in the courtiers' mouths is the king's own stated reasoning (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 4.25–27; cf. 1, 67).
- **Rāma instructed in vows** - Rāma and Sītā are told to observe fast and lie on darbha grass through the night before the consecration; the city is decked for festival (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 6–7).

## Mantharā and Kaikeyī (sargas 7–13)

- **Mantharā sees the festivities** - **Mantharā**, the hunchbacked maidservant Kaikeyī brought from Kekaya, learns of the coronation from a nurse and rushes to her mistress in fury (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 7–8).
- **The poisoning of Kaikeyī's mind** - Mantharā argues that with Rāma crowned, Kausalyā will dominate, Kaikeyī will be reduced to a servant, and **Bharata will be killed or exiled** as a rival; she rejects Kaikeyī's first, generous reaction (Kaikeyī had rewarded the news-bearer) (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 8–9).
- **The plan: invoke the old boons** - Mantharā reminds Kaikeyī of two boons Daśaratha once promised her, and coaches the demands: **(1) Bharata crowned, (2) Rāma exiled to the Daṇḍaka forest for fourteen years** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 9–11).
  - *Where Mantharā's knowledge comes from:* the text closes the loop itself — "I do not know what happened there; **you yourself told me** this story long ago" (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 9.18–19). The weapon is one Kaikeyī handed her maid in an unguarded confidence.
- **The "anger chamber" (krodhāgāra)** - Kaikeyī strips off her ornaments, dons soiled garments, and casts herself on the floor of the *kopa-bhavana* / *krodhāgāra* (sulking-house) to await the king (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 9–10).

### The two boons: the battlefield backstory (variants)

The boons stem from a past war in which Kaikeyī saved Daśaratha's life. The texts disagree on the mechanism:

- *Vālmīki (Ayodhyā 9.16):* in the war of the gods and asuras, Daśaratha fought for **Indra against Śambara** at Vaijayanta and was **twice** brought back alive by Kaikeyī — once carried unconscious out of the field, and again when he was struck down by the demons' weapons. **Two rescues, two boons**: the arithmetic of the boons is the arithmetic of the rescues, which is why there are exactly two and why either alone would not have sufficed.
- *Variant — chariot pin/axle (Pauranic, e.g. Brahma Purāṇa tradition):* When the **axle-pin fell out (or the axle broke)** mid-battle, Kaikeyī thrust her **finger/hand into the hole** to hold the wheel, suffering injury; Daśaratha rewarded her with two boons. [folk/Pauranic embellishment]
- *Variant — Telugu Dvipada Rāmāyaṇa:* Kaikeyī out-does Śambara in his own art of **illusion (māyā)**, a warrior-sorceress in her own right.
- *Variant — Kṛttivāsa (Bengali):* Kaikeyī earns the **first** boon in the Śambara war and the **second** later by **sucking pus from Daśaratha's battle-wound**.

## Daśaratha Trapped by His Word (sargas 10–13)

- **The king's grief and pleading** - Finding Kaikeyī prostrate, the doting Daśaratha swears by Rāma to grant whatever she asks; she binds him to the oath before naming her price (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 10–11).
- **The demands spoken** - Kaikeyī claims her two boons: **Bharata to be consecrated**, and **Rāma to wear bark and matted hair and dwell in Daṇḍaka forest fourteen years** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 11).
- **Daśaratha's collapse** - The king faints, rages, begs, and reasons through the night; Kaikeyī is immovable, taunting him with the duty of the truthful Ikṣvākus (cf. ancestor **Śaibya/ Hariścandra**-type truth-ideal) (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 12–13).
- **The Truth-of-the-Raghus** - Daśaratha is destroyed not by Kaikeyī's power but by the dynastic vow **"Raghus do not break their word"** (*raghukula-rīti... prāṇa jāhiṁ baru bacanu na jāī*, famously in Tulsīdās); he cannot revoke a granted boon (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 12–13; Tulsīdās, *Rāmacaritamānasa*, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa).

## Rāma Accepts Exile (sargas 14–19)

- **Sumantra summons Rāma** - At dawn the charioteer-minister **Sumantra** finds the king broken; Kaikeyī herself sends for Rāma (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 14–16).
- **Kaikeyī delivers the sentence** - Because Daśaratha cannot speak, **Kaikeyī announces** the boons; Rāma accepts instantly, untroubled, for a father's word is dharma (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 16–19).
- **Rāma's equanimity** - Rāma shows no anger toward Kaikeyī or his father, asking only that they be cared for; this serene obedience is the kāṇḍa's ethical centerpiece (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 18–19).

## The Three Companions Insist (sargas 20–31)

- **Kausalyā's lament** - Rāma's mother **Kausalyā** grieves and at first would forbid him; Rāma reconciles her to his duty and asks her blessing (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 20–21, 24).
- **Kausalyā's benediction-rite** - Reconciled, she performs a full *svastyayana* over her son before he goes — sipping water, kindling the fire with offerings of curds, ghee, white mustard and darbha, muttering the protective mantras, binding a herb-amulet on his hand, and invoking by name every quarter-guardian, mountain, ocean, and the gods of both the Vedas and the epic to keep him in the forest (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 25). It is the longest blessing any mother gives in the epic, and the only armour Rāma leaves with.
- **Lakṣmaṇa's fury** - **Lakṣmaṇa** rages, urging armed resistance and seizure of the throne; Rāma calms him, insisting on submission to dharma and to their father's truth (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 21–23).
- **Sītā insists on accompanying him** - **Sītā** refuses to stay, declaring a wife's place is at her husband's side; she answers Rāma's warnings of forest hardship with vows of devotion and threatens to die if left behind (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 26–30).
- **Lakṣmaṇa granted leave to follow** - Lakṣmaṇa begs and is permitted to attend Rāma and Sītā as servant-protector in exile (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 31).
- **The Vāruṇa weapons taken to the forest** - Rāma sends Lakṣmaṇa to fetch the **weapons Varuṇa gave at Janaka's sacrifice** — the bows, the inexhaustible quivers, the armour and the swords — deposited since the wedding in the house of their preceptor. Two men going out to live on roots and fruit arm themselves first with divine weaponry: the exile is a banishment on its face and a campaign in its equipment (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 31).
- **Giving away wealth** - Before leaving, Rāma distributes his possessions to brahmins, dependents, and the poor (e.g. to the brahmin **Trijaṭa**) (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 32).

## Departure from Ayodhyā (sargas 32–42)

- **Bark garments** - Kaikeyī produces bark/tree-fiber robes (*valkala / cīra*); Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa don them. Sītā fumbles donning hers over her silks (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 37).
  - *Variant (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 37):* Vasiṣṭha intervenes, rebuking Kaikeyī and ruling that **Sītā need not wear bark**; she may go robed and ornamented — the boon bound only Rāma.
- **The city's revolt of grief** - The citizens of Ayodhyā wail and resolve to follow Rāma into the forest, abandoning the city to Kaikeyī (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 33, 36).
- **Daśaratha's curse-memory foreshadowed** - The king, dying inside, orders his treasury and army to escort Rāma; Kaikeyī objects that Bharata should inherit a full kingdom, not an emptied one (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 34–37).
- **Sumantra drives them out** - Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa mount Sumantra's chariot; the people and the king run after it; Rāma bids Sumantra drive faster to spare them (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 39–40).
- **Sumitrā's charge to Lakṣmaṇa** - Alone among the mothers, **Sumitrā** sends her son away without a word of protest, giving instead the line that defines him: *"Know Rāma to be Daśaratha, know Sītā to be me, know the forest to be Ayodhyā — go, my child, and be happy"* (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 40.9).
- **Daśaratha repudiates Kaikeyī** - Returning to the palace, the king formally casts his queen off: *"Do not touch my limbs. I do not wish to look at you. You are neither wife nor kin of mine"* — and renounces, in this world and the next, the marriage-contract he made taking her hand around the fire. He goes further and refuses his own funeral: if Bharata is glad of the kingdom, let no death-offering made to him by Bharata reach him (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 42.6–9). The repudiation stands until Daśaratha withdraws it in person, from the sky, at Yuddha 119.

## The Forest Road: Tamasā to the Ganges (sargas 43–53)

- **First night at the Tamasā** - The three camp on the **Tamasā** river; Rāma slips away before dawn to elude the citizens still following (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 45–46).
- **Through Kosala to the Vedaśrutī, Gomatī, Syandikā** - They cross the southern Kosala countryside and several rivers, Rāma pointing out the land he is leaving (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 49).
- **Guha of Śṛṅgaverapura** - At the Ganges they are received by **Guha**, chief of the Niṣādas (a tribal hunter-king) and Rāma's friend, who offers hospitality and lodging (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 50).
- **Matted hair (jaṭā)** - On the morning of the crossing Rāma asks Guha for the **milky latex of a banyan (vaṭa-kṣīra / nyagrodha)** and mats his own and Lakṣmaṇa's hair with it into ascetic locks (*jaṭā*); bark-clad and matted, the two now look like sages (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 52.67–70).
- **Sītā's vow to the Gaṅgā** - As they push off, Sītā prays to the goddess with folded hands and names her price of thanksgiving for a safe return: a hundred thousand cows, fine cloth and food to the brahmins, **a thousand jars of surā (spirituous liquor) and jellied meat with cooked rice**, and worship of every shrine and sacred spot along the banks (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 52.88–90). At the next crossing she vows the like to the **Yamunā** — a thousand cows and a hundred jars of surā (2.55.19–20). Both are plain Vālmīki, and both sit awkwardly against the later vegetarian and teetotal readings of Sītā — the corpus records the text, not the reception.
- **Crossing the Ganges; Sumantra turned back** - With Guha's boat they cross the Ganges; Rāma sends the weeping **Sumantra back to Ayodhyā**, refusing to keep the chariot and charging him to console Daśaratha (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 51–52).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's night-watch lament** - On the far bank Lakṣmaṇa keeps vigil, grieving aloud over their fall from royalty (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 53).

## Bharadvāja and Chitrakūṭa (sargas 54–56)

- **Sage Bharadvāja's hermitage** - At the Gaṅgā–Yamunā confluence (Prayāga) they reach **Bharadvāja**, who advises them to settle at **Chitrakūṭa** for its beauty and seclusion (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 54–55).
- **Crossing the Yamunā; the offering to the fig tree** - They raft across the Yamunā; Sītā prays to a great fig (Śyāma) tree for safe return (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 55).
- **Vālmīki met on the road** - Before they climb to Citrakūṭa the three come with folded palms to the hermitage of the sage **Vālmīki** himself and salute him; he welcomes them. This is plain vulgate text, not a variant reading — the poet walks into his own poem here, and will take Sītā in at the end of it (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 56.16).
- **The leaf hut at Chitrakūṭa** - They settle on Citrakūṭa hill beside the river the text here names **Mālyavatī**, not Mandākinī: the popular name for the Citrakūṭa river enters the narrative only later, from Bharadvāja's directions and the idyll sargas onward (2.92–95), and Vālmīki never reconciles the two names. Lakṣmaṇa builds a leaf-and-timber hermitage (*parṇaśālā*) of branches, bamboo and thatched leaves (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 56.38). [scholarly: an unresolved doublet in the text's own geography]
- **The house-warming with a black antelope** - Before entering, Rāma has Lakṣmaṇa kill a **black antelope**, roast it, and offer it to the deities of the dwelling with the prescribed mantras — a *vāstu-śānti*, the ordinary kṣatriya house-entry rite performed on a hut of leaves (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 56.22–26).

## Sumantra's Return and Daśaratha's Death (sargas 57–77)

- **Sumantra reports** - The charioteer returns to Ayodhyā and relays Rāma's words; the city and Daśaratha are crushed anew (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 57–59).
- **The horses that would not move** - The chariot-horses are described as having shed tears and balked, an omen of doom (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 59–60).
- **Daśaratha confesses the killing of the ascetic's son** - In Kausalyā's chamber the dying king recalls his youthful crime. Hunting by sound (*śabda-vedhī*) on the bank of the **Sarayū** in the rains, he loosed at what he took for an elephant drinking and struck a boy filling a water-pot for his blind ascetic parents. Dying, the boy stops the king's self-reproach with a point of law: *"I am no brahmin — do not let that fear trouble you; I was born of a **śūdrā** mother by a **vaiśya** father"*, so no brahmahatyā attaches (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 63.21, 63.53). The blind father, told of it, curses Daśaratha to **die likewise of grief for a son** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 64).
  - *Naming variant:* **Vālmīki leaves the boy unnamed throughout** — he is only "the ascetic's son." The name **Śravaṇa (Śravaṇa-kumāra)**, and the names of his parents (e.g. **Śāntanu and Jñānavatī**), belong to later tradition and to the Purāṇic and folk retellings. [folk tradition]
- **Death of Daśaratha** - At midnight, calling "Rāma! Rāma! Lakṣmaṇa! Sītā!", Daśaratha dies of grief — the curse fulfilled (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 64).
- **The body preserved** - With no son present to perform rites, the queens and ministers place the king's body in a vat of oil (*taila-droṇī*) to await Bharata (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 66).

## Bharata Recalled and His Refusal (sargas 67–83)

- **The hymn on kinglessness (arājaka)** - With the king dead and no heir on the ground, the assembled brahmins and ministers press Vasiṣṭha with the epic's set-piece on **anarchy**: in a land without a king the thunder brings no rain, the son does not obey the father nor the wife the husband, no man owns property or wife, merchants do not travel, ascetics do not walk safely, gardens and sacrifices fail, and *"as a river without water, a forest without grass, a herd without a herdsman, so is a kingdom without a king"* (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 67). It is the political argument the whole kāṇḍa is written against — and Rāma will refuse it anyway.
- **Messengers to Kekaya** - Ministers led by Vasiṣṭha send swift envoys to fetch **Bharata**, telling him only to come urgently, not why; the sarga tracks their ride city by city and river by river — Aparatāla, Pralamba, the Mālinī, across the Gaṅgā at Hastināpura, through Kurukṣetra, the Śaradaṇḍā, Kuliṅgā, Abhikāla, the Ikṣumatī, the Bāhlīka country and Mt. Sudāman to Girivraja (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 68). The corresponding catalogue of **Bharata's return route** runs at 2.71, and the two together are the epic's most detailed piece of north-Indian road geography.
- **Bharata's evil dreams** - Bharata, troubled by nightmares (of his father falling, oil, a shaved-headed figure), hurries home (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 69).
- **The truth revealed** - In Ayodhyā Bharata learns of his father's death, Rāma's exile, and **his own mother's** authorship of it (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 70–72).
- **Bharata denounces Kaikeyī** - Bharata recoils in horror, condemns Kaikeyī, disowns the throne, and refuses the kingdom she won for him; he performs Daśaratha's funeral rites (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 73–77).
- **Bharata's self-curse litany** - Before Kausalyā, who half-suspects him, Bharata swears himself clear with a long imprecation-catalogue — if this was done with his knowledge or consent, may he take on the guilt of the man who kills a brahmin, who abandons his master, who kills a cow, who deserts his wife, who sleeps at sunrise, who sells his learning, who lies to a friend, who eats alone, who drinks liquor. This is plain vulgate narrative, not a recension's addition: it is the mechanism by which Bharata's innocence is established in law rather than merely asserted (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 75).
- **Śatrughna and Mantharā** - Śatrughna, enraged, seizes and beats the hunchback **Mantharā**; Bharata stops short of letting her be killed (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 78).

## Bharata Marches to Chitrakūṭa (sargas 80–100)

- **The road built ahead of the army** - Before a soldier moves, Bharata orders a **highway cut to Citrakūṭa**: surveyors, well-diggers, tree-fellers and bridge-builders level the ground, span the rivers, sink wells and tanks, plaster and flag the route and plant it with trees and flowering creepers, so that the march to fetch a barefoot ascetic goes out along a royal road (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 80). The sarga is one of the epic's few pieces of civil engineering in detail.
- **The army to the forest** - Bharata sets out with the queens, ministers, Vasiṣṭha, and a great host — not to enjoy rule but to **bring Rāma back** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 83–84).
- **Guha's armed stand at Śṛṅgaverapura** - Guha does not merely suspect Bharata; he **musters his Niṣāda kinsmen on the bank**, puts five hundred boats and their crews on alert, and declares that if this army means Rāma harm the Gaṅgā will be held against it. Only when Bharata's grief convinces him does he stand down — and then welcome the host with the hospitality of his own people, **honey, roots, fruit and meat** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 84–85). He then guides the way and reports how Rāma matted his hair and crossed (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 86).
- **The crossing of the Gaṅgā** - Guha gathers **five hundred boats** from every quarter, crewed by his own fisher-kin, and ferries the host over in relays; horses go in the boats, elephants swim across "like winged mountains" under their mahouts, and the rest cross on rafts, on inverted earthen pots, and by arm (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 89.10–20).
- **Bharadvāja's feast** - At Prayāga the sage, by ascetic power, conjures a miraculous **divine banquet** — Viśvakarman-built houses, rivers running with wine and payasa, celestial nymphs, food without end — for Bharata's whole army, and by dawn every trace of it has vanished (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 90–91).
- **Bharadvāja absolves Kaikeyī** - Taking leave the next day, Bharata bitterly blames his mother for the ruin of the house, and the sage — who knows the whole matter by ascetic sight — answers that **no fault attaches to Kaikeyī**: the exile will end in happiness, and the good of it will fall to gods and sages. It is the only absolution Kaikeyī receives from anyone in Vālmīki (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 92.29–30).
- **The Citrakūṭa idyll** - Two sargas of the exiles' life before it is interrupted. Rāma shows Sītā the hill's mineral-streaked crags, its flowering trees, its deer and peacocks, and then the **Mandākinī** — swans and geese on its coloured sand-bars, petals turning on the water, sages at the fords — telling her that with this before him he does not miss the kingdom, and asking her to take the mountain for Ayodhyā, the river for the Sarayū, and the forest creatures for the citizens. They bathe together and pass the day on the bank: the only untroubled happiness the three are given in the whole epic (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 94–95).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's rage from the treetop** - Seeing the dust and hearing the drums of an approaching army, Lakṣmaṇa climbs a sāla, sights Bharata's banner, and comes down blazing: Bharata comes to kill them, he will kill Bharata first, he will hold the kingdom by the bow this day. **Rāma rebukes him** — what would he do with a kingdom bought at the price of a brother, and Bharata comes in love, not in arms (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 96–97).
- **The brothers' reunion** - At Citrakūṭa, Bharata and Śatrughna fall at Rāma's feet; Rāma, learning of their father's death, faints, and bathes and offers the water-oblation in the Mandākinī (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 99–103).
- **The forest funeral** - The śrāddha Rāma can afford is made of what the forest has: the pulp of the **iṅgudī** mixed with jujube, laid on a mat of kuśa grass as the *piṇḍa*, offered with the line that measures how far the house has fallen — *what a man eats himself, of that his gods partake* (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 103.29–30).

## The Great Debate and the Sandals (sargas 100–112)

- **The kaccit catechism** - Before a word about the throne, Rāma cross-examines Bharata on the running of the kingdom in a hundred questions each opening *kaccit* — "I trust that…": are the treasury and army in good order, are the ministers few and tested and never let to speak singly on state business, is the astrologer honoured, are the fourteen faults of a king avoided and the eight-fold policy kept, is the soldier paid on time, is the farmer's tax light, are the mines worked, the dams kept, the widows and the sick and the beasts provided for, does he sleep in the last watch and wake in the third, and does he never dismiss a suppliant with a hard word (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 100). The passage is the Rāmāyaṇa's compact manual of *rājadharma*, and the model for the *kaccit* sections of the Mahābhārata.
- **Bharata begs Rāma to return** - Bharata pleads with Rāma to take the throne, declaring himself unwilling to rule; Kausalyā and the sages add their voices (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 100–105).
  - *Note on Kaikeyī:* the popular memory of a **repentant Kaikeyī** at Citrakūṭa is not in Vālmīki. She travels with the party and is present, but after her last exchange with Bharata at 2.73 the text gives her no scene of remorse, no plea, and effectively no further speech — she goes silent for the rest of the epic. The repentance is supplied by Tulsīdās and by the modern retellings. [late text / popular tradition]
- **Rāma refuses for dharma** - Rāma insists he must honor their father's word and complete the full fourteen years; a long discourse on duty, truth, and a son's obligation follows (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 101–105).
- **Bharata's counter-offer: he will serve the exile instead** - Cornered, Bharata makes the proposal the retellings drop — that the sentence be **transferred**: let Rāma go back and be consecrated, and Bharata himself will wear the bark and keep the fourteen years in the forest in his brother's place. Rāma refuses: a father's allotment is not a debt one man may pay for another (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 105–106).
- **The rājya-śulka: the kingdom was already promised** - Rāma then gives the argument that reframes the entire quarrel. Long ago, when Daśaratha married Kaikeyī, he pledged the kingdom to her father as *rājya-śulka* — the **bride-price of the throne**, the succession covenanted to her son at the wedding. Kaikeyī's boons did not steal the kingdom; they called in a debt the king had contracted before Rāma was born (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 107.3). It is the strongest textual footing any sympathetic reading of Kaikeyī has, and Rāma is the one who states it.
- **Jābāli's heterodox argument** - The minister-sage **Jābāli** advances a near-materialist (Cārvāka-like) case: a man has no kinsman and is kin to none, he comes alone and goes alone; a father is only the occasion of a body; the śrāddha wastes food on the dead; the injunctions of the ritual books were written by clever men to make others give — therefore let Rāma take the kingdom, which is here, and let the next world alone. Rāma rejects it fiercely, saying that if he acted so he would license the whole world to act so, and that a king without truth is a well with a covered mouth (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 108–109).
  - *Note (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 109):* Jābāli afterward clarifies he argued only to persuade Rāma, not from real disbelief — a passage much discussed by commentators on Hindu epistemology, and the epic's fullest hearing of the nāstika position from the mouth of a man of the court. [scholarly]
- **Vasiṣṭha's cosmogony and the law of the eldest son** - Vasiṣṭha argues the point from the beginning of the world. All was water; **Brahmā** arose self-existent within it, took the form of a **boar** and lifted the earth out; from him came Marīci → Kaśyapa → Vivasvat → **Manu** → **Ikṣvāku**, first king of Ayodhyā. From that first king the house has kept one rule: *"In the whole line of Ikṣvāku the eldest son alone becomes king; while the eldest lives, no younger son is made king"* — and Vasiṣṭha charges Rāma not to be the one who breaks an eternal custom of his own race (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 110, esp. 110.35–36). Rāma still declines. The Varāha account here is the Rāmāyaṇa's own creation-myth, and it assigns the boar to **Brahmā**, not to Viṣṇu as the Purāṇas do. → compare [Varāha in the Daśāvatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md). [scholarly: an older stratum than the Purāṇic Varāha]
- **The pādukā (sandals) enthroned** - Bharata, defeated in argument, asks for Rāma's **gold-adorned wooden sandals (pādukā)**; Rāma steps into them and gives them to Bharata as tokens of sovereignty. Bharata vows to **enthrone the sandals**, rule only as their deputy, live as an ascetic, and **enter fire if Rāma does not return on the very day the fourteen years end** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 112).
  - *Tulsīdās emphasis (Rāmacaritamānasa, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa):* the pādukā episode is rendered with intense bhakti; the sandals are venerated as Rāma's living presence, a paradigm of *prapatti* (surrender) and ideal servant-rule (*rāma-rājya* in trust).

## Bharata's Regency at Nandigrāma (sargas 112–119)

- **The return to Ayodhyā** - Bharata carries the pādukā back, the army following; he refuses to enter the capital or palace (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 112–115).
- **Rule from Nandigrāma** - Bharata installs the sandals on the throne, places the royal canopy and chowries over them, and governs from the village of **Nandigrāma** outside Ayodhyā, himself wearing bark and matted hair, eating ascetic fare — sharing Rāma's exile in spirit (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 115).
- **The sages abandon Citrakūṭa — and Khara is named** - The ascetics of the hill come to Rāma and leave: a demon called **Khara**, they tell him, *Rāvaṇa's younger brother*, has cleared the ascetics out of Janasthāna and will not tolerate Rāma's presence either. This is the **first time the antagonist's house is named in the epic's forward narrative** — the war arrives as a piece of local news from frightened neighbours, two kāṇḍas before it is fought (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 116.11–12).
- **Rāma leaves Citrakūṭa** - Rāma does not follow the sages out at once; but with the hermitage emptied, the ground unsettled by Bharata's visit, and the whole country now knowing where he lives, he resolves to move deeper in, and departs for the hermitage of **Atri and Anasūyā** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 116–117).
- **Anasūyā honors Sītā** - The chaste sage-wife **Anasūyā** instructs Sītā in wifely dharma (*pātivratya*) and gifts her divine unfading garments, ornaments, and cosmetics (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 117–118). → the fuller treatment is at [Atri and Anasūyā](../atri-anasuya.md).
- **Sītā tells her own story** - Asked by Anasūyā how she was married, Sītā answers in her own voice and at length — Janaka's ploughing of the sacrificial ground and her rising from the furrow, her being raised as a king's daughter, the bow set as her *vīrya-śulka* and the kings who could not lift it, Rāma's coming with Viśvāmitra, the breaking, the four weddings, Kaikeyī's boons, and her own insistence on the forest. It is the epic's **only first-person narration of Sītā's origin**, and the events of the Bāla Kāṇḍa reach us here a second time in the voice of the woman they were about (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 118).
- **Toward Daṇḍaka** - The kāṇḍa ends as Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa enter the deep **Daṇḍaka forest**, opening the demon-haunted action of the Āraṇya Kāṇḍa (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 119). → continues in the [Āraṇya Kāṇḍa](3-aranya-kanda.md).

## Major Variants Across Traditions

- *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Sanskrit, base text):* the human-tragedy framing above; Rāma's perfect obedience and Bharata's spotless renunciation are the moral core.
- *Tulsīdās, Rāmacaritamānasa (Avadhi, 16th c.) [late text]:* heavy bhakti coloring; famed couplets on Raghu-kula truth (*raghukula rīti sadā cali āī...*) and the worship of the pādukā; Kaikeyī's fall and repentance dramatized at length.
- *Kamban, Irāmāvatāram (Tamil) [late text]:* recasts the emotional psychology of Kaikeyī, Daśaratha, and Bharata with distinctive lyric intensity; Bharata's grief and devotion expanded.
- *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa (within Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa) [late text]:* Vedāntic/advaitic frame — Rāma is the supreme Brahman knowingly enacting a līlā; Sītā is often Māyā; the exile is a cosmic drama rather than mere misfortune.
- *Krittivāsi Rāmāyaṇ (Bengali) [late text]:* devotional/regional retelling; alternate boon-backstory (pus-sucking, see above) and emotive expansions of the queens' grief.
- *Buddhist Dasaratha Jātaka [scholarly]:* a strikingly different early version — Rāma (Bodhisattva) is sent away by his father to *protect* him from a scheming junior queen (not banished by her demand); on return the king is already dead; emphasizes equanimity over grief. Often cited in dating/transmission debates.
- *Regional Kaikeyī rehabilitations [folk/literary]:* several modern and folk readings (and works like Mallika Sengupta / contemporary novels, and older Telugu/Maithili songs) portray Kaikeyī as a knowing agent of the divine plan who accepts eternal blame so the avatāra's mission can proceed. [scholarly/folk]

## Themes and Doctrinal Hooks

- **Dharma-saṃkaṭa** - The kāṇḍa is the epic's great study of conflicting duties — filial obedience vs. kingship vs. truth — central to Hindu ethics; links to [puruṣārthas](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/) and the dharma debates of the [Bhagavad Gītā](../). 
- **Satya (truthfulness) as supreme** - Daśaratha dies rather than break his word; the Ikṣvāku truth-ideal echoes the [Sūryavaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) ancestor-kings.
- **Karma and the curse** - Daśaratha's death realizes the blind ascetic's curse, laid on him for the son he killed by sound on the Sarayū — a textbook illustration of karmic ripening across time.
- **Rājadharma** - The *kaccit* catechism (2.100) and the *arājaka* hymn (2.67) sit on either side of Rāma's refusal, and between them state the epic's whole political theory: the kingdom cannot be left without a king, and it cannot be taken by a man who would break his father's word to have it.
- **Ideal servant-rule (pādukā / rāma-rājya in trust)** - Bharata's regency models renunciatory, custodial kingship — a recurring Hindu political ideal.
