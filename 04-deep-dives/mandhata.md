# Māndhātṛ — the Archetypal Cakravartin "Born from His Father's Side"

> Where this sits: a deep-dive into **Māndhātṛ (Māndhātā)**, the paradigmatic universal-emperor of the Ikṣvāku / [Sūryavaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md), the king whose just *digvijaya* rule became the proverbial yardstick of dharmic kingship ("since the days of Māndhātā"). Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Cosmic slot: Śveta-Varāha Kalpa › 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara › 28th Mahāyuga, the deep dynastic past of the Solar line — many generations above Triśaṅku, Hariścandra, and Rāma.

**Māndhātṛ** (also **Māndhātā**, son of **Yuvanāśva** of the Ikṣvāku line) is Hindu tradition's standard-issue *cakravartin* — the world-emperor whose name is invoked across the Mahābhārata and Purāṇas as the gold standard of righteous sovereignty. His legend is a chain of marvels: a birth without a mother (from his father's flank, after the king drank a consecrated fertility-water), a naming by Indra himself, a conquest of the entire earth and beyond, the **Saubhari parable** of the sage who wed all fifty of his daughters, and his death battling the asura Lavaṇa. He lends his name to the **Māndhātā / Omkāreśvar** island *tīrtha* in the Narmadā. Principal sources: **Mahābhārata, Vana Parva** (and Droṇa Parva); **Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6–9.7**; **Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.2**.

## The strange birth (the motherless king)

- **Yuvanāśva's childlessness** - The Ikṣvāku king **Yuvanāśva** (son of Prasenajit / Saṃhatāśva in varying lists) had many wives but no son, and in grief retired to the forest to the hermitage of sages (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.25–27; Mahābhārata, Vana Parva 126*).
- **The consecrated fertility-water** - The ṛṣis performed an **Indra-yajña** (a putra-kāmeṣṭi / fertility rite) and left a vessel of mantra-charged **water** on the altar, to be drunk by the king's queen so she would conceive a mighty son (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.28–30; Vana Parva 126*).
- **The king drinks by mistake** - At night, parched, **Yuvanāśva himself drank the consecrated water** — unaware it was the conception-charged offering (in the Mahābhārata, he drinks it thinking it ordinary water; in the Bhāgavata, the sages awake to find the vessel empty). The potency thus lodged in the *king*, not a queen (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.31–33; Mahābhārata, Vana Parva 126*).
- **Born from the side** - In due course the child grew within Yuvanāśva and was delivered by splitting open the king's **right side / flank**, the father surviving by the sages' grace (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.34–36; Vana Parva 126*). [scripture]
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva):* the boy emerges from the **left side** in some recensions; the Aśvins (divine physicians) attend so that Yuvanāśva is not killed by the delivery.

## "Whom shall he suckle?" — the name and Indra's milk

- **The naming crux** - The motherless infant needed a wet-nurse, and the gods asked, *"kaṃ ayaṃ dhāsyati?"* — **"whom shall this child suckle?"** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.37; Mahābhārata, Vana Parva 126*).
- **Indra answers "mām dhāsyati"** - **Indra** declared **"mām ayaṃ dhāsyati"** — **"he shall suckle me"** — and put his own finger (yielding nectar/milk) into the infant's mouth. From this the child was named **Māndhātṛ** (*Māndhātā*), a folk-etymology from *mām dhāsyati* ("he will suck me") (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.37–38; Vana Parva 126*). [scripture]
  - *Variant reading (grammatical):* the more straightforward derivation is from the root *dhā* in the sense "to suck/nourish," but the Purāṇic-epic tradition uniformly preserves the **Indra "mām dhāsyati"** word-play as the charter of the name.
- **Reared on nectar** - Nourished by Indra's *amṛta*-finger, the child grew at marvellous speed, becoming a vast youth within days — already marked as a being of more-than-mortal vigor (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.38*).

## The world-emperor — paradigm of dharmic rule

- **Trasaddasyu, "terror of dacoits"** - Māndhātṛ became so mighty an emperor that he won the epithet **Trasaddasyu** ("he before whom robbers/dasyus tremble") — Indra himself bestowing it, for his single-handed suppression of lawlessness across the earth (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.39; Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.2*).
- **Cakravartin of the seven continents** - He conquered and ruled the **entire earth, the seven dvīpas**, "from where the sun rises to where it sets," all of it acknowledging his single sovereignty — the textbook *digvijaya* (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.40; Mahābhārata, Droṇa Parva*).
- **"Since the days of Māndhātā"** - His reign became the **proverbial benchmark of just kingship**: the epics repeatedly invoke Māndhātṛ as the model whose conduct a good king should match, and the Mahābhārata devotes a dialogue (the **Māndhātā–Indra / Māndhātā–Viṣṇu instruction** in the *Mokṣadharma/Rājadharma* and *Vana Parva* strata) to royal duty as he learned and embodied it (*Mahābhārata, Vana Parva & Śānti Parva*). [scholarly]
- **Sacrificer and giver** - He performed lavish sacrifices, his yajña-ground reaching the boundaries of his realm, and his charity was without limit — the complete picture of the dharma-king who unites conquest, ritual, and generosity (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.2; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.40–41*).
- **Marriage and sons** - Māndhātṛ wed **Bindumatī** (daughter of Śaśabindu / in some lists Caitraratha), and fathered sons including **Purukutsa**, **Mucukunda**, and **Ambarīṣa**, and (in most lists) **fifty daughters** (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.2; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.41*).
  - *Variant on Ambarīṣa:* the **Ambarīṣa son of Māndhātṛ** is a distinct figure from the famous **Ambarīṣa son of Nābhāga** (the Sudarśana-devotee of the Durvāsā episode); some retellings conflate the two. [scholarly] — see [Ambarīṣa & Durvāsā](ambarisha-durvasa.md).

## The Saubhari parable — the sage who wed fifty daughters

A celebrated inset (especially **Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.39–54** and **Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.2**) attaches to Māndhātṛ's daughters; it is a meditation on the snares of sense-enjoyment.

- **The aged ascetic underwater** - **Saubhari**, a great sage, performed austerities submerged in the Yamunā (or in the Kālindī). Observing the contented coupling of fish, desire awoke in him and he resolved to **marry and beget progeny** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.39–41*).
- **The demand to Māndhātṛ** - Saubhari approached Māndhātṛ and asked for **one of his daughters** in marriage. Reluctant to refuse a powerful sage yet repelled by his withered age, the king tried to evade with a condition: let the girl choose him of her own will (*svayaṃvara*) (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.42–45*).
- **The youthful transformation** - By yogic power Saubhari made himself **supremely beautiful and youthful**, entered the inner apartments, and **all fifty princesses simultaneously desired him** and fell to quarrelling over him — so the king gave **all fifty daughters** to the one sage (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.46–48; Viṣṇu Purāṇa IV.2*). [scripture]
- **The palace of pleasures** - By his powers Saubhari conjured fifty palaces of unimaginable luxury, dwelling with each wife so that **each believed he was hers alone** — a vivid image of the bottomless multiplication of craving (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.49–50*).
- **The disillusion and renunciation** - In time Saubhari recognized that his single act of weakness (watching the fish) had bred this whole web of attachment; he abandoned the palaces, returned to the forest, and his wives followed him into ascetic life and attained liberation through devotion to him (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.6.51–54*). [scripture]
  - *Reading:* the Saubhari episode is told as Śuka's cautionary lesson to Parīkṣit on how even a great yogī's mind, once turned to enjoyment, "spreads like fire fed with ghee" — framing Māndhātṛ's daughters as the occasion of a renunciation-parable rather than mere genealogy.

## Death and afterlife of the legend

- **Death by Lavaṇāsura** - In a widely-attested tradition, Māndhātṛ met his end in battle against the asura **Lavaṇa (Lavaṇāsura)** — the Madhu-line demon of Madhuvana, wielder of Śiva's invincible trident, later slain by Rāma's brother **Śatrughna**. Māndhātṛ, for all his world-conquest, was killed by Lavaṇa because he attacked while the demon held the *triśūla* (*Uttara-rāmāyaṇa / Padma Purāṇa traditions; cf. Mahābhārata allusions*). [late text]
  - *Variant (Purāṇic dynastic lists):* several Purāṇas simply continue the Solar genealogy past Māndhātṛ through Purukutsa without a death-narrative, the Lavaṇa-death being preserved chiefly in the Rāma-cycle's backstory to Lavaṇāsura.
- **The Māndhātā / Omkāreśvar tīrtha** - The sacred island in the **Narmadā** at **Omkāreśvar** (one of the twelve *jyotirliṅgas*) is traditionally called **Māndhātā**, said to be named for the king, who is held to have performed austerities to Śiva there and won the liṅga's manifestation on the spot (*sthala-purāṇa / regional Māhātmya tradition*). [regional]

## Themes & significance

- **The flawless cakravartin** - Where Triśaṅku is the king undone by hubris and Hariścandra the king tested by truth, **Māndhātṛ is the king who simply succeeds** — the unproblematic ideal of universal dharmic empire that the tradition holds up as the measure of all later kings. (See [Triśaṅku](trishanku.md) for the contrasting "stuck between worlds" Ikṣvāku.)
- **Birth-marvel as legitimation** - The motherless birth from the father's side and the suckling by Indra mark Māndhātṛ as more-than-human from the outset, sacralizing the *cakravartin* ideal: the perfect emperor is half-divine in origin.
- **The two clocks** - Māndhātṛ belongs to **scriptural time** — the deep manvantara-past of the Solar dynasty, placed by the Purāṇas many generations above Rāma. He has **no fixed historical-clock date**; the "since Māndhātā" idiom treats him precisely as immemorial antiquity rather than a datable reign.
