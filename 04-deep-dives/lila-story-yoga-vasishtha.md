# The Story of Queen Līlā — the two universes in the mind

> *Cosmic address: the flagship idealist parable (Līlā-upākhyāna) of the [Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha](../90-literature-corpus/yoga-vasishtha.md), **Utpatti Prakaraṇa** (Book III, chs. ~15–58) — narrated by **Vasiṣṭha** to a young **Rāma** in Daśaratha's assembly at Ayodhyā in [Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md); the tale itself is set in no fixed world or age. Composition: medieval Kashmir [scholarly] [late text]. A deep-dive episode-file.*
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Up to [README](../README.md)

The story of Queen **Līlā** is the *Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha's* single most elaborate demonstration of its governing thesis: that the world is **nothing but an appearance in consciousness (cit)**, a projection (*nirmāṇa*) of mind, with no substance of its own. A widow-fearing queen, granted mind-borne travel by the goddess **Sarasvatī**, learns that her whole kingdom is a dream unfolding inside the death-hut of an unremembered village brahmin, that her dead husband has already died *onward* into a further nested world, and that "space" and "time" run at different rates in each dream and enclose one another like reflections in facing mirrors. Where the [Cūḍālā-Śikhidhvaja tale](chudala-shikhidhvaja.md) dramatizes *jīvanmukti* (liberation-while-living), the Līlā tale dramatizes **ontology** — it is the corpus's great charter of subjective idealism, the answer to the pupil-question *"how does a world arise at all?"*

---

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural/cyclic frame** — Vasiṣṭha tells the tale to Rāma in **Tretā Yuga**, in Daśaratha's court at Ayodhyā, to establish the metaphysics that the rest of his instruction assumes: that saṃsāra is a self-woven mental fabric, dissolvable by understanding. The story *within* the tale has no yuga-address at all — deliberately, since its whole point is that its "worlds" are unreal locations in mind. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Utpatti Prakaraṇa, III ~15–58)
- **Academic/historical frame** — The Utpatti Prakaraṇa belongs to the Kashmiri **Mokṣopāya** stratum, composed c. **950 CE** [scholarly], later Vedānticized into the vulgate *Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* (final shape by c. the 14th century); see the [text profile](../90-literature-corpus/yoga-vasishtha.md) for the dating and recension debate. [late text]

## A note on the name-collisions (read first)

- **Two Vasiṣṭhas** — the sage **Vasiṣṭha** who *narrates* the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha to Rāma is **not** the brahmin **Vasiṣṭha** *inside* the tale. The latter is an ordinary village householder — a namesake — whose unfulfilled wish for kingship germinates the whole story. The text plays on the name deliberately; keep them apart. [scholarly]
- **Two (or three) Līlās** — the queen of the outer frame (**Līlā**, wife of Padma) is doubled by a second **Līlā**, the queen of the inner world (wife of Vidūratha). They are the same jīva refracted through nested dreams; the text names both Līlā and treats their eventual meeting as the reunion of one consciousness with its own projection. Some retellings call the inner queen **Līlā the second** or, in the ambient world, **Arundhatī** (a further namesake of the sage Vasiṣṭha's wife). [disputed]
- **This Sarasvatī** — the goddess who grants the boons is **Sarasvatī** as *jñāna-śakti* / the Goddess of the mind, functionally the Devī; she is not narrowly the consort-of-Brahmā of the Purāṇic rosters. [scholarly]

## The setting: Padma, Līlā, and the fear of widowhood (III ~15–19)

- **King Padma and Queen Līlā** — in an unnamed land rules the virtuous, powerful **Padma** with his devoted queen **Līlā**. They are wholly attached to one another; Līlā's one dread is the ordinary dread of a wife — that her husband will die first and leave her a widow. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~15)
- **Līlā's resolve and the two boons** — to defeat this, Līlā secretly practises worship and austerity and propitiates the goddess **Sarasvatī**, asking two boons: **(1)** that when Padma dies, his *jīva* (soul) shall **not leave the palace** but remain in the same chamber; and **(2)** that Sarasvatī will **appear whenever Līlā calls on her**. Both are granted — the pivots on which the plot turns. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~16)
- **Padma's death** — in time Padma falls ill and dies. Grief-stricken, Līlā covers his body with heaps of flowers to preserve it and forbids its cremation, invoking her first boon so that his spirit stays near. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~17–18)
- **Sarasvatī appears** — Līlā calls the goddess, who materializes and consoles her: Padma's soul has *not* gone far — it is present in this very chamber, but Līlā cannot see it, because it now inhabits **a world of its own making**. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~19)

## The first revelation: the kingdom inside a hut (III ~20–29)

- **The unreal palace** — Sarasvatī's first teaching stuns Līlā: the palace, the kingdom, the very reign of Padma that Līlā takes for solid reality is **itself a mental appearance**, no more substantial than the world Padma has now entered. Reality is one undivided consciousness; all "worlds" are ripples in it. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~20–22)
- **The dead brahmin Vasiṣṭha** — Sarasvatī discloses the origin of Līlā's whole world. In a remote hamlet there lived an aged, learned brahmin named **Vasiṣṭha** with his wife **Arundhatī** (namesakes, not the ṛṣi and his star-wife). Watching a king pass in splendour, the brahmin conceived a fierce desire to become an emperor — a wish he carried unfulfilled to his death. Dying with that single vāsanā, his mind, needing no external place, **projected an entire kingdom within the ether of his own death-chamber**, and became its emperor: **King Padma**. Arundhatī, dying soon after, became **Līlā**. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~23–26)
- **Space collapses** — the staggering demonstration: Padma's vast realm — its mountains, armies, and decades of history — exists entire **within the few feet of air in the corner of the dead brahmin's hut**, which hut is itself within the space of Līlā's own chamber. Great does not contain small and small does not contain great; both are ideas, and an idea has no size. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~27)
- **Time collapses** — the companion demonstration: though Padma seems to have reigned for **many decades**, in the frame of the brahmin's hut only **a handful of days** have passed since Vasiṣṭha died. Duration, like extent, is a rate internal to each dream; there is no common clock. This is the corpus's clearest statement of the relativity of experienced time (*kāla*). (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~27–28)
- **Līlā's disbelief** — Līlā cannot accept it, so Sarasvatī offers proof: they will *go and see* the hut. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~29)

## Travelling by mind: the journey to the hut (III ~30–35)

- **Leaving the body** — Sarasvatī instructs Līlā to still the senses and mind; the two abandon their bodies in a trance (the text describes a withdrawal into *nirvikalpa* stillness), and their subtle, mind-made forms rise into the **space of pure consciousness (cid-ākāśa)**, through which alone one travels between worlds. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~30–31)
- **Arrival at the hut** — they descend into the humble cottage of the dead brahmin, in its own village and countryside — and there indeed lie the corpses of **Vasiṣṭha and Arundhatī**, the whole ambient world going about its small life, unaware that a mighty empire (Padma's, and Līlā's own) is dreaming itself inside a corner of the room. Līlā, seeing her "real" kingdom nested inside this hut, begins to grasp that *all* worlds are like this. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~32–34)
- **The regress without end** — Sarasvatī adds the vertiginous corollary: this hut-world is **itself** a projection within some other mind, which is within another, "creation within creation without beginning or end" — a bottomless nesting, because mind can imagine mind imagining. There is no floor of the real to reach; only consciousness, ever awake, is real. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~35)

## The second nesting: the world of Vidūratha (III ~36–49)

- **Padma has died onward** — where, then, is Padma's soul *now*? Sarasvatī reveals a further nesting: at the instant of Padma's death, his own accumulated vāsanā spun **yet another world**, in which he has been reborn as the young **King Vidūratha** — and *that* realm exists inside the space of Padma's death-chamber, exactly as Padma's realm existed inside the brahmin's hut. Dream nests within dream. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~36–38)
- **The second Līlā** — in Vidūratha's world reigns a queen who is also named **Līlā** — the same consciousness projected again. Sarasvatī and the first Līlā travel by mind into this third world to find Padma's jīva living as Vidūratha, and there behold the second Līlā at his side. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~39–41)
- **The invasion and the war** — Vidūratha's kingdom is attacked by a rival monarch (named in the vulgate as the lord of **Sindhu**); a great battle unfolds, which Sarasvatī and the first Līlā watch as unseen witnesses from the sky. The war-cantos are among the Utpatti Prakaraṇa's set-pieces. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~42–47) [disputed]
- **The boon to the second Līlā** — the second Līlā, too, worships Sarasvatī and is granted a boon; learning that her husband's death is near, she wishes to accompany his soul and to understand the truth of the worlds. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~48)

## The return, the twin Līlās, and the revival (III ~50–58)

- **Vidūratha falls** — mortally wounded in battle, Vidūratha dies. His jīva, released, does not wander: guided by the accumulated tendency and by Sarasvatī's grace, it **returns along the nesting** to Padma's preserved corpse in the flower-heaped chamber. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~50–52)
- **Padma revives** — the body of Padma, kept by Līlā's first boon, stirs and **comes back to life**: the same consciousness that lived as brahmin-Vasiṣṭha → Padma → Vidūratha is now re-seated in Padma's frame, its long chain of "deaths" exposed as changes of mental scene, never a real ending. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~53–54)
- **The two Līlās meet** — the first Līlā (Padma's queen) and the second Līlā (Vidūratha's queen), brought together, are seen to be **one being wearing two projected forms**; the second, having asked to come, remains, and the household is reconstituted in full knowledge that all of it — palace, queen, and king alike — is appearance. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~55–56)
- **Liberation in the dream** — Līlā, now knowing every world to be consciousness and no world to be a prison, lives on **liberated while acting**, ruling beside the revived Padma without the terror that began the tale. Death has lost its meaning for one who sees that nothing was ever born. Vasiṣṭha holds the whole story up to Rāma as proof that the wise dwell in the world as in a picture — present, engaged, and free. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* III ~57–58)

## The teaching: worlds as mind-appearance

- **Dṛṣṭi-sṛṣṭi / ajāta-vāda** — the tale is the narrative body of the *Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha's* radical idealism: seeing *is* creating (*dṛṣṭi-sṛṣṭi*), and in truth nothing has ever really been created at all (*ajāta-vāda*) — the "worlds" are unborn, mind-projected, void of substance. Cf. [māyā and avidyā](../05-concepts-and-dharma/maya-and-avidya.md) and the uncompromising non-dualism of the [Ribhu-Gītā](ribhu-gita.md) and [Avadhūta-Gītā](avadhuta-gita.md). (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Utpatti Prakaraṇa)
- **Relativity of space and time** — that a kingdom fits in a hut, and decades pass in days, makes Līlā the corpus's premier statement that extension and duration are internal to each observer's mind, not features of a shared external world — a point modern readers routinely compare to relativity and to dream-logic. [scholarly]
- **Death as a change of projection** — the chain brahmin → Padma → Vidūratha → Padma-revived reframes death: the jīva does not travel to another *place* but merely takes up another *idea*; liberation is waking from the idea altogether. Cf. [karma, saṃsāra, mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md).
- **The power of a single vāsanā** — an entire cosmos springs from *one* dying wish (the brahmin's craving for kingship); the tale is a warning about the world-making force of unquenched desire, and an argument for emptying the mind of it.

## Kin-stories and afterlife

- **Frame kin in the Utpatti Prakaraṇa** — Līlā sits beside the book's other mind-only demonstrations: the **three non-existent princes** (Aindava) who are and are not, the ogress **Karkaṭī**, the sage **Gādhi's** long delusion, and the tales of Śukra — all arguing that the world is *citta-mātra*. For the whole architecture and the roster of embedded tales see the [Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha profile](../90-literature-corpus/yoga-vasishtha.md) and the [tales catalog](yoga-vasishtha-tales-catalog.md). (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Book III)
- **Sister-parable within the corpus** — the companion long-tale [Cūḍālā and Śikhidhvaja](chudala-shikhidhvaja.md) (Nirvāṇa Prakaraṇa) supplies the *practical* half — how to live liberated — where Līlā supplies the *metaphysical* half — why there is nothing external to be bound by. Related non-dual dialogues include [Tripura-Rahasya](tripura-rahasya.md); a kindred vision of nested other-worlds appears in [Vipaścit in hell](vipashchit-in-hell.md).
- **Modern resonance** — the Līlā tale is a favourite of the twentieth-century Vedānta revival for its "many worlds" vividness; it circulates worldwide through Vihari Lala Mitra's complete translation and Swami Venkatesananda's condensed *Supreme Yoga*, and is frequently cited in comparisons of Advaita idealism with modern physics and philosophy of mind. [scholarly]
- **Chapter-numbering caveat** — the chapter figures above (~15–58) follow the Nirṇaya Sāgara vulgate of the Utpatti Prakaraṇa and are marked approximate ("~"): recensions, the Mokṣopāya critical text, and the *Laghu-Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* abridgement divide the chapters differently, and the tale is variously cited as III.15–60. [disputed] [scholarly]
