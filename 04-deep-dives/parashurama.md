# Paraśurāma avatāra

> Cosmic address: the **sixth** of the [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md), born in the **[Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** of the present **[Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)** and — as a **cirañjīvi** — surviving across **[Dvāpara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md)** and **[Kali](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)** to the very end of the Mahāyuga.
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · See also: [The Cirañjīvis](./the-chiranjivis.md) · [Kalki avatāra](./kalki.md) · [Karṇa](./karna.md) · [Bhīṣma](./characters/bhishma.md) · [Droṇa](./characters/drona.md) · [Ambā and Śikhaṇḍin](./characters/amba-shikhandi.md) · [Bāla Kāṇḍa](./ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md)

**Paraśurāma** (परशुराम, "Rāma-with-the-axe") is called simply **Rāma**, **Jāmadagnya** or **Bhārgava** in the epics. The compound name *Paraśurāma* appears in the Purāṇas, for example *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* 4.7.36 (*paraśurāma-saṃjñam*). He is the brahmin-warrior avatāra, born to the Bhṛgu sage **Jamadagni** and **Reṇukā**. He avenges his father's murder by clearing the earth of kṣatriyas **twenty-one times** (*triḥsapta-kṛtvaḥ*). He gives the conquered earth to **Kaśyapa**, and because Kaśyapa then bars him from it, he lives on Mahendra mountain or on coastland that the sea gives up for him. As a deathless figure (*cirañjīvi*) he meets [Rāma Dāśarathi](./ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md), who has just broken Śiva's bow. He teaches [Bhīṣma](./characters/bhishma.md), [Droṇa](./characters/drona.md) and [Karṇa](./karna.md), and the *Kalki Purāṇa* makes him the teacher of [Kalki](./kalki.md).

The main Sanskrit narratives are:
- **Mahābhārata**: *Āraṇyaka/Vana* 3.115–117, which Akṛtavraṇa tells the Pāṇḍavas at Mahendra, and *Śānti* 12.49, which Kṛṣṇa tells Yudhiṣṭhira.
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa** 9.15–16.
- **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** 4.7 and 4.11, a shorter genealogical account.
- **Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa** 2.3.21–47 and 2.3.57–58, the longest Purāṇic cycle.

These sources disagree on many details, and the disagreements are recorded below as variants. The earliest attested reclamation story is in the Mahābhārata (12.49.57–59); the Konkan and Kerala charter-myths are later and regional.

**Two clocks.** Scripturally Paraśurāma is a single deathless figure threading one Mahāyuga from Tretā to Kali; his "appearances" in the Rāmāyaṇa, Mahābhārata, and Kalki Purāṇa are the *same* immortal at different points of that cycle — see [The Cirañjīvis](./the-chiranjivis.md). Even within scripture his placement varies:
- *Variant (Mahābhārata 12.326.77):* the Nārāyaṇīya list of future avatāras says *tretāyuge bhaviṣyāmi rāmo bhṛgukulodvahaḥ*, "in the Tretā age I shall be Rāma of the Bhṛgu line".
- *Variant (Mahābhārata 1.2.3):* the Parvasaṃgraha places his slaughter "at the junction of Tretā and Dvāpara" (*tretādvāparayoḥ saṃdhau*).
- *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 1.3.20):* he is the **sixteenth** of twenty-two avatāras, not the sixth. The "sixth" comes from the later ten-avatāra (Daśāvatāra) list.

Claims that map his Konkan/Kerala land-grants onto historical Brahmin migrations or particular dynasties belong to a separate, regional-historical clock and are flagged below.

## I. Lineage and the wish-cow

- **The Bhārgava house** — Jamadagni is the son of the Bhārgava sage **Ṛcīka** and **Satyavatī**. Satyavatī is the daughter of King **Gādhi** of Kānyakubja; Ṛcīka wins her with a bride-price of a thousand white horses with one black ear each, which he obtains from Varuṇa. (MBh CE 3.115.9–17; BhP 9.15.5–7; VP 4.7.13–16)
  - **Reṇukā's father** is King **Prasenajit** in the Mahābhārata (MBh CE 3.116.2). The Purāṇas make her the daughter of **Reṇu** (BhP 9.15.12), and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa adds that Reṇu was of the Ikṣvāku line (VP 4.7.35).
  - **The five sons** — Rāma is the youngest of five. The Mahābhārata names the four elder brothers as Rumaṇvat, Suṣeṇa, Vasu and Viśvāvasu (MBh CE 3.116.4, 3.116.10). The Bhāgavata says only "Vasumat and the others" (BhP 9.15.13).
  - **His divine status** — the Bhāgavata calls him a portion (*aṃśa*) of Vāsudeva, "the ender of the Haihaya line" (BhP 9.15.14). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa calls him a portion of Nārāyaṇa (VP 4.7.36).
- **Why a brahmin bears arms: the exchange before his father's birth** — Satyavatī and her mother were each given a rite meant to produce a son, and they exchanged them. Because of the swap, Satyavatī's son would be "a brahmin of kṣatriya conduct" (*brāhmaṇaḥ kṣatravṛttiḥ*). Satyavatī begged that the fate pass to her grandson instead, so her son Jamadagni was born calm and the warrior nature fell on Rāma. (MBh CE 3.115.25–28; 12.49.18–29)
  - *Variant (MBh CE 3.115.23–24):* in the CE text the two women swap the **trees** they were told to embrace (the *aśvattha* and the *udumbara*). The **two pots of *caru*** (sacrificial porridge) in this chapter are a starred insertion (\*0570).
  - *Variant (MBh 12.49.8–26; BhP 9.15.8–11; VP 4.7.17–23):* these texts tell it as a swap of the two *carus*. *Anuśāsana* 13.4 combines the two forms: the women exchange both the *carus* and the trees (MBh CE 13.4.27–39). One *caru* was charged with brahmin power, the other with kṣatriya power, and the swap is also used to explain why Satyavatī's brother Viśvāmitra, a kṣatriya by birth, became a brahmin.
  - *Background (MBh CE 1.169.11 ff., the Aurva episode):* Vasiṣṭha tells Parāśara how the heirs of King **Kṛtavīrya** slaughtered the Bhṛgus to recover wealth their family had given them, and how the unborn **Aurva** survived. This is an older Bhārgava–Haihaya feud, and Kṛtavīrya is the father of Paraśurāma's enemy Kārtavīrya Arjuna.
  - *Scholarly reading:* the Bhārgava stories in the epic can be read as one long cycle of vengeance between Bhṛgus and Haihayas, which Paraśurāma completes; see R. P. Goldman, *Gods, Priests, and Warriors: The Bhṛgus of the Mahābhārata* (1977). [scholarly]
- **The hermitage cow** — The cow at the centre of the tragedy is Jamadagni's sacrificial milch-cow. The Mahābhārata calls her the *homadhenu* ("cow for the oblations") and says that only her **calf** was carried off (MBh CE 3.116.21). The Bhāgavata calls her *haviṣmatī*, *havirdhānī* and *agnihotrī*: through her Jamadagni feasts the king and his whole army, and she is taken together with her calf (BhP 9.15.24–26, 9.15.36). The Brahmāṇḍa describes her as the best of divine cows, one from which whatever is desired can be milked (2.3.28.28–30). The name **Kāmadhenu** itself comes from popular retellings and from the chapter heading in Tagare's translation, not from the verses. [late text]
  - *Correction note:* some retellings call her "Nandinī". In the Mahābhārata, Nandinī is **Vasiṣṭha's** cow in the Viśvāmitra story (MBh 1.165), not Jamadagni's. The name "Suśīlā", found in some retellings, has no attested Sanskrit source. [disputed]
- **Weapons from Śiva** — Duryodhana tells Śalya that Rāma, son of Jamadagni, propitiated Śiva for weapons over many years. At Śiva's command he slew the dānavas who were oppressing the gods, and Śiva then gave him divine weapons (*astrāṇi divyāni*), which Rāma later passed on to Karṇa. This passage does not single out the axe. (MBh CE 8.24.131–158)
  - *Variant (Brahmāṇḍa 2.3.32.56–61):* among the weapons Śiva grants are the Pāśupata, the Brahmāstra and the Nārāyaṇāstra, an armour called *trailokya-vijaya*, and the **paraśu**. Brahmāṇḍa 2.3.42.3 later recalls the axe as the gift of Gaṇeśa's father. [late text]
  - The **Bhāgavata** describes no grant at all; Rāma simply takes up "his terrible axe" (BhP 9.15.28).
  - The **Rāmāyaṇa** shows him with the axe on his shoulder and a bow in his hand (Rām CE 1.73.18).
  - **Other weapons:** his Vaiṣṇava bow came from Viṣṇu through Ṛcīka and then Jamadagni (Rām CE 1.74.21–22). Karṇa's bow **Vijaya** passed from Indra to the Bhārgava and then to Karṇa (MBh CE 8.22.37–38).

## II. The beheading and restoration of Reṇukā

- **Reṇukā's lapse** — While her sons are out gathering fruit, **Reṇukā** goes to bathe. She sees **Citraratha, king of Mārttikāvata**, garlanded with lotuses and sporting in the water with his wives, and she feels desire for him. She comes back to the hermitage wet, unsteady and frightened. Jamadagni sees that she has lost her composure and her "brahmin radiance" (*brāhmī lakṣmī*), and he reproaches her. (MBh CE 3.116.5–9)
  - *Variant (BhP 9.16.2–4):* she has gone to the Gaṅgā for water. There she watches a **Gandharva king**, "longing for Citraratha", sporting with apsarases, and she forgets the time of her husband's fire-offering. She returns afraid of his curse.
  - *Note:* the name "Citrasena", found in some retellings, is not in either text.
- **The father's command** — Jamadagni orders his sons, one after another, to kill their mother.
  - **In the Mahābhārata**, the four elder sons stand bewildered and say nothing. He curses them, and they lose their reason and become "like beasts and birds". **Rāma** arrives last, takes up the axe and cuts off his mother's head. (MBh CE 3.116.10–14)
  - *Variant (BhP 9.16.5–6):* the sons refuse, and Rāma **kills his brothers together with his mother**. He does so because he knows the power of his father's austerity.
- **The boon and restoration** — Pleased with his son, Jamadagni offers him boons.
  - **In the Mahābhārata**, Rāma asks for six things: that his mother rise again; that she not remember being killed; that he himself not be touched by the sin; that his brothers regain their normal nature; that he be unrivalled in battle; and that he live long. All are granted. (MBh CE 3.116.15–18)
  - *Variant (BhP 9.16.7–8):* he asks that everyone he killed live again and not remember it. They rise "as if from sleep".
  - *Variant (South Indian / folk — Reṇukā–Yellammā / Māriyammaṉ):* in Deccan and Tamil temple and village traditions, the axe falls on Reṇukā and on a low-caste woman who had sheltered or embraced her. When they are revived in haste, the heads are **joined to the wrong bodies**, and the two resulting figures are worshipped as goddesses. Which figure is called Yellammā, Māriyammaṉ or Mātaṅgī varies from telling to telling. This story is absent from the Mahābhārata, the Bhāgavata and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa. [folk][regional]

## III. Kārtavīrya Arjuna and the murder of Jamadagni

- **Sahasrārjuna the thousand-armed** — **Arjuna Kārtavīrya**, son of Kṛtavīrya, is a Haihaya king. The Mahābhārata calls him lord of **Anūpa** (MBh CE 3.116.19), and the Bhāgavata places his capital at **Māhiṣmatī** on the Narmadā (BhP 9.15.22, 9.15.26).
  - **His thousand arms** come from worshipping **Dattātreya** (BhP 9.15.17–19; VP 4.11.11–12). In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, one of Dattātreya's boons is that he will die only at the hands of a world-famous man (VP 4.11.12), and after an 85,000-year reign he is killed by Paraśurāma (VP 4.11.20).
  - **Kārtavīrya and Rāvaṇa** — the Bhāgavata adds that while sporting in the Revā (Narmadā) he dammed the river with his arms. When **Rāvaṇa** objected, Arjuna captured him and later let him go "like a monkey" (BhP 9.15.20–22).
  - *Variant (MBh 12.49.30–38):* Kārtavīrya lets Agni burn the world, including the hermitage of **Āpava**, whom the text calls the son of Varuṇa (12.49.35) and whom tradition identifies with Vasiṣṭha. Āpava curses him that "Rāma will cut off your arms in battle". This text also calls Kārtavīrya himself virtuous, generous and devoted to brahmins (12.49.38).
  - *Apparatus note:* in Āraṇyaka 3.115 the Dattātreya boon, the golden *vimāna* and Viṣṇu's resolve to kill Arjuna occur only in App. I No. 15, inserted after CE 3.115.8.
- **The theft at the hermitage** — The versions differ on who took what.
  - **Mahābhārata, Āraṇyaka:** Kārtavīrya arrives while Jamadagni's sons are away. Reṇukā receives him with honour, but he is drunk with pride, spurns the welcome, carries off the **calf** of the homa-cow while it bellows, and breaks down the great trees. (MBh CE 3.116.19–21)
  - *Variant (BhP 9.15.23–26):* he comes while hunting and is feasted with his army through the cow. Coveting her, he has his men drag the cow and her calf to Māhiṣmatī.
  - *Variant (MBh 12.49.39–40):* **Arjuna's sons** take the calf **without their father's knowledge**.
  - *Variant (Brahmāṇḍa 2.3.26–29):* Kārtavīrya stays the night as Jamadagni's guest (2.3.27). His minister **Candragupta** advises him to seize the cow. The king's men beat Jamadagni senseless, the cow breaks free and rises into the sky, and they take only the calf (2.3.28; 2.3.29.8–23). [late text]
- **First slaying of Kārtavīrya** — Again the accounts differ.
  - **Mahābhārata, Āraṇyaka:** told by his father, Rāma attacks Kārtavīrya, takes up his bow and cuts off the thousand arms with *bhalla* arrows (MBh CE 3.116.22–24). The battle books recall that Kārtavīrya was slain by Bhārgava Rāma (MBh CE 8.4.52).
  - *Variant (BhP 9.15.27–36):* Rāma pursues him to Māhiṣmatī with his axe. Alone, he destroys **seventeen akṣauhiṇīs** (army divisions). He cuts off the arms with the axe, then the head, and brings the cow and calf home.
  - *Variant (MBh 12.49.41):* Rāma cuts off Arjuna's arms and brings the calf back from the women's quarters.
  - *Sequence variant (BhP 9.15.38–9.16.1):* Jamadagni rebukes his son. Brahmins are honoured for forbearance, he says, and killing a consecrated king is worse than killing a brahmin. Rāma atones with a year's pilgrimage. Only after this does the Bhāgavata tell the Reṇukā episode, so its order of events is the reverse of the Mahābhārata's.
- **Murder of Jamadagni** — In revenge, Kārtavīrya's heirs fall on the hermitage while Rāma is away.
  - **Mahābhārata, Āraṇyaka:** Jamadagni does not fight back and cries "Rāma, Rāma!" as they **kill him with arrows**. Rāma comes back carrying firewood and laments over his father. (MBh CE 3.116.25–29; 3.117.1–4)
  - *Variant (MBh 12.49.42–43; BhP 9.16.10–12):* they **cut off his head**. In the Bhāgavata he is sitting in the fire-house with his mind fixed on the Lord, and they carry the head away despite Reṇukā's pleas.
  - **The twenty-one blows** — in the Bhāgavata, Reṇukā beats herself and cries out "Rāma, Rāma!", but the blows are not counted (BhP 9.16.13).
  - *Variant (Brahmāṇḍa 2.3.30.28–32):* Reṇukā strikes her breast **twenty-one times**, and Rāma vows to wipe out the kṣatriyas as many times. This is the source of the popular link between her blows and the number of purges. The Mahābhārata and Bhāgavata state the number twenty-one but do not connect it to her. [late text]

## IV. The twenty-one-fold extermination of the kṣatriyas

- **The vow of annihilation** — Rāma cremates his father and vows the death of the whole kṣatriya order. He kills Kārtavīrya's sons and their followers, and then **twenty-one times** makes the earth empty of kṣatriyas (*triḥsaptakṛtvaḥ pṛthivīṃ kṛtvā niḥkṣatriyām*). (MBh CE 3.117.5–9; also 1.58.4; 12.49.44–56)
  - *Variant (BhP 9.16.16–19):* at Māhiṣmatī he builds a mountain of the princes' heads and makes a terrible river of their blood.
  - *Variant (MBh 12.49.47–55): the purges begin again.* After the first purge Rāma withdraws to the forest out of pity. Thousands of years later **Parāvasu**, a grandson of Viśvāmitra, mocks him in public: kings such as **Pratardana**, who gathered at Yayāti's sacrifice, are still alive, so his vow was false. Rāma takes up arms again and kills even infants, killing each child as soon as it is born, and kṣatriya women hide some of their sons.
- **The five blood-lakes at Samanta-pañcaka** — At **Samantapañcaka** Rāma makes **five lakes of blood** and offers his ancestors libations of that blood. His forefathers, led by **Ṛcīka**, appear and stop him: "forbear". The region near the lakes is the holy Samantapañcaka, and in the interval between Dvāpara and Kali the Kuru–Pāṇḍava battle is fought there. (MBh CE 1.2.3–9; 3.117.9–10) — this is why the same field is later the site of the Kurukṣetra war.
  - *Variant (BhP 9.16.19):* he makes **nine** lakes of blood (*hradān nava*).
- **The great gift (mahā-dāna)** — The accounts differ on what was given to whom.
  - **Mahābhārata, Āraṇyaka:** with a great sacrifice Rāma pleases Indra and gives the earth to the officiating priests (3.117.11). To **Kaśyapa** he gives a golden altar ten *vyāmas* long and nine high, and the brahmins who divide it among themselves with Kaśyapa's consent become the **Khāṇḍavāyanas** (3.117.12–13). The next verse then says that, having given the earth to Kaśyapa, he dwells on **Mahendra** (3.117.14). This passage mentions no Aśvamedha and no ban.
  - *Variant (MBh 12.49.56–58):* he gives the whole earth to Kaśyapa as the fee (*dakṣiṇā*) at the end of an **Aśvamedha** (horse sacrifice). Kaśyapa tells him: "Go to the far shore of the southern ocean; you must never live in my realm."
  - *Variant (Rām CE 1.74.25; 1.75.13–15):* because of Kaśyapa's ban, Rāma has promised never to spend a night on earth. He asks Rāma Dāśarathi to spare his power of movement so he can keep that promise.
  - *Variant (BhP 9.16.20–24):* Rāma sets his father's head back on the body and sacrifices. He gives the east to the *hotṛ* priest, the south to the *brahman*, the west to the *adhvaryu*, the north to the *udgātṛ*, the intermediate directions to the other priests, the centre to **Kaśyapa**, Āryāvarta to the *upadraṣṭṛ*, and what remains to the *sadasya* priests. After his closing bath in the Sarasvatī, **Jamadagni is restored to a conscious body and becomes the seventh of the Seven Sages** (*saptarṣi*).
  - *Variant (survival of the line):* the texts explain in two ways how kings still existed after the purges.
    - *Mahābhārata 1.58.4–8:* after the purges, kṣatriya women who want children go to brahmins, who father a new kṣatriya generation on them.
    - *Mahābhārata 12.49.63–78:* the unprotected Earth sinks to Rasātala, and Kaśyapa holds her up on his thigh (*ūru*), which the text gives as the origin of her name *urvī*. Earth then names the heirs she has hidden: princes of the Haihaya line; Viḍūratha's son, raised by bears on Mount Ṛkṣavat; Saudāsa's son, protected by Parāśara; Śibi's son Gopati, raised by cows; Pratardana's son Vatsa, raised among calves; Aṅga, guarded by Gautama on the Gaṅgā; Bṛhadratha, guarded by monkeys on Gṛdhrakūṭa; and three descendants of Marutta guarded by the ocean. Kaśyapa consecrates them as kings.
- **Retirement to Mahendra** — Rāma settles on **Mahendra** mountain as an ascetic. In the Āraṇyaka, the Pāṇḍavas reach Mahendra after crossing Kaliṅga and the Vaitaraṇī and going to the sea. Rāma shows himself to the ascetics there on the fourteenth and eighth days of the lunar fortnight, and he receives Yudhiṣṭhira. (MBh CE 3.114.4–26; 3.115.6; 3.117.14–18) The Bhāgavata says he "still lives today on Mahendra, having laid down the rod" (BhP 9.16.26). The usual identification with **Mahendragiri** in the Eastern Ghats (Odisha) fits this coastal route, but it is a later geographical identification, not a statement of the text.

## V. Reclaiming the western coast (Konkan / Kerala)

- **Land from the sea** — The versions are layered, from the oldest to the most regional.
  - **Mahābhārata (12.49.57–59):** Kaśyapa sends Rāma beyond the southern sea. The **ocean**, afraid of Jamadagni's son, creates for him the land of **Śūrpāraka**, the *Aparānta* or western border-country. No axe or arrow is thrown in this version.
  - *Variant (Brahmāṇḍa 2.3.57–58):* Gokarṇa, a shrine dear to Śiva, has been submerged by the digging of Sagara's sons, and its sages come to Rāma on Mahendra. **Varuṇa** submits to him. Rāma breaks his bow, casts away his arrow and hurls his **sacrificial ladle** (*sruvā*), which falls at **Śūrpāraka**, generally identified with modern Sopārā near Mumbai. The sea then withdraws from a strip said to stretch 400 *yojanas* from south to north. [late text]
  - *Variant (popular and regional retellings):* he **throws his axe**, or in some tellings a winnowing basket (*śūrpa*, a folk etymology of Śūrpāraka), into the sea from the Western Ghats, and the reclaimed coast of the **Konkan** and **Kerala** becomes **Paraśurāma-kṣetra**, settled with brahmins. Konkan traditions attach this to the *Sahyādri-khaṇḍa*, which is assigned to the Skanda Purāṇa. [regional][late text]
  - *Variant (Kerala — Keralolpatti):* the **Keralolpatti** chronicle has Paraśurāma reclaim Kerala, install the **64 brahmin gramas (settlements)** and the social order of Malabar, and consecrate its temples — the regional charter-myth of Kerala's creation and its Nambudiri brahmins. [regional][late text]
    - Popular summaries say the 64 settlements were split between the Tulu and Malayalam regions (32 and 32). They also disagree on the axe-throw, which goes from Gokarṇa to Kanyākumārī in some tellings and the other way in others. [regional][disputed]
  - *Variant (Tuluva / Konkan):* parallel legends make him founder of **Tuḷu-nāḍu** and the western *Saptakoṅkaṇa*, the patron of coastal Paraśurāma-kṣetra shrines (Gokarṇa, Śūrpāraka/Sopara, Udupi region). [regional][folk]
  - *Two-clocks note:* attempts to read these grants as memory of historical Brahmin migration into the Konkan/Kerala littoral are a **historical-clock** interpretation laid over the scriptural figure; the texts present it as a single avatāra's deed, not a dated event. [disputed]

## VI. Across the two epics — the deathless preceptor

- **Breaking Śiva's bow (Rāmāyaṇa)** — It is **Rāma Dāśarathi** who breaks Śiva's bow, at Mithilā. Paraśurāma meets him afterwards, on the road back to Ayodhyā after the weddings, when the party is surrounded by ill omens, a great wind and darkness. (Rām CE 1.73.6–17)
  - **The challenge:** Paraśurāma explains that Viśvakarman made two bows, one for Śiva and one for Viṣṇu, and that when the two gods fought, Viṣṇu proved the stronger. The Vaiṣṇava bow passed from Viṣṇu to Ṛcīka and then to Jamadagni. He dares Rāma to string it and promises a duel if he can. (Rām CE 1.74.1–28)
  - **The outcome:** Rāma strings the bow and sets an arrow to it. Rāma will not take a brahmin's life, so he offers to destroy either Paraśurāma's power of movement or the worlds he has won by austerity. Paraśurāma becomes "drained of vigour" (*nirvīryo jāmadagnyaḥ*) and recognises Rāma as Viṣṇu, the slayer of Madhu. He asks Rāma to spare his movement and destroy the worlds, then goes back to Mahendra. Rāma gives the bow to Varuṇa. (Rām CE 1.75.1–1.76.3; vulgate 1.74–76)
  - The CE passage calls the weapon only "the Vaiṣṇava bow"; the name **Śārṅga** is not used there.
  - *Variant (MBh 3.97.27, App. I No. 14; G Vana §99):* in this vulgate passage, which the CE keeps out of its main text, Paraśurāma goes to Ayodhyā to test Rāma. Rāma Dāśarathi takes the Bhārgava's bow from his hand and strings it, and Paraśurāma sees the whole cosmos in Rāma's body. The arrow strips him of his energy (*tejas*), and he lives humbled on Mahendra for a year. Then his ancestors send him to bathe at the Dīptoda ford on the Vadhūsarā river, where he regains it.
  - See [Rāmāyaṇa deep-dives](./ramayana/).
- **Guru of Bhīṣma** — Gaṅgā tells Śaṃtanu that whatever weapons Jāmadagnya knows are also established in their son Devavrata (MBh CE 1.94.35), and before their duel Bhīṣma calls himself Rāma's pupil (*śiṣyo 'smi tava bhārgava*, 5.178.16). (Udyoga Parva, *Ambopākhyāna*)
  - **How the duel comes about:** **Ambā** was rejected after Bhīṣma carried her off. Her maternal grandfather, the royal sage **Hotravāhana**, sends her to Rāma, and Rāma's companion Akṛtavraṇa takes up her cause (MBh CE 5.174.22–5.177). Rāma orders Bhīṣma to take her back. Bhīṣma refuses, and they fight at **Kurukṣetra** (5.178–179).
  - **The duel:** it lasts **twenty-three days** (5.183.27).
  - **The *prasvāpa* weapon:** in a dream, eight radiant brahmins remind Bhīṣma of the *prasvāpa* ("sleep") weapon, which Rāma does not know (5.184.6–18). When Bhīṣma is about to release it, the gods cry out from the sky and **Nārada** tells him not to dishonour his brahmin teacher, so he withdraws it (5.186.1–7).
  - **Rāma is called off:** Rāma's **forefathers**, his father and grandfather, stand around him. They tell him that war is the kṣatriya's duty and study the brahmin's, and that Bhīṣma is a Vasu whose death is reserved for Arjuna (5.186.9–20). Rāma refuses to retreat (5.186.21–22). The sages led by **Ṛcīka**, with Nārada, then tell Bhīṣma to withdraw, and he refuses too, citing his vow never to turn from battle (5.186.23–26). Finally the sages led by Nārada, together with Bhīṣma's mother **Gaṅgā**, step into the middle of the field. They tell Rāma, "Bhīṣma cannot be slain by you, nor you by Bhīṣma", and the ancestors make Rāma lay down his weapons (5.186.27–30).
  - **The verdict:** Rāma praises Bhīṣma as without equal among kṣatriyas (5.186.35). He tells Ambā that he has done his utmost and has been "worsted by Bhīṣma" (*nirjito hy asmi bhīṣmeṇa*), so she must turn to Bhīṣma himself (5.187.1–4; cf. 5.186.8). So the two verdicts differ: the ancestors rule that neither can slay the other, while Rāma's own words concede defeat (5.187.4).
  - See [Ambā and Śikhaṇḍin](./characters/amba-shikhandi.md).
- **Guru of Droṇa** — **Droṇa** hears that Jāmadagnya is giving away all his wealth to brahmins before leaving for the forest. When Droṇa arrives, Rāma says he has already given his gold to the brahmins and the earth to Kaśyapa, and only his body and his weapons remain. Droṇa asks for all the weapons, with the methods of releasing and recalling them, and Rāma gives him the whole science of arms. (MBh CE 1.121.15–23; G Ādi §131) — see [Droṇa](./characters/drona.md).
- **Guru and curser of Karṇa** — Droṇa refuses to teach Karṇa the **Brahmāstra**, so Karṇa goes to Mahendra and introduces himself as "a brahmin of the Bhṛgu line" (MBh CE 12.2.9–16).
  - **The worm:** one day Rāma, weak from fasting, falls asleep with his head in Karṇa's lap. A blood-sucking worm bores into Karṇa's thigh, and Karṇa bears it without moving rather than wake his teacher. The blood wakes Rāma, and his glance kills the creature. It was the asura **Gṛtsa**, cursed by Bhṛgu long ago to become the worm **Alarka** for abducting Bhṛgu's wife, and Rāma's glance frees him. (MBh CE 12.3.4–24)
  - **The curse:** "No brahmin could bear such pain; you are a kṣatriya." Karṇa confesses that he is a *sūta*, born between the brahmin and kṣatriya orders. Rāma curses him: because he got the weapon by deceit, the Brahmāstra will fail him at the time of his death, when he fights an equal. Rāma also tells him that no kṣatriya on earth will equal him in battle. (MBh CE 12.3.25–32; G Śānti §3)
  - *Variant (MBh CE 8.29.4–7; G Karṇa §42):* Karṇa tells Śalya that **Indra**, acting for Arjuna, took the worm's body to expose him.
  - **A second, separate curse:** while practising near the sea, Karṇa accidentally kills a brahmin's homa-cow. The brahmin curses him that the earth will swallow his chariot-wheel in battle (MBh CE 12.2.19–29).
    - *Variant (MBh CE 8.29.31–39):* in Karṇa's own retelling he shot the **calf** of the homa-cow with an arrow (8.29.33).
    - At Karṇa's death both curses come true together. His wheel falls to the ground (8.66.42a), "his chariot reeling by the brahmin's curse, and the weapon received from Rāma no longer appearing to him" (MBh CE 8.66.42c).
  - See [Karṇa](./karna.md).
- **Other Mahābhārata appearances** — At the Kuru court, during Kṛṣṇa's peace mission, Jāmadagnya tells the parable of King **Dambhodbhava** to warn Duryodhana against fighting Nara and Nārāyaṇa, that is, Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa (MBh CE 5.94).
- **Preceptor of Kalki** — The *Kalki Purāṇa* makes Paraśurāma Kalki's teacher. [late text] — see [The Cirañjīvis](./the-chiranjivis.md).
  - **The teaching:** after his sacred-thread ceremony, Kalki goes to Rāma's hermitage on **Mahendra**, where he learns the Vedas and their auxiliary sciences, the science of arms and the sixty-four arts. When Kalki offers a teacher's fee, Rāma asks only that Kalki carry out his mission against Kali. (*Kalki Purāṇa* 1.3.1–11)
  - **Who arms him:** Kalki's weapons and mount come not from Rāma but from **Śiva**, whom Kalki praises next (1.3.12 ff.).
  - **A later visit:** the deathless Rāma visits Kalki at Śambhala on pilgrimage and teaches Kalki's wife Ramā the **Rukmiṇī-vrata**, a vow for obtaining a son (ch. 30–31 in the continuous chapter numbering of Bhūmipati Dāsa's translation).
  - On this account one avatāra serves as *guru* to another, which closes the Daśāvatāra loop. Note that Paraśurāma and Rāma Dāśarathi already overlap in the Rāmāyaṇa.

## Paraśurāma and Gaṇeśa's tusk (Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa)

- **Breaking the tusk** — After killing Kārtavīrya and his allies, Rāma bathes in the Narmadā and goes to **Kailāsa** with Akṛtavraṇa to see Śiva and Umā (Brahmāṇḍa 2.3.41.15–18).
  - **The quarrel at the door:** **Vināyaka (Gaṇeśa)** stops him at the door because Śiva and Umā are in private. They argue and then struggle hand to hand until Skanda separates them. Rāma raises his axe, and Gaṇeśa lifts him up, whirls him through the worlds and sets him down again (2.3.41.34–55).
  - **The blow:** Rāma hurls the axe, and Gaṇeśa catches it on his left tusk, which is cut off (2.3.42.1–4). The text notes that the axe was a gift from Gaṇeśa's father (2.3.42.3); Brahmāṇḍa 2.3.32.56–61 identifies Śiva as its giver.
  - **The aftermath:** Pārvatī protests to Śiva (2.3.42.11–13), and Gaṇeśa is thereafter **Ekadanta**, "the one-tusked" (2.3.42.39). [late text]
  - This is one of several Purāṇic accounts of how Gaṇeśa lost his tusk — see [Gaṇeśa and Kārttikeya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md).

## VII. Why he matters

- **The warrior-priest paradox** — Paraśurāma has *kṣātra* (warrior) power in a *brāhmaṇa* body. He is the one avatāra whose duty is **the violent correction of the warrior class**, a counterweight to the kingly avatāras Rāma and Kṛṣṇa. (*Mahābhārata*; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 9.16) [scholarly]
  - The texts themselves draw the moral. The caru story explains the paradox (MBh 3.115.25–28). Jamadagni's rebuke praises forbearance as the brahmin's true strength (BhP 9.15.39–41). Rāma's own ancestors twice restrain him, at Samantapañcaka (MBh 1.2.6) and in the duel with Bhīṣma (5.186.9–30).
  - For the Bhārgava cycle as a whole, see Goldman (1977).
- **The avatāra who does not die** — No source records his death. His death-free status rests on three statements:
  - the Mahābhārata boon of long life (MBh CE 3.116.18);
  - the Bhāgavata's present tense, "he still lives on Mahendra" (BhP 9.16.26);
  - his role in the future. The Bhāgavata names him as one of the **Seven Sages of the next (eighth, Sāvarṇi) Manvantara** (BhP 8.13.15–16; cf. 9.16.25), and the *Kalki Purāṇa* has him alive at the end of Kali.
  - Calling him the only Daśāvatāra figure who never dies is a traditional inference from these passages, not a claim any one text makes. See [The Cirañjīvis](./the-chiranjivis.md).
