# Jaratkāru and Manasā

> Cosmic address: a *Vaivasvata-Manvantara* tale of the present [Mahāyuga's](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md) deep past, embedded in the **Āstīka sub-parva of the [Ādi Parva](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md)** — the human-side hinge that makes the serpent-saving birth of Āstīka possible, and so the counterweight to Janamejaya's snake-sacrifice frame.
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · See also: [Nāgas & the Serpent Races](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md) (Vāsuki, the sarpa-satra) · [Mahābhārata · Ādi Parva](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md) (the Āstīka frame) · [Pitṛs & Ancestors](../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md) (the debt to the fathers) · [Ruru & Pramadvarā](ruru-pramadvara.md) (a parallel serpent-and-marriage cycle in the same Ādi-Parva preamble)

The tale of **Jaratkāru** — the gaunt, lifelong-celibate ascetic who beholds his own ancestors hanging head-down over a pit, learns their salvation depends on his begetting a son, and is forced to abandon his vow of brahmacarya — and his marriage to **Manasā**, the nāga-maiden and sister of the serpent-king **Vāsuki** (who, by a wonderful coincidence, bears the very same name **Jaratkāru**), is the **human pivot of the entire snake-sacrifice frame** of the *Mahābhārata*. From this reluctant union is born **Āstīka**, half-brāhmaṇa, half-nāga, who alone can halt King Janamejaya's *sarpa-satra* and save the surviving serpents from extinction. In its later Bengali afterlife the nāga-bride is elevated into the independent folk-goddess **Manasā**, the snake-mother of the *Manasā-maṅgala* poems and the **Behulā–Lakhindar** legend — a regional cult quite distinct from the epic's frame-tale.

---

## Where it sits in the timeline

- **Frame** - A *Vaivasvata-Manvantara* episode of the present [Mahāyuga's](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md) ancestral past, narrated as part of the *Mahābhārata*'s long preamble. The marriage and the birth of Āstīka precede King Janamejaya's reign; the events are recounted **as flashback** within the court-frame at the *sarpa-satra* itself (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka sub-parva). [scripture]
- **Narrative occasion** - Told by Sauti to the ṛṣis at Naimiṣāraṇya, inside the **Āstīka Parva** (the fifth minor parva of the Ādi Parva), as the explanation of *who Āstīka was* and *why he had the standing* to stop the serpent-holocaust (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva ~1.13–53 Critical Edition). [scripture]
- **Two clocks** - On the **scriptural clock** this is a deep-past Manvantara event within the unbroken epic frame; the **Bengali Manasā cult** (Manasā-maṅgala) is a **historical-Kali** literary-religious phenomenon, datable to roughly the 13th–18th centuries CE in Bengal/Assam, and must not be collapsed into the epic timeline. [scholarly]

## Who Jaratkāru is

- **A great Yāyāvara ascetic** - Jaratkāru is a wandering sage of the **Yāyāvara** line, of immense *tapas*, who roams the earth observing extreme austerity, fasting, and lifelong celibacy (*brahmacarya*), bathing in *tīrthas* and owning nothing (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
- **The name** - "Jaratkāru" is glossed in the epic itself: *jara* = "wasting / consumption" and *kāru* = "huge / fierce," i.e. one whose huge body he reduced to gauntness by fierce austerities — "he who wears away the great." The same etymology is applied to the nāga-maiden he weds, who bears the identical name (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva). [scripture]

---

## The Pitṛs in the pit (the trigger)

- **The ancestors hanging head-down** - Wandering, Jaratkāru comes upon his own **Pitṛs (forefathers)** suspended head-downward over a deep **pit (gartā)**, clinging to a single rope of *vīraṇa*-grass roots — and that rope is being **gnawed away by a rat** (the rat is glossed as Time/Kāla). They are about to fall into the abyss (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
- **Their plight explained** - The ancestors tell the unrecognizing sage that they are sinking toward hell because their **sole surviving descendant** — an ascetic named Jaratkāru — has refused to marry and beget offspring; **the rope is their lineage**, the single remaining strand is that sonless sage, and the gnawing rat is the wasting of their hope. Without a son to continue the line and offer the ancestral rites, they are lost (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). See [Pitṛs & Ancestors](../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md) on the **pitṛ-ṛṇa** (debt to the fathers) and why progeny is a ritual obligation. [scripture]
- **The recognition and the vow** - Stricken, Jaratkāru reveals that **he is that very descendant**, and pledges to **marry and father a son** to rescue them — but, true to his ascetic temper, only on **strict conditions** (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
  - *Theme:* this is the corpus's sharpest dramatization of the conflict between **mokṣa-bound renunciation** and the **householder's debt of progeny** — the ascetic is compelled back into the world precisely *to discharge the pitṛ-ṛṇa*. Compare the renunciation-vs-lineage tension running through the [Pitṛs & Ancestors](../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md) node. [scholarly]

## The marriage conditions

- **A same-named bride, given freely** - Jaratkāru vows he will wed only a girl who (1) bears **his own name**, (2) is **given to him freely / as alms (bhaikṣya-vat)** by her kin, and (3) whom he need not himself support — almost impossible terms, half-designed to keep his vow intact (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
- **A further condition of departure** - He stipulates that the moment his wife **does anything displeasing to him**, he will **abandon her and resume his wandering** — an exit-clause built into the marriage from the start (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]

## Manasā, the nāga-bride (also named Jaratkāru)

- **Vāsuki's sister** - The serpent-king **Vāsuki** has a sister whom the epic names **Jaratkāru** — and, in later/parallel usage, **Manasā**. Vāsuki has been seeking to give her in marriage precisely to **a sage named Jaratkāru**, because the nāgas know by prophecy (the curse of their mother **Kadrū**) that *her son alone* can save the serpent-race from Janamejaya's coming fire (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
  - *The serpents' stake:* Kadrū had cursed her nāga sons to be **burnt in Janamejaya's sarpa-satra** for refusing to deceive Vinatā; **Brahmā** consoled the serpents that **a brāhmaṇa-born son of Jaratkāru** would deliver the righteous among them — so Vāsuki has long been holding his sister for exactly this match (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva). See the curse-and-rescue arc under [Nāgas & the Serpent Races](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md). [scripture]
- **The match completed** - When Jaratkāru announces his terms, the nāgas present their maiden: she is **named Jaratkāru**, she is **offered freely**, and **Vāsuki himself undertakes to maintain her** — every condition met. The ascetic weds her (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
  - *Variant (the two names):* the *Mahābhārata* calls the bride **Jaratkāru** (matching her husband); the names **Manasā / Manasā-devī** for Vāsuki's sister and Āstīka's mother belong to the **Purāṇic and especially Bengali folk strata**, where she becomes a major goddess in her own right. The epic itself does **not** develop her as "Manasā." [scholarly] [regional]

## The abrupt departure

- **The single displeasure** - The couple agree that she will never act against his wishes. One day Jaratkāru, his head resting in her lap, **sleeps past sunset**; fearing he will miss the **twilight (sandhyā) prayers** and incur sin, Manasā gently **wakes him** — and he, holding even this to be a displeasure that voids the marriage-pact, **rises in anger to leave** (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
  - *Variant (the grievance):* most tellings have him reproach her for implying that the **Sun would dare set before his devotions were done** — i.e. his pride at being woken; the act of waking him, harmless in itself, is construed as the contractual "displeasure." [variant]
- **The unborn son guaranteed** - As he departs, the distraught Manasā cries that she is **childless** and his rescue of her family is undone. Jaratkāru reassures her that **a son already lives in her womb**, blazing like fire, who will be a great sage — uttering the words **"asti"** ("there is," i.e. *he exists*), from which the boy will be named **Āstīka**. He then leaves to resume his austerities (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
  - *Etymology of Āstīka:* the name is derived in the epic from his father's parting **"astīti"** ("[the child] exists") — hence **Āstīka**, "He-who-is." [scripture]

## Āstīka, saviour of the serpents

- **Birth and rearing** - **Āstīka** is born to Manasā/Jaratkāru in Vāsuki's realm and reared among the nāgas, schooled in the Vedas by the sage **Cyavana** (Bhārgava line) — half brāhmaṇa by his father, half nāga by his mother, and so uniquely fit to mediate (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
- **The snake-sacrifice** - When King **Janamejaya**, to avenge his father **Parīkṣit** (slain by the nāga **Takṣaka**), undertakes the **sarpa-satra** — a great fire-rite drawing serpents to their death — the nāgas, perishing in the flames, send the young **Āstīka** to intervene (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
- **The boon that halts the holocaust** - Āstīka enters the sacrificial hall, praises the rite and the king with consummate hymns, and Janamejaya, delighted, grants him a **boon**. At the very moment **Takṣaka** is being dragged toward the fire, Āstīka asks that the **sacrifice cease** — saving Takṣaka in mid-air and the **surviving serpents** with him. The king honours his word and stops the rite (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
  - *Frame-significance:* it is at **this same halted sattra** that **Vaiśampāyana** first recites the *Mahābhārata* to Janamejaya — so the Jaratkāru–Manasā marriage is, structurally, the **enabling cause** of both the serpents' survival and the epic's own telling. Cross-link the [Ādi Parva](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md) frame and the [Nāgas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md) sarpa-satra section. [scripture]
- **The blessing** - The grateful serpents grant Āstīka a boon; he asks that **whoever recites his story** be safe from snakebite — the origin of the protective invocation of Āstīka's name still used against serpents (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Āstīka Parva). [folk]

## Manasā as Bengali folk-goddess (the regional overlay)

- **From epic bride to snake-mother** - In eastern India — especially **Bengal, Assam and parts of Bihar/Odisha** — Vāsuki's sister is elevated into the independent **serpent-goddess Manasā** (Manasā-devī, *Bisahari*, *Padmāvatī*, *Janguli*), worshipped as the controller of snakes and curer/sender of snakebite, invoked for fertility and protection. This is a **distinct folk-cult**, not the Sanskrit epic's frame-tale, though it borrows the nāga-sister identity. [regional] [folk]
- **The Manasā-maṅgala poems** - A whole genre of **maṅgala-kāvya** (Bengali narrative-devotional poetry, c. 13th–18th c. CE) tells Manasā's struggle to win human worship. Poets include **Bijoy Gupta**, **Bipradas Pipilai**, **Ketakādās Kṣemānanda**, and others (*Manasā-maṅgala / Manasā-vijaya*). [regional] [late text]
- **The Behulā–Lakhindar legend** - The cult's central story: the goddess Manasā, spurned by the proud merchant **Cānd Sadāgar** (Cā̃d, a devotee of Śiva who refuses to worship her), destroys his sons. On the wedding night of his last son **Lakhindar (Lakṣmīndara)** — though sealed in an iron bridal chamber — Manasā sends a snake that kills the groom. His devoted bride **Behulā** floats with his corpse down the river on a raft for months, reaches the gods' assembly, dances to win their favour, and secures Lakhindar's revival and her family's restoration — finally bending Cānd to honour Manasā (*Manasā-maṅgala*; Behulā–Lakhindar cycle). [regional] [folk]
  - *Variant note:* the iron-chamber-and-snake, the corpse-raft journey, and Cānd's grudging left-handed flower-offering are the cult's signature episodes; details vary widely by poet and locale. The **epic Manasā/Jaratkāru has none of this** — the Behulā legend is a wholly **regional development**. [regional] [disputed]
- **Festival** - Manasā is worshipped chiefly in the rainy season (**Nāga-pañcamī** and a dedicated **Manasā-pūjā**, often last day of Śrāvaṇa), when snakes are abroad — a fertility-and-protection rite of the agrarian east. [regional] [folk]

## Variant accounts across the texts

- **Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, Āstīka sub-parva (~1.13–53)** - The **core narrative**: the Pitṛs in the pit; Jaratkāru's same-name/free-gift conditions; the wedding to **Vāsuki's sister (named Jaratkāru)**; the waking-at-sunset displeasure; the abrupt departure and the "asti" prophecy; the birth and triumph of **Āstīka** at the snake-sacrifice. The bride is **not** called Manasā here. [scripture]
- **Purāṇic strata (Bhāgavata, Brahmavaivarta, Devī-Bhāgavata, etc.)** - Develop **Manasā** as a goddess — variously daughter of **Kaśyapa** (born "of the mind," *manasā*) or of **Śiva**, sister of Vāsuki, wife of the sage **Jaratkāru/Jagatkāru**, mother of **Āstīka** — and supply her divine parentage and quarrels (notably with **Śiva's consort**). Names and parentage differ sharply between texts. [late text] [disputed]
- **Bengali Manasā-maṅgala (c. 13th–18th c. CE)** - The **folk epic**: Manasā's campaign for worship, the **Cānd Sadāgar** feud, and the **Behulā–Lakhindar** martyrdom-and-revival. A regional-Kali literary cult, chronologically and theologically separate from the Sanskrit frame-tale. [regional] [folk]
- *Variant (the bride's name):* **Jaratkāru** (Mahābhārata) = **Manasā** (Purāṇic/Bengali) — the same figure across strata, but the epic keeps her namesake-of-her-husband, while the later tradition makes her a great goddess. [scholarly]

## Themes and significance

- **The debt to the ancestors** - The whole tale turns on the **pitṛ-ṛṇa**: even a perfected ascetic cannot reach release while his fathers fall for want of a son. It is the corpus's starkest statement that **progeny is a ritual duty**, not merely a desire — see [Pitṛs & Ancestors](../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md). [scholarly]
- **Renunciation vs. householdership** - Jaratkāru is the ascetic dragged back into the world to beget one son and no more; his exit-clause and abrupt departure dramatize the **incompatibility of the renouncer's path with the gṛhastha's obligation** — he discharges the debt and flees. [scholarly]
- **Human hinge of the serpent-frame** - Where [Uttaṅka](uttanka.md) supplies the *grudge* that starts the snake-sacrifice, Jaratkāru and Manasā supply the *remedy* that stops it: their son Āstīka is the only being able to halt the holocaust. The marriage is the load-bearing human event of the whole Āstīka frame. Cross-link the [Nāgas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md) sarpa-satra cycle. [scripture]
- **Brāhmaṇa–nāga synthesis** - Āstīka's half-brāhmaṇa, half-serpent birth makes him the perfect mediator between Janamejaya's brahminical rite and the doomed nāgas — a charter for **reconciliation rather than extermination**, the *Mahābhārata*'s characteristic refusal to let even the snake-sacrifice run to genocide. [scholarly]
- **The goddess of the snakes** - In the east, the same nāga-sister becomes **Manasā**, whose Behulā–Lakhindar legend turns the serpent's lethal power into a story of **wifely devotion overcoming death** — a folk reflex echoing [Sāvitrī](savitri-satyavan.md)'s reclaiming of her husband from Yama. [regional] [folk]
