# King Babhruvāhana and the Preta — the Ghost-Tale Charter of the Preta-khaṇḍa

> Cosmic address: a *preta*-narrative the text itself sets **in the Tretā Yuga** of the present [28th Mahāyuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) (Vaivasvata Manvantara), but told from outside time — **Viṣṇu narrating to Garuḍa** in the **Preta-khaṇḍa** of the [Garuḍa Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/garuda-purana.md) (chs. 4–5; retold in the *Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra*, ch. 7 of the 1911 Wood–Subrahmanyam translation). It is the **narrative charter** of the Purāṇa's death-rite doctrine: the story that dramatizes *why* [śrāddha and the pitṛ-rites](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md) are cosmologically necessary.
> Parent: [Deep Dives](README.md)
> **Disambiguation:** this king is **not** [Babhruvāhana the son of Arjuna](characters/babhruvahana.md) and Citrāṅgadā of Maṇipura (a late-Dvāpara figure of the *Mahābhārata*). The Garuḍa Purāṇa's Babhruvāhana is a separate king of the city **Mahodaya**, placed by the text in the **Tretā age**; the two share only the name.

The tale is short — a king lost on a hunt meets a deformed ghost under a fig tree — but it carries the whole weight of the Garuḍa Purāṇa's funerary teaching. The *preta* (the restless departed, stuck between death and ancestorhood) explains **how a life full of merit can still end in ghosthood** if no one performs the rites, prescribes the exact ceremonies that free it (*śrāddha*, the Nārāyaṇa rite, *vṛṣotsarga* — the release of a bull, and the golden gift-pot), pays the king with a jewel, and is duly released to heaven when the king performs them. Every household recitation of the Garuḍa Purāṇa's afterlife chapters ultimately points back to this story as its proof-case: **even a stranger's śrāddha saves the dead**.

---

## I. The Frame — Garuḍa's Question, Viṣṇu's Answer

- **Garuḍa asks how the dead escape Yama's torments** — trembling "like a leaf of the holy fig-tree," **Garuḍa** asks **Viṣṇu (Keśava)** how beings sunk in the ocean of transmigration, their reason clouded by sin, may escape the torments of the servants of [Yama](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/yama.md); Viṣṇu answers with the teaching that frames this tale. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.1–5; *Garuḍa Purāṇa*, Preta-khaṇḍa)
- **The transmission chain** — the whole Preta-khaṇḍa runs on the Purāṇa's frame: **Viṣṇu → Garuḍa**, relayed through Kaśyapa and Vyāsa to the **Sūta**, who recites it to the sages — so the ghost-tale reaches us as Sūta's report of Viṣṇu's own words. (*Garuḍa Purāṇa*, frame; see the [Garuḍa Purāṇa profile](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/garuda-purana.md))
- **Position in the khaṇḍa** — the episode stands near the head of the Preta-khaṇḍa (chs. 4–5 in the Veṅkaṭeśvara-edition numbering), immediately after the opening descriptions of dying and the road to Yama — i.e. the text first states the doctrine, then **grounds it in this story**. (*Garuḍa Purāṇa*, Preta-khaṇḍa 4–5)
  - *Variant (numbering) [scholarly]:* chapter counts of the Preta-khaṇḍa differ widely across recensions (~34–49 chapters), and the digest most readers know — **Naunidhirāma's *Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra***, which quotes a now-lost recension — carries the tale as its **chapter 7**, "Babhruvāhana's Sacrament for the Departed One." Citations below follow the Sāroddhāra's verse numbers where verse-precision matters.

## II. The Lost King — the Hunt

- **Babhruvāhana of Mahodaya, in the Tretā age** — "In the Tretā age there reigned over the delightful city of **Mahodaya** a king named **Babhruvāhana**" — powerful, firm in righteousness, a sacrificer and lord of gifts, a lover of brāhmaṇas, protecting his subjects as though they were his own sons. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.17–19)
- **The wounded deer** — hunting with his army, the king enters a thick forest crowded with beasts and birds, sights a deer, and wounds it severely with an arrow; the deer flees out of sight into the deep forest **carrying the arrow with it**, and the king follows its blood-traces. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.20–23)
- **Lost, exhausted, at the lake and the fig tree** — separated from his army, hungry, throat parched, fainting in the heat, the king reaches a lake of cool water fragrant with lotus-pollen, bathes and drinks, and sits beneath a great **fig tree** with wide, shade-giving boughs. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.24–27)
  - *Motif note:* the lost-hunter opening — a king drawn by a deer out of the ordered world into a liminal wilderness where the dead can speak — is a stock Purāṇic device for staging encounters with the other world; compare the king-and-corpse frame of the [Vetāla tales](vetala-panchavimshati-vikram-and-betal.md) [folk] and the hunter-frame of the [Śivarātri legend](shivaratri-hunter-legend.md).

## III. The Preta Appears

- **A terrible apparition** — under the tree the king beholds a **preta** "of terrible appearance, humpbacked and fleshless, with hair erect, dirty, and with senses discomposed by hunger and thirst" — the classic deformed, starving ghost-body of the unritualized dead. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.28)
- **The preta rejoices at the meeting** — far from menacing the king, the ghost declares itself **blessed by his mere presence**: "I have escaped the condition of the Departed and reached the highest condition, by being in touch with you… I am highly blessed" — contact with the righteous king is itself the hinge of its coming release. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.29–31)
- **The king's questions** — the astonished king asks what evil deeds produced so dreadful a state: "Tell me in detail the cause of your condition… Who are you, and **by what gifts will your condition as Departed pass away?**" — the question that turns the encounter into a ritual instruction. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.32–33)

## IV. The Preta's Autobiography — Sudeva of Vaidiśa

- **A pious merchant, not a sinner** — the ghost answers: "By caste I am a **vaiśya**, by name **Sudeva**," formerly of the prosperous town **Vaidiśa**; in life he pleased the gods with fire-offerings, satisfied the forefathers, gladdened the twice-born with gifts, and fed the poor, the blind and the wretched. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.34–39)
- **"All this has proved fruitless"** — yet all that merit failed at the one decisive point: "I have **no offspring, no companion, no relative and no friend** … who will perform for me the ceremonies for the higher body." Dying **sonless and kinless**, with no one to make the offerings, he fell into the preta-state despite a virtuous life. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.40)
- **The sixteen śrāddhas rule** — the preta states the doctrinal crux: without the **sixteen monthly śrāddhas** (the ṣoḍaśa-śrāddha sequence of the first year), "the condition as Departed becomes **firmly fixed**, even if hundreds of annual śrāddhas are performed for him" — timely rites, not later abundance, effect the passage from preta to [pitṛ](../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md). (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.41)
  - *Doctrinal echo:* this is the narrative form of the Preta-khaṇḍa's teaching that the ten days of *piṇḍa-dāna* and the first-year rites build and settle the departed's new body; without them the dead wanders hungry — see [śrāddha & pitṛ-rites](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md) and [narakas & the afterlife](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/narakas-and-afterlife.md).

## V. The Rites Prescribed — the Preta's Own Liturgy

- **The Nārāyaṇa rite (Nārāyaṇa-bali)** — the preta begs the king to perform for him "the great **Nārāyaṇa** rite," with all the ceremonies for the higher body, accompanied by Vedic mantras — the Vaiṣṇava rite prescribed precisely for those whose deaths leave them ritually stranded. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.46–49)
- **The golden Nārāyaṇa image and the fivefold worship** — an image of Nārāyaṇa is to be fashioned of gold and clothed in a pair of yellow garments; forms of Viṣṇu are worshipped in the several directions together with Śiva, with fire-offerings of clarified butter, curds and milk. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.50–55)
- **Vṛṣotsarga — the release of a bull** — the preta asks for the **setting free of a bull**, the rite the Garuḍa Purāṇa tradition treats as singularly potent for cutting off the preta-condition. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.46–56)
- **The thirteen pots and the golden ghee-pot** — he further prescribes **thirteen sets of pots** given to brāhmaṇas, and above all the **gift of the pot for the departed**: a vessel of refined gold **filled with clarified butter and milk**, with images of **Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śaṅkara (Śiva)** set within it and the **guardians of the directions** figured at its neck, to be given with faith to a worthy twice-born — "This gift, O King… is superior to all other gifts in removing great sins." (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.56–63)
- **The jewel and the vanishing** — as the king's army at last tracks him down with elephants, horses and chariots, the preta presents the king with a **great jewel**, repeats its plea, and becomes **invisible**. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.64–65)

## VI. The Release

- **The king performs the rites** — returned to Mahodaya, Babhruvāhana duly performs the prescribed ceremonies for "the dweller in the upper body" on the preta's behalf — a king ritually standing in for the son the merchant never had. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.66–68)
- **Sudeva attains heaven** — "released by these sacred gifts," the departed one **attains heaven**: preta-hood ends and the passage the rites exist to secure is completed. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.68)
- **The stranger's śrāddha doctrine** — the text draws the moral explicitly: "By the śrāddha performed **even by a stranger** the departed attain a happy state — what wonder then that when the **son** performs it the father should reach it!" (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.68)
- **Phala-śruti** — whoever hears or causes others to hear this history "never goes to the departed condition, though they may have acted sinfully." (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.69)

## VII. Why This Tale Matters — the Charter of the Preta-Rite Doctrine

- **The narrative charter of the Preta-khaṇḍa** — the episode converts the khaṇḍa's ritual injunctions into a story with a witness: a righteous ghost, a royal performer, and a verified result. It is the Garuḍa Purāṇa's own answer to "how do we know the rites work?" — the *preta itself* testifies to what binds it and what frees it. (*Garuḍa Purāṇa*, Preta-khaṇḍa 4–5)
- **Merit is not enough; rites are structural** — the tale's sharpest teaching is that Sudeva was **virtuous**: preta-hood here is not punishment for sin but the *mechanical* result of missing rites — the funerary sequence is presented as cosmologically necessary plumbing, not optional piety. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.34–41)
- **The sonlessness anxiety** — the story dramatizes the dharmaśāstric dread of dying **aputra** (sonless), with no one owing you *piṇḍa* — the same anxiety that drives the [pitṛ-debt](../05-concepts-and-dharma/pancha-maha-yajnas-and-the-three-debts.md) doctrine and the ancestor-feeding pilgrimages to [Gayā](../06-sacred-geography/gaya-pitru-tirtha.md) and the [Vaitaraṇī at Jajpur](../06-sacred-geography/vaitarani-jajpur-viraja-kshetra.md).
- **And its consolation** — simultaneously it *relieves* that dread: **anyone** — even a passing stranger-king — can perform the rites effectually. Release does not finally depend on biology but on someone's willingness to act. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.68)
- **The exchange logic** — the jewel the preta pays the king marks the transaction as *dharmic reciprocity*, not charity: the living give rites, the dead give blessing and wealth — the compact underlying all [pitṛ-cult](../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md) practice. (*Garuḍa-Purāṇa-Sāroddhāra* 7.64–65)
- **Kali-yuga application** — though set in the Tretā age, the tale is recited today precisely because the Preta-khaṇḍa's liturgy (piṇḍa-dāna, ṣoḍaśa-śrāddha, *vṛṣotsarga*, Nārāyaṇa-bali) is the working funerary manual of the present age; the Tretā setting lends it the authority of deep time. (*Garuḍa Purāṇa* tradition)

## VIII. Parallels and Kindred Ghost-Tales — cataloged, not merged

- **Dhundhukārī the preta (Bhāgavata-māhātmya)** — the other great Purāṇic ghost-release story: the wicked **Dhundhukārī**, murdered and become a preta, is freed not by rites but by hearing a **seven-day Bhāgavata recitation** — the *bhakti* counter-model to the Garuḍa Purāṇa's ritual model. The two charters coexist and are not harmonized by the texts. (see [Gokarṇa & Dhundhukārī](bhagavata-mahatmya-gokarna-dhundhukari.md); *Padma Purāṇa*, Bhāgavata-māhātmya)
- **Ajāmila (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.1–3)** — the Vaiṣṇava limit-case in the other direction: the **Name of God** overrides Yama's jurisdiction entirely — where the Babhruvāhana tale magnifies rites, the Ajāmila tale relativizes them. (see [Ajāmila](ajamila.md))
- **Naciketas before Yama (Kaṭha Upaniṣad)** — the oldest template of a living person receiving death-lore from the other side, here as Upaniṣadic metaphysics rather than ritual prescription. (see [Naciketas](nachiketa-katha-upanishad.md))
- **King and talking ghost (Vetāla cycle)** — the folk-literary cousin: a king in a liminal forest interrogating an articulate spirit; entertainment-frame rather than liturgy-frame. (see [Vikram & Betāl](vetala-panchavimshati-vikram-and-betal.md)) [folk]
- **Pretas among the spirit-classes** — on pretas, bhūtas and piśācas as a class of beings (and the preta→pitṛ passage), see [vidyādharas, siddhas & spirits](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md) and [pitṛs & ancestors](../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md).

## IX. Text, Recensions, and the Two Clocks

- **Scriptural clock** — the tale is expressly set in the **Tretā Yuga** ("In the Tretā age there reigned… Babhruvāhana," *Sāroddhāra* 7.17) of the current Mahāyuga; the framing Viṣṇu–Garuḍa dialogue is trans-temporal Purāṇic revelation. (see [Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md))
- **Academic clock [scholarly]** — the Garuḍa Purāṇa is a layered first-millennium-CE compilation (core estimates span the 4th–11th centuries, developed form by c. 900–1000 CE), and the **Preta-khaṇḍa is widely regarded as an appended/accreted section** [late text]; the ***Sāroddhāra*** is a still later **digest by Naunidhirāma**, quoting a now-lost recension, and reached global circulation through the **Ernest Wood & S. V. Subrahmanyam translation (1911)**. Popular "Garuḍa Purāṇa" afterlife lore — including the widely-read form of this tale — largely derives from the Sāroddhāra rather than the Mahā-Purāṇa proper.
- **Which text says what [scholarly]** — the Mahā-Purāṇa's Preta-khaṇḍa (chs. 4–5, Veṅkaṭeśvara ed.) and the Sāroddhāra (ch. 7) tell the same story with differing verse detail; verse-level quotations above follow the Sāroddhāra. Chapter numbering should not be transposed between recensions.
- **Name-collision, again** — scholars treat the Garuḍa Purāṇa's Tretā-age king and the Mahābhārata's [Babhruvāhana of Maṇipura](characters/babhruvahana.md) as unrelated bearers of a common royal name; no Purāṇic passage identifies them. [scholarly]
