# Tāpoi and the Khudurukuṇī Oṣā — the Merchant's Sister and the Goddess of Broken Rice

> Cosmic address: a *Kali-Yuga vrata-charter folk tale* of coastal Odisha, set in the undated age of the **sādhabas** (Odia seafaring merchants) and re-performed every year on the **Sundays of Bhādraba** (Aug–Sep), when unmarried girls keep the **Khudurukuṇī Oṣā** for the goddess **Maṅgaḷā**. Historically [scholarly] an **undatable Odia oral ballad** — no canonical author or date — preserving the social memory of Kaliṅga's maritime trading age.

The **Tāpoi kathā** is the best-loved persecuted-heroine tale of Odia household religion: **Tāpoi**, the cherished only daughter of a rich sādhaba family, is left in the care of her seven sisters-in-law when her **seven brothers sail away on a trading voyage**. Poisoned against her by a wicked mendicant woman, the sisters-in-law strip her of comfort — rags to wear, **khuda (broken rice)** to eat, goats to herd in the wild. In her misery the girl worships the goddess **Maṅgaḷā** with the only offering she has, a handful of broken rice; the goddess protects her until the ships return, the truth comes out, and the tormentors are punished. The tale is the recited kathā — and the founding charter — of the **Khudurukuṇī Oṣā**, the Bhādraba-Sunday vow of unmarried girls of the Odia coast, and behind its household drama stands a whole vanished economy: the **boita** ships of Kaliṅga and the women who waited for them. [folk] [oral tradition] [regional]

## I. The tale and its tradition

- **An oral kathā, not an authored text** — The Tāpoi story circulates as an anonymous **Odia oral ballad and vrata-kathā**: told by grandmothers, recited at the oṣā, and printed in cheap chapbook and palm-leaf versions whose wording varies from district to district. No canonical author, date, or single fixed text exists; the "source" is the living recitation tradition of the **Khudurukuṇī Oṣā** itself. (Tāpoi kathā; Odia oral tradition) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **Genre: the vrata-charter tale** — Like the [Satyanārāyaṇa Vrata Kathā](satyanarayana-vrata-katha.md) (whose merchant hero also sails with laden boats) and the [Lakṣmī Purāṇa](lakshmi-purana-shriya-chandaluni.md) of the Mārgaśira-Thursday vrata, the kathā exists to explain and empower a rite: it narrates the first performance of the worship it charters, and warns what befalls those who scorn the goddess. See the genre survey at [vrata-kathās](vrata-kathas-catalog.md). [folk] [regional]
- **Kin of the merchant-and-goddess epics** — Its nearest literary relatives are the eastern-Indian merchant tales in which a goddess proves herself on a trading family: the Bengali [Caṇḍīmaṅgal's Dhanapati and Śrīmanta voyages](chandimangal-kalketu-and-srimanta.md) and the [Manasā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/manasa.md) cycle of Chand Sadagar (cf. [Jaratkāru and Manasā](jaratkaru-and-manasa.md)) — though Tāpoi's goddess is benign throughout, and the tale's engine is not divine coercion but household cruelty. Catalogued with the vernacular narrative corpus at [regional and oral epics](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md). [regional]
- **The sādhaba world** — The tale assumes its audience knows the **sādhabas**: the seafaring merchant caste-communities of the Odia coast, whose **boitas** (great sailing vessels) left for Southeast Asia and Sri Lanka and returned months later. The family's wealth, the brothers' long absence, and the women-run household they leave behind are all facts of that world. (Tāpoi kathā; Odia maritime lore) [regional] [folk]

## II. The kathā — the pampered daughter

- **A sādhaba house of seven sons and one daughter** — A wealthy sādhaba merchant of the Odia coast has **seven sons** and, born last and long-wished-for, a single daughter, **Tāpoi** — the darling of the whole house, raised in luxury, denied nothing. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The golden moon** — In the tale's most famous emblem of her pampering, the child Tāpoi cries for **the moon itself as a toy**, and her doting father commissions goldsmiths to fashion her a **moon of gold**. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (chapbook versions):* in many tellings the golden moon is **still unfinished** when calamity overtakes the family — a companion (or the malicious mendicant of the next act) taunts Tāpoi that a girl who cannot even get her golden moon is not so cherished after all, and the taunt sets the tragedy in motion; other versions pass directly from the parents' deaths to the voyage. Details of the taunt and the toy vary widely by telling. [oral tradition] [disputed]
- **The parents die** — Tāpoi's mother and father die while she is still unmarried, leaving her to the care of her brothers — and, in practice, of their wives. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The seven brothers sail** — The seven brothers fit out their **boita** and depart on a long **trading voyage across the sea**, entrusting Tāpoi to the seven sisters-in-law with instructions that she be kept as tenderly as ever. The tale's crisis is precisely the sādhaba household's structural fact: the men gone for months on the monsoon circuit, the women alone at home. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]

## III. The torment

- **The mendicant's poison** — A wandering **mendicant woman** (a beggar-brāhmaṇī in common tellings) visits the house and, offended or simply malicious, turns the sisters-in-law against the girl: why should the sister live in silk and idleness while the wives toil? (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **Rags, broken rice, and the goats** — Six of the sisters-in-law strip Tāpoi of her fine clothes and jewels, dress her in **rags**, feed her **khuda — the broken rice-grains** that are the meanest food of a rice country — and send her out daily to **herd the goats** in the scrub, barefoot and alone. The pampered daughter of the house becomes its drudge. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The kind youngest sister-in-law** — In the standard telling **the youngest sister-in-law alone stays kind**, secretly pitying the girl and easing her lot where she can — a mercy the tale remembers when punishment is dealt at the end. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]

## IV. Maṅgaḷā of the broken rice

- **The lost goat** — One day a goat strays and is lost. Terrified of her sisters-in-law's cruelty, Tāpoi wanders the wilds weeping into the night, searching for it. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The worship with khuda** — In her extremity Tāpoi turns to the goddess **Maṅgaḷā** and worships her with the only offering she possesses — her handful of **broken rice** — praying for the goat, for her own deliverance, and for her brothers' safe return. The goddess, pleased by devotion that has nothing left to give but gives it, **restores the lost goat and takes the girl under her protection**. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (the girls at worship):* in many tellings Tāpoi comes upon **unmarried girls keeping Maṅgaḷā's worship on a Bhādraba Sunday** and joins them, offering her khuda when the others offer delicacies — so that the tale contains the oṣā itself, first performed by its own heroine. [oral tradition]
- **The goddess's promise** — Maṅgaḷā assures Tāpoi that her sufferings are ending: the boita is coming home. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]

## V. Return and vindication

- **The boita returns** — The seven brothers' ship comes back laden from across the sea. At the landing-place they find not the sister they left in silk but a **ragged goatherd girl** — and in the recognition scene (the kathā's tear-jerking set piece) Tāpoi pours out everything that was done to her. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (the hidden sister):* tellings differ on the staging — in some the youngest brother finds her weeping among the goats by the ghat; in others she steals aboard or is brought to the ship before she reveals herself. [oral tradition]
- **The punishment of the sisters-in-law** — The brothers resolve on a reckoning. In the widespread telling, **Tāpoi is seated veiled in state as the goddess Maṅgaḷā**, and each sister-in-law is summoned to worship her; as each bows, the deception of each is exposed, and the **six cruel wives have their noses cut off** — the classic folk punishment of the treacherous woman — while **the kind youngest is spared and honored**. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (softened endings):* the nose-cutting is standard in traditional recitations but is softened in some modern retellings to disgrace, contrition, and forgiveness; as ever the corpus records both rather than choosing. [oral tradition] [disputed]
- **The charter** — Restored to her place (and, in many tellings, married thereafter with due splendor), Tāpoi's vindication becomes the precedent: because Maṅgaḷā accepted a wronged girl's broken rice and set her world right, **unmarried girls keep the goddess's oṣā ever after**. (Tāpoi kathā) [folk] [oral tradition]

## VI. The oṣā it charters — Khudurukuṇī

- **The observance** — The **Khudurukuṇī Oṣā** is kept on the **Sundays of the month of Bhādraba** (Bhādrapada, Aug–Sep) by **unmarried girls**, above all in the **coastal districts of Odisha** (the old sādhaba tracts — Balasore/Bhadrak, Kendrapara, Jajpur, Cuttack, Puri, Ganjam). Girls rise early to **gather flowers** and bathe, install and adorn an image of **Maṅgaḷā**, and worship her in the evening with the **Tāpoi kathā recited** as the rite's liturgy. (Khudurukuṇī Oṣā practice) [folk] [regional]
- **The goddess who delights in broken rice** — The oṣā's defining offering is **khuda**: broken rice, sweetened and cooked, is central to the bhoga, in memory of Tāpoi's offering. The epithet **Khudurukuṇī** is popularly glossed as **"she who delights in khuda"** — a goddess whose charter-story obliges her to prize the poorest offering on the coast. (Khudurukuṇī Oṣā practice; popular etymology) [folk] [regional]
- **What the girls pray for** — The vow is kept for the **welfare of brothers** — the tale's sailors — and for the girls' own good fortune and future households: a sister's rite in a landscape where brothers once disappeared over the horizon for months at a time. (Khudurukuṇī Oṣā practice) [folk] [regional]
- **Maṅgaḷā in the Odia pantheon** — Maṅgaḷā ("the auspicious one") is a beloved **regional goddess of Odisha**, worshipped in village shrines across the coast (see the [grāmadevatā frame](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/gramadevatas-village-deities.md)) and assimilated to the great Goddess (cf. [Devī forms](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-forms-and-navadurga.md)). Her most famous seat is **Kakatpur** in the Prāchī valley (Puri district); in [Jagannātha temple tradition](../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md) it is **Maṅgaḷā of Kakatpur who reveals in dream the location of the sacred dāru (neem) trees** to the search party of the Nabakalebara rite. Coastal lore makes her a guardian of the sādhabas and their voyages. (Odia temple and folk tradition) [regional] [folk]

## VII. A window on maritime Kaliṅga [scholarly]

- **The historical seafaring behind the tale [scholarly]** — Kaliṅga (coastal Odisha) sustained **overseas trade with Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia** — the lands remembered as Suvarṇabhūmi/Suvarṇadvīpa (Burma, Java, Sumatra, Bali) — attested from the early historic period and flourishing into medieval times. The kathā's boitas, its months-long absences, and its wealthy merchant household are the folk memory of that economy. [scholarly]
- **The festival cluster of the sea** — Ta'poi's oṣā belongs to a whole Odia calendar of maritime remembrance: on **Kārtika Pūrṇimā**, **Boita Bandāṇa** sets miniature boats afloat at dawn with the cry *"Ā kā mā bai…"* (popularly glossed as abbreviating the auspicious sailing months), and Cuttack's great **Bali Jātrā** fair commemorates the voyages to Bali; the autumn sailing season itself tracked the **turn of the monsoon winds** [scholarly]. The Khudurukuṇī Sundays fall earlier, in Bhādraba — the waiting season of the women at home. (Odia festival tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **A women's-eye view of the trade [scholarly]** — Scholars of Odia folklore read the kathā as the **women's version of the maritime age**: where Boita Bandāṇa remembers the departure, Tāpoi remembers the household the sailors left behind — its precarious dependents, its sister-in-law politics, and a girl's security hanging on absent brothers. Folklorists also note the tale's kinship with the worldwide **persecuted-heroine (Cinderella-type) tale family**, here fused to a goddess-vrata charter. [scholarly]
- **Living afterlife** — The kathā remains a staple of Odia children's literature, school readers, Odissi dance-drama, and film adaptations, and the oṣā is still kept on the coast — a folk observance, never absorbed into any Sanskrit purāṇic canon. (contemporary Odia culture) [regional]

## VIII. The two clocks

- **Scriptural/cyclic frame** — The tale is set in a **timeless Kali-Yuga past** of the sādhaba coast: no yuga-marker, no vaṁśa anchor, no dated king. Its time signature is **calendrical and recurring** — the Sundays of Bhādraba, every year, when the kathā is recited and Tāpoi's broken-rice offering is made again.
- **Academic/historical frame [scholarly]** — An **undatable oral ballad**: the maritime world it remembers spans roughly the early historic through medieval centuries of Kaliṅga's Southeast Asia trade; the kathā itself survives only in modern oral recitation, palm-leaf and chapbook prints, with no authorial or manuscript date. The Khudurukuṇī Oṣā is living coastal-Odia practice, documented by folklorists of Odisha as a principal girls' oṣā of the Bhādraba season. [scholarly]
