# Dambhodbhava — the World-Conqueror Humbled by Nara-Nārāyaṇa

> Where this sits: a warning *upākhyāna* narrated by **Paraśurāma inside the Mahābhārata war-frame** — told to Duryodhana in the Kaurava court during Kṛṣṇa's failed peace embassy. Parent: [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · Embedded in the [Udyoga Parva](./mahabharata/05-udyoga-parva.md) (Bhagavad-yāna sub-parva) · Canonical companion: [Nara–Nārāyaṇa](./nara-narayana.md). Keep the **two clocks** distinct: the inset events happen "in days of yore" (*purā*) during the twin sages' age-spanning *tapas*; the *telling* is fixed to the eve of Kurukṣetra at the Dvāpara–Kali cusp.

The **Dambhodbhava-upākhyāna** (Mahābhārata, Critical Edition **5.94**; Ganguli, Udyoga Parva Section **XCVI**) is the epic's compact parable against martial vainglory — and one of its most explicit **charter texts identifying Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa as the ancient sages Nara and Nārāyaṇa**. A universal emperor who cannot stop asking "is anyone my equal in battle?" is directed to two emaciated ascetics in the Himālaya, insists on fighting them against every courtesy and warning, and is routed — army and all — by **Nara wielding nothing but a handful of grass-blades**. Paraśurāma's punchline to Duryodhana: those two sages now stand on the Pāṇḍava side as Arjuna and Kṛṣṇa; make peace before the grass-blades become Gāṇḍīva's arrows.

---

## I. The frame — Paraśurāma warns Duryodhana

- **Setting: the Bhagavad-yāna court scene** — After [Kṛṣṇa's embassy speech](./mahabharata/05-udyoga-parva.md) for peace in the Hāstinapura assembly, great *ṛṣi*s present in the court add their voices to dissuade **Duryodhana** from war. **Rāma Jāmadagnya ([Paraśurāma](./parashurama.md))** speaks first among them, offering an old tale as a mirror for the prince's pride. (Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva, Bhagavad-yāna; CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **A triptych of court-parables** — Paraśurāma's Dambhodbhava tale opens a sequence of admonitory narrations in the same scene: **Kaṇva** follows with the story of **Mātali** (the charioteer's search among the nāgas for a son-in-law, ending with Sumukha), and **Nārada** with the long tale of [Gālava and Mādhavī](./galava-madhavi.md) against obstinacy. All three are aimed at the same unmoved listener, [Duryodhana](./characters/duryodhana.md). (Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva, Bhagavad-yāna; Ganguli Udyoga XCVI–CXXIII)
- **Why Paraśurāma is the fitting teller** — The speaker is himself the paradigm of invincible martial power renounced: the Bhārgava who annihilated the kṣatriyas twenty-one times and withdrew to austerity (see [Jamadagni & Paraśurāma](./jamadagni-parashurama.md)). A warning about the futility of challenging ascetics carries maximum weight from the warrior-ascetic. [scholarly]

## II. Dambhodbhava, emperor "born of arrogance"

- **A sārvabhauma of days of yore** — "There was in days of yore a king named **Dambhodbhava**," Paraśurāma begins — a universal sovereign (*sārvabhauma*), master of the whole earth, whose power no rival checked. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **The name as the moral** — *Dambha* means arrogance, hypocrisy, ostentatious pride; **Dambhodbhava** = "he who springs from arrogance." The king is a personification of the vice the tale condemns — the same *dambha* the [Bhagavad Gītā](./bhagavad-gita.md) later lists among demonic qualities (Gītā 16.4). [scholarly]
- **The daily boast** — Rising each morning, the king would ask his brāhmaṇas and kṣatriyas: **"Is there anyone — be he śūdra, vaiśya, kṣatriya, or brāhmaṇa — who is superior, or even equal, to me in battle?"** Swollen with this question he roamed the earth, hungry for a challenger. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **The brāhmaṇas' rebuke and disclosure** — Learned brāhmaṇas, unafraid, told him to stop glorifying himself; when he persisted in his demand, they named his superiors: **two among men whom he could never equal in battle — the ascetics Nara and Nārāyaṇa**, then performing austerities on the mountain **Gandhamādana**. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
  - *Note (Badarī and Gandhamādana):* the Udyoga text locates the twins on **Gandhamādana**; the wider epic and Purāṇic tradition fixes their hermitage as **Badarikāśrama (Badarī)** on the Gandhamādana range — the same site; see [Badrināth / Badarikāśrama](../06-sacred-geography/badrinath-badarikashrama-kshetra.md) and [Nara–Nārāyaṇa](./nara-narayana.md). (Mahābhārata; Bhāgavata Purāṇa)

## III. The march to the hermitage and the demand for battle

- **The expedition against two ascetics** — Stung, Dambhodbhava at once mustered his army — the classical **six-limbed force (*ṣaḍ-aṅga-bala*)** — and marched to Gandhamādana to seek the sages out. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **Finding the twins wasted by tapas** — He discovered **Nara and Nārāyaṇa** emaciated by hunger, thirst, wind, sun, and long austerity — outwardly the least warlike beings imaginable. He approached and touched their feet, and was received with courtesy: the sages asked after his welfare and what they might do for him. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **"Give me battle"** — The king announced that he had conquered the entire earth by the strength of his arms and had come to fight the sages, demanding combat as the hospitality (*ātithya*) he had long sought. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **The sages' repeated refusal** — The *ṛṣi*s answered that their hermitage was a place from which **wrath and covetousness were banished**: no battle could happen there, no weapons existed there, and no crookedness dwelt there; let him seek combat elsewhere, for the earth held many kṣatriyas. Though dissuaded again and again, Dambhodbhava kept insisting on a fight. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)

## IV. Defeat by grass-blades

- **Nara takes up the iṣīkā reeds** — At last **Nara** picked up **a handful of grass-blades (*iṣīkā* reeds)** and said: "Fight, then, O kṣatriya eager for war — take up all your arms and marshal your troops." (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **The rout of the six-limbed army** — Dambhodbhava's soldiers loosed showers of arrows; Nara hurled the grass-blades, which became irresistible missiles that **filled the sky and pierced the eyes, ears, and noses of the attacking host** without slaying them — until the very welkin seemed white with grass-blades. The army was helpless against an unarmed ascetic. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
  - *Note:* the humble reed made an all-penetrating missile by ascetic power is a recurring epic motif of *tapas* trumping weaponry — compare the blade of grass Brahmāstra-charged elsewhere in the epic; see [Divyāstras & divine weapons](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/divyastras-and-divine-weapons.md). [scholarly]
- **The surrender** — Seeing his forces mutilated and the sky whitened, the emperor **fell at the sage's feet crying for protection (*śaraṇa*)**. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **Nara's sermon of dismissal** — Granting him safety, Nara commanded the king: **be obedient to the brāhmaṇas and be righteous; never act so again; conquer your senses; be humble and free of vanity; never insult anyone, high or low; solicitous of the good of all, go and rule.** Chastened, Dambhodbhava saluted the *ṛṣi*s, returned to his city, and practiced righteousness ever after. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)

## V. The application — Arjuna is Nara, Kṛṣṇa is Nārāyaṇa

- **The charter identification** — Paraśurāma drives the point home: exceedingly great was that ancient feat of **Nara** — and **Nārāyaṇa is superior even to Nara** by countless further qualities. Those very two are now born among men: **Arjuna (Dhanañjaya) is Nara and Kṛṣṇa (Vāsudeva/Hṛṣīkeśa) is Nārāyaṇa** — the eternal *ṛṣi*s descended by their own will. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **The specific warning** — Let Duryodhana therefore cast off pride and make peace **before the arrows loosed from [Arjuna's](./characters/arjuna.md) Gāṇḍīva** — no mere grass-blades this time — consume the Kaurava host; challenging Nara-Nārāyaṇa embodied is Dambhodbhava's folly repeated at the scale of the whole kṣatriya race. (Mahābhārata CE 5.94 / Ganguli Udyoga XCVI)
- **One node in the epic's Nara-Nārāyaṇa theology** — This tale is the Udyoga Parva's contribution to a thread running through the whole epic: Bhīṣma and Droṇa invoke the same identification when begging Duryodhana for peace, and the [Nārāyaṇīya](./narayaniya.md) of the Śānti Parva gives the doctrine its fullest form (the soul Nara eternally attending the Lord Nārāyaṇa). The deep-dive on the twins is [Nara–Nārāyaṇa](./nara-narayana.md); the roster line sits in [Mahābhārata minor episodes](./mahabharata-minor-episodes.md). (Mahābhārata, Udyoga & Śānti Parvas) [scholarly]
- **The warning ignored** — Like Nārada's Gālava tale after it, the parable fails: Duryodhana refuses to yield even a needle-point of land, the embassy collapses, and the war the tale was told to avert follows. (Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva, Bhagavad-yāna)

## VI. Variants and later reception

- *Variant (folk/modern conflation with Sahasrakavaca):* popular retellings — televised Mahābhāratas, oral kathā, and modern devotional literature — merge Dambhodbhava with the demon **Sahasrakavaca ("thousand-armored")**: by a boon he wears a thousand armors, each breakable only by a foe with a thousand years of *tapas*, whose breaker instantly dies; **Nara and Nārāyaṇa alternate — one meditating while the other fights, each reviving the other** — and shatter 999 armors, whereupon the demon flees with one armor left and is **reborn as [Karṇa](./karna.md)**, born wearing the last *kavaca* (and *kuṇḍala*). This narrative is **not in Mahābhārata 5.94**, which knows no armors, no boon, and no Karṇa link; it circulates as late/folk material sometimes vaguely attributed to Purāṇic sources. Catalog it, do not merge it into the epic tale. [folk] [oral tradition] [late text] [disputed]
- *Variant (numbering across editions):* the episode is **CE 5.94** (Bhagavadyāna sub-parvan of the Udyoga Parva); in Ganguli's translation it is Udyoga Parva **Section XCVI**; vulgate/Bombay numbering places it around Udyoga 96. Same text, different verse-count conventions. [scholarly]
- **Ethical afterlife of the tale** — Retellers use Dambhodbhava as the stock exemplum of *dambha*: strength without humility invites humiliation, and the truly powerful (the ascetic, the avatāra) need no weapons. Its pairing of comic disproportion (an empire's army vs. a fistful of grass) with a serious charter claim (who Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna really are) is why the epic places it at the war's last diplomatic hinge. [scholarly]

## VII. The two clocks

- **Scriptural/cyclic frame** — The inset tale is dated only as *purā*, "of old," within the twin sages' perpetual, age-spanning *tapas* at Badarī/Gandhamādana — effectively a cross-yuga deep past. The *frame* — Paraśurāma's telling in the Kaurava court — belongs to the last days of **Dvāpara Yuga**, on the eve of Kurukṣetra at the Dvāpara–Kali junction (Kali reckoned from 3102 BCE in the traditional count). (Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva)
- **Academic/historical frame** — [scholarly] Text-historically, the Udyoga Parva's Bhagavadyāna belongs to the Mahābhārata's long composition and redaction, conventionally placed c. **400 BCE – 400 CE**; the Nara-Nārāyaṇa identification of Kṛṣṇa-Arjuna is generally read as part of the epic's Vaiṣṇava theological layering. The Critical Edition (Pune, 1933–66) fixes the episode as 5.94.
