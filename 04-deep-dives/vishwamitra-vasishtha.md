# Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha — Kṣatriya to Brahmarṣi

> **Where this sits:** A deep-dive into the longest-running ṛṣi rivalry in Hindu tradition — the kṣatriya king Viśvāmitra's long struggle to win **brahmarṣi** status, with the brahmarṣi Vasiṣṭha as rival and, finally, witness. Its episodes thread through the [Ṛgveda](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md), the [Aitareya Brāhmaṇa](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md), the [Rāmāyaṇa](ramayana/) & [Mahābhārata](mahabharata/), and the Purāṇas. Vasiṣṭha is the hereditary purohita of the Ikṣvākus of the **[Sūryavaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)**; Viśvāmitra is by birth a Kuśika of the Lunar line, descended through Bharata in the Mahābhārata (13.4.2–6) and through Purūravas's son Amāvasu in the Viṣṇu Purāṇa (4.7.1–11), but his career runs through Ikṣvāku kings — Triśaṅku, Ambarīṣa, Hariścandra, Kalmāṣapāda, Rāma.
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](./) · Cosmic address: Śveta-Varāha Kalpa › 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara › 28th Mahāyuga, mostly **[Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** (Viśvāmitra survives into Rāma's era), with Ṛgvedic seer-tradition layered atop.

The Viśvāmitra–Vasiṣṭha cycle is the paradigmatic Hindu narrative of **earned vs. born status**: whether a kṣatriya can, by tapas alone, vault into brahmin-hood and ultimately into the rank of **brahmarṣi** ("brahman-seer"). That is the Rāmāyaṇa's frame, and the texts disagree sharply on tone. The Ṛgveda shows both men as priests of the Bharata king Sudās, though whether it records a quarrel between them is disputed. The Aitareya Brāhmaṇa makes Viśvāmitra an adoptive father and ritual founder, with Vasiṣṭha sitting at the same rite. The Rāmāyaṇa frames the whole arc as a moral parable of tapas vs. wrath. The Mahābhārata and Purāṇas amplify the cruelty and the mutual destruction, and one Mahābhārata passage denies the premise outright, saying Viśvāmitra was never truly a kṣatriya (Mahābhārata 13.4.59, §9 below). All are catalogued below; none is harmonised.

**How the Rāmāyaṇa is cited here:** sarga numbers are those of the vulgate/Southern text at valmikiramayan.net, which sibling pages also use. In the Baroda Critical Edition (CE) the same sargas in this stretch run **one lower** (vulgate 19 = CE 1.18; vulgate 51–65 = CE 1.50–1.64; vulgate 74 = CE 1.73), and verse references marked "CE" are to that edition. Mahābhārata references are to the BORI Critical Edition.

## Dramatis personae & the two names

- **Viśvāmitra (Kauśika, Gādheya, Gādhija)** - born a kṣatriya king, son of **Gādhi**. The Rāmāyaṇa gives the line as Kuśa (a son of Prajāpati) → Kuśanābha → Gādhi → Viśvāmitra (Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa 51 = CE 1.50.18–19). The Mahābhārata makes Gādhi a king of **Kānyakubja** (MBh 1.165.3) and traces him through the Bharata line, Ajamīḍha → Jahnu → … → Kuśika → Gādhi (MBh 13.4.2–6). **Kauśika** ("of Kuśa's/Kuśika's line") is a patronymic used of him throughout, not a birth-name he later lost. In the Rāmāyaṇa he is already called **Viśvāmitra** while he rules as king (Bāla 51 = CE 1.50.19–20); the popular claim that "Viśvāmitra" was a title won by tapas is not Vālmīki's.
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa):* Kuśāmba does tapas for a son equal to Indra, and **Indra himself is born as his son Gādhi**, "Kauśika" (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.7.9–11).
  - *Variant (the caru exchange — Mahābhārata Anuśāsana Parva; Viṣṇu Purāṇa):* the Bhārgava sage **Ṛcīka**, married to Gādhi's daughter **Satyavatī**, prepares two consecrated portions of rice (caru): one charged with brahman-power for his wife, one with kṣatriya-power for her mother, Gādhi's queen. Mother and daughter swap them. Satyavatī begs that the warrior nature pass to her grandson rather than her son, so she bears the brahmin **Jamadagni**, and the grandson is the warrior-brahmin **Paraśurāma**; Gādhi's queen bears **Viśvāmitra**, a kṣatriya's son with a brahmin's nature (MBh 13.4.24–47; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.7.17–36). Viśvāmitra is therefore Jamadagni's maternal uncle and Paraśurāma's great-uncle, not his cousin. → [Jamadagni & Paraśurāma](jamadagni-parashurama.md).
- **Vasiṣṭha** - one of the **Saptarṣi**; in the Rāmāyaṇa he is "Brahmā's son" (*brahmaṇaḥ sutaḥ*, Bāla 52 = CE 1.51.6), while the Ṛgveda calls him **Maitrāvaruṇa**, born "from the mind of Urvaśī", a drop the gods held in a lotus, and born at a sacrifice from the seed Mitra and Varuṇa poured into a pitcher (RV 7.33.11–13). The Mahābhārata also calls him Varuṇa's son, **Āpava** (MBh 1.93.5). He is the hereditary **purohita of the Ikṣvākus**, husband of **Arundhatī**, and keeper of the wish-cow. He is already a brahmarṣi from the outset; the drama is entirely Viśvāmitra's to resolve.
- **Title at stake:** the rank-ladder Vālmīki walks him up is **rājarṣi (royal seer) → ṛṣi → maharṣi → brahmarṣi**. Brahmā grants each rank in turn; at the last, Viśvāmitra will not count it as won until **Vasiṣṭha** says it (Bāla 65 = CE 1.64.13–15). → rung by rung, with the verses, at [Viśvāmitra, §III](characters/vishvamitra.md).

## 1. The Kāmadhenu / Nandinī conflict — the spark

- **The visit and the hospitality** - In the Rāmāyaṇa King Viśvāmitra is touring the earth at the head of an akṣauhiṇī when he reaches Vasiṣṭha's hermitage (Bāla 51 = CE 1.50.21–28). Vasiṣṭha presses hospitality on him and feasts the entire host using his wish-cow (Bāla 52–53 = CE 1.51.12–1.52.7).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva):* Viśvāmitra arrives hunting, tired and thirsty (MBh 1.165.5–6).
  - *Variant (Rāmāyaṇa Bāla Kāṇḍa):* the cow is **Śabalā**, also called *kalmāṣī* ("the speckled one"), *kāmadhuk* and *kāmadhenu*; she produces every food and drink for the army (Bāla 52–53 = CE 1.51.20–1.52.4; CE 1.53.1). The Critical Edition's Bāla Kāṇḍa never calls her **Surabhi**; that name belongs to the Mahābhārata and Purāṇic strands, and importing it here would merge two cow-traditions the Rāmāyaṇa keeps apart.
  - *Note on who is telling the story:* the whole Viśvāmitra saga (Bāla 51–65) is **not narrated by Viśvāmitra on the road**. It is told at **Mithilā by Śatānanda**, son of Gautama and Ahalyā and Janaka's family priest, to Rāma (Bāla 51 = CE 1.50.16). Śatānanda's first questions are whether his mother has been shown to Rāma and honoured him (CE 1.50.4–8). So the cycle is a *report on* Viśvāmitra rather than his own testimony, and its narrator is the son of the woman Rāma has just freed.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, Vāsiṣṭha-upākhyāna):* the cow is **Nandinī** (MBh 1.165.15–24). The tale is told by a gandharva to Arjuna.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, the Vasus' theft):* Vasiṣṭha's sacrificial cow is a daughter of **Surabhi** by Kaśyapa (MBh 1.93.8–9). This is the basis for the later "Nandinī, daughter of Kāmadhenu/Surabhi". [The timeline catalogues both: **Śabalā** in the Rāmāyaṇa, **Nandinī** in the Mahābhārata.]
- **The demand and refusal** - Viśvāmitra offers a hundred thousand cows for her, then fourteen thousand gold-harnessed elephants, eight hundred golden chariots, eleven thousand horses and a crore of cows (Bāla 53 = CE 1.52.9, 17–20). Vasiṣṭha refuses: his oblations, rites and offerings to gods and ancestors all depend on her (CE 1.52.13–14, 22–24). Viśvāmitra drags her off by force (Bāla 54 = CE 1.53.1).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* the offer is an *arbuda* (a hundred million) of cows **or his kingdom** (MBh 1.165.16).
- **The cow's army** - Śabalā breaks free, runs back to Vasiṣṭha and asks why he has abandoned her. He answers that a king is stronger than he is; she replies that a brahmin's power is greater than a kṣatriya's, and he tells her to create an army (*sṛjasva*) (Bāla 54 = CE 1.53.5–17). From her bellow come **Pahlavas**; when Viśvāmitra cuts them down, **Śakas mingled with Yavanas**. Then from her bellow come **Kāmbojas**, from her udder Pahlavas, from her womb Yavanas, from her dung Śakas, and from her hair-pores **mlecchas, Hārītas and Kirātas**, and they destroy his army (Bāla 54–55 = CE 1.53.18–1.54.4). [The list is a charter-myth explaining foreign/mleccha peoples as cow-born.]
  - *Vulgate only:* **Barbaras** appear among the peoples routed by Viśvāmitra's missiles (vulgate Bāla 54.23); the translator at valmikiramayan.net notes that other manuscripts read Pahlavas there.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* Nandinī produces Pahlavas from her tail, Śabaras and Śakas from her dung, Yavanas from her urine, and Puṇḍras, Kirātas, Dramiḍas, Siṃhalas, Barbaras, Daradas and mlecchas from her foam (MBh 1.165.35–36). In this telling **none of Viśvāmitra's soldiers is killed**; the army is driven off three yojanas (MBh 1.165.39–40).
- **The sons slain** - Viśvāmitra's **hundred sons** rush at Vasiṣṭha and are **burnt to ash by his huṃkāra** alone (Bāla 55 = CE 1.54.5–7). Viśvāmitra puts his one remaining son on the throne and goes to the Himālaya to do tapas (CE 1.54.11–13). The Mahābhārata's constituted text has no slaying of his sons.
- **The weapons contest** - **Śiva** grants him the whole science of archery and every astra known to gods, dānavas, ṛṣis, gandharvas, yakṣas and rākṣasas (Bāla 55 = CE 1.54.15–18). He returns, burns the hermitage, and looses the āgneya, then a long roster of named missiles, and finally the **brahmāstra**; Vasiṣṭha's **brahmadaṇḍa (brahmin's staff)** swallows every one (Bāla 55–56 = CE 1.54.20–1.55.17).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* the missile duel, with Vasiṣṭha parrying by a bamboo staff, is in an inserted passage that the Critical Edition prints only in its apparatus (MBh 1.165.40, appendix passage 97); it is not part of the constituted text.
- **The lesson learned** - Defeated and humiliated, Viśvāmitra cries *dhig balaṃ kṣatriyabalaṃ brahmatejobalaṃ balam* — "shame on kṣatriya-strength; the strength of brahman-power alone is strength!" He resolves on the tapas "that makes one a brahmin" (Bāla 56 = CE 1.55.23–24) — the hinge of the entire cycle.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* the same half-verse, ending instead "tapas alone is the highest strength" (MBh 1.165.42). He gives up his kingdom, does tapas, attains brahminhood and drinks soma with Indra (MBh 1.165.43–44).

## 2. The escalating mutual feud (Mahābhārata / Purāṇic amplification)

- **Slaughter of Vasiṣṭha's hundred sons** - The bitter Mahābhārata telling (Ādi Parva, Vāsiṣṭha-upākhyāna) runs as follows; → full treatment at [Kalmāṣapāda (Saudāsa)](kalmashapada-saudasa.md).
  - **The curse.** The Ikṣvāku king **Kalmāṣapāda** meets **Śakti**, eldest of Vasiṣṭha's hundred sons, on a narrow path, and whips him when he will not give way. Śakti curses him to become a man-eater (MBh 1.166.1–10).
  - **The quarrel behind it.** The feud is over **which sage will be the king's sacrificial priest** (*yājya-nimittam*) (MBh 1.166.11).
  - **The rākṣasa.** Viśvāmitra, standing by unseen, orders a rākṣasa named **Kiṃkara** to enter the king (MBh 1.166.14–17). A brahmin who is served human flesh by the king's cook repeats the curse (MBh 1.166.20–33).
  - **The killings.** The possessed king eats Śakti, and then, at Viśvāmitra's direction, the rest of Vasiṣṭha's sons (MBh 1.166.36–38).
- **Vasiṣṭha's restraint and grief** - Vasiṣṭha bears the grief and "never thought of destroying the Kuśikas" (MBh 1.166.39–40). He tries to die instead, and nothing will kill him:
  - he throws himself from a peak of Meru and lands as if on cotton (MBh 1.166.41–42);
  - he enters a forest fire, which turns cool (MBh 1.166.42–43);
  - he sinks in the sea with a stone tied to his neck, and the waves throw him ashore (MBh 1.166.44–45);
  - he binds himself with cords and drowns himself in a river in flood; the river cuts the cords, and he names it **Vipāśā** ("unbound"; the Beas) (MBh 1.167.4–6);
  - he leaps into a Himālayan river, which flees from him in a hundred streams and is called **Śatadru** (the Sutlej) (MBh 1.167.8–9).
  - *Note:* the same two rivers, as **Vipāś and Śutudrī**, are the ones Viśvāmitra's Ṛgvedic hymn persuades to let the Bharatas cross (RV 3.33.1, 3.33.11–12). The epic itself does not draw the link.
- **Parāśara** - Turning home, Vasiṣṭha hears the Veda being recited behind him. It is the unborn son of Śakti, reciting from the womb of his widow **Adṛśyantī**, and Vasiṣṭha gives up his wish to die (MBh 1.167.11–15).
  - He frees Kalmāṣapāda from the rākṣasa with a huṃkāra and consecrated water (MBh 1.168.3–4). At the king's request, he fathers his heir **Aśmaka** on the queen (MBh 1.168.12–25). The cause is given later: a brahmin woman whose husband the cursed king had eaten had cursed him to die if he lay with his wife, and foretold that Vasiṣṭha would father his son (MBh 1.173.16–24).
  - The child is named **Parāśara** because he revived Vasiṣṭha when he was *parāsu*, "as good as dead" (MBh 1.169.3).
  - Told that his father was eaten, Parāśara resolves to destroy all the worlds. Vasiṣṭha talks him out of it with the story of the Bhārgava **Aurva** (MBh 1.169.9–1.171). Parāśara instead begins a **sacrifice to burn all rākṣasas**, which **Pulastya** and Vasiṣṭha persuade him to end (MBh 1.172.1–16).
  - So Vasiṣṭha's side of the feud closes in restraint, but it is not bloodless.
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa):* Parāśara himself recalls that his father was "eaten by a rākṣasa set on by Viśvāmitra" and that he began the rākṣasa-sattra in anger (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.1.12–14).
  - Links forward to the [Mahābhārata corpus](mahabharata/).
- **The Sarasvatī episode (Vasiṣṭhāpavāha)** - The Mahābhārata's tīrtha tour tells another round of the feud.
  - Vasiṣṭha's hermitage is at Sthāṇutīrtha on the Sarasvatī, on the east, with Viśvāmitra's to the west, and the two contend daily in tapas (MBh 9.41.3–9).
  - Viśvāmitra orders the river to bring Vasiṣṭha to him "so that I may kill him today". Sarasvatī, afraid of both sages' curses, tells Vasiṣṭha, who bids her obey (MBh 9.41.10–23).
  - She cuts away her own bank and carries him across. When she sees Viśvāmitra reaching for a weapon, she carries him away again, eastward (MBh 9.41.26–34).
  - Viśvāmitra curses her to flow mixed with blood for a year, and rākṣasas gather to drink it (MBh 9.41.35–9.42.3). The place is called **Vasiṣṭhāpavāha**, "where Vasiṣṭha was carried off" (MBh 9.41.39).
- **Note on tone:** the Rāmāyaṇa keeps Vasiṣṭha a serene victor and makes Viśvāmitra the striver; the Mahābhārata and Purāṇas dwell on atrocity before reconciliation. Both are preserved here, not harmonised.

## 3. Centuries of tapas, and the rank-by-rank ascent

- **The staircase** - The Rāmāyaṇa (Bāla Kāṇḍa 57–65) hands the ranks over one at a time:
  - **rājarṣi** after a thousand years of austerity in the south (Bāla 57 = CE 1.56.3–5);
  - **ṛṣi** after a thousand years at Puṣkara (Bāla 63 = CE 1.62.1–2);
  - **maharṣi** after a thousand years on the Kauśikī (Bāla 63 = CE 1.62.14–18);
  - **brahmarṣi** only at the end (Bāla 65 = CE 1.64.10–11).

  He receives the first rank with bitterness, as proof his tapas has borne no fruit (CE 1.56.6–8). At the third he asks outright for brahmarṣi and is told he has not yet mastered his senses (CE 1.62.19–21). The last thousand years are done under a **vow of silence** (*mauna*) (CE 1.64.2), and it is the conquest of anger, rather than any further heat, that buys the title.
  - *Vulgate only:* the vulgate adds Viśvāmitra's resolve not to speak, not to breathe and not to yield to anger until he is a brahmin (vulgate Bāla 64.17–19). It also has **Indra**, disguised as a brahmin, begging the meal Viśvāmitra has cooked at the end of his thousand-year fast; he gives it all without a word (vulgate Bāla 65.4–7). The Critical Edition says only that "many obstacles" assailed him and anger never entered (CE 1.64.3).
  - → rung by rung, with Brahmā's verses, at [Viśvāmitra, §III](characters/vishvamitra.md).
- **What the ladder does to the rivalry** - Brahmā and the devas award every rank, the last included: they hail him *brahmarṣe* and grant him brahminhood (CE 1.64.10–11). Viśvāmitra makes his acceptance conditional on Vasiṣṭha saying it (CE 1.64.13–15).
  - Every reset on the way up is charged to desire or wrath: Menakā (§5), the Triśaṅku curses (§4) and the Rambhā curse (§5). The Rāmāyaṇa's own word for what he must conquer is *kāma-krodha*, desire and anger (CE 1.63.11).
  - So the cycle's arithmetic is fixed early: **tapas accumulates, desire and wrath spend it**, and only the rival's own mouth closes the account (§9).

## 4. The Triśaṅku-svarga episode

- **The episode in outline** - The Rāmāyaṇa tells it in stages (Bāla 57–60 = CE 1.56.9–1.59.33). → full treatment, the Harivaṃśa prelude of the three sins and the twelve-year famine, and the astronomical afterlife, at [Triśaṅku](trishanku.md); Viśvāmitra's side of it at [Viśvāmitra, §IV](characters/vishvamitra.md).
  - **The refusals.** Vasiṣṭha tells the Ikṣvāku king **Triśaṅku** that a sacrifice to reach heaven in his own body is "impossible". When the king goes over his head to Vasiṣṭha's hundred sons, they **curse him to become a caṇḍāla** (CE 1.56.12–1.57.8).
  - **The curse on Vasiṣṭha's sons.** Viśvāmitra takes up the outcast king's cause "out of compassion" (*kāruṇya*, CE 1.57.12) and summons the sages to the rite. Vasiṣṭha's sons and the sage **Mahodaya** refuse with contempt: how can gods or brahmins eat the offering of a caṇḍāla whose priest is a kṣatriya? Viśvāmitra curses the sons to be burnt to ash and reborn for **seven hundred births** as corpse-handling, dog-eating Muṣṭikas, and Mahodaya to become a Niṣāda (CE 1.58.12–22).
  - **The ascent.** The gods do not come for their shares, so Viśvāmitra sends Triśaṅku up by his own tapas. **Indra** turns the king back to fall head-downward (CE 1.59.10–17).
  - **The counter-heaven.** Viśvāmitra halts him in mid-air and creates a second set of Seven Seers and a new garland of stars in the southern sky. He vows, "I will make another Indra, or the world shall have none", and begins to create gods (CE 1.59.18–22).
  - **The compromise.** The gods agree that his stars shall stand outside the path of Vaiśvānara, with Triśaṅku among them **head-downward** (CE 1.59.27–31).
- *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa — a non-rivalry cause):* Satyavrata, become a caṇḍāla as Triśaṅku, feeds **Viśvāmitra's wife and children** with venison through a twelve-year drought, and the grateful sage raises him bodily to heaven (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.3.21–24). The Mahābhārata's brief allusion has a royal seer **Mataṅga**, "who had become a hunter", supporting Viśvāmitra's wife in a famine, Viśvāmitra then sacrificing for Mataṅga, and Viśvāmitra creating a counter-row of stars beginning from Śravaṇa (MBh 1.65.31–34, in Menakā's speech). The passage does not name Triśaṅku.
- **Why it belongs to the rivalry** - In the Rāmāyaṇa it is the one occasion on which Viśvāmitra performs a rite Vasiṣṭha has declared impossible and makes it work. He proves he can do what his rival will not, curses his rival's sons doing it, and comes out of it further from brahmarṣi than he went in: he calls it "a great obstacle" and leaves the south (CE 1.60.1–2).

## 5. The Menakā and Rambhā disruptions

- **Menakā — ten years** - In the Rāmāyaṇa the apsarā **Menakā** comes to bathe at Puṣkara. Viśvāmitra invites her to stay, and she lives in his hermitage for **ten years** (*pañca pañca ca varṣāṇi*). He then realises "this is all the gods' doing, to steal my tapas", **sends her away with gentle words** rather than a curse, and resumes harder tapas on the Kauśikī in the north (Bāla 63 = CE 1.62.4–14).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* **Indra** explicitly sends her, fearing for his throne. She protests that she is afraid of the man "who deprived the great Vasiṣṭha of his beloved sons", and asks for the wind and Kāma as allies; the wind lifts her garment (MBh 1.65.20–42, 1.66.1–5).
  - **Birth of Śakuntalā** - In the Mahābhārata their daughter, abandoned by the Mālinī river in the Himālaya, is protected by birds (*śakunta*, hence her name) and raised by the sage Kaṇva (MBh 1.66.8–17). She later becomes mother, by King Duṣyanta, of **Bharata**, eponym of Bhārata-varṣa. This is the Mahābhārata's material, not Vālmīki's; Kālidāsa's *Abhijñāna-śākuntalam* dramatises it [late kāvya]. → [Śakuntalā & Bharata](shakuntala-bharata.md) · [Sūryavaṃśa & Candravaṃśa lineages](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **Rambhā — ten thousand years of stone** - Indra tries again with **Rambhā**, promising to stand beside her with Kāma and a cuckoo in a spring-flowering tree (Bāla 64 = CE 1.63.1–6).
  - Viśvāmitra sees the trap and **curses her to stand as stone for ten thousand years** (*daśa varṣa-sahasrāṇi*, CE 1.63.11; vulgate Bāla 64.12), until a brahmin of great austerity releases her (CE 1.63.11–12). The "thousand years" of popular retelling is a slip; the vulgate and the Critical Edition agree on ten thousand.
  - The curse works and ruins him: unable to hold back his anger he loses his tapas, and finds no peace because his senses are unconquered (CE 1.63.13–15). The vulgate adds his vow from that hour never again to yield to anger and not to speak at all (vulgate Bāla 64.17).
  - This is the moral turning point of the cycle. → both disruptions in full at [Viśvāmitra, §VI](characters/vishvamitra.md).

## 6. The Hariścandra wager (truth-test)

- **The ordeal in outline** - Viśvāmitra strips the Ikṣvāku king **Hariścandra** of his kingdom and wealth as dakṣiṇā, and keeps demanding more. The king sells his queen **Śaivyā** and his son into servitude, and himself to a caṇḍāla of the burning-ground at Kāśī. At the nadir, with the boy dead of snakebite, he demands the cremation fee from his own grieving wife rather than break his master's rule. The gods then declare the whole thing a test and restore all three (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 7–8; Devī-Bhāgavata 7.18–27). → full treatment, both strata and the Kāśī topography: [Hariścandra — the King Who Never Lied](hariscandra.md).
  - *Names:* the queen is **Śaivyā** in both the Mārkaṇḍeya and the Devī-Bhāgavata. The son is **Rohita / Rohitāśva** in the Devī-Bhāgavata (the wisdomlib transcription of Pargiter's Mārkaṇḍeya prints "Rohitāsya"). The name **Tārāmatī** for the queen is found in neither and belongs to later retellings [later/popular].
  - *How it starts (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 7):* out hunting, Hariścandra hears women crying. They are the sciences (*vidyā*) that Viśvāmitra is mastering by tapas. Possessed by the obstacle-spirit Vighnarāja, the king threatens the sage, then submits and promises him his whole kingdom. **Vasiṣṭha is absent**: he has been living in the waters of the Gaṅgā for twelve years (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 9).
  - *How it starts (Devī-Bhāgavata 7.18):* Hariścandra disturbs Viśvāmitra's tapas for "Siddharūpiṇī". The sage sends a demon **boar** to ravage the royal gardens, then meets the lost king in the forest disguised as an old brahmin.
- **Why it belongs to the rivalry** - Only the **Devī-Bhāgavata** frames the ordeal as a **wager between the sages**, and only in that text are the two sages contending over a third party's virtue.
  - After Vasiṣṭha has served as hotṛ at Hariścandra's Rājasūya, the two meet in **Indra's hall**. Vasiṣṭha declares there never was nor will be a king so truthful. Viśvāmitra retorts that Hariścandra once **cheated Varuṇa** over the promised son, so he is a liar. Each sage stakes **all the merit he has earned since birth** (Devī-Bhāgavata 7.17.41–59).
  - The **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa has no wager** and keeps Vasiṣṭha offstage until the end.
  - In both texts Viśvāmitra plays **guru-tester rather than enemy** at the close. → see [Hariścandra §The wager between the sages](hariscandra.md).
- **The sequel: the Āḍi–Baka war** - The ordeal is the cause of the sages' most grotesque fight.
  - *Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 9:* emerging from the water after twelve years, Vasiṣṭha hears what was done to his royal disciple. He curses Viśvāmitra to become a **baka** (heron), and Viśvāmitra curses him back into an **āḍi** (Pargiter translates it "mainā"). The birds grow to two thousand and three thousand and ninety yojanas, and their fighting shakes mountains and seas. **Brahmā** restores their forms and reminds them that Viśvāmitra had in fact helped the king to heaven; ashamed, they embrace (Pargiter's translation, canto IX).
  - *Variant (Devī-Bhāgavata 6.12–13):* the same curses (Viśvāmitra a crane, Vasiṣṭha a *śarāri*/āḍi bird). They nest on trees by **Mānasarovara** and fight for ten thousand years, until Brahmā frees both and makes them friends. Vyāsa tells it as proof that even Vasiṣṭha, "residing on the banks of the Ganges", was not free of love and hatred (Devī-Bhāgavata 6.12–13).

## 7. The Śunaḥśepa sacrifice (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa — the oldest layer)

- **The oldest Hariścandra story, and Viśvāmitra's part in it** - In the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa the story runs:
  - Hariścandra vows his first-born to **Varuṇa** and keeps deferring the sacrifice.
  - His son **Rohita** buys a substitute: **Śunaḥśepa**, middle son of the starving ṛṣi **Ajīgarta Sauyavasi**, for a hundred cows. For a second hundred the father agrees to bind his own child, and for a third to slay him (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.15–16).
  - At that rite **Viśvāmitra is the hotṛ, Jamadagni the adhvaryu, Vasiṣṭha the brahman and Ayāsya the udgātṛ** (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.16). The two rivals officiate side by side.
  - Bound to the stake, the boy prays himself free in Ṛgvedic verses (RV 1.24–30). He sits in Viśvāmitra's lap, and Viśvāmitra refuses to return him to Ajīgarta: "the gods have given him to me." He becomes **Devarāta Vaiśvāmitra**, whose descendants are the **Kāpileyas and Bābhravas** (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.17).
  - Viśvāmitra had **a hundred and one sons**. When he orders them to accept Devarāta as senior, the fifty older than **Madhuchandas** refuse and are cursed: their offspring become the **Andhras, Puṇḍras, Śabaras, Pulindas and Mūtibas** beyond the borders. Madhuchandas and the younger fifty accept (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.18).
  - → full treatment: [Hariścandra](hariscandra.md); the sons' curse: [Viśvāmitra §V](characters/vishvamitra.md).
  - *Later genealogy (Viṣṇu Purāṇa):* the "Bhārgava" Śunaḥśepa, given by the gods and so named Devarāta, heads the list of Viśvāmitra's sons, whose lines become the many **Kauśika gotras** (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.7.37–39). The Aitareya itself names only the Kāpileyas and Bābhravas.
- *Variant (Rāmāyaṇa Bāla 61–62 = CE 1.60–1.61):* the episode is moved to **Ambarīṣa** of Ayodhyā.
  - Indra steals Ambarīṣa's sacrificial animal, and the king buys **Śunaḥśepa**, middle son of the Bhārgava **Ṛcīka**, for a hundred thousand cows (CE 1.60.5–21).
  - At Puṣkara the boy throws himself on Viśvāmitra's mercy. Viśvāmitra asks his own sons, led by Madhuṣyanda, to take his place. They refuse, and he curses them to live a thousand years as dog-eaters "like the sons of Vasiṣṭha" (CE 1.61.1–16).
  - He gives the boy **two divine verses**. Sung at the stake to **Indra and Indra's younger brother (Upendra)**, they win him long life from Indra (CE 1.61.17–25).
  - There is no adoption and no Devarāta in this version.
- *Variant (Devī-Bhāgavata 6.13 and 7.14–17):* the human sacrifice is kept. Vasiṣṭha, as family priest, **advises the ailing Hariścandra to buy a brahmin boy** (Devī-Bhāgavata 6.13.7–9). Viśvāmitra begs the king to free Śunaḥśepa and, refused, gives the boy a **Varuṇa-mantra** that frees him (Devī-Bhāgavata 6.13.10–36; Varuṇa's release retold at 7.17.1–22). At the assembly that follows, it is **Vasiṣṭha who rules that the boy is Viśvāmitra's son** (Devī-Bhāgavata 7.17.23–40).
- *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa):* the Purāṇa keeps the Aitareya's priestly roster at Hariścandra's sacrifice (Bhāgavata 9.7.22).
- **Why this layer matters to the rivalry** - Here Viśvāmitra is a **ritual founder and adoptive father**, not a wrathful rival, and he shares the rite with Vasiṣṭha. The oldest stratum of his legend has him building a lineage rather than fighting one.
  - The Mārkaṇḍeya's ordeal of §6 drops the human sacrifice. The Devī-Bhāgavata keeps both stories in sequence, and makes Hariścandra's evasion of Varuṇa the very charge Viśvāmitra raises in the wager.
  - The corpus keeps them as distinct strata — **Aitareya = oldest, ritual-founding; Rāmāyaṇa = rescue by mantra; Purāṇic = later, ethical-testing** — and does not harmonise them.

## 8. Viśvāmitra in the Ṛgveda (the seer, not the striver)

- **Seer of Maṇḍala 3** - In the Ṛgveda itself Viśvāmitra is a living seer, the traditional author of **Maṇḍala 3**, the family book of the Viśvāmitras and Kuśikas (RV 3.1.21, 3.53.12–13 name the Viśvāmitras; 3.26, 3.29, 3.30 the Kuśikas). Maṇḍala 7 is ascribed to Vasiṣṭha (Macdonell & Keith, *Vedic Index* II, 274).
- **The Gāyatrī mantra** - He is the seer (*draṣṭṛ*) of **RV 3.62.10 — the Sāvitrī / Gāyatrī**, *tat savitur vareṇyaṃ bhargo devasya dhīmahi / dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt* (Ṛgveda 3.62.10).
- **The rivers hymn (RV 3.33)** - "**Kuśika's son**" persuades the rivers **Vipāś and Śutudrī** to lower their waters so that the **Bharatas** can cross — a famous dialogue-hymn (Ṛgveda 3.33.1, 5, 9–12). The hymn itself does not name Sudās.
- **Viśvāmitra and Sudās** - **RV 3.53** links Viśvāmitra with the Bharata king **Sudās**. "When Viśvāmitra escorted Sudās, Indra was pleased with the Kuśikas" (3.53.9). The Kuśikas are bidden to loose Sudās's horse to win him wealth (3.53.11). "This prayer of Viśvāmitra protects the Bharata people" (3.53.12).
- **Vasiṣṭha and Sudās** - **Maṇḍala 7** celebrates Sudās's victory in the **Battle of the Ten Kings** (*dāśarājña*), won through the Vasiṣṭhas' prayers (RV 7.18, 7.33.3–6, 7.83.6–8). RV 7.18.21 names **Parāśara** and **Śatayātu** beside Vasiṣṭha.
- **The rivalry in the Vedic layer** [scholarly] [disputed] - None of these hymns names the other sage, and whether the Ṛgveda records a quarrel at all is contested. The case is laid out in Macdonell & Keith's *Vedic Index* (II, 274–276, s.v. Vasiṣṭha):
  - **For a rivalry:** Viśvāmitra was "certainly at one time" Sudās's purohita (Yāska, *Nirukta* 2.24, also calls him so). He seems to have been deposed from that post, and Macdonell and Keith read the hymn of Sudās's triumph as showing that he then joined Sudās's enemies.
  - **Against:** **Oldenberg** held that the strife is not in the Ṛgveda at all.
  - **Śakti's death:** **Geldner** read RV 3.53.15–16 and 21–24 as a compressed account of Viśvāmitra's contest with Śakti and his revenge; Macdonell and Keith doubt it. Verses 21–24 are the traditional **Vasiṣṭha-dveṣiṇyaḥ** ("Vasiṣṭha-hating" imprecations). The Nirukta commentator Durga declined to explain them because he was a Vasiṣṭha himself, and "what the verses really mean is not at all certain" (*Vedic Index* II, 275 n. 9, citing the Bṛhaddevatā 4.117 ff.).
  - **Ritual texts:** the Taittirīya Saṃhitā and Pañcaviṃśa Brāhmaṇa mention Vasiṣṭha's sons being slain and his overcoming the Saudāsas. Later tradition (Ṣaḍguruśiṣya; the Śāṭyāyanaka) tells of Śakti's death at the hands of Sudās's servants, and the Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa (2.390), like the Śāṭyāyanaka, says the Saudāsas cast him into the fire. Yet the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa says Vasiṣṭha anointed Sudās Paijavana with the great anointing of Indra (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 8.21) (*Vedic Index* II, 275–276).
  - This corpus records the epic feud as possibly built on a real Vedic priestly competition for the Bharata court, not as an established fact.

## 9. The winning of "Brahmarṣi"

- **Vasiṣṭha's word** - In the Rāmāyaṇa the gods, led by Brahmā, come to Viśvāmitra, hail him *brahmarṣe*, and grant him brahminhood and long life (Bāla 65 = CE 1.64.10–12). It is **Viśvāmitra himself** who sets the last condition: if he truly has brahminhood, let Om, *vaṣaṭ* and the Vedas accept him, and let **Vasiṣṭha, son of Brahmā, say so** (CE 1.64.13–15).
- **The reconciliation** - Propitiated by the gods, Vasiṣṭha **makes friendship with him** (*sakhyaṃ cakāra*) and says, "You are a brahmarṣi, no doubt of it" (*brahmarṣitvaṃ na saṃdehaḥ*). Viśvāmitra in turn honours Vasiṣṭha, and then wanders the earth, his aim fulfilled (Bāla 65 = CE 1.64.16–19). Neither the Critical Edition nor the vulgate has an embrace scene or an approach by Viśvāmitra to Vasiṣṭha's door; it is the gods who prevail on Vasiṣṭha.
  - *Variant [folk]:* a night scene in which Viśvāmitra, coming sword in hand, overhears Vasiṣṭha **praising Viśvāmitra's tapas** to Arundhatī; his murderous intent dissolves and he surrenders, earning the title by the conquest of his own wrath. It is found neither in Vālmīki nor in the Mahābhārata passages cited on this page.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva):* no ladder and no Vasiṣṭha. After his defeat Viśvāmitra simply does tapas, attains brahminhood, and drinks soma with Indra (MBh 1.165.43–44).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Śalya Parva):* at a Sarasvatī tīrtha, after his soldiers have ravaged Vasiṣṭha's hermitage and been routed by Śabaras from Vasiṣṭha's cow, Viśvāmitra endures the gods' repeated attempts to break his vows. **Brahmā** then grants his request, "may I be a brahmin" (MBh 9.39.12–29). The same tīrtha is where **Sindhudvīpa** and **Devāpi** also attained brahminhood (MBh 9.39.10).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva):* **Dharma**, disguised as Vasiṣṭha, comes hungry to Viśvāmitra's hermitage but does not wait for the meal the sage is cooking. When Viśvāmitra brings it, "Vasiṣṭha" says he has already eaten, tells him to wait, and goes. Viśvāmitra stands motionless for **a hundred years**, living on air, with the hot food held on his head, until "Vasiṣṭha" returns, eats it and declares himself pleased. By Dharma's word Viśvāmitra passes from kṣatriya-hood to brahminhood (MBh 5.104.8–18). This is the frame of the **Gālava** story.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Anuśāsana Parva — the premise denied):* because Ṛcīka's brahman-power was placed in the caru that Gādhi's queen ate (§Dramatis personae), "this Viśvāmitra is not a kṣatriya" at all. He was born a brahmarṣi, and his many sons founded brahmin gotras (MBh 13.4.46–59), though the same passage still calls him a kṣatriya who founded a brahmin line (MBh 13.4.47). On this view nothing was earned against birth, which cuts against the Rāmāyaṇa's whole thesis; the corpus records it as a variant, not a correction.
- **Result** - The Viṣṇu Purāṇa lists Viśvāmitra among the **Saptarṣis of the present (Vaivasvata) manvantara**, alongside Vasiṣṭha, Kāśyapa, Atri, Jamadagni, Gautama and Bharadvāja (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.1.32).
  - He is the most famous figure in tradition to rise from kṣatriya birth to brahmarṣi by tapas, though not the only one the epic knows (Sindhudvīpa and Devāpi above).
  - This is the cycle's whole point in the Rāmāyaṇa: **status can be earned**, but only through total mastery of self, and only when freely acknowledged by one already established.

## 10. Afterlife of the cycle — Viśvāmitra & Rāma

- **The boy-guru of Rāma** - Now a serene brahmarṣi, Viśvāmitra opens the **Rāmāyaṇa proper**. He arrives at Daśaratha's court (vulgate Bāla 18 = CE 1.17.23) and takes the young **Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa** to guard his sacrifice. He has them kill **Tāṭakā**; at the sacrifice Rāma **hurls Mārīca a hundred yojanas into the sea and kills Subāhu** (Bāla 30 = CE 1.29.14–19). He gives Rāma the **divine astras**, narrates the Gaṅgā-descent, and leads the brothers to **Mithilā** and the bow. He takes his leave for the northern mountain after the weddings (Bāla 74.1 = CE 1.73.1). → see [Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive](ramayana/).
- **Why he needs a boy at all** - This is the question the whole Bāla Kāṇḍa turns on, and Viśvāmitra answers it himself: he is **under dīkṣā**, the consecration of the sacrifice. "I am not minded to loose my anger … such is that observance; no curse is to be uttered during it" (Bāla 19 = CE 1.18.7; paraphrased; cf. the translation by Desiraju Hanumanta Rao at valmikiramayan.net). A man who could burn Mārīca to ash with a word may not spend a word in anger while the rite stands. So the brahmarṣi who conquered his own wrath must **borrow a kṣatriya's arm**, which is why Daśaratha's son, not the sage, kills.
- **The armorer's own arsenal — the sons of Kṛśāśva** - What Viśvāmitra hands over is not technique but **lineage**.
  - The missiles he commands are the children of the sage **Kṛśāśva** by Dakṣa's daughters **Jayā** and **Suprabhā**, fifty sons each; Suprabhā's fifty are the *saṃhāras* (Bāla 21 = CE 1.20.13–17). They are conferred on Rāma as a strike-roster and a matching roster of *saṃhāras*, and appear before him in bodily form to ask what he wishes done (Bāla 27–28 = CE 1.26–1.27).
  - That is the epic's own theory of what a divine weapon is. It also answers why a **brahmarṣi rather than a war-guru** arms Rāma, which is the rivalry's whole point about what the last rank confers.
  - → parentage, the two rosters and the donor question in full: [The sons of Kṛśāśva — the astra-genealogy](krishashva-astra-genealogy.md); object registry: [Divyāstras](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/divyastras-and-divine-weapons.md).
- The tranquil teacher of the Bāla Kāṇḍa is the narrative reward of the entire tapas arc: the man who once burned with kṣatriya wrath now **bestows weapons yet teaches restraint** — and, bound by his own vow, cannot lift one himself.
