# Hariścandra — the King Who Never Lied

> Cosmic address: a Sūryavaṃśa (Solar-dynasty) king of the **Tretā Yuga** (the Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa frame is older still, projecting him into Ṛgvedic antiquity). A deep-dive episode-file. Up: [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · Lineage: [../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) · Antagonist-sage: [./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md](./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md)

**Hariścandra** (सत्यहरिश्चन्द्र, *Satya-Hariścandra*, "Hariścandra of the Truth") is the proverbial truth-teller of Hindu memory — the king who, rather than utter one falsehood, surrendered his kingdom, sold his queen and son, took service under an outcaste at the burning-ground of Kāśī, and stood ready to demand his fee even for the cremation of his own dead child. His name became a byword (a "Hariścandra" = an impossibly honest man) and the touchstone for *satya* (truth) as the supreme *dharma*. Two quite different legends bear his name: an archaic Vedic sacrifice-tale ([Aitareya Brāhmaṇa](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md)) in which his son is to be killed and the brahmin boy **Śunaḥśepa** is substituted; and the far more famous Purāṇic ordeal-by-truth (**Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa**, **Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa**) driven by the sage **Viśvāmitra**. Both are catalogued below.

---

## Frame & placement

- **Lineage and seat** - Hariścandra is a king of the **Sūryavaṃśa / Ikṣvāku** line, ruling **Ayodhyā** (the Kosala capital); in the Purāṇic genealogies he is son of **Triśaṅku** (the king who Viśvāmitra hung in the sky, see [./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md](./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md)) and an ancestor of **Rāma** (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.13–18; Purāṇic vaṃśa-lists). See [../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **When** - The Purāṇic ordeal is set in the **Tretā Yuga**, the age that still tolerates such public proofs of dharma; the Vedic Śunaḥśepa frame is undated but projects him into hoary Ṛgvedic antiquity, prior to the Purāṇic narrative architecture. The exemplary placement matters more than a fixed mahāyuga slot — Hariścandra is the *standing exemplar of truth*, invoked across ages.
- **Why two stories** - The single name carries (a) the **Vedic** "son-sacrifice / Śunaḥśepa" myth and (b) the **Purāṇic** "sold-into-slavery-but-never-lied" myth; the **Devī Bhāgavata** consciously stitches the two together, having Viśvāmitra cite the *old* Śunaḥśepa affair as grounds for testing the king afresh.

## The Vedic legend — Hariścandra, Rohita & Śunaḥśepa (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa)

The **earliest** extant Hariścandra story, told to frame the *rājasūya* consecration and the famous self-redeeming hymns of Śunaḥśepa (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa **7.13–18**; retold Śāṅkhāyana / Sāṅkhyāyana Śrauta Sūtra 15.17–27).

- **The vow to Varuṇa** - Hariścandra, who has a hundred wives but no son, is counselled by **Nārada** to pray to **Varuṇa**; Varuṇa grants a son on the condition that the boy be **sacrificed back to Varuṇa**. A son, **Rohita** (Rohitāśva), is born (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.13–14). See [Varuṇa among the lokapālas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md).
- **The endlessly postponed sacrifice** - When Varuṇa comes to claim the child, the king pleads a series of ritual delays — *let him cut his teeth, grow his teeth, shed them, regrow them; let a kṣatriya's son first bear arms* — until the boy reaches manhood (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.14).
- **Rohita flees** - Told at last he is to die, **Rohita takes his bow and escapes to the forest**, wandering for six years; **Indra**, in the guise of a brahmin, repeatedly meets him and urges *"wander on"* (*caraiveti*) — the celebrated hymn to the virtue of wandering (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.15).
- **Varuṇa's curse** - For the broken vow Varuṇa afflicts Hariścandra with **dropsy / a swelling belly-illness** (*jalodara*) (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.15).
- **The purchased substitute** - In the sixth year Rohita meets a starving ṛṣi, **Ajīgarta Sauyavasi** (a descendant of Aṅgiras), with three sons; Rohita **buys the middle son, Śunaḥśepa, for one hundred cows** to be sacrificed in his place. The father will not sell the eldest, the mother will not give up the youngest — so the middle boy is taken (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.16).
- **The father as executioner** - At the altar no one will bind or slay the boy until **Ajīgarta himself agrees — for another hundred cows to bind him, and a third hundred to butcher his own son** — a grotesque mark of the brahmin's degradation (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.16).
- **Śunaḥśepa's self-rescue by hymn** - Bound to the stake, **Śunaḥśepa prays in turn to Prajāpati, Agni, Savitṛ, Varuṇa, the Viśvedevas, Indra, the Aśvins, and Uṣas** (the Dawn); as he recites, his **bonds fall away of themselves** and Hariścandra is simultaneously **cured** (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.16–17).
- **Adoption by Viśvāmitra** - **Viśvāmitra**, the *hotṛ*-priest of the rite, **adopts Śunaḥśepa as his eldest son**, renaming him **Devarāta** ("god-given"); when fifty of Viśvāmitra's own hundred sons refuse to accept the foundling as elder brother, the sage **curses them to become outcastes** — an aetiology of the Andhras, Puṇḍras, Śabaras, etc. (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.17–18). See [./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md](./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md).
  - *Note [scholarly]:* In this archaic version Hariścandra is **morally compromised** — he tries to substitute a brahmin child for his own — and the hero is really **Śunaḥśepa**. The later "King Who Never Lied" inverts this entirely, making Hariścandra a paragon. The two should not be conflated. [disputed identity]

## The Purāṇic legend — the ordeal of truth (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa)

The famous tale, the basis of all popular retellings, drama, and cinema, is told in the **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa** (chs. **7–8**, the *Hariścandra-upākhyāna*, in the dialogue of the birds). Narrated by sage [Mārkaṇḍeya](./markandeya.md). Tretā Yuga.

### The wager between the sages

- **Vasiṣṭha's praise, Viśvāmitra's doubt** - In the [Devī Bhāgavata](../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md) framing (carried into popular Mārkaṇḍeya retellings), **Vasiṣṭha** praises Hariścandra as the most truthful of kings; **Viśvāmitra** disputes it and undertakes to **prove the king will break** under pressure — the rivalry of the two great sages is the engine (cf. [./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md](./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md)).
- **The hunt and the cry** - While hunting, Hariścandra hears women crying; they are personified **Vidyās** (sciences/arts) whom Viśvāmitra is mastering by tapas. The king "rescues" them, **disrupting Viśvāmitra's penance** — giving the sage his pretext (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 7).
- **The open-ended promise** - To appease the furious sage, Hariścandra promises him **whatever he asks**. Viśvāmitra claims the **dakṣiṇā (sacrificial fee) for a rājasūya / aśvamedha** and, separately, **demands the entire kingdom** — "everything except your body, your wife, and your son" (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 7).
  - *Variant (dream-gift):* In one strand, the king **gives away his whole kingdom in a dream** and, on waking, treats the dream-donation as a binding vow that must be honoured in waking life — a striking proof of his refusal to let even a sleeping word be false. [folk] / [late text]

### Progressive deprivation

- **The kingdom surrendered** - Hariścandra hands over Ayodhyā and all its wealth to Viśvāmitra, keeping only the bark-cloth on his back, his queen, and his son (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 7).
- **The added dakṣiṇā** - Viśvāmitra then demands a **separate gold dakṣiṇā** for the sacrifice — money the now-destitute king does not have. He is given a grace period (often **one month**) to pay, and sets out for **Kāśī (Vārāṇasī)**, holiest of cities, to earn it (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8). See [../06-sacred-geography/sapta-puri-and-sacred-cities.md](../06-sacred-geography/sapta-puri-and-sacred-cities.md).
- **The harassment in Kāśī** - In Kāśī, Viśvāmitra (or his agent, sometimes named **Nakṣatra**) dogs the king, demanding payment of the debt while leaving him no means to earn it (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8).

### The selling of the family

- **The queen and son sold** - To raise the dakṣiṇā, Hariścandra **sells his queen and son into bondage** to an old brahmin, the queen weeping as she is led away with the boy clinging to her (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8).
  - *Queen's name:* given as **Śaivyā** in the Purāṇic Sanskrit; the South-Indian and stage tradition overwhelmingly calls her **Candramatī / Tārāmatī (Chandramati)**; the son is **Rohitāśva** (the same Rohita of the Vedic tale, now an innocent child). [variant naming]
- **The king sells himself to a Cāṇḍāla** - The sale of wife and son still does not cover the fee; the remainder is exacted by **selling Hariścandra himself** — and the only buyer is a **Cāṇḍāla (outcaste keeper of the cremation ground)**. The kṣatriya recoils at slavery to an untouchable, but Viśvāmitra, as his "owner," **sells him to the Cāṇḍāla for gold** anyway (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8).
  - *Cāṇḍāla's identity:* the outcaste is revealed to be **Yama / Dharma himself in disguise** (in some retellings named **Vīrabāhu / Pravīra**), the whole arrangement being a divine test. See [Yama / Dharma](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md).

### The cremation ground of Kāśī

- **Tax-collector of the dead** - The Cāṇḍāla sets Hariścandra to **guard the burning-ghat and collect the cremation-fee (the "death-tax") from every corpse** — a share for the master, a share for the king/state, a share for himself — the once-emperor reduced to levying a toll on the dead (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8). The ghat is memorialized as **Hariścandra Ghāṭ** in Vārāṇasī, one of its two cremation ghāṭs to this day. See [../06-sacred-geography/sapta-puri-and-sacred-cities.md](../06-sacred-geography/sapta-puri-and-sacred-cities.md).
- **Months of degradation** - Clad in rags among ash and bones, Hariścandra serves faithfully, never lamenting that he was a king, never lying about a fee.

### The supreme test — the dead son

- **Rohitāśva's death by snakebite** - In the brahmin's service, the boy **Rohitāśva, gathering kuśa-grass or flowers, is bitten by a serpent and dies** (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8).
- **The mother at the pyre** - The grief-maddened queen **Śaivyā / Candramatī** carries her son's body by night to the very cremation ground — and meets the husband she does not at first recognize, in his outcaste's rags (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8).
- **The unbearable demand** - Recognizing his own wife and dead son, Hariścandra is bound by his duty to his master: he tells her **she cannot cremate the child without paying the fee**. Having nothing, she begins to **tear her own sari** to offer half as payment — the heart of the tale, in which truth/duty is held even against a parent's grief (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8).
  - *Variant (the near-execution):* in a widespread dramatic strand, the queen is meanwhile **falsely accused of murdering the local king's son** and condemned to death — and **Hariścandra, as the Cāṇḍāla's executioner, is ordered to behead his own wife**; he raises the sword rather than break his bond, at which the gods intervene. [folk] / [stage tradition]

### Restoration

- **The gods appear** - At the instant of the king's unbroken truth, **all the devas descend** — led by **Indra, Dharma/Yama, and Viśvāmitra himself** — declaring the ordeal a divine test that Hariścandra has passed without a single lie or lapse of duty (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8).
- **The son revived, the family restored** - **Rohitāśva is restored to life**; the disguised Cāṇḍāla resumes his form as **Yama/Dharma**; queen and kingdom are returned, and Viśvāmitra acknowledges the king's victory (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8).
- **Heaven won — and shared** - Indra invites Hariścandra to **Svarga in the body**; the king **refuses to ascend alone**, insisting his subjects share his merit, and obtains for the people of Ayodhyā **entry to heaven (at least for a time)** — a final stroke of selfless dharma (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8). See [../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md).
  - *Variant (the floating city / pride lapse):* in a popular coda, as Hariścandra ascends, the sage **Nārada** praises him so highly that the king feels a flicker of **pride**, whereupon his sky-borne city/chariot begins to **fall**; he must confess the lapse, recover humility, and is then re-raised — Hariścandra's "city in the sky" being explained as a mirage/atmospheric city (*gandharva-nagara*). [folk] / [late text]
- **The sages reconciled** - **Vasiṣṭha**, learning how Viśvāmitra had tormented his protégé, is wroth; **Brahmā** pacifies him, explaining the test was meant to **exalt** the king, not destroy him (Mārkaṇḍeya P. 8). Cf. the long Vasiṣṭha–Viśvāmitra feud in [./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md](./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md).

## The Devī Bhāgavata version (the bridge text)

- **Vyāsa to Janamejaya** - The **Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa** (Book **7**, the *Hariścandra-ākhyāna*) retells the Mārkaṇḍeya ordeal but **explicitly invokes the old Vedic legend**: Viśvāmitra justifies testing the king by recalling that Hariścandra had once **agreed to sacrifice Śunaḥśepa** (the Aitareya frame) — the only major source to harmonize the two Hariścandras (Devī Bhāgavata 7.x). [scholarly]
- **Devī-centric framing** - As a Śākta text, it bends the resolution toward the **Goddess** as the ultimate power behind the deliverance, consonant with the Purāṇa's [Devī Gītā](../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md) theology in the same book.

## The Mahābhārata reference (the reward seen from above)

- **The only earthly king in Indra's hall** - In the **Mahābhārata** (Sabhā Parva), **Nārada** tells Yudhiṣṭhira that among all kings only **Hariścandra**, by performing the **Rājasūya** and gratifying brahmins with limitless gifts, attained a **permanent seat in the assembly of the gods beside Indra** — the boon held up to spur Yudhiṣṭhira to his own rājasūya (MBh Sabhā Parva ~11–12). This is the *fruit* of the legend viewed from heaven, with the ordeal itself left implicit. See [../04-deep-dives/mahabharata/02-sabha-parva.md](./mahabharata/02-sabha-parva.md).
  - *Note [scholarly]:* The epic gives no sale-of-family ordeal — it presupposes only Hariścandra's fame for truth and lavish sacrifice. The full ordeal is a **Purāṇic elaboration** of the epic's bare praise.

## Significance & afterlife

- **Charter of *satya*** - Hariścandra is the scriptural anchor of **truth as the highest dharma** — *satyam eva jayate* ("truth alone triumphs") finds its narrative exemplar in him; he is cited wherever an unbreakable promise is at stake.
- **Theatre and cinema** - The legend, especially in its Candramatī-and-the-pyre form, became one of the most-staged plays in India (Bhāratendu Hariścandra's Hindi drama; countless Marathi/Kannada/Telugu *Satya Harishchandra* productions) and the subject of India's **first full-length feature film, *Raja Harishchandra* (Dadasaheb Phalke, 1913)**. [post-classical reception]
- **Gandhi's formative tale** - M. K. Gandhi recorded in his autobiography that the **Hariścandra play seen in boyhood** seized him and set his lifelong devotion to truth — a key modern transmission of the legend. [modern reception]
- **Geography** - **Hariścandra Ghāṭ** at Vārāṇasī (one of the two active cremation ghāṭs, alongside Maṇikarṇikā) preserves the burning-ground episode in living pilgrimage. See [../06-sacred-geography/sapta-puri-and-sacred-cities.md](../06-sacred-geography/sapta-puri-and-sacred-cities.md).
