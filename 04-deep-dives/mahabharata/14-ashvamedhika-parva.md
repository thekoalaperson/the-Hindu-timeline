# Āśvamedhika Parva — The Book of the Horse Sacrifice

> **Cosmic address:** Dvāpara Yuga → Mahābhārata war's aftermath → 14th of the 18 parvas. Sits in [Kali Yuga's eve], part of the [Mahābhārata deep-dive series](README.md). Up one level: [04-deep-dives/mahabharata](README.md). The events here unfold in the [28th Mahāyuga's Dvāpara Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md), shortly before the [Kali Yuga begins (3102 BCE)](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md).

The fourteenth book of the Mahābhārata, recounting Yudhiṣṭhira's imperial **Aśvamedha** (horse sacrifice) of post-war atonement and sovereignty, the **Anugītā** (Kṛṣṇa's "sequel" to the Bhagavad Gītā), Arjuna's year-long campaign following the wandering horse — including his death at the hands of his own son **Babhruvāhana** and revival — and the celebrated parable of the **golden-headed mongoose** that deflates the whole grand ritual. Traditional (Gītā Press / Calcutta) text: **2 sub-parvas, 96 chapters, ~2,742 verses**; Critical Edition: **92 chapters, 2,741 verses**.

## Structure (sub-parvas)

- **Āśvamedhika (Aśvamedhika) Parva proper** - chapters 1–15: grief, counsel, gold expedition, Parikṣit's revival, launch of the sacrifice (Mahābhārata Book 14).
- **Anugītā Parva** - chapters 16–96 (the philosophical core, chs. 16–51, plus the horse-campaign narrative chs. 52–96): Kṛṣṇa's recapitulated teaching and Arjuna's conquests (Mahābhārata Book 14).
  - *Note (scholarly):* the Anugītā philosophical block (chs. 16–51) is widely regarded as a later interpolation / medieval expansion grafted onto the Aśvamedha frame [scholarly].

## Frame: grief, counsel, and the treasury

- **Yudhiṣṭhira's renewed grief** - even after Bhīṣma's instruction (Śānti/Anuśāsana Parvas), Yudhiṣṭhira sinks back into sorrow over the slain kinsmen; **Vyāsa and Kṛṣṇa** console him and prescribe the **Aśvamedha** as expiation (prāyaścitta) for the bloodshed and as a seal of universal sovereignty (MBh 14.1–3).
- **The empty treasury** - Yudhiṣṭhira protests the kingdom is impoverished by war; Vyāsa recalls the **gold of King Marutta**, buried since an ancient sacrifice in the Himālaya (MBh 14.3–5).
- **The gold expedition to Himavat** - a party journeys near **Mount Meru / Himavat** to recover Marutta's hoard, worship Śiva and the guardian Yakṣas/Kinnaras, and haul the treasure back to fund the rite (MBh 14.63–65, by some orderings).

## Parikṣit: stillbirth and revival

- **Aśvatthāman's Brahmāstra and the dead heir** - the foetus of **Uttarā** (Abhimanyu's widow) was struck in the womb by Aśvatthāman's weapon (from the Sauptika Parva); the child **Parikṣit** is born seemingly dead (MBh 14.65–69).
- **Kṛṣṇa revives Parikṣit** - Kṛṣṇa enters the lying-in chamber and, invoking his own lifelong truth, righteousness, and brahmacarya-of-the-spirit ("If I have never turned from battle, by that truth let this child live"), restores the infant to life — securing the **continuity of the Pāṇḍava (Kuru/Sūryavaṃśa-by-marriage) line** through Parikṣit, future king and father of Janamejaya (MBh 14.68–69). See [lineages](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).

## The Anugītā — Kṛṣṇa's "sequel" to the Gītā

> Chs. 16–51. Set in Indraprastha/Hastināpura *after* the war, in peace, not on the battlefield. See the original at [Bhagavad Gītā deep-dive](../bhagavad-gita.md) (if present) and the war book [Bhīṣma Parva](06-bhishma-parva.md).

- **Arjuna's confession** - Arjuna admits he has **forgotten** the wisdom Kṛṣṇa spoke before the war and asks to hear it again (MBh 14.16).
- **Kṛṣṇa cannot repeat it verbatim** - Kṛṣṇa says the Gītā was uttered in a heightened **yogic state (yoga-yukta)** and cannot be reproduced word-for-word; instead he relays an **old conversation (purāṇa itihāsa)** carrying the same import — hence "Anu-gītā," the *after/alongside-song* (MBh 14.16).
- **Three nested dialogues** (Kṛṣṇa narrates all three):
  - **A Siddha and a Brāhmaṇa (descendant of Kāśyapa)** - a perfected sage instructs a brahmin on rebirth, the fruits of action, transmigration of souls, and the path to liberation (MBh 14.16–19).
  - **The Brāhmaṇa and his wife (the "Brahmin's wife" dialogue)** - an allegory of the senses, mind, and the inner sacrifice; the body as the field of an internal yajña; the seven priests, the gates, and the indwelling Self (MBh ~14.20–34).
  - **A teacher (guru) and his pupil** - exposition of the **guṇas** (sattva, rajas, tamas), the **āśramas** (stages of life), tapas, yoga, the elements, and the means of mokṣa (MBh ~14.35–51).
- **Subject matter** - transmigration, karma and its ripening, the three guṇas, the four āśramas, prāṇa/breath disciplines, and emancipation — echoing the Mukhya Upaniṣads. See [concepts: karma/saṃsāra/mokṣa](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md) and [ātman/brahman](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md).
  - *Variant (scholarly):* the Anugītā is held by many academics to be a **medieval addition / "corruption" of the original epic**, doctrinally closer to later Sāṃkhya-Yoga and Upaniṣadic strata than to the Gītā itself [scholarly] [late text].

## The wandering horse and Arjuna's campaign

- **Release of the sacrificial horse** - the consecrated stallion (black with a white forehead, by ritual prescription) is set loose to roam for a **year**; whichever realm it enters must either submit to Yudhiṣṭhira or fight. **Arjuna** is appointed its protector, following with an army; instructed by Kṛṣṇa and Yudhiṣṭhira to **conquer without needless slaughter**, pacifying rather than annihilating (MBh 14.71–73).
- **Trigarta** - the surviving warriors/grandsons of the Trigarta kings (whose elders died in the war) attack to avenge their dead; Arjuna subdues them after a hard fight (MBh 14.73).
- **Prāgjyotiṣa** - **Vajradatta**, son of the slain **Bhagadatta**, rides his war-elephant against Arjuna; a fierce three-day elephant-vs-archer duel ends in Vajradatta's submission (MBh 14.74–75).
- **Saindhava (Sindhu) country** - the land of the dead **Jayadratha**; its forces, roused by the bereaved **widows** and the boy-heir (Jayadratha's son by **Duḥśalā**, Dhṛtarāṣṭra's daughter), give battle. Arjuna spares them at Duḥśalā's plea when her grandson faints/falls (MBh 14.78).
- **Maṇipura (Maṇalūra) — Babhruvāhana** - the climactic episode (see below) (MBh 14.79–82).
- **Rājagṛha / Magadha** - **Meghasandhi**, grandson of Jarāsandha, challenges Arjuna and is defeated, then submits (MBh 14.82–83).
- **Cedi, Kāśī, Aṅga, Kosala, Andhra, Niṣāda, Draviḍa, Gāndhāra and others** - the horse traverses many further realms; some kings honor it, some are subdued; Arjuna's circuit effectively re-asserts Pāṇḍava paramountcy across the subcontinent (MBh 14.83–85).
  - *Variant (Jaiminīya Aśvamedha, ~12th c.):* this far shorter Vyāsa account is dwarfed by the independent **Jaiminīya Aśvamedhaparvan**, a hugely popular alternate text with many extra adventures (Babhruvāhana's underworld quest, the tale of Mayūradhvaja/Mayuradhwaja who saws himself in half, the king Yauvanāśva, Caṇḍī, etc.) — a separate devotional retelling, not part of the critical Mahābhārata [late text] [folk tradition].

## Babhruvāhana — Arjuna slain by his own son

- **The son in Maṇipura** - **Babhruvāhana** is Arjuna's son by **Citrāṅgadā**, princess of Maṇipura. Per the marriage pact (made in the [Ādi Parva](01-adi-parva.md)), the boy stayed in his mother's kingdom as its heir and did not join the Pāṇḍava household (MBh 14.79).
- **Arjuna provokes him** - when the horse reaches Maṇipura, Babhruvāhana comes out **peacefully, in homage**, with gifts. Arjuna, bound by kṣatriya code to fight any realm the horse enters, **rebukes him as unmanly** for not offering battle (MBh 14.79–80).
- **Ulūpī's intervention** - **Ulūpī**, the Nāga princess (Arjuna's other wife, Babhruvāhana's step-mother), rises from the earth and **goads Babhruvāhana to fight his father** (MBh 14.80).
- **Babhruvāhana kills Arjuna** - in the duel the son's arrows strike Arjuna down dead; grief-stricken, Babhruvāhana resolves to die beside him; **Citrāṅgadā** laments and reproaches Ulūpī (MBh 14.81).
- **Revival by the Nāga gem** - Ulūpī summons the **Mṛtasaṃjīvanī (Saṃjīvana / Nāgamaṇi)**, the serpent-realm jewel of resurrection, and places it on Arjuna's chest; Arjuna **returns to life** (MBh 14.81).
- **The hidden purpose — expiation of Bhīṣma's death** - Ulūpī reveals she **engineered the whole event**: the **Vasus** (whose incarnation Bhīṣma was) had cursed Arjuna for killing Bhīṣma unfairly (from behind Śikhaṇḍī); Gaṅgā's anger was appeased only by the decree that Arjuna must **fall by his own son's hand** to expiate the sin. Babhruvāhana's deed thus **discharged the curse**, and the gem undid the death (MBh 14.81–82).
  - *Variant (Jaiminīya Aśvamedha):* Babhruvāhana, after killing Arjuna, **descends into Pātāla/Nāgaloka** and wars against the serpents (in some tellings with armies, including a **mongoose host**) to seize the **amṛta** and/or Saṃjīvanī jewel to revive his father — a six-chapter expansion absent from Vyāsa's epic [late text].
  - *Variant (regional/folk):* some traditions soften the provocation — Babhruvāhana fights only because **duty/Ulūpī compel him**, and the episode is read as a lesson that even the unconquerable Arjuna must bow to **karmic justice** [folk tradition].

## The sacrifice itself and the golden-headed mongoose

- **The grand Aśvamedha at Hastināpura** - the horse returns; Yudhiṣṭhira performs the full rite with Vyāsa officiating, lavish **dakṣiṇā (gifts)**, feeding of multitudes, and the prescribed offerings — proclaiming him **cakravartin / samrāṭ** (MBh 14.85–89).
- **The mongoose (nakula) appears** - as the assembly congratulates itself, a **mongoose with one side golden and the other ordinary**, with a thundering human voice, declares the entire grand sacrifice **"not equal to a single prastha (handful) of barley flour"** once given by a poor brahmin (MBh 14.90).
- **The gleaner family's tale (the unchavṛtti brahmin)** - during a terrible **famine**, a brahmin family lived by **uñcha-vṛtti** (gleaning leftover grain like pigeons). Having gathered, ground, and cooked a tiny meal of **saktu (barley flour)** after long starvation, a **hungry guest** arrived. In turn the **brahmin, his wife, his son, and his daughter-in-law** each gave away their entire portion to the guest, choosing death over violating the law of hospitality (atithi-dharma) (MBh 14.90–92).
- **The guest was Dharma** - the guest revealed himself as **Dharma** (the god of righteousness); the family ascended to heaven in a celestial chariot. As the offered flour scattered, the mongoose **rolled in the leftover crumbs**, turning **half its body to gold** — and has wandered sacrifices ever since, seeking to turn its *other* half gold, but **no rite has matched that gift of selfless compassion** (MBh 14.92–93).
- **Moral** - the parable subordinates the most opulent ritual to **inner devotion, compassion, and sacrifice given from one's own want** — quality of heart over quantity of offering. The story humbles even Yudhiṣṭhira's imperial Aśvamedha (MBh 14.92–93).
  - *Variant (interpretive):* read by some as a **Brahminical critique of grandiose royal Vedic ritualism**, elevating ahiṃsā-tinged ethics and the gift (dāna) above animal sacrifice [scholarly].
  - *Variant (identity of the mongoose):* later/folk glosses identify the mongoose as **Dharma himself in disguise** (or a being cursed by an angry Krodhā/sage), testing the proud sacrificers [folk tradition].
