# Strī Parva — The Book of the Women

> Cosmic address: [Kali Yuga](../../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) → the immediate aftermath of the Mahābhārata war at the Dvāpara–Kali junction (war traditionally ~3138 BCE; Kali begins 3102 BCE).
> Parent: [Mahābhārata deep-dives](./README.md) · Previous: [10-Sauptika Parva](./10-sauptika-parva.md) · Next: [12-Śānti Parva](./12-shanti-parva.md)
> The eleventh of the eighteen parvas. The fighting is over; this book is the great threnody — the women come out to the corpse-strewn plain, Gāndhārī laments and curses Kṛṣṇa, and the dead are cremated and given water.

The Strī Parva (~730 ślokas in the Pune Critical Edition; the vulgate runs longer) is the shortest of the war-cycle books and the emotional pivot of the whole epic: victory is shown as indistinguishable from catastrophe. It collects Vidura's and Vyāsa's consolation-philosophy, the women's procession to Kurukṣetra, Gāndhārī's panoramic lament granted by her divine eye, her curse that the Yādavas will perish as the Kurus did, and the collective funeral rites. See the [Suryavaṃśa & Chandravaṃśa lineages](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) for the slain, [Kṛṣṇa](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) as the cursed avatāra, and the [Mahābhārata corpus entry](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md). The curse's fulfilment is narrated in [Mausala Parva](./16-mausala-parva.md).

## Structure — the sub-parvas (upa-parvas)

- **Two-fold scheme (classical / Sacred-Texts vulgate):** the parva splits into **Jalapradānika Parva** (sections ~1–15) and **Strī-vilāpa Parva** ("Lament of the Women," sections ~16–27). (Sacred-Texts / Ganguli; Wikipedia)
  - *Variant (alternate four-fold table of contents):* some lists give **Viśoka Parva** (~ch. 1–8, Vidura's "Dispeller of Grief" discourse), **Strī Parva proper** (~ch. 9–25), **Śrāddha Parva** (ch. 26, funeral rites), **Jalapradānika Parva** (ch. 27, water-offering). (Vyāsa Online; Grokipedia) — both schemes cover the same material; the count of upa-parvas is 2 or 4 depending on edition.
- *Note:* the Mahābhārata's own parva-saṃgraha (table of contents in Ādi Parva) traditionally counts the upa-parvas differently again; numbering of sub-books is not fixed across recensions.

## Events — in narrative order

### Dhṛtarāṣṭra's grief and Vidura's consolation (Viśoka / Jalapradānika, ~ch. 1–8)

- **The blind king collapses in grief** - Dhṛtarāṣṭra, having lost all hundred sons (the [Kauravas](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)) and every grandson, sinks into despair; Sañjaya and Vidura attend him. (Mbh XI; Sacred-Texts m11/m11003)
- **Vidura's discourse on death and the soul** - Vidura consoles him by treating grief as a disease of the mind curable by wisdom, expounding the impermanence of the body and the deathlessness of the soul (a precursor to the [ātman/brahman](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman.md) and [karma–saṃsāra–mokṣa](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/karma-samsara-moksha.md) teachings). (Mbh XI.2–7)
- **The parable of the man in the well** - Vidura's famous allegory of saṃsāra: a brāhmaṇa lost in a dense forest (the world), pursued by beasts and a great elephant (death/year), falls into a covered well (the body); he hangs from a creeper gnawed by black-and-white rats (the days and nights), a great serpent waits at the bottom (Time/death), bees swarm above — yet drops of honey (sense-pleasures) fall on his tongue and he clings to life, forgetting his peril. (Mbh XI.5–6)
  - *Scholarly note:* this image migrated west via Persian, Arabic and Greek into the Christian legend of Barlaam and Josaphat (the "man in the well" / "honey and the abyss"), one of the best-attested transmissions of an Indian parable into medieval Europe. [scholarly] (Pradip Bhattacharya; Wikipedia)
- **Vyāsa's teaching** - the sage Vyāsa adds his own consolation on the inevitability of death and the working-out of fate/karma, urging Dhṛtarāṣṭra not to blame the living. (Mbh XI)

### Going out to the field; the iron statue of Bhīma (~ch. 9–15)

- **The procession to Kurukṣetra** - Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Gāndhārī, Kuntī, Draupadī and the Kaurava and Pāṇḍava women set out from Hāstinapura to the battlefield to perform the rites; the Pāṇḍavas, Kṛṣṇa and Yuyutsu accompany the blind king. (Mbh XI)
- **The iron statue of Bhīma [key episode]** - Dhṛtarāṣṭra calls Bhīma forward to embrace him, but intends to crush him to death in revenge for Duryodhana; Kṛṣṇa, foreseeing it, pushes forward an **iron effigy (āyasa-pratimā) of Bhīma** instead. The king crushes the statue to powder, vomits blood, and believes he has killed Bhīma — then weeps with remorse, thinking he has slain a son out of anger. Kṛṣṇa reveals the substitution and reproaches him; the king repents and embraces the real Bhīma. (Mbh XI.11–12; Sacred-Texts / Ganguli)
- **Gāndhārī's threat against Yudhiṣṭhira** - as Yudhiṣṭhira approaches, Gāndhārī's wrath nearly breaks loose; through the gap of her blindfold her glance scorches the king's fingernails. Vyāsa and Kṛṣṇa restrain her, and she relents before her grandsons' killer, accepting his humility. (Mbh XI; Vyāsa Online)
  - *Variant emphasis (some retellings):* Gāndhārī's burning glance falling on Yudhiṣṭhira's toenails (rather than fingernails) — a detail that varies by translation. [folk tradition]

### Gāndhārī's lament over the slain — the panoramic threnody (Strī-vilāpa, ~ch. 16–25)

- **The divine eye** - by the power of her lifelong austerity (and, in many tellings, a boon of vision from Vyāsa), Gāndhārī surveys the entire field of Kurukṣetra from afar and describes the carnage corpse by corpse to Kṛṣṇa — the structural heart of the book. (Mbh XI.16ff)
- **Duryodhana** - she sees her eldest son slain, his thighs shattered by Bhīma's mace, and faints; the women of his household, with his queen, wail over him. (Mbh XI)
- **The widows on the field** - she describes the Kaurava and allied women wandering the plain, their hair loose, embracing the bodies of husbands, sons and brothers, driven half-mad with grief — the dominant image of the parva. (Mbh XI.16–23)
- **Abhimanyu and Uttarā** - the young son of [Arjuna](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md), killed in the cakra-vyūha, mourned by his pregnant widow Uttarā (whose unborn son [Parīkṣit](../../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/recorded-history.md) will continue the line). (Mbh XI)
- **Karṇa** - Gāndhārī laments the sūta-born hero; Kṛṣṇa and the field reveal his valour. The deeper grief of Karṇa belongs to Kuntī (below). (Mbh XI)
- **Droṇa and Kṛpī** - the slain preceptor mourned; his beheading by Dhṛṣṭadyumna recalled. (Mbh XI)
- **Bhīṣma** - the grandsire, still alive on his bed of arrows awaiting the northern solstice to die, is recalled among the fallen patriarchs. (Mbh XI)
- **Jayadratha and Duḥśalā** - the Sindhu king (slayer of Abhimanyu) mourned by Duḥśalā, Gāndhārī's only daughter — a doubled grief for the queen. (Mbh XI)
- **Śalya, Bhūriśravas, Bāhlīka, Śakuni, Bhagadatta, Vivimśati and the kings** - Gāndhārī moves across the allied dead, naming the kings of many realms and their lamenting wives, building a catalogue of the entire warrior aristocracy of the age destroyed in eighteen days. (Mbh XI.18–25)

### Gāndhārī's curse on Kṛṣṇa [pivotal]

- **The accusation** - Gāndhārī charges Kṛṣṇa with allowing the slaughter though he had the power and knowledge of weapons to stop it, and with permitting the Kurus and Pāṇḍavas — kinsmen — to annihilate one another. (Mbh XI.25; Sacred-Texts)
- **The curse** - in her grief she curses that **in the thirty-sixth year** Kṛṣṇa will witness his own [Yādava](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) (Vṛṣṇi–Andhaka) kin destroy one another in internecine slaughter exactly as the Kurus did, that the Yādava women will wail as the Bhārata widows now wail, and that Kṛṣṇa himself will die alone and ingloriously in the wilderness. (Mbh XI.25)
- **Kṛṣṇa's acceptance** - Kṛṣṇa accepts the curse with a faint smile, telling Gāndhārī she has only confirmed what he himself willed — that none could destroy the Vṛṣṇis but themselves, and that he had already resolved on it; he gently turns her anger back, noting that she too, by not restraining Duryodhana, shares the burden of the war. (Mbh XI.25; Sacred-Texts)
  - *Variant (causal layering):* the Yādava destruction is also attributed to an **earlier brāhmaṇa curse** — the sages Viśvāmitra, Kaṇva and Nārada curse the Yādava youths (who mocked them with the disguised Sāmba) that an iron mace/pestle will be born to destroy the clan. Gāndhārī's curse and the sages' curse are presented as converging causes; both are fulfilled in [Mausala Parva](./16-mausala-parva.md). [catalog both] (Mbh XVI; Wikipedia "Mausala Parva")
  - *Variant (theological reading):* later Vaiṣṇava commentary frames the curse not as a binding imposed on the avatāra but as Kṛṣṇa's own līlā — the divine choosing the manner of his withdrawal from the world at the dawn of [Kali Yuga](../../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md). [scholarly]

### Kuntī reveals Karṇa's birth

- **The secret disclosed** - Kuntī tells the grieving Pāṇḍavas that [Karṇa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md), whom they killed, was their own eldest brother — her son by Sūrya, born before her marriage. The brothers, above all Yudhiṣṭhira, are overwhelmed with fresh grief and remorse for having slain their kinsman. (Mbh XI; some editions place the full Karṇa-revelation at the opening of Śānti Parva)
  - *Variant (placement):* in the vulgate the disclosure and Yudhiṣṭhira's anguished cursing of all women (that they shall never keep a secret) is given prominence at the junction of Strī and [Śānti Parva](./12-shanti-parva.md). [catalog placement variant]

### The funeral rites — cremation and water-offering (Śrāddha / Jalapradānika, ~ch. 26–27)

- **The roll of the dead and the survivors** - Dhṛtarāṣṭra asks Yudhiṣṭhira the toll of the war; Yudhiṣṭhira answers that **1,660,020,000** (one billion six hundred sixty million and twenty thousand) men have fallen, and **24,165** heroes are unaccounted for / missing. (Mbh XI.26; Sacred-Texts / Ganguli)
  - *Variant (survivor figure):* some readings give **240,165** as the number of heroes who escaped with life; the enormous totals are universally read as hyperbolic/symbolic rather than literal demography. [scholarly] (Anandatirtha; Quora-cited ślokas)
- **Mass cremation ordered** - at Yudhiṣṭhira's command, **Vidura** (assisted by Sudharman, Dhaumya, Sañjaya, Indrasena and others) gathers the bodies and burns them in their thousands of heaps with sandal, aloe and ghee, with proper Vedic rites and the chanting of sāman-verses, by night with torches. (Mbh XI.26)
- **Honouring the fallen kings by name** - the named champions — Duryodhana and his brothers, Karṇa, Droṇa, Bhīṣma (to be cremated after his later death), Śalya, Drupada, Virāṭa, Dhṛṣṭadyumna, Abhimanyu, the sons of Draupadī (the Upapāṇḍavas), Ghaṭotkaca, Jayadratha, Bāhlīka, Śakuni and the allied kings — are given individual funeral honours. (Mbh XI.26)
- **The water-offering on the Gaṅgā (jala-pradāna)** - both sides descend to the Ganges and, led by the women, offer water-libations (tarpaṇa) for the dead. The whole assembly stands "a spectacle of sorrow," widows of both armies side by side. (Mbh XI.27)
- **Kuntī's libation for Karṇa** - prompting Kuntī to make the offering openly for Karṇa as a son is what reveals the secret (in the editions that place the revelation here). (Mbh XI.27)
- **The river of grief** - the parva closes with the survivors immersed in the Gaṅgā, the war's aftermath imaged as a flood of tears; the narrative then turns to Yudhiṣṭhira's despondent reluctance to rule, carried into the [Śānti Parva](./12-shanti-parva.md). (Mbh XI.27)

## Themes and cross-links

- **The anti-war heart of the epic** - victory and defeat collapse into the same field of corpses; the Strī Parva is the Mahābhārata's most sustained meditation that "war is easy to begin and impossible to end." Read alongside [Bhagavad Gītā](./bhagavad-gita.md) (war's beginning) as its counterweight.
- **Consolation literature (śoka-nāśana)** - Vidura's and Vyāsa's discourses form a classic Hindu treatment of grief, death and the soul; see [karma–saṃsāra–mokṣa](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/karma-samsara-moksha.md) and [ātman/brahman](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman.md).
- **The curse as plot-engine** - Gāndhārī's curse sets the clock (36 years) for the dissolution of the [Yādavas](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) and Kṛṣṇa's departure, narrated in [Mausala Parva](./16-mausala-parva.md), marking the close of the Dvāpara age and the full onset of [Kali Yuga](../../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md).
