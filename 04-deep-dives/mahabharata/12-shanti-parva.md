# Śānti Parva — The Book of Peace

> Cosmic address: [Kali Yuga](../../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) → the immediate aftermath of the Mahābhārata war at the Dvāpara–Kali junction (war traditionally ~3138 BCE; Kali begins 3102 BCE).
> Parent: [Mahābhārata deep-dives](./README.md) · Previous: [11-Strī Parva](./11-stri-parva.md) · Next: [13-Anuśāsana Parva](./13-anushasana-parva.md)
> The twelfth and **longest** of the eighteen parvas. The war is over; the dying Bhīṣma, stretched on his bed of arrows, becomes the epic's great teacher, instructing the grief-paralysed Yudhiṣṭhira in the whole science of dharma — kingship (rājadharma), conduct in calamity (āpaddharma), and liberation (mokṣadharma).

The Śānti Parva ("Book of Peace") is the encyclopaedic heart of the Mahābhārata's didactic ambition: a vast post-war discourse in which Bhīṣma, awaiting his chosen death at the winter solstice (uttarāyaṇa), pours out the accumulated wisdom of statecraft and salvation. It is by far the largest book of the epic — traditionally **365 adhyāyas (chapters)** in the Northern/vulgate recension; **353 chapters** in the Pune Critical Edition; roughly **14,000+ ślokas** (Ganguli's vulgate runs ~13,700). Together with the [Anuśāsana Parva](./13-anushasana-parva.md) that follows, it forms the colossal "Bhīṣma's teaching" block (the two are sometimes treated as one continuous instruction split by the question of whether one may take up arms in extremity). See [rājadharma & dharma](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas.md), [karma–saṃsāra–mokṣa](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/karma-samsara-moksha.md), [ātman/brahman](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman.md), the [Sūryavaṃśa/Candravaṃśa lineages](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md), [Kṛṣṇa as avatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md), and the [Mahābhārata corpus entry](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md).

## Structure — the three sub-parvas (upa-parvas)

The body of the parva is the three "anuśāsana" (instruction) sections, framed by an opening block on Yudhiṣṭhira's despair and his approach to Bhīṣma.

- **Rājadharma-anuśāsana Parva** ("Instruction in the Duties of Kings," ~ch. 1–130; ~4,716 ślokas) — statecraft, the king's duties, the origin and theory of kingship, daṇḍa (punishment), war and peace, taxation, ministers, fortification. (Wikipedia; Vyāsa Online)
- **Āpaddharma-anuśāsana Parva** ("Instruction in Duties for Times of Distress," ~ch. 131–173; ~1,649 ślokas) — what is permissible when ordinary dharma cannot be followed; survival, the relaxing of caste/diet rules in famine, prudent alliance among enemies. (Wikipedia; Sacred-Texts)
- **Mokṣadharma Parva** ("Duties leading to Liberation," ~ch. 174–365; ~7,351 ślokas) — the largest and most philosophical block: Sāṃkhya, Yoga, the nature of the self, cosmology, renunciation, and dozens of teaching-narratives on attaining mokṣa. (Wikipedia; Sacred-Texts)
- *Variant (chapter counts):* the Critical Edition's 353 vs. the vulgate's 365 reflects passages many Western scholars regard as later didactic accretions; some Mokṣadharma material is suspected to be among the latest strata of the epic. [scholarly] (Wikipedia; Fitzgerald, *The Mahābhārata* vol. 7)
- *Note:* the Mahābhārata's own parva-saṃgraha (table of contents in the Ādi Parva) and various manuscript traditions number these upa-parvas slightly differently; the three-fold scheme above is the standard one.

## Frame — Yudhiṣṭhira's despair and the approach to Bhīṣma (~ch. 1–55)

- **Yudhiṣṭhira's grief and wish to renounce** - Sickened by the slaughter of kin, elders, and the Brahmin Droṇa, Yudhiṣṭhira declares the kingdom worthless and resolves to abandon it for the forest as an ascetic. (Mbh XII.1–7)
- **The brothers and Draupadī argue against renunciation** - Arjuna, Bhīma, Nakula, Sahadeva, Draupadī, and Devasthāna in turn refute him, defending the householder's life, kṣatriya duty, the necessity of wealth (artha) and rule, and the merit of a righteous reign over barren asceticism. (Mbh XII.8–22)
  - *Sub-episode:* Arjuna's praise of daṇḍa and wealth; Bhīma's charge that renunciation now is cowardice masquerading as virtue; Draupadī's rebuke invoking the duty of a king.
- **Vyāsa and the story of the parrot-merchant / consolations** - Vyāsa, Devala, and Nārada console him; Vyāsa argues that the slain went to heaven by kṣatriya-dharma and that expiation, not flight, is the king's path. (Mbh XII.23–36)
- **The prāyaścitta (expiation) question — the story of Indra and Vṛtra-slaying / the aśvamedha prescribed** - To purge the sin of the war Yudhiṣṭhira is told he must rule and later perform the horse-sacrifice (carried out in the [Āśvamedhika Parva](./14-ashvamedhika-parva.md)). (Mbh XII.36–37)
- **Kṛṣṇa directs Yudhiṣṭhira to Bhīṣma** - Kṛṣṇa proclaims Bhīṣma the one repository of all knowledge who will die when he wills it; the Pāṇḍavas and Kṛṣṇa go to the field where Bhīṣma lies on his bed of arrows. (Mbh XII.46–51)
  - *Sub-episode:* **Kṛṣṇa restores Bhīṣma's faculties** - Kṛṣṇa grants Bhīṣma freedom from pain and clarity of mind for the duration of the teaching; Bhīṣma's hymn to Kṛṣṇa (a stuti of Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa). (Mbh XII.47, 52–54)
- **Bhīṣma accepts the role of teacher** - Reluctant at first ("how can a dying man teach?"), Bhīṣma, empowered by Kṛṣṇa, agrees to instruct Yudhiṣṭhira until the sun turns north. The discourse begins. (Mbh XII.54–55)

## Rājadharma-anuśāsana — the duties of a king (~ch. 56–130)

- **The supremacy of rājadharma** - Bhīṣma declares the king's dharma the foundation of all dharmas, the refuge of the four āśramas and varṇas; without the king (and daṇḍa) the strong devour the weak (mātsya-nyāya, the "logic of the fishes"). (Mbh XII.56–59)
- **The origin of kingship and of daṇḍa (Daṇḍa-nīti)** - In primeval anarchy men appealed to Brahmā; Brahmā composed a vast treatise on polity (the Daṇḍanīti), Śiva and others abridged it, and the first king was instituted to wield the rod of punishment. (Mbh XII.59)
  - *Variant (Manu / Pṛthu accounts):* the first consecrated king is variously named — **Manu Vaivasvata** consenting to rule on the people's promise of a tax-share, and elsewhere **Pṛthu son of Vena**, from whom the earth (Pṛthivī) is milked and named. (Mbh XII; cf. [Purāṇic Pṛthu](../../00-time-and-cosmology/manvantara-system.md))
  - *Variant:* daṇḍa itself is personified and given a divine genealogy (born of Brahmā's energy, identified with Viṣṇu/Dharma in some passages). [disputed across sections]
- **Duties of the four varṇas and the king as their guardian** - The king must keep each varṇa to its dharma; protection of subjects is his cardinal merit, oppression his cardinal sin. (Mbh XII.60–65)
- **Taxation like the bee and the calf** - The king should draw revenue gently — as the bee takes honey without harming the flower, as the calf and herdsman take milk without draining the cow — never crushing the people's capacity. (Mbh XII.71, 88)
- **Ministers, spies, fortification, the seven limbs of the state** - Treatment of the saptāṅga (king, minister, ally, treasury, realm, fort, army); choice of counsellors; secrecy of counsel; the circle of kings (maṇḍala) and the six measures of foreign policy (ṣāḍguṇya). (Mbh XII.69, 80–87, 100–103)
- **Conduct in victory and the ethics of war** - Rules of righteous (dharmayuddha) warfare, treatment of the conquered, when peace is preferable to victory. (Mbh XII.95–103)
- **Discourse of Vāmadeva to King Vasumanas** - The sage Vāmadeva instructs King Vasumanas that the righteous king wins without battle, ruling by virtue. (Mbh XII.92–93)
- **The dialogue of King Janaka and the Kosala king / friendship as policy** - Illustrations that a king secures realms through righteousness and alliance rather than mere force. (Mbh XII.106)
- **The story of the dog who became a tiger (and lion, and beyond)** - A ṛṣi's pet dog, successively transformed for protection into ever-fiercer beasts, finally turns on its master and is reduced again to a dog — a parable that one should not raise the base to high station, and that ingratitude must be checked. (Mbh XII.116–117)
- **The dialogue of Bhīṣma on truth, forgiveness, and the king's self-restraint** - Long sections on the king's personal virtues: truth, self-control, forgiveness balanced with firmness, the danger of anger and pride. (Mbh XII.118–130)
- **The "tiger and jackal" / animal-counsellor parables** - Moral tales on discerning true from flattering counsel. (Mbh XII.111ff)

## Āpaddharma-anuśāsana — dharma in times of distress (~ch. 131–173)

- **The principle of āpaddharma** - When ordinary dharma cannot be maintained (famine, mortal danger, anarchy), conduct otherwise forbidden may become permissible; survival of the dharmic order takes precedence, and one returns to normal dharma when the calamity passes. (Mbh XII.130–132)
- **Viśvāmitra eats dog-flesh in the twelve-year famine** - In a drought said to span the Tretā–Dvāpara junction, the starving sage Viśvāmitra steals a haunch of dog-meat from a Caṇḍāla's hut; he debates the Caṇḍāla over purity and necessity, eats to preserve his life (and thus the Veda he carries), then makes rain by penance and is purified. The paradigm case that life-preservation can override caste and dietary law. (Mbh XII.139)
  - *Variant readings:* later commentators and some traditions soften the act (he is interrupted, or does not actually eat, or it is allegory); Bibek Debroy and others discuss the textual ambiguity. [disputed] [scholarly]
- **The cat and the mouse — Palita and Lomaśa** - A wise mouse (Palita) and a cat (Lomaśa) caught in a hunter's snare form a temporary alliance against common enemies (owl and mongoose); the mouse frees the cat at the last possible instant, trusting no one fully — a lesson in expedient alliance with an enemy under necessity. (Mbh XII.136–138)
- **Kāyavya the virtuous robber** - A Niṣāda brigand who robs only to protect the helpless, never harms ascetics, women, or the weak, and observes dharma even in banditry — illustrating that dharma persists even in a degraded calling. (Mbh XII.133–135)
- **The fowler and the pigeons** - A bird-catcher caught in a storm is given selfless hospitality by a captured pigeon's mate and by the pigeon itself (who immolates itself for its guest); moved, the fowler renounces violence and takes to asceticism. A celebrated parable of ātithya (guest-hospitality) and ahiṃsā. (Mbh XII.143–149)
- **The discourse of the ungrateful / Gautama and the crane Rājadharman** - Stories on the deadly sin of ingratitude (kṛtaghnatā) and the bonds of friendship and trust. (Mbh XII.169–173)
- **Practical apad-counsel** - On guarding the treasury, on whom to trust, on alliances among rivals, on a king's recovery after defeat or exile, on protecting wealth that the dharmic order may be restored. (Mbh XII.131–168)
- **The duties of the weak king / recovery from ruin** - Strategies (conciliation, gifts, dissension, force — sāma, dāna, bheda, daṇḍa) for a ruler reduced to extremity. (Mbh XII.140–142)

## Mokṣadharma — the duties leading to liberation (~ch. 174–365)

The largest block: Bhīṣma turns from the world to the soul, expounding Sāṃkhya and Yoga, the metaphysics of the self, cosmology, and a great chain of teaching-dialogues, most reported as conversations between ancient sages and kings.

### Doctrine

- **The nature of mokṣa, ātman, and rebirth** - Liberation from saṃsāra through knowledge; the immortal self contrasted with the perishable body; the workings of [karma](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/karma-samsara-moksha.md). (Mbh XII.174–204)
- **Sāṃkhya and Yoga expounded** - The twenty-five (and in places twenty-six, adding Īśvara/puruṣottama) tattvas, prakṛti and puruṣa, the three guṇas; Yoga as discipline complementary to Sāṃkhya knowledge. (Mbh XII.300–311, 314–320)
  - *Variant:* the parva oscillates between **classical (atheistic) Sāṃkhya** (24/25 principles) and a **theistic 26-principle Sāṃkhya** that adds a supreme Lord — both are recorded side by side. [scholarly]
- **Cosmology and the four-fold/expanded Vedānta (the Nārāyaṇīya theology)** - The **Nārāyaṇīya** section (ch. ~321–339) sets out the worship of Nārāyaṇa as the supreme Brahman and the **Pāñcarātra**/vyūha doctrine (Vāsudeva–Saṃkarṣaṇa–Pradyumna–Aniruddha); a foundational text for [Vaiṣṇava](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md) theology. (Mbh XII.321–339)
  - *Variant (sectarian):* parallel passages exalt **Śiva/Rudra** as supreme (the Rudra-stuti and origin-of-Rudra accounts), so the parva preserves both Vaiṣṇava and Śaiva supremacy claims. (Mbh XII)

### Teaching-narratives and dialogues (selected, exhaustive of the famous ones)

- **Bhīṣma's narration of Vyāsa instructing his son Śuka** - The renunciate Śuka, having mastered the Vedas, questions Vyāsa on the highest good; Vyāsa teaches the path to Brahman, the duties above all duties, and Śuka attains liberation, merging into the elements. A central renunciation-cycle. (Mbh XII.224–250)
- **Sulabhā and King Janaka (Sulabhā-Janaka samvāda)** - The female ascetic Sulabhā, doubting Janaka's claimed liberation-while-ruling, enters his mind by yogic power and out-argues him in a famous debate on whether one truly free can remain a king; a landmark on gender, embodiment, language, and mokṣa. (Mbh XII.308)
- **Yājñavalkya and King Janaka** - Yājñavalkya expounds the twenty-five principles, creation, the seasons, and the science of the self leading to release. (Mbh XII.298–306)
- **Pañcaśikha and King Janaka** - The Sāṃkhya teacher Pañcaśikha frees Janaka from grief and the fear of death by analysis of the perishable elements. (Mbh XII.211–212, 218–219)
- **The dialogue of the brāhmaṇa and his wife / and the dialogue of a brāhmaṇa and the fowler (Dharma-vyādha)** - The "righteous butcher" of Mithilā teaches a proud Brahmin that dharma lies in doing one's own duty without attachment, even in a "low" trade — one of the epic's most quoted ethical parables. (Mbh XII.261–264; the Vyādha-gītā, sometimes counted in Vana Parva tradition but reprised here)
- **Tulādhāra and Jājali** - A heavenly voice tells the penance-proud Brahmin Jājali that the merchant Tulādhāra of Vārāṇasī is greater in righteousness; Tulādhāra teaches that true dharma is universal harmlessness (ahiṃsā) and honest, non-injurious livelihood, not ritual or austerity. (Mbh XII.253–256; some editions ~261–264)
- **The Brahmin Dharmāraṇya and the Nāga Padmanābha** - On the doubt of which dharma secures mokṣa; Padmanābha tells of a Brahmin who won liberation by uñchavṛtti (gleaning fallen grain), dispelling the seeker's doubt. (Mbh XII.353–354)
- **The dialogue of the ocean and the rivers; the gleaner (uñchavṛtti) cycle** - Praises of the minimal, non-acquisitive life as the surest path to release. (Mbh XII.353–360)
- **The conversation of a sire and son (Pitā-putra samvāda)** - A young son rouses his father from worldly toil with the urgency of death ("the day that is gone returns not"); a poem on impermanence and timely renunciation. (Mbh XII.169 / 175 in some numbering)
- **The colloquy of Manu and Bṛhaspati** - On the nature of the universe, the self, and the conduct that leads to the highest. (Mbh XII.200–206)
- **The Hymn of the Frog / story of Vṛtra and Indra and the "decline" (the Bhṛgu–Bharadvāja dialogue)** - Bhṛgu teaches Bharadvāja cosmology, the creation of beings, the order of the elements, and the origin of the varṇas (with the famous statement that varṇa-distinctions arose from conduct, not birth, in the beginning). (Mbh XII.182–192)
- **Nārada, Asita Devala, Jaigīṣavya, and other ascetic exemplars** - Short dialogues illustrating yogic powers, equanimity, and the conquest of desire (e.g., Jaigīṣavya's serene indifference instructing Asita Devala). (Mbh XII.222, 288–290)
- **The story of the worm and the sage** - A worm, taught by a sage to meditate on the supreme, is reborn upward through the orders of being to godhood — a parable of devotion and the soul's ascent. (Mbh XII.286–287)
- **The discourse on the field and the knower / the body as a city of nine gates** - Allegory of the body as a chariot/city and the self as its sovereign, anticipating Vedāntic and Gītā imagery. (Mbh XII; Mokṣadharma)

### Close of the discourse

- **Bhīṣma completes the teaching** - Having covered rāja-, āpad-, and mokṣa-dharma (and continuing into dāna-dharma in the [Anuśāsana Parva](./13-anushasana-parva.md)), Bhīṣma blesses Yudhiṣṭhira and dismisses the assembly until the sun's northward turn. (Mbh XII.365)
- *Note:* the Śānti Parva ends with Bhīṣma still alive on the arrow-bed; his actual death awaits the uttarāyaṇa at the end of the Anuśāsana Parva.
