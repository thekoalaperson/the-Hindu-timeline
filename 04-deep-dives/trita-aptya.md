# Trita Āptya — the sage in the well and the scapegoat of the gods

> *Where this sits: a deep-dive into **Trita Āptya** — the shadowy "third" water-born ṛṣi-deity of the [Ṛgveda](../90-literature-corpus/vedas/rigveda.md), Indra's understudy who slew the three-headed son of Tvaṣṭṛ and released the cows, the seer betrayed into a well by his brothers **Ekata** and **Dvita**, and — with them — the divine **Āptya** trio onto whom the gods and the sacrifice **wipe off their blood-guilt**. The classic Vedic scapegoat, and cognate of the Avestan **Thraētaona / Thrita**. ⬆ Parent index: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Master index: [README](../README.md)*

**Trita** ("the Third") is one of the oldest and most eroded figures in the Ṛgveda: named ~40 times, hero of no hymn of his own, always half-eclipsed by [Indra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/indra.md), whose dragon-fights he doubles. He carries the epithet **Āptya** — "watery, sprung of the waters" (*āpaḥ*) — and around that name the tradition grows two memories: a **mythic hero** who kills the three-headed monster and presses soma, and a **ritual scapegoat** who, with brothers **Dvita** ("the Second") and **Ekata** ("the First"), absorbs the impurity the gods incur when they kill. The betrayal-in-the-well tale is the human face of the same figure. He has until now appeared here only as a name in a Cākṣuṣa-manvantara son-list ([Manvantara 06](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-06-chakshusha.md)); this file is his canonical home.

---

## Dramatis personae & the name

- **Trita Āptya** — *Trita* = "the Third" (the numeral, cognate with Latin *tertius*, Avestan *θrita*); *Āptya* = "belonging to the waters / son of Āpas," read by the tradition as his birth-origin. The Ṛgveda uses **Āptya** of him seven times in four hymns. (Ṛgveda; Yāska, *Nirukta* 4.6) [scholarly]
- **Ekata and Dvita** — "the First" and "the Second," Trita's brothers, who complete the numeral-trio **1–2–3**. Dvita appears once beside Trita in the Ṛgveda and once alone in an Agni context; Ekata is almost purely a Brāhmaṇa-and-epic figure, summoned to fill out the set. (Ṛgveda; Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.2.3; Mahābhārata) — see [Brāhmaṇa legends catalog](brahmana-legends-catalog.md).
- **Parentage — unstable by design.** The three are given no single fixed father: born of the **waters** (from the name Āptya), or from **Agni's spittle** (Śatapatha), or made sons of the ṛṣi **Gautama** (Mahābhārata), or listed among the **sons of Cākṣuṣa Manu** in a Purāṇic vaṃśa. (SB 1.2.3.1; Mahābhārata, Śalya Parva; Bhāgavata Purāṇa) — the corpus keeps all four rather than choosing.
  - *Variant (Cākṣuṣa son-list):* the Bhāgavata's twelve sons of Cākṣuṣa Manu by Naḍvalā include a **Trita** beside Kutsa and Puru; whether this is the Āptya or a mere homonym is unclear. ([Manvantara 06 — Cākṣuṣa](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-06-chakshusha.md)) [disputed — likely a name-reuse]
- **Divine company** — travels "at the side of Indra as a Brahman follows a king" (SB 1.2.3.2); linked in the Ṛgveda to the **Maruts**, **Vāyu** and **Agni** (whom he kindles), and rescued in one telling by **Bṛhaspati** or the **[Aśvins](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ashvins.md)**.

## 1. Trita in the well — the two tellings

Trita fallen into a pit, praying his way out, is the story most attached to his name. It survives in two independent registers that must not be merged.

- **The Ṛgvedic seed — RV 1.105.** The hymn whose refrain is *vittám me asyá rodasī* — "**Take note of this my (plight), O Heaven and Earth**" — is the cry of one abandoned and afflicted; verse 17 names **Trita in the well (kūpe)**, buried and calling on the gods, "**like a hotṛ set to a task**," who hear him from afar. (Ṛgveda 1.105.17; the refrain runs through all 19 verses)
  - *Attribution note:* the Anukramaṇī assigns RV 1.105 to **Trita Āptya himself** (some authorities read **Kutsa Āṅgirasa**); Sāyaṇa's commentary supplies the frame — three ṛṣis found a well, and as Trita drew water the other two **pushed him down and shut him in**, whereupon he composed the hymn. (Sarvānukramaṇī; Sāyaṇa on RV 1.105) [scholarly]
  - *Allusion:* RV 9.34.4 and other passages glance at Trita hidden or pent in a hollow, treating the well-episode as already known to the hymnists. (Ṛgveda 9.34.4) [scholarly]
- **The epic elaboration — Mahābhārata, Śalya Parva.** In the Sarasvatī-tīrtha catalogue, the sage-king Balarāma comes to **Udapāna** ("the Well"), and the story is told: three ascetic brothers **Ekata, Dvita, and Trita**, radiant with tapas, journeyed with cattle they had earned by sacrificing for kings; on the road a **wolf** frightened the herd, and **Trita, running ahead, fell into a deep dry well**. His brothers, **coveting his cattle, abandoned him** and went on. (Mahābhārata, Śalya Parva ~35–36)
  - **The mental sacrifice.** Trapped, Trita — a soma-knowing priest — **performed an entire soma-sacrifice in imagination**: he made the **pebbles at the bottom of the well into soma-grains**, a creeper into the soma-plant, the well-water into clarified butter (ghee), and mentally recited the chants and poured the offerings. The imagined rite reached the gods. (Mahābhārata, Śalya Parva ~36)
  - **Rescue and curse.** The gods — **led by Bṛhaspati** — came, marvelled at the sacrifice performed in a pit, and **granted him boons and lifted him out**. Freed, Trita **cursed his greedy brothers to become wolves and beasts of prey** (their descendants the langurs / wild dogs, in some tellings), turning the wolf that started the disaster into their own fate. (Mahābhārata, Śalya Parva ~36) [the epic's moralized version of the Vedic pit-motif]
- **Why two tellings.** The Ṛgveda knows an afflicted Trita **in a well** but gives no motive; the Mahābhārata supplies **betraying brothers, coveted cattle, a mental soma-rite, and a curse** — a folktale grown around the ancient image. The hymn is the seed, the epic the flowering; neither is "the" story. [scholarly]

## 2. The dragon-slayer's understudy — Triśiras, Vṛtra, and the cows

Trita's other Ṛgvedic face is martial: he is the god who does Indra's deeds, or does them **for** Indra, pressing the soma that makes the killing possible.

- **Slayer of the three-headed — RV 10.8.** Urged and aided by Indra, **Trita Āptya smote the three-headed, seven-rayed son of Tvaṣṭṛ** — **Triśiras Viśvarūpa** — struck away his heads, and **drove out the cows** the monster had penned. (Ṛgveda 10.8.8–9; cf. 2.11.19, 2.34.10)
  - *Cross-node:* the three-headed **Viśvarūpa "Triśiras," son of [Tvaṣṭṛ](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishvakarma-and-tvashtar.md)**, is elsewhere Indra's own victim and the origin of Indra's **brahmahatyā** sin — the Veda splits the same kill between Indra and Trita, and it is this shared deed the scapegoat-mechanism below is built to launder. (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 2.5.1)
- **At Indra's Vṛtra-fight.** Trita, "**exhilarated by the soma**," helps Indra shatter **Vṛtra** and Vala, and battles the demon **Arbuda**; several hymns let Trita and Indra stand in for one another as the club-wielder who breaks the enclosure of the waters. (Ṛgveda 1.187; 8.7.24; 10.99.6) [scholarly — Trita as Indra's older or doubled self]
- **Soma-presser.** Trita is the one **at whose pressing-stones the soma is prepared** for the gods; his name became so bound to soma-preparation that ritual language speaks of soma cleansed "at the seat of Trita." (Ṛgveda 9.34.4; 9.102.1–3; 8.52.1) [scholarly]

## 3. The scapegoat mechanism — the Āptyas and the gods' blood-guilt (Śatapatha 1.2.3)

Here the trio's deepest ritual function appears: they are the **drains for divine guilt**, the beings onto whom the sin of killing-in-sacrifice is wiped and then passed further down. This is the passage the brief centres on.

- **Origin from Agni and the waters — SB 1.2.3.1.** Agni was fourfold; three earlier Agnis perished, and the **fourth, in fear, hid in the waters**. Drawn out by the gods, from his **spittle** upon the waters sprang the **Āptya deities — Trita, Dvita, and Ekata** — the "watery ones," born of Agni-in-the-waters. (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.2.3.1)
- **They walk with Indra — SB 1.2.3.2.** The three Āptyas "**roamed about with Indra, as nowadays a Brahman follows in the train of a king**"; and when **Indra slew Viśvarūpa the three-headed son of Tvaṣṭṛ** — Trita in fact striking the blow — **Indra himself stayed free of the sin, "for he is a god."** The guilt had to go somewhere. (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.2.3.2)
- **The wiping-off — SB 1.2.3.3–4.** Because the Āptyas "**knew of the killing**," the people held them guilty; the resolution is a ritual transfer: "**the sacrifice shall wipe it off upon them**." This is enacted every soma-rite when the **rinse-water of the offering-dish and the water in which the priest has washed his fingers is poured out** with the words *"For Trita thee! For Dvita thee! For Ekata thee!"* — the impurity of the god-sacrificer draining onto the trio. (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.2.3.3–5)
- **The chain does not stop at them — SB 1.2.3.4.** The Āptyas, in turn, wipe the guilt **onward** "**upon him who offers a sacrifice without a dakṣiṇā** (priestly fee)." Hence the doctrine: **the sacrifice wipes the guilt onto the Āptyas, and the Āptyas wipe it onto the stingy sacrificer.** To block that, the gods ordained the **Anvāhārya rice-mess as a fixed dakṣiṇā**, so no offering is fee-less and the guilt finds no final resting-place among the pious. (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.2.3.4–5)
- **The theological point.** Trita Āptya is the **Vedic scapegoat proper** — not a sinner, but a designated **carrier of others' sin**, the mechanism by which the gods' unavoidable violence is displaced off the divine and, ritually, off the generous patron. It is the oldest Indian articulation of guilt-transfer, later refracted into the vocabulary of **prāyaścitta** (expiation). See [Pāpa, puṇya & prāyaścitta](../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md). [scholarly — Macdonell §22; Keith]

## 4. Wiper of evil dreams and evil — the everyday scapegoat

- **The dream-drain — RV 8.47.** In this Āditya hymn, worshippers pray **Uṣas (Dawn) to carry away bad dreams (*duṣvapnya*) to Trita Āptya**, "**who wears them, like ornaments, upon his limbs**," and to consign to him all evil, sin and misfortune so that the sleeper wakes clean. (Ṛgveda 8.47.13–18) — the ritual scapegoat of §3 in a domestic key.
- **The Atharvan afterlife.** The **Atharvaveda** develops the same idea: guilt, disease and defilement are dispatched "to Trita, to Dvita, to Ekata" — the trio become the standing address for **whatever the community wants gone**. ([Atharvaveda](../90-literature-corpus/vedas/atharvaveda.md); AV 6.113; 19.56–57) [scholarly]
- **The Sāmavedic telling.** The **Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa**, richest reservoir of Vedic myth, carries its own narrative of Trita Āptya within the Jyotiṣṭoma exposition — the "third" figure bound up with the pressing of soma and the removal of impurity — one more Brāhmaṇic witness that the well-and-scapegoat Trita and the soma-presser Trita are one figure. ([Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa](../90-literature-corpus/vedas/brahmanas/jaiminiya-brahmana.md) 1.184) [scholarly — JB numbering is edition-dependent; the text survives only in partial manuscripts] [disputed]

## 5. The Avestan cognate — Thraētaona, Āthwya, and Thrita

Trita is one of the surest **Indo-Iranian** inheritances: the same figure survives in the Avesta, split (as in the Veda) between a healer-priest and a dragon-slaying hero.

- **Aptya = Āthwya.** The Avestan **Āθwya (Āthwya)** — "the watery one," father of **Θraētaona (Thraētaona / later Persian Ferīdūn/Fereydun)** — corresponds exactly to Vedic **Āptya**; and **Θraētaona**, "the third-born," answers to **Trita**, both from PIE **\*Tritos**, "the Third." (Yasna 9; Yašt 5) [scholarly]
- **The dragon-slayer.** As Vedic Trita smote the **three-headed** son of Tvaṣṭṛ, Avestan **Thraētaona slew Aži Dahāka, the three-headed, three-jawed dragon**, and freed the two women it had penned — the same "third man kills the three-headed monster and releases what was captive" myth carried on both sides of the Indo-Iranian split. (Yašt 5.33–34; 9.13–14; Yasna 9.7–8) [scholarly]
- **The healer-priest.** Avestan **Thrita** is remembered as **the third mortal to press Haoma** (= soma) and as the **first physician**, to whom Ahura Mazda gave "**ten thousand healing plants**" — matching Vedic Trita the soma-presser to whom sin and disease are dispatched. (Yasna 9.10; Vidēvdād 20.1–4) [scholarly]
- **The reconstruction.** Comparativists read **\*Trito Āptyo** as a shared Indo-Iranian inheritance (cf. the wider "Third" hero who slays the serpent) — Trita's Ṛgvedic obscurity being the fading of a **once-major god** displaced by the rising Indra, who took over his dragon-fight. [scholarly]

## 6. The two clocks

- **Scriptural clock.** Trita Āptya belongs to the deep ṛṣi-and-deva stratum of the present **Vaivasvata Manvantara** (and, in one vaṃśa, a Trita is set among the sons of the *earlier* Cākṣuṣa Manu — [Manvantara 06](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-06-chakshusha.md)): a soma-knowing sage contemporary with the gods' primordial dragon-wars, active "when Indra slew Viśvarūpa," i.e. before the human-dynastic history of the Solar and Lunar lines. The Śalya-Parva well-episode is likewise placed in "a former age" (*pūrva-yuga*). (Ṛgveda; Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa; Mahābhārata)
- **Academic clock.** [scholarly] Trita is a **Ṛgvedic (and pre-Ṛgvedic, Indo-Iranian) deity**, his core hymns among the older layers, **c. 1500–1000 BCE**; the scapegoat-doctrine is **Brāhmaṇa-prose, c. 900–600 BCE**; the well-and-brothers folktale is an **epic elaboration** centuries younger still. The Avestan cognate points the figure back **before c. 1500 BCE**, into the shared **Indo-Iranian** period. Never collapse the frames.
