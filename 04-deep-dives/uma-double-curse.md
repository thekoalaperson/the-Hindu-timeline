# Umā's double curse — the barren wives of the gods and the many-lorded Earth

> Where this sits: two lines of imprecation in the **Bāla Kāṇḍa** (**1.36.19–24**) that quietly govern how divine parentage works for the rest of the Rāmāyaṇa. Interrupted by the gods in the hundredth year of her union with **Rudra** and robbed of the son it would have produced, **Umā** curses the wives of the gods to **bear no children**, and curses the **Earth**, who took the seed instead, to be **the wife of many** and to have **no joy of a son**. The epic then invokes the first curse by name one sarga later (1.37.5–6) as the reason the gods must get their war-leader on **Gaṅgā** and not on their own wives.
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Kāṇḍa frame: [Bāla Kāṇḍa](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md) · Cosmic address: Śveta-Vārāha Kalpa › 7th ([Vaivasvata](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)) Manvantara — an event of the deep past, narrated by [Viśvāmitra](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) on the road to Mithilā in the [Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md).

This is a small entry with a very long reach. It is the Rāmāyaṇa's own explanation of a rule the epic otherwise applies without comment: **the gods do not beget on their wives.** Skanda is got on Gaṅgā and nursed by the Kṛttikās; the entire vānara army of the fourth, fifth and sixth books is fathered by devas on apsarases, gandharvīs, yakṣīs, nāga-women and she-bears; Hanumān is Vāyu's son by **Añjanā**. Vālmīki states the rule once, in Umā's mouth, in anger, and never explains it again.

The **birth of Skanda** itself — the reed-bed, the six Kṛttikās, the six faces, the war on Tāraka — is treated at [Gaṇeśa & Kārttikeya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md) and [Tāraka-vadha](tarakasura-vadha.md); the Śaiva marriage-cycle at [Satī, Dakṣa & Pārvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md). This file is the home of the **curse**, which those files do not carry.

## The scene — Bāla Kāṇḍa 35–36

- **Where it is told** — Viśvāmitra narrates it on the bank of the Gaṅgā, en route to Mithilā, as part of the tīrtha-origin cycle he tells the boys on the road: **Himavān** and Menā, their two daughters **Gaṅgā** and **Umā**, Gaṅgā's surrender to the gods, and then Umā's marriage and its consequence. (*Bāla* 35–36)
- **A hundred years of union without issue** — Rudra weds Umā and their union runs **a hundred years by the gods' reckoning** without producing a child; the text says plainly that in all that time "he had no son." (*Bāla* 36.5–7)
- **The gods' alarm** — All the celestials, Brahmā at their head, come to Śiva with the objection that the **worlds cannot bear** the offspring of that potency, and beg him to hold his *tejas* within himself and practise austerity with Umā rather than beget upon her. (*Bāla* 36.7–13)
- **Śiva's answer, and the problem it creates** — He consents — but the potency is already stirred, and he asks the question the whole disaster turns on: *who then shall hold it?* The gods answer: the **Earth**. (*Bāla* 36.14–15)
- **The seed goes to the Earth** — Śiva releases it into Dharaṇī, who is filled with it to her mountains and forests; unable to bear it, the gods dispatch **Agni** to consolidate it, and it becomes a white mountain and a divine reed-forest "equal to the Sun and Fire," out of which **Kumāra** will be born. (*Bāla* 36.15–18)

## The first curse — the wives of the gods

- **The grievance** — Umā's anger is double: she has been **denied a son**, and the gods have intruded on her marriage to enforce it. The text gives her reddened eyes and a curse pronounced on the spot. (*Bāla* 36.19–21)
- **The terms** — Because they stopped her from bearing a child, **the wives of the gods shall from that day be without offspring** — *aprajāḥ*. It is aimed with precision: not at the gods, who came and asked, but at their **wives**, who are made to suffer exactly the deprivation Umā has been made to suffer. (*Bāla* 36.22)
- **It is not a threat but a state of the world** — Vālmīki treats it as taking effect immediately and permanently. From this verse on, in this epic, a deva who wants a son must find a mother outside his own marriage. (*Bāla* 36.22) [scholarly]

---

## The second curse — the Earth

- **Why the Earth is cursed at all** — She is the one who **accepted** what was meant for Umā. The curse follows the seed. (*Bāla* 36.23)
- **The terms** — The Earth shall be **of many forms**, shall be the **wife of many** — many will possess her — and shall **have no gratification of a son**. (*Bāla* 36.23–24)
- **What it charters** — The first clause is the epic's etiology for the Earth's variegation; the second is its etiology for **kingship itself** — that the earth is held serially and severally by many lords rather than belonging to one, the premise of every digvijaya and every dynastic quarrel in the corpus. The third is the sentence Umā actually cared about: the Earth will take a child and get no joy of it. (*Bāla* 36.23–24) [scholarly]
  - *The corpus notes, without pressing it:* Vālmīki's Earth bears no son anywhere in this epic. The one child she is given is **Sītā**, found in a furrow and taken back into the ground at the end of the Uttara Kāṇḍa. [scholarly] — see [Sītā](characters/sita.md)
- **Both withdraw** — With the curses spoken, Śiva departs and settles into austerity **together with Umā** on a peak sprung from the Himālaya; Viśvāmitra closes the tale there and turns to the descent of Gaṅgā. Vālmīki's Umā is not repudiated for the curse and not reconciled to the loss — the marriage simply moves to the mountain and out of the poem. (*Bāla* 36.25–27)

## The payoff the text itself makes — Bāla 37

- **The gods come back with the same problem** — Needing a **commander** for the deva army against the asuras, the gods go to **Brahmā** and ask how they are to get one, since Śiva has gone into austerity. (*Bāla* 37.1–4)
- **Brahmā cites the curse by name** — He reminds them that **by Umā's curse they cannot beget offspring upon their own wives**, and directs them elsewhere: **Agni** must place Śiva's potency in **Gaṅgā**, who will bear the child, and Umā will not be offended by that. This is Vālmīki's own invocation of 36.22, one sarga later, as an operative rule of the world. (*Bāla* 37.5–8)
- **And so Skanda is born outside a marriage** — Agni carries the potency to Gaṅgā; she cannot hold its heat and sets it down on the Himālaya's slopes; the reed-forest turns to gold; the six **Kṛttikās** nurse the child, who takes six faces to drink from all six; the gods name him **Kārttikeya** and anoint him their commander in a single day. (*Bāla* 37.9–30) — full treatment at [Gaṇeśa & Kārttikeya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md) and [Tāraka-vadha](tarakasura-vadha.md)

## The wider payoff — the surrogate-fathered vānara host

- **The rule and the army** — At [Bāla](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md) 17, Brahmā orders the gods, gandharvas and sages to beget vānara sons of matching might — and every one of them is got **on apsarases, yakṣīs, gandharvīs, nāga-women and she-bears**, never on a deva's wife: Indra→Vālin, Sūrya→Sugrīva, Bṛhaspati→Tāra, Kubera→Gandhamādana, Viśvakarman→Nala, Agni→Nīla, the Aśvins→Mainda and Dvivida, Varuṇa→Suṣeṇa, Parjanya→Śarabha, **Vāyu→Hanumān**. (*Bāla* 17) — see [Vānaras & Ṛkṣas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vanaras-and-rikshas.md)
- **The connection, stated for what it is** — Vālmīki does **not** name Umā's curse at sarga 17; the join is made by the corpus, on the strength of Brahmā's own statement of the rule at 37.5–6. Read together, the curse supplies the mechanism the vānara commission takes for granted: the gods must father the army on other women because their wives can bear them nothing. It is the strongest available explanation, and it is a **reading, not a citation**. [scholarly]
- **Why it matters to the epic's shape** — Every vānara in Kiṣkindhā, on the bridge and in the war is a **half-divine child of an irregular union**, and Hanumān above all. The most-loved figure in the story exists because of a curse laid on the gods' marriages by an insulted wife, ten sargas before his mother is mentioned. [scholarly] — see [Hanumān](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/hanuman.md)

## Variants — how other canons tell the interrupted union

- *Variant (Śiva Purāṇa, Rudra-saṃhitā / Kumārasambhava):* the fullest Purāṇic Skanda-cycle keeps the interrupted union and the seed too potent for any womb, but frames it around **Tāraka's boon** — the gods need a son of Śiva and are impatient rather than afraid — and distributes the anger differently, with Pārvatī's imprecation variously falling on the gods themselves, on Agni, on Gaṅgā or on the Kṛttikās. The **paired curse on the deva-wives and on the Earth**, in these terms, is Vālmīki's. (*Śiva Purāṇa*, Rudra-saṃhitā, Kumāra-khaṇḍa; Kālidāsa, *Kumārasambhava*) [late text] — see [Tāraka-vadha](tarakasura-vadha.md)
- *Variant (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva):* the archaic Skanda birth — Agni and **Svāhā**, the ṛṣi-wives, the Kṛttikās, the reeds — carries **no Umā curse at all**, and Śiva is secondary in that layer. The two epics' accounts of the same god's birth do not share this element. (*Mahābhārata*, Āraṇyaka Parva) [scholarly]
- *Variant (the Earth-clause omitted):* many retellings of the Rāmāyaṇa's Skanda passage carry the curse on the deva-wives and drop the curse on the Earth entirely, since only the first has a narrative consequence in the epic. Vālmīki gives both, in consecutive verses, and the corpus records both. [textual]

## Two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic** — The union, the interruption and the curses belong to the deep past of the present **Vaivasvata Manvantara**, long before the Ikṣvāku kings; Skanda's generalship follows immediately; Viśvāmitra narrates the whole of it to Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa in the **Tretā Yuga** of the current 28th Mahāyuga, on the road to Mithilā.
- **Academic / text-critical** — Bāla 35–37 sits in the **Bāla Kāṇḍa**, treated by historical-critical scholarship as a later frame around the older core books; within it the Gaṅgā–Umā cycle is one of the tīrtha-origin insets. Its Skanda account is nevertheless **independent of, and in places incompatible with, both the Mahābhārata's archaic version and the developed Purāṇic one** — three parallel birth-traditions for one god, all preserved. Do not harmonize them. [scholarly]
