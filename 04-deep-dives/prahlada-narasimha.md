# Prahlāda & Narasiṃha — the Man-Lion's Descent

> The fourth descent of the Daśāvatāra and the climax of the Satya-yuga demon-cycle: a boon-armoured tyrant, his unkillable boy-devotee son, and the **Man-Lion** who bursts from a pillar to thread every loophole of an "unbreakable" boon.
> Sits in [Satya-yuga of Mahāyuga 28](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/01-satya-yuga.md); the second killing within the first birth of the [Jaya–Vijaya three-birth curse](jaya-vijaya.md) (the first being [Varāha's slaying of Hiraṇyākṣa](varaha-hiranyaksha.md)) and a key entry in the [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md).
> Up one level: [Deep Dives README](README.md). · Sibling cycle: [Gajendra Mokṣa](gajendra-moksha.md) · index entry in the [Bhāgavata Tales Catalog](bhagavata-tales-catalog.md).

The **Prahlāda–Narasiṃha** cycle is told at greatest length in the **Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Skandha 7, chapters 1–10** (SB 7.1–7.10), narrated by **Nārada to Yudhiṣṭhira**, who has just seen Śiśupāla merge into Kṛṣṇa and asks how an enemy of God could attain him (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.1.14–21*). It is the longest sustained avatāra-narrative in the Purāṇa's early skandhas. Its frame is Nārada's teaching that the mind fixed on God even through enmity (*vairānubandha*) or hatred (*dveṣa*) reaches him (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.1.25–30*). Later writers call this *dveṣa-bhakti*; the compound is their label, not the text's. Against it stands the boy's pure *bhakti*. The episode belongs to the first of the three demonic births of Viṣṇu's gatekeepers: **Hiraṇyakaśipu** is the elder of the two asura brothers (*hiraṇyakaśipur jyeṣṭho*, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.1.39*), his younger sibling Hiraṇyākṣa already slain by Varāha. His death by **Narasiṃha** is the fourth of the ten classical avatāras. Other major tellings differ substantially and are catalogued below: the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* (1.16–20), the *Matsya Purāṇa* (161–163) with its near-twin in the *Harivaṃśa* (31), the *Narasiṃha Purāṇa* (40–44) and the Śaiva sequel in the *Liṅga* and *Śiva Purāṇas*.

*Verse numbers for the Bhāgavata follow the GRETIL e-text. The BBT/Vedabase edition is offset by one in parts of SB 7.1 (one higher: its 7.1.36 is GRETIL 7.1.35) and SB 7.8 (one lower: its 7.8.14 is GRETIL 7.8.15). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa is cited by chapter from Wilson's translation, which has no verse numbers.*

## The curse-frame and the setup (SB 7.1–7.2)

- **Jaya–Vijaya, first of three births** — Nārada explains that Hiraṇyakaśipu and Hiraṇyākṣa are Viṣṇu's Vaikuṇṭha doorkeepers **Jaya and Vijaya**. Brahmā's sons the Kumāras ("Sanandana and the others"; the BBT translation names all four) cursed them to be born as asuras, and the curse would end after three births (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.1.35–38*). This is birth #1 in the *Bhāgavata*'s curse-arithmetic, followed by Rāvaṇa–Kumbhakarṇa and Śiśupāla–Dantavakra (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.1.39–46*; recapped at 7.10.35–38). The curse itself is narrated at *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 3.15–16*. SB 7.1 names no yuga for the first birth; the *Matsya Purāṇa* (161.2) and *Harivaṃśa* (31.32) set Hiraṇyakaśipu's tapas "formerly, in the Kṛta-yuga" (*purā kṛtayuge*). See [Jaya–Vijaya](jaya-vijaya.md).
- **Sons of Diti and Kaśyapa** — Hiraṇyakaśipu and Hiraṇyākṣa are the *daitya* sons of **Diti** by the sage **Kaśyapa**. They were conceived when Diti, overcome by desire, pressed Kaśyapa to unite with her at the forbidden evening twilight, and the gods dreaded the birth that followed (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 3.14–3.17*, not SB 7.2). The lineage is also given at *Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.15*. Readers translate the name in different ways. *Kaśipu* means "cushion, mat" in the lexicons, so the name is commonly glossed "golden cushion" or "clad in gold". Both renderings are translators' glosses.
- **Grief and vow of vengeance** — When **Hiraṇyākṣa** is killed by Viṣṇu "in the form of a boar" (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.2.1*), the killing that came when Varāha raised the Earth (*dharoddhāre*, 7.1.40; told at SB 3.13–19), Hiraṇyakaśipu vows to cut off Viṣṇu's head with his trident (7.2.6–8). He orders the daityas to destroy sacrifice, Vedic study and the brāhmaṇas and cows that sustain the gods (7.2.10–16). He then consoles his mother **Diti**, his sister-in-law **Ruṣābhānu** and his nephews (Śakuni, Śambara and others) with a discourse on the soul's immortality. The discourse includes the tale of King Suyajña's widows instructed by **Yama disguised as a boy** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.2.17–61*).

## The boon of near-invulnerability (SB 7.3–7.4)

- **The terrible tapas at Mandara** — In a valley of **Mandara** mountain, Hiraṇyakaśipu stands on his toes with arms raised. His austerity is so severe that fire from his head scorches the worlds and the gods flee to Brahmā (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.3.2–13*). Brahmā finds his body buried in an anthill and grass, its flesh eaten by ants. He revives it with water from his *kamaṇḍalu*, and Hiraṇyakaśipu rises young and golden (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.3.14–23*).
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 161.3; Harivaṃśa 31.33):* the tapas is spent dwelling or fasting in water, in silence; no anthill appears. The Matsya gives eleven thousand years (*jalavāsī*, with ritual bathing and silence, 161.3); the Harivaṃśa gives eleven thousand five hundred (*daśa varṣasahasrāṇi śatāni daśa pañca ca*, *jalopavāsa*, 31.33). The *Narasiṃha Purāṇa* has only "many thousands of years" without food (40.2).
- **The cleverly-worded boon** — His aim in undertaking the tapas was to make himself unconquerable, free of old age and death, without rival and sole king (*vyadhitsata*, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.3.1*). Before Brahmā he asks instead for a death hedged by exclusions (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.3.35–38*):
  - not from any being created by Brahmā (7.3.35);
  - **not inside or outside** a dwelling, **not by day or night**, not by anything else (*anyasmāt*) or by **weapons** (*āyudhaiḥ*), **not on the ground or in the sky**, **not by men or animals** (7.3.36);
  - not by anything **lifeless or living**, nor by gods, demons or great serpents (7.3.37).

  He further asks for no rival in battle, sole lordship over embodied beings, the glory of the world-guardians and the unfailing yogic powers (7.3.37–38). Brahmā grants these "rarely given" boons (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.4.1–2*).
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 161.11–15; Harivaṃśa 31.41–45):* in the Matsya–Harivaṃśa list he is not to be killed by gods, asuras, gandharvas, yakṣas, serpents, rākṣasas, men or piśācas, nor cursed by ṛṣis. He is not to die by missile or hand-weapon (*na cāstreṇa na śastreṇa*), by mountain or tree, or by anything **dry or wet**. The Matsya adds "not by day or by night" (161.13); the constituted Harivaṃśa has "nor by any other means" (*na cānyena*, 31.43) in its place. He also asks to become the sun, moon, wind, fire and the other cosmic powers (Matsya 161.14–15; Harivaṃśa 31.44–45). The inside/outside and earth/sky clauses are absent from the Matsya and the constituted Harivaṃśa text. In the GRETIL Harivaṃśa they appear only in unnumbered inserted lines after 31.43, together with day/night and the exception that his death will be one who can destroy him "with a single blow of the hand" (*pāṇiprahāreṇaikena*). [interpolation]
  - *Variant (Narasiṃha Purāṇa 40.6–9):* a long list of its own: not by dry or wet, water, fire, wood, insect, stone or wind; not by weapon, trident or mountain; not by men, gods, asuras, gandharvas, rākṣasas, kiṃnaras, yakṣas, vidyādharas, serpents, monkeys, beasts or the Mothers; not inside or outside, not by any other cause of death, not by day or night. There is no earth/sky clause.
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.17):* the narrative says only that he was "relying on a boon from Brahmā". Wilson's note cites the *Vāyu* for "not to be slain by any created being" and the *Kūrma* for the added "except by Viṣṇu" (translator's note).
- **Conquest of the three worlds** — Armoured by the boon, Hiraṇyakaśipu conquers the three worlds and lives in Indra's palace. He takes the sacrificial shares of the gods and makes the guardians bow to him (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.4.5–19*). A long time passes while he, drunk with power, "proud and transgressing scripture", lives as one who has incurred the brāhmaṇas' curse (*brahma-śāpam upeyuṣaḥ*, 7.4.20). The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* adds that he usurped the functions of Indra, the sun, wind, fire, water, the moon, Kubera and Yama (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.17*).
- **The Lord's promise** — The oppressed worlds and their guardians take refuge in the Lord, and an unseen voice assures them that when Hiraṇyakaśipu turns against his own peaceful son **Prahlāda**, the Lord will kill him despite Brahmā's boons (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.4.21–28*).

## Prahlāda, the boy-devotee (SB 7.4–7.7)

- **A devotee born in the demon house** — **Prahlāda** is the greatest of Hiraṇyakaśipu's four sons (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.4.30*). His mother is **Kayādhu**, daughter of the dānava Jambha, and his brothers are Saṃhlāda, Anuhlāda and Hlāda (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.18.12–13*; the same four sons at *Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.15*). He is a perfected Viṣṇu-devotee from childhood: calm, friendly to all and seeing the Lord in everything (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.4.31–42*).
- **Taught in the womb** — While Hiraṇyakaśipu was away at Mandara, **Indra** seized the pregnant queen. **Nārada** made him release her and sheltered her in his āśrama, where he instructed both her and the unborn Prahlāda (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.7.2–15*).
- **The schooling that fails** — Prahlāda is sent to **Ṣaṇḍa and Amarka**, the sons of Śukra (the asura-preceptor), to learn politics and statecraft. He hears the lessons but rejects their logic of friend and enemy (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.5.1–3*). Asked by his father what is best, he answers that the embodied soul should leave the "dark well" of household life for the forest and take refuge in Hari (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.5.4–5*).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.17):* his teacher is an unnamed *guru*; the Bhārgava priests offer to re-educate him only after the fire-ordeal.
- **The nine limbs of devotion** — At a second examination, seated on his father's lap, Prahlāda names the best thing he has learned: **navadhā bhakti**. Its nine limbs are *śravaṇa, kīrtana, smaraṇa, pāda-sevana, arcana, vandana, dāsya, sakhya, ātma-nivedana*, and the passage became a foundational *bhakti* charter (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.5.23–24*). The enraged king throws him from his lap and orders him killed (7.5.33–38).
- **Teaching his classmates** — The king accepts the teachers' advice to hold him in Varuṇa's nooses until Śukra returns and to keep instructing him (7.5.48–53). Prahlāda uses the teachers' absences to preach the futility of material life and devotion to Hari to the other daitya boys (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.5.54–57, 7.6–7.7*).
- **"Where is your God?"** — When the teachers report the boys' conversion, the king summons Prahlāda (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.8.1–5*). Prahlāda says that the Lord is the strength of all, his father included (7.8.8–11). Hiraṇyakaśipu then asks: "If he is everywhere, why is he not seen in this pillar?" (*kasmāt stambhe na dṛśyate*, 7.8.13). The pillar exchange is in SB 7.8, not 7.5.

## The failed killings (SB 7.5; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.17–1.20)

- **Every execution fails (Bhāgavata)** — Rākṣasas strike the meditating boy with **tridents**, but the weapons have no effect because his mind is absorbed in the Lord (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.5.39–41*). The Bhāgavata then lists the other methods in a single verse-pair without narrating them (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.5.43–44*):
  - the elephants of the quarters (*diggaja*) and great serpents;
  - black-magic rites (*abhicāra*) and hurling from heights (*avapātana*);
  - illusions (*māyā*), confinement, poison and starvation;
  - cold, wind, fire and water;
  - crushing under heavy stones or mountains (*parvatākramaṇa*).

  The Bhāgavata gives no broken tusks, no broken fangs and no sea-casting episode.
- *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.17–1.20, the narrated sequence):* the Viṣṇu Purāṇa tells the ordeals one by one:
  - **weapons** fail (1.17);
  - the serpents Kuhaka, Takṣaka and Andhaka bite him and report "our fangs are broken" (1.17);
  - the elephants of the skies trample him, but their "tusks were blunted against his breast" (1.17);
  - he is set in a **fire** of piled wood fanned by the wind-god and says it "burneth me not" (1.17);
  - he eats **poison** in his food unharmed (1.18);
  - the priests' **kṛtyā**, a fiery female spirit, shatters its trident on his breast and turns back to destroy the priests, whom Prahlāda revives by prayer (1.18);
  - he is thrown from the palace summit and Earth receives him (1.19);
  - he survives the thousand illusions of **Śambara**, foiled by the Sudarśana disc (1.19);
  - a withering wind is "drunk up" by Janārdana dwelling in his heart (1.19);
  - he is bound in nooses (*nāga-pāśa*, translator's note) and cast into the **ocean** with mountains piled on him, and his bonds burst as he meditates (1.19–1.20).
- **Holikā and the fire** — In the widely told fire-ordeal, the demoness **Holikā** sits in a pyre holding Prahlāda. She is usually called Hiraṇyakaśipu's sister and said to be fire-proof by a boon or a magic shawl. The protection passes to the devotee, **Holikā burns** and Prahlāda survives. The story is the etiology of the festival **Holī / Holikā Dahana**. **Holikā appears nowhere in SB 7 or in Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.17–20.** The Viṣṇu Purāṇa's fire-ordeal (1.17) has no Holikā. Purāṇic references to Holikā describe her Phālguna full-moon worship (attributed to the *Nārada Purāṇa*; locus not verified) without the Prahlāda pyre, and the Purāṇic source of the pyre story has not been traced. ## The pillar and the Man-Lion (SB 7.8)

- **The challenge at the pillar** — Taunting Prahlāda's claim that Hari is everywhere, Hiraṇyakaśipu threatens to cut off his head (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.8.12–14*). He seizes his **sword**, leaps from his throne and strikes a **pillar (stambha)** of the assembly hall with his **fist** (*sva-muṣṭinā*, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.8.15*). In the numbered verses Prahlāda gives no spoken "yes, he is in the pillar". The text says instead that the Lord appeared "to make true the words of his servant" (*satyaṃ vidhātuṃ nija-bhṛtya-bhāṣitam*, 7.8.18). The explicit "yes" belongs to retellings and chapter summaries.
  - *Variant (Narasiṃha Purāṇa 44.8–16):* he draws the sword Candrahāsa and makes the same pillar challenge. Prahlāda, remembering his earlier words, bows with joined palms. The pillar splits, and the form is first seen reflected in the demon's sword-blade (44.13).
- **Narasiṃha bursts forth** — From the pillar emerges a form "neither beast nor man" (*na mṛgaṃ na mānuṣam*, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.8.18*). It has a lion's face and mane, molten-gold eyes, fearsome teeth and razor-like tongue on a vast human body, and its roar shakes the cosmos (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.8.16–22*). *Harivaṃśa 31.65* puts it as "half the body of a man, half of a lion".
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 161–163):* **no pillar and no persecuted child.** Viṣṇu, petitioned by the gods, takes a body half man and half lion (161.37) and comes into Hiraṇyakaśipu's splendid assembly hall, a hall "not supported by pillars" (*stambhair na vibhṛtā*, 161.45). Prahlāda sits there among the court of daitya chiefs (161.79). With divine sight he alone recognises the god and describes the whole universe within the man-lion's body (162.2–13). Hiraṇyakaśipu orders the "beast" seized. A long battle with the daitya hosts follows (162–163), until Narasiṃha tears the demon apart with his great claws in combat (163.94).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 31.54–67):* a shorter account of the same type. Viṣṇu, petitioned by the gods, goes to Hiraṇyakaśipu's hall in a body "half of a man, half of a lion" (31.64–65) and kills the proud daitya, guarded by his hosts, "with a single hand" (*ekapāṇinā*, 31.67). Prahlāda does not appear in this account. The killing with sharp claws is only in an unnumbered inserted line before 31.67. [interpolation]
  - *Variant (Liṅga Purāṇa 1.95.13–17):* no pillar and no cycle of ordeals. When the dānavas beat Prahlāda at his father's order and the blows fail, the Lord appears "there itself" as the man-lion, glances kindly at the son, splits the father with his sharp claws and kills his kinsmen (J. L. Shastri tr.).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.20):* Viṣṇu appears to Prahlāda in yellow robes after the ocean-ordeal and grants him boons. On Prahlāda's request he pardons the father's sin, and Hiraṇyakaśipu repents, embraces his son and treats him kindly. The killing follows only as a single line: he was "put to death by Viṣṇu in the form of the man-lion", with no pillar.
- **The threshold killing — every loophole closed** — The two fight: club first, then sword and shield, with the Lord letting the demon slip free before seizing him again (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.8.23–28*). Then "at the door" (*dvāri*), placing him "on his thigh" (*ūrum*), Hari tears him open with his **nails (nakha)** "as Garuḍa tears a venomous snake" (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.8.29*). He wears the entrails as a garland, tears out the heart and slaughters the demon's retinue (7.8.30–31). The lap-and-nails image recurs in the avatāra list at *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 1.3.18*.
  - **Twilight** is not stated in the numbered SB 7.8 verses; it comes from chapter summaries and commentary. It is explicit in the *Narasiṃha Purāṇa*: "at twilight (*saṃdhyā-kāle*), at the house-door, setting the enemy on his thigh" (*Narasiṃha Purāṇa 44.29*). There the killing comes after Narasiṃha has destroyed wave after wave of armed daityas, including a force of 88,000 (44.17–28). The *Śiva Purāṇa* likewise has him emerge from a pillar of the hall "at dusk", drag the demon to the threshold, place him on his lap and tear him open with his claws (*Śatarudra-saṃhitā* 10.17–22, J. L. Shastri tr.).
  - The usual clause-by-clause mapping of how each condition of the boon is voided is a commentarial and popular reading. SB 7.8 does not spell it out, though Brahmā recalls the "no being created by me" boon at 7.10.27:
    - not a being created by Brahmā → the Lord himself, in a form "neither beast nor man";
    - neither day nor night → twilight (*Narasiṃha Purāṇa 44.29*; commentarial for SB);
    - neither inside nor outside → the doorway (*dvāri*);
    - neither on earth nor in sky → upon his thigh;
    - by no weapon → bare nails.
- *[scholarly] Vedic precedent:* scholars (e.g. D. A. Soifer, *The Myths of Narasiṃha and Vāmana*, 1991) compare the loophole-killing to **Indra's slaying of Namuci**. The Ṛgveda says only that Indra severed Namuci's head "with foam of water" (*Ṛgveda 8.14.13*). The *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* gives the pact: Indra had sworn to slay him "neither by day nor by night, neither with staff nor with bow, neither with the palm of my hand nor with the fist, neither with the dry nor with the moist". He struck with foam, "neither dry nor moist", when the night was clearing but the sun had not yet risen (*Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 12.7.3.1–3*, Eggeling tr.). The *Bhāgavata* retells only the dry/wet clause, given as a boon to Namuci (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.11.37–40*).

## The pacifying of the wrath (SB 7.8–7.10)

- **The wrath unquenched** — After the kill the gods led by **Brahmā, Śiva and Indra** offer hymns, standing "not very far off" (*nātidūracarāḥ*, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.8.37–56*), but none dare approach the still-furious Lord (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.9.1*). The gods send **Lakṣmī (Śrī)** herself, and she too holds back, never having seen or heard of such a form (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.9.2*).
- **Prahlāda alone pacifies him** — **Brahmā** sends the child **Prahlāda** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.9.3*). Prahlāda prostrates, and the Lord lifts him and places a hand on his head (7.9.4–6). The boy then offers the **Prahlāda-stuti** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.9.8–50*), and the Lord, pacified, speaks kindly (7.9.51).
  - *Variant (who soothes):* a popular strand has **Lakṣmī** approach and calm him, linked with the **Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha** form of worship. Regional temple legend at Ahobilam has Narasiṃha wed the forest-girl Chenchu Lakṣmī. The Bhāgavata foregrounds **Prahlāda** as the one who succeeds, sent by Brahmā after Lakṣmī would not approach. [regional] [folk]
  - *Variant (Liṅga Purāṇa 1.95–96; Śiva Purāṇa, Śatarudra-saṃhitā 10–12):* in the Śaiva account the gods, unable to calm Narasiṃha's world-threatening rage, appeal to **Śiva** (Liṅga 1.95). Śiva sends **Vīrabhadra**, who first tries persuasion and then takes a bird-beast form identified as **Śarabha** (Liṅga 1.96; in the Śiva Purāṇa, Śiva himself becomes Śarabha, ch. 12). The Śarabha seizes the man-lion and carries him off. Narasiṃha praises Śiva with a hymn of names, and Vīrabhadra flays his hide. Śiva wears it, and the lion's face (Liṅga) or head (Śiva Purāṇa) is set in his skull-garland (Liṅga 1.96.97–98, 115; Śiva Purāṇa 12.35–36). The Liṅga's own summary at 1.95.59–63 says simply that Mahādeva took the Śarabha form and took the man-lion's life. The episode is Śaiva sectarian and rejected by Vaiṣṇava tradition. Later Vaiṣṇava lore counters with a still fiercer Narasiṃha form (Gaṇḍabheruṇḍa), but that counter-story's locus is not verified. [late text] [disputed] See [Mythic Beasts and Composites](../08-beings-and-bestiary/mythic-beasts-and-composites.md).
- **The boon refused, then accepted** — Offered any boon (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.9.52–55*), Prahlāda declines. "One who seeks blessings from you is not a servant but a merchant (*vaṇik*)" (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.4–6*). If he must choose, he asks that no desire ever sprout in his heart (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.7*), the scriptural template of desireless *bhakti*. The Lord nonetheless orders him to rule the daityas until the end of the manvantara (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.11–13*). Only then does Prahlāda ask that his father be forgiven (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.15–17*).
- **Hiraṇyakaśipu purified** — The Lord replies that the father is purified along with twenty-one generations of forefathers (*triḥ-saptabhiḥ pitā pūtaḥ*, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.18*), having been touched by the Lord at death (7.10.22). The curse is not yet spent: Nārada recaps that the pair were reborn as Rāvaṇa–Kumbhakarṇa and Śiśupāla–Dantavakra, and only after the third birth did they regain the Lord (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.35–38*; 7.1.45–46). See [Jaya–Vijaya](jaya-vijaya.md).
- **Prahlāda enthroned** — At Narasiṃha's instruction Prahlāda performs his father's funeral rites and is **consecrated king by the brāhmaṇas** (*abhiṣikto dvijātibhiḥ*, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.23–24*). Brahmā praises the Lord, who warns him never again to grant boons to asuras, as one would not give nectar (*amṛta*) to cruel snakes (7.10.25–30; "milk" is the BBT translator's rendering). After the Lord vanishes, **Brahmā**, with Śukra (Kāvya) and the sages, makes Prahlāda lord of the daityas and dānavas (*prahrādam akarot patim*, 7.10.33). In the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* he becomes sovereign of the daityas only after his father's death, and at the end of his rule he attains final liberation (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.20*). Prahlāda's son is **Virocana**, whose son is **Bali** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.18.16*; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.21*). Bali is the devotee-king of the later **Vāmana** descent (*Bhāgavata 8.15–23*), which links this cycle forward in the Daśāvatāra. See [Vāmana & Bali](vamana-bali.md) and [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md).

## Place in the avatāra-sequence and the two clocks

- **Fourth of the Daśāvatāra** — In the standard ten-avatāra lists Narasiṃha is **fourth**, after Matsya, Kūrma and Varāha and before Vāmana. An example is Jayadeva's *Gītagovinda* 1, the Daśāvatāra hymn (*keśava dhṛta-narahari-rūpa*). Narasiṃha is still within the **Satya-yuga** group of descents. The *Bhāgavata*'s own longer list of twenty-two avatāras counts him **fourteenth** (*caturdaśaṃ nārasiṃham*, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa 1.3.18*). In the Harivaṃśa's prādurbhāva sequence he follows Varāha directly and precedes Vāmana (*Harivaṃśa 31.21, 31.31, 31.68*), as he does in the *Narasiṃha Purāṇa*'s own sequence (chapters 39, 40–44, 45). See [Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md).
- **Scriptural clock** — Cyclically the event is placed in **Satya-yuga of the 28th Mahāyuga of the present (Vaivasvata) Manvantara**, the same age as Hiraṇyākṣa/Varāha and the demon-pair's first birth. The *Matsya* (161.2) and *Harivaṃśa* (31.32) name the Kṛta-yuga explicitly. See [Satya-yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/01-satya-yuga.md).
- **Historical clock** — Academically the narrative has **no datable historical anchor**. [scholarly]
  - The *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* is usually placed c. 9th–10th century CE, with some scholars arguing earlier.
  - The man-lion icon is attested in sculpture from the **early centuries CE**: Kuṣāṇa-period Mathurā and the Kondamotu (Andhra) Narasiṃha group of c. 4th century CE.
  - The Matsya–Harivaṃśa account is generally reckoned older than the Bhāgavata's.
  - R. C. Hazra dated the core of the *Narasiṃha Purāṇa* to the later 5th century CE, with later additions (as reported in secondary summaries of his *Studies in the Upapurāṇas*, 1958). That would make its core earlier, not later, than the present Bhāgavata. [disputed]
- **Vedic and liturgical seed** — The **Narasiṃha-gāyatrī** (*vajranakhāya vidmahe tīkṣṇadaṃṣṭrāya dhīmahi*) is preserved in the series of deity-gāyatrīs in the *Mahānārāyaṇa Upaniṣad* (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 10), between the Nārāyaṇa- and Āditya-gāyatrīs (verse 30 of that section in the sanskritdocuments.org e-text, the eighth of the deity-gāyatrīs; anuvāka numbering differs between the Drāviḍa and Āndhra recensions). The later *Nṛsiṃha-pūrva-* and *-uttara-tāpanīya Upaniṣads* build a whole theology on him. [late text]
- **Cult and iconography** — Narasiṃha is among the most widely worshipped Viṣṇu forms in South India (Ahobilam, Yadagirigutta, Simhachalam), in fierce (*Ugra-Narasiṃha*) and benign (*Lakṣmī-Narasiṃha*, *Yoga-Narasiṃha*) modes. [regional]
