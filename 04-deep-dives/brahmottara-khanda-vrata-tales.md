# Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa vrata tales — Sīmantinī, Bhadrāyu's Śiva-kavaca, Mahānandā's rudrākṣa

> Cosmic address: three *charter-tales* from a single Śaiva upa-khaṇḍa — the **Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa** of the [Skanda Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md)'s Brahma-khaṇḍa (see the [khaṇḍa map](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana-khanda-map.md)). None sits on a yuga-date; each is a *proof-text* for a standing observance — the **Somavāra-vrata**, the **Śiva-kavaca**, and **rudrākṣa** — in force across the present **7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara** on the [ritual calendar](../00-time-and-cosmology/hindu-calendar-and-panchanga.md).
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Text profile: [Skanda Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md) · Vow catalog: [Festivals & Vratas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md)

The **Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa** ("the latter Brahma section") is a 22-chapter Śaiva upa-khaṇḍa appended to the Brahma-khaṇḍa of the [Skanda Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md). It is not narrative-driven but **ritual-driven**: a garland of *māhātmyas* (glorifications) of the Pañcākṣara mantra, the vibhūti (holy ash), the Pradoṣa and Caturdaśī observances, the Somavāra (Monday) vow, the Śiva-kavaca hymn, and the rudrākṣa bead — each stapled to an exemplary tale that "proves" the rite objectively works. Three of those tales are grouped here because they share the khaṇḍa's engine (an unlikely devotee saved by one Śaiva technicality) and are, moreover, **internally cross-linked**: the heroine of the first (Sīmantinī) is the mother-in-law of the hero of the second (Bhadrāyu). The frame is the Purāṇa's standard one — **Sūta** reciting to the ṛṣis of Naimiṣa what Vyāsa transmitted — with a nested frame for the third tale.

---

## The text and its argument

- **Where it sits** — [Skanda Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md), **Brahma-khaṇḍa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa, chapters 8–20**. The relevant blocks: **Sīmantinī / Somavāra-vrata** (chs. 8–9), **Bhadrāyu / Śiva-kavaca** (chs. 10–14), and **Mahānandā / rudrākṣa** (ch. 20, within the wider ash-and-bead material of chs. 15–21). (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 8–20)
- **What it argues** — like its Śiva-Purāṇic cousins ([Guṇanidhi](gunanidhi-kubera.md), the [Śivarātri hunter](shivaratri-hunter-legend.md)) and the Vaiṣṇava [Ajāmila](ajamila.md), the khaṇḍa insists the **rite's power does not wait on the worshipper's caste, sex, purity, or even sincerity** — a princess averts widowhood, a poisoned corpse becomes an emperor, and a courtesan and her pet animals reach Śiva-loka, each by a single Śaiva act. The tales are the drama; the *phala-śruti* (fruit-declaration) is the point. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 8, 12, 20)
- **Dating [scholarly]** — the extant Skanda Purāṇa is a centuries-long, ever-growing compilation (oldest manuscript ~810 CE), and its individual khaṇḍas accreted **as late as the 13th–15th c. CE**; the Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa belongs to this late medieval Śaiva stratum [late text]. Traditionally, of course, it is timeless Śiva-vākya transmitted by Vyāsa. The two clocks are kept separate throughout. [scholarly]

## Sīmantinī and the Somavāra-vrata (chs. 8–9)

- **The doomed horoscope** — **Sīmantinī**, daughter of **King Citravarmā** of Āryāvarta, is warned by astrologers that she will be **widowed while still young**. Seeking a remedy, she is taught by **Maitreyī, wife of the sage Yājñavalkya**, to observe the **Somavāra-vrata**: fasting every Monday and worshipping **Śiva and Gaurī** together, for *saubhāgya* (marital felicity) and long life of the husband. She keeps it faithfully from girlhood. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 8)
- **The marriage** — she is wed to **Candrāṅgada**, prince and son of **King Indrasena** of **Niṣadha** — the house of [Nala and Damayantī](nala-damayanti.md). (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 8)
  - *Note (name):* the printed Motilal translation gives the husband as **Candrāṅgada**; some gap-lists and retellings spell him **Citrāṅgada**. This file follows the text's *Candrāṅgada*; do not confuse him with the Mahābhārata's Citrāṅgada (Śāntanu's son) or Arjuna's son of that name.
- **The drowning** — boating on the **Yamunā** with companions, Candrāṅgada's craft is caught in a **whirlpool and sinks**; the prince goes under and is given up for dead — the horoscope apparently fulfilled. Sīmantinī, told of it, does not abandon the vow; she keeps her Mondays as a widow-presumptive. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 8)
- **Rescue in Pātāla** — **Nāga maidens** find the sunken prince alive and bear him down to **Pātāla**, where the serpent-king **Takṣaka** receives him. Learning of Candrāṅgada's devotion to Śiva, Takṣaka honours him and sends him back to the surface laden with gifts — a **wish-yielding horse** and divine ornaments. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 9)
- **The reunion** — Candrāṅgada resurfaces at the river-bank where Sīmantinī comes to bathe, reveals himself, and the couple is reunited; he goes on to **recover his father's usurped kingdom**, and (the *phala*) they reign and enjoy life for **ten thousand years** with **eight sons and a daughter**. The vow that "restored a drowned husband" becomes the khaṇḍa's charter for Somavāra observance. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 9)
  - *Parallel (restoring the dead spouse):* the motif rhymes with [Sāvitrī reclaiming Satyavān](savitri-satyavan.md) from Yama — but the mechanism differs: Sāvitrī argues [Yama](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/yama.md) down by wit and dharma; Sīmantinī's husband is never actually killed, only lost, and it is the **objective merit of the Monday vow** (mediated by Śiva-devout nāgas) that returns him. The two are structural cousins, not variants.

## Bhadrāyu, the Śiva-kavaca, and the tiger (chs. 10–14)

- **The poisoned queen** — **Vajrabāhu**, king of **Daśārṇa**, has a favoured queen, **Sumati** (Sumatī), whom the jealous co-wives **poison** while she is pregnant. Sickened and slandered, mother and unborn child are **abandoned in the forest** on the king's order; there the child is born and, weakened by the poison, **dies**. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 10)
- **Resuscitation by holy ash** — the yogin **Ṛṣabha**, a great Śaiva, comes upon the dead infant, **touches him with Śiva's bhasma (vibhūti)**, and restores him to life — reviving and healing the mother too. He names the boy **Bhadrāyu** ("of auspicious span") and, as the child grows, **teaches him the Śiva-kavaca** — the "armour of Śiva," a protective hymn — together with the Pañcākṣara. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 10–11)
- **The Śiva-kavaca given** — chapter 12 sets out the **Śiva-kavaca** itself (the *Amogha Śiva-kavaca*, "the unfailing armour"), a limb-by-limb *aṅga-nyāsa* invoking Śiva to guard the wearer; recited or worn, it renders Bhadrāyu invincible in arms. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 12)
- **Victories and marriage** — armed with the kavaca, a divine **sword and conch**, Bhadrāyu **single-handedly routs the Magadha army** and **rescues his father Vajrabāhu**, whose family and kingdom are restored. On Ṛṣabha's counsel, **King Candrāṅgada of Niṣadha** — *the very Candrāṅgada of the Sīmantinī tale* — gives his daughter **Kīrtimālinī** to Bhadrāyu in marriage. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 13)
  - *The binding thread:* Kīrtimālinī is the daughter of Candrāṅgada and Sīmantinī. The khaṇḍa thus **stitches its two vrata-heroes into one lineage** — the Somavāra-vow princess of chs. 8–9 becomes the mother-in-law of the Śiva-kavaca prince of chs. 10–14. This is a deliberate authorial weave, not a coincidence to be smoothed over.
- **The tiger test** — hunting in the forest, Bhadrāyu hears a **Brahmin woman crying** as a **tiger carries her off**. He cannot kill the beast; an **aged Brahmin** then demands, as recompense, that the king surrender his **own queen Kīrtimālinī** — and Bhadrāyu, holding a king bound to satisfy a suppliant Brahmin, **gives up his wife and prepares to enter the fire** himself. At the brink, the tiger, the woman, and the Brahmin dissolve: it is **Śiva and Pārvatī** ([Umā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md)) in disguise, testing him. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 14)
  - *Parallel (self-offering to the disguised god):* the "give up wife / give up self to a suppliant" ordeal is the Śaiva analogue of [Śibi](shibi.md)'s flesh-for-the-dove and the [Śveta / mongoose dāna tales](dana-upakhyanas-shveta-and-the-mongoose.md) — the test-by-disguise pattern, here resolved not in loss but in grace.
- **The boon** — Śiva, pleased, grants **Bhadrāyu, Kīrtimālinī, and their parents** the supreme boon of **Śiva-sāyujya** (union/liberation in Śiva), revives the "Brahmin woman," and confirms Bhadrāyu's sovereignty. Sumati and Bhadrāyu are reunited with the repentant **Vajrabāhu**, who takes them home. The Śiva-kavaca — proven to raise the dead and armour the living — is the chapter's charter. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 14)

## Mahānandā the courtesan and the rudrākṣa (ch. 20)

- **The nested frame** — the tale is told by the sage **Parāśara** to **King Bhadrasena of Kāśmīra**, to explain why the king's son **Sudharmā** and the minister's son **Tāraka** scorn jewelled ornaments and will wear **only rudrākṣa** — a taste, Parāśara reveals, carried over from a previous birth. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 20)
- **The pious courtesan** — **Mahānandā** is a wealthy **courtesan (gaṇikā)** of **Nandigrāma**, and, despite her calling, a devout worshipper of Śiva "**devoted to truth and piety**." She keeps a pet **monkey** and a **cock (rooster)**, both **adorned with rudrākṣa** and trained to dance. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 20)
- **The vow and the fire** — a **Vaiśya devotee** of Śiva purchases three days' company with a jewelled bracelet; Mahānandā swears a solemn oath on his heart. He entrusts to her a **jewelled Śiva-liṅga**, dear to him "as his own vital breath." The dance-pavilion **catches fire**; the monkey and cock escape but the **liṅga is destroyed**. The grief-stricken Vaiśya throws himself into the fire; Mahānandā, bound by her oath, **enters the flames after him** — whereupon **Śiva reveals** that the whole ordeal tested her truthfulness, stops her, grants her **liberation**, and bears her and all her household to his realm. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 20)
- **The rudrākṣa rebirth** — by the merit of the rudrākṣa they were made to wear, the **monkey is reborn as Prince Sudharmā** and the **cock as the minister's son Tāraka** — hence their inborn craving for the bead. The moral: even **animals**, even worn **without understanding**, rudrākṣa yields royal, Śiva-ward births — the khaṇḍa's proof of the [rudrākṣa](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/sacred-flora-and-fauna.md)'s objective potency. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 20)
  - *Companion tale:* ch. 20 is paired with the wider **rudrākṣa- and bhasma-māhātmya** (chs. 15–21), and its "unwitting piety of a low-status devotee" engine is the exact Śaiva pattern of [Guṇanidhi](gunanidhi-kubera.md) and the [Śivarātri hunter](shivaratri-hunter-legend.md).

## What the three share

- **One argument, three rites** — each tale certifies a distinct Śaiva observance (Somavāra fast · Śiva-kavaca hymn · rudrākṣa bead) by an **unlikely beneficiary** (a doomed girl · a poisoned corpse · a courtesan and her animals). The khaṇḍa's thesis is the **impersonal efficacy of Śaiva ritual**, independent of the performer's status or even awareness — the same doctrine the [pāpa-puṇya / prāyaścitta](../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md) literature debates and these tales dramatise. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 8, 12, 20)
- **The disguised-god test** — two of the three (Bhadrāyu's tiger, Mahānandā's fire) turn on **Śiva testing a devotee in disguise**, resolved by revelation and boon — the standard Śaiva bhakti ordeal. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 14, 20)
- **A woven lineage** — Sīmantinī → Candrāṅgada → their daughter Kīrtimālinī → Bhadrāyu links the first two tales into a single family across chs. 8–14, so the collection reads as one continuous Śaiva-vrata saga rather than three loose insets. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 8–14)

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural/cyclic frame** — the tales carry **no yuga-date**; Niṣadha's link to [Nala and Damayantī](nala-damayanti.md) loosely locates the Sīmantinī–Bhadrāyu cycle in the deep dynastic past, but the Purāṇa's interest is the **standing** rite, not the calendar year. Their outputs — the [Somavāra-vrata](../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md), the recited **Amogha Śiva-kavaca**, and rudrākṣa devotion — are living fixtures of the present **Vaivasvata Manvantara**. (Skanda Purāṇa, Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa 8–20)
- **Academic/historical frame [scholarly]** — the Brāhmottara-khaṇḍa is a **late medieval Śaiva accretion** to the ever-growing Skanda Purāṇa (khaṇḍas datable to c. 13th–15th c. CE) [late text]. Historically it reads as a **ritual-promotional digest**: chartering Monday-fasts, the Śiva-kavaca stotra (still recited today), and the rudrākṣa cult, and — in the courtesan tale — extending Śaiva grace pointedly across the boundaries of caste, gender, and even species. [scholarly]
