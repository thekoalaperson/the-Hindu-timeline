# Bharata's conquest of the Gandharvas — founding of Takṣaśilā and Puṣkalāvatī

> Cosmic address: a coda of **Rāma-rājya** in the **[Tretā-yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** of the present 28th Mahāyuga, 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara — the closing sargas of Vālmīki's **[Uttara-kāṇḍa](ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md)**, where Rāma settles his brothers' and nephews' sons over the frontier kingdoms.
> Parent: [Deep Dives](README.md) · Master index: [README](../README.md) · Companion profile: [Bharata & Śatrughna and their brides](characters/bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md) · Beings: [Gandharvas & Kinnaras](../08-beings-and-bestiary/gandharvas-and-kinnaras.md)
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* the campaign sits in the Tretā-yuga reign of Rāma; the cities it claims to found — **Takṣaśilā (Taxila)** and **Puṣkalāvatī (Charsadda)** — are datable archaeologically to **[scholarly] c. 6th century BCE onward**, so the episode is best read as an epic **eponymous charter** for the historical **Gāndhāra** janapada, not a datable event. [scholarly]

Near the very end of the **Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa**, after the great deeds of the war are long past, the Uttara-kāṇḍa gathers the loose ends of the dynasty: Rāma disperses the next Ikṣvāku generation across the map. One of these settlements is a full miniature war-narrative — **Bharata's** conquest of the **Gandharvas** who hold the fertile land along the **Sindhu (Indus)**, and the planting of two cities there for his sons **Takṣa** and **Puṣkala**. It is the epic's own account of how the northwestern country of **Gāndhāra**, with its twin capitals, came to be Ikṣvāku territory. The tale is terse, martial, and etiological — a foundation legend attached to the two most famous cities of the ancient northwest.

---

## I. The setting — Yudhājit's embassy

- **Yudhājit's proposal** — **Yudhājit**, king of the **Kekayas** and **brother of Kaikeyī** — hence **Bharata's maternal uncle** — sends an embassy to Ayodhyā during Rāma's reign, urging that the rich, unclaimed **country of the Gandharvas** on both banks of the **Sindhu** be conquered and brought under Ikṣvāku rule (Vālmīki *Uttara* 100 [Shastri]).
- **The envoy Gārgya** — The message is carried by the brahmin **Gārgya** (Gārgya, of the Āṅgirasa line), Yudhājit's family priest, who is received with honour at Rāma's court and lays the plan before the king (Vālmīki *Uttara* 100).
- **The land and its holders** — The territory is described as exceptionally beautiful and prosperous, watered by the Sindhu and set about with cities, held by a martial race of **Gandharvas** skilled in arms and in illusion (*māyā*). Their king is the Gandharva **Śailūṣa (Shailusha)**, and his host numbers **three crores** (thirty million) — often given as "the three crore sons of Śailūṣa," a warrior clan rather than the harp-bearing court-musicians of Svarga (Vālmīki *Uttara* 100).
  - *Disambiguation:* these **Gandharvas** are a fighting people occupying an earthly frontier, not the celestial-musician [Gandharvas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/gandharvas-and-kinnaras.md) of the deva-courts; Vālmīki uses the ethnonym for the inhabitants of the **Gāndhāra** land (see §IV). [scholarly]
- **Rāma's assent and the appointment of Bharata** — Rāma agrees to the conquest and appoints **Bharata** to lead it, sending with him his **two sons, Takṣa and Puṣkala**, born of Bharata's wife **Māṇḍavī**. The kingdom-to-be-won is destined for the two boys from the outset — the war is framed from the first as a **city-founding expedition for the next generation** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 100).

## II. The campaign and the Saṃvarta weapon

- **The march** — Bharata sets out with Gārgya and a large army, travelling for about **a month and a half** through the Kekaya country and across intervening lands to reach the Gandharva territory on the Sindhu (Vālmīki *Uttara* 101 [Shastri]).
- **The seven-night battle** — When the two hosts meet, the fighting is furious and **lasts seven days and nights** without decision, for the Gandharvas are masters of magical illusion and no ordinary assault can break them (Vālmīki *Uttara* 101).
- **The Saṃvarta astra** — Unable to prevail by conventional means, **Bharata looses the Saṃvarta weapon** (*saṃvartaṃ nāma... astram*) — the "weapon of dissolution," named for the **saṃvartaka** cataclysm that ends a world-age. In a single discharge it annihilates the entire host of **three crore Gandharvas**, leaving the field empty (Vālmīki *Uttara* 101).
  - *Note on the name:* the *saṃvarta / saṃvartaka* is the fire-and-wind of universal dissolution at **pralaya**; the astra borrows that apocalyptic register to convey total, instantaneous destruction. It is not one of the routinely named divine missiles (brahmāstra, āgneya, etc.) but a weapon of world-ending scale, fittingly deployed against an entire nation. [scholarly]
- **Total victory** — With the Gandharvas destroyed, the whole country falls to Bharata without further resistance, and he turns at once from conqueror to city-builder (Vālmīki *Uttara* 101).

## III. The founding of Takṣaśilā and Puṣkalāvatī

- **Two cities for two sons** — On the conquered land Bharata lays out **two magnificent cities**, one for each son, both described as richly built, well-planned, thronged with people and adorned with gardens, groves, and fine buildings (Vālmīki *Uttara* 101).
- **Takṣaśilā for Takṣa** — In the **Gandhāra country** Bharata founds **Takṣaśilā (Taxila)** and consecrates his elder son **Takṣa** as its king (Vālmīki *Uttara* 101). The name is read as *Takṣa-śilā*, "the rock/hewn-stone of Takṣa," an **eponymous derivation** from the prince. [scholarly]
- **Puṣkalāvatī for Puṣkala** — In the (adjacent) **Gandharva country** — the land west of the region seated on Takṣaśilā — he founds **Puṣkalāvatī (Puṣkarāvatī)** and installs his younger son **Puṣkala** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 101). The city-name is likewise derived from the prince **Puṣkala**.
- **Bharata's stay and return** — Bharata **remains in the two cities about five years**, settling and prospering them, before returning to Ayodhyā and to Rāma's side; the sons remain as the founding kings of the frontier (Vālmīki *Uttara* 101).
- **Part of a wider dispersal** — This settlement is one of a cluster in the closing Uttara-kāṇḍa: alongside Bharata's sons over Gāndhāra, **Lakṣmaṇa's** sons **[Aṅgada](characters/angada.md)** and **Candraketu** are set over Kārupada/Aṅgadīyā and Candrakāntā, and Rāma's own sons **[Kuśa and Lava](uttara-ramayana-lava-kusha.md)** over Kuśāvatī and Śrāvastī — the epic scattering the Ikṣvāku line across the subcontinent's edges (Vālmīki *Uttara* 100–108).

## IV. Disambiguation — the Gandharvas, the several Bharatas, and the several Takṣas

- **Gandharva (people) vs. Gāndhāra (place)** — In this episode "Gandharva" names an **earthly frontier people** holding the Sindhu valley, and the land is the historical **Gāndhāra**. The corpus's [celestial Gandharvas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/gandharvas-and-kinnaras.md) — musician-attendants of the gods, husbands of the apsarases — are a **different referent** despite the shared word; the shared root has invited the epic pun that a "Gandharva" race holds the "Gāndhāra" country. Do not merge the two. [scholarly]
- **This Bharata is Rāma's brother** — the second son of **Daśaratha** by **Kaikeyī**, the sandal-throned regent — **not** [Bharata son of Duṣyanta and Śakuntalā](shakuntala-bharata.md) (the cakravartin ancestor of the Bhārata line), and not Bharata the sage of the *Nāṭyaśāstra*. Three unrelated Bharatas; only the Rāmāyaṇa prince founds Takṣaśilā. [scholarly]
- **This Takṣa is Bharata's son** — **not** the nāga **Takṣaka** of the Mahābhārata (who bites Parikṣit and is hunted in Janamejaya's snake-sacrifice), and not the Takṣaka of the Khāṇḍava burning. The two Takṣas are homophonous but distinct; the city's eponym is the Ikṣvāku prince. [scholarly]
- **Note on the Mahābhārata's Takṣaśilā** — the *same city* is where **Janamejaya** later holds his **sarpasatra** and where **Vaiśampāyana** first recites the Mahābhārata; that association is with the naga-Takṣaka episode, not with Bharata's founding — an independent tradition attached to the same place. [scholarly]

## V. The historical Takṣaśilā and Puṣkalāvatī [scholarly]

- **Takṣaśilā = Taxila** — the archaeological site near Rawalpindi in **Punjab, Pakistan**: successive city-mounds at **Bhir Mound**, **Sirkap**, and **Sirsukh**, occupied from roughly the **6th century BCE** and famed as a centre of learning; excavated in the early 20th century by **John Marshall**; today a UNESCO World Heritage site. [scholarly]
- **Puṣkalāvatī = Charsadda** — near the Kabul–Swat river confluence in **Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan**; the **Bala Hisar** mound marks the ancient capital, the **Peukelaōtis** of the Greek sources; it served as the older capital of **Gāndhāra** before political weight shifted toward Taxila. [scholarly]
- **Gāndhāra the janapada** — one of the **sixteen mahājanapadas**, its capitals given precisely as **Takṣaśilā and Puṣkalāvatī**; the region appears as *Gadāra / Gandāra* in the **Achaemenid** inscriptions of **Darius I (c. 518 BCE)**, confirming its early historical reality on the Indus frontier. [scholarly]
- **Charter, not chronicle** — the eponymous derivations (Takṣa → Takṣaśilā, Puṣkala → Puṣkalāvatī) are the classic form of a **foundation legend**: an epic explanation that ties two real, prestigious cities of the far northwest into the Ikṣvāku dynasty. The historical cities long predate any datable "Rāma" and the derivation is folk-etymological; the value of the passage is that it records **how the tradition claimed** Gāndhāra as its own. [scholarly]
  - *Variant etymology (Buddhist):* the **Jātakas** know Takkasilā well but attach it to their own stories and glosses, without Bharata's charter; the Rāmāyaṇa's eponymous account is the Brahmanical claim on the city. [scholarly]

## VI. Variants and textual placement

- **A late stratum of Vālmīki** — the whole conquest and city-founding belong to the **Uttara-kāṇḍa**, widely regarded by scholars as the **latest layer** of the Rāmāyaṇa; the episode is absent from the epic's older core and some retellings omit it entirely. Its purpose is dynastic-geographic housekeeping in Rāma's long reign. [late text] [scholarly]
- **The Saṃvarta weapon's rarity** — that a whole nation is destroyed by a single **pralaya-scaled astra** is unusual even by epic standards; the choice underscores that this is a **founding myth** in which the land is emptied and re-peopled under a new (Ikṣvāku) order rather than merely defeated. [scholarly]
- **City-name spellings** — the sources give **Puṣkalāvatī / Puṣkarāvatī** and, for the elder city, uniformly **Takṣaśilā**; the constant across recensions is the eponymous pairing of prince and city. [variant]
- **Which land holds which city** — Vālmīki seats **Takṣaśilā in the Gāndhāra country** and **Puṣkalāvatī in the Gandharva country**; retellings that flatten "Gandharva" and "Gāndhāra" into one region lose the (probably deliberate) two-country framing. [variant]
