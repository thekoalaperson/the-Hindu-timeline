# Pārijāta-haraṇa — Krishna's war with Indra for the Pārijāta tree

> Where this sits: the **longest connected set-piece of the [Harivaṃśa](harivamsha.md)'s Viṣṇu Parva** and one of the crowning episodes of the Dwārakā years of [Kṛṣṇa-līlā](krishna-lila.md) — provoked by co-wife rivalry over **Satyabhāmā**, it carries Kṛṣṇa (on [Garuḍa](../08-beings-and-bestiary/)) into **Amarāvatī** to uproot the wish-tree **Pārijāta** from Indra's heaven, forces a full war between the avatāra and the king of the gods, is closed by **Aditi**'s blessing, and culminates in Satyabhāmā's **Puṇyaka-vrata** — the vow in which she gives Kṛṣṇa away and buys him back.
>
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Related: [Kṛṣṇa-līlā](krishna-lila.md) · [Harivaṃśa](harivamsha.md) · [Samudra-manthana](samudra-manthana.md) · [Indra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/indra.md) · [Divine objects & treasures](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/divine-objects-and-treasures.md). Two clocks: as *itihāsa* this falls in the Dwārakā stretch of Kṛṣṇa's life, the 28th Mahāyuga of the present (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, on the **Dvāpara/Kali cusp**; textually its home is the **Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva** (composed [scholarly] c. 1st–3rd century CE as the epic's *khila*), with the compressed **Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.30–31** and the devotional **Bhāgavata X.59**, and a long later afterlife in *kāvya* and dance ([scholarly] 16th–17th century CE).

---

## Setting — the tree, the earrings, and the visit to heaven

- **The Pārijāta as a churning-treasure** — the celestial coral-tree rose from the **[Samudra-manthana](samudra-manthana.md)** (the churning of the ocean of milk), a wish-granting *kalpa-vṛkṣa* of never-fading, ever-fragrant night-blooms; it was set in **Indra's Nandana grove**, where it perpetually granted **Śacī (Indrāṇī/Paulomī)** her every desire (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva 69; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.30*). See [Divine objects & treasures](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/divine-objects-and-treasures.md).
  - *Origin-variant (Harivaṃśa 69):* the tree was first fashioned by **Kaśyapa** for the delight of **Aditi**; when the churning-gods would have carried it off with Mount Mandara, Indra begged Śiva to leave it as **"Śacī's sporting tree," and Śiva then grew a whole Pārijāta forest on Mandara's slopes for Umā** — a frame that makes the tree doubly Indra's to guard. [late text]
- **The return of Aditi's earrings** — the episode is welded to the preceding **[Narakāsura-vadha](krishna-lila.md)**: having slain Naraka (Bhauma), Kṛṣṇa recovers **Aditi's stolen earrings (kuṇḍala)** and Varuṇa's parasol, and flies with **Satyabhāmā** on **Garuḍa** to **Amarāvatī** to restore the earrings to Aditi in person (*Bhāgavata X.59; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.30; Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva*).
- **Aditi's boon to Satyabhāmā** — Aditi, mother of the gods, receives them with honour and grants Satyabhāmā the boon of **unfading youth and beauty** for as long as she lives beside Kṛṣṇa; this same visit lets Satyabhāmā see the Pārijāta glittering in Śacī's garden and covet it (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.30; Bhāgavata X.59*).
  - *Note:* in the tighter **Viṣṇu Purāṇa / Bhāgavata** frame the theft flows directly out of this heavenly visit; the **Harivaṃśa** instead builds an elaborate separate cause — the Nārada intrigue below — and treats the whole cycle as an independent, greatly expanded *upākhyāna*. [scholarly]

## The provocation — Nārada, the single blossom, and Satyabhāmā's pride

- **The blossom given to Rukmiṇī** — the wandering sage **[Nārada](narada.md)**, ever the divine catalyst, brings a single Pārijāta flower into Kṛṣṇa's court; Kṛṣṇa bestows it on his chief queen **Rukmiṇī** (an incarnation of Lakṣmī). Its fragrance suffuses her and its news reaches the co-wives (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva; Bhāgavata X.59*).
- **Nārada fans the co-wife rivalry** — Nārada then goes to **Satyabhāmā** and pointedly tells her that the whole tree stands in Indra's heaven and that Kṛṣṇa gave its one flower to her rival; stung, the proud daughter of Satrājit demands that Kṛṣṇa fetch the **entire Pārijāta** and plant it in *her* courtyard, not Rukmiṇī's (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva; Bhāgavata X.59*).
  - *Note (the Nārada engine):* the episode belongs to the recurring **Nārada-kalaha** pattern — the sage engineers a quarrel whose only purpose is to produce a greater *līlā* and, here, to expose that **devotion outweighs display** (the Rukmiṇī/Satyabhāmā contrast that runs through the whole cycle). [scholarly]
- **Kṛṣṇa's promise** — bound by his word to Satyabhāmā, Kṛṣṇa first sends **Nārada as an envoy** to ask Indra to *lend* the tree for the fulfilment of Satyabhāmā's religious vow (the Puṇyaka, below); Nārada foresees Indra's refusal — the tree grants Śacī all her wishes and no god will surrender it — and Kṛṣṇa vows that if it is denied he will **take it by force** (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva 69 ff.*).

## The uprooting and the war with Indra (*Indra-yuddha*)

- **The theft from Nandana** — Kṛṣṇa, with Satyabhāmā, rides **Garuḍa** into Indra's paradise and **uproots the Pārijāta** to carry it to Dwārakā; the garden's guardians and **Śacī** protest that the tree is hers (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.30*).
- **Indra takes the field** — the affront to his queen and his heaven forces **Indra** himself to give chase with the assembled **Ādityas, Maruts, and Vasus**, wielding the **Vajra** and mounted on **[Airāvata](../08-beings-and-bestiary/)**, to recover the tree by arms (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva 76 "Indra Fights with Kṛṣṇa"; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.30*). See [Indra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/indra.md).
- **Avatāra against the king of the gods** — Kṛṣṇa on Garuḍa meets Indra's every weapon; the battle turns on the paradox that **Indra is worsted by his own supreme source** — Viṣṇu incarnate defeating the very deva-rāja who rules by Viṣṇu's grace. Kṛṣṇa breaks Indra's advance and Indra, his Vajra failing, withdraws (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva 76; Bhāgavata X.59; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.30*).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata X.59 — a token war):* the Bhāgavata treats the fight almost wryly — **Indra fights only for form**, is defeated, and does not truly grudge his Lord the tree; the accent is theological (the god must not seem to *give* what is really being *taken* by the Godhead) rather than martial. [late text]
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa — the full battle):* the Harivaṃśa develops a **sustained, multi-chapter combat** with the massed host of heaven, making it the martial climax of the Viṣṇu Parva; this is the version the brief flags as the "longest late set-piece." [scholarly]
- **Aditi's mediation and Indra's condition** — the quarrel is composed by the elders of heaven: **Aditi** (Indra's own mother, whose earrings Kṛṣṇa had just restored) reconciles the combatants, and Indra yields the tree, honouring Kṛṣṇa. Indra lays down one condition — that the Pārijāta will bloom on earth **only while Kṛṣṇa walks it, and return to Svarga when the avatāra departs** (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.30*).
  - *Note:* the reconciliation is the point. As with the [Bāṇāsura war](banasura-usha-aniruddha.md), the set-piece exists to be **resolved into concord** — heaven's order is affirmed, Indra keeps his dignity, and the avatāra's supremacy is shown without permanent injury to the deva-rāja. [scholarly]

## The tree at Dwārakā — devotion outweighs possession

- **Planted in Satyabhāmā's court** — the Pārijāta is brought to **Dwārakā** and set, as promised, by **Satyabhāmā's** apartments (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.31; Bhāgavata X.59*).
- **The flowers fall in Rukmiṇī's yard** — in the widely-retold turn, though the tree stands at Satyabhāmā's door its **blossoms drift down into Rukmiṇī's courtyard** — a sign that Rukmiṇī's quiet devotion, not Satyabhāmā's proud possession, draws the tree's grace; the motif rhymes with the whole Rukmiṇī/Satyabhāmā theology (*popular Vaiṣṇava retelling; cf. Bhāgavata gloss*). [folk]
- **Cross-node:** the Pārijāta is cataloged among the churning-treasures and *kalpa-vṛkṣas* in [Divine objects & treasures](../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/divine-objects-and-treasures.md); Kṛṣṇa's Dwārakā queens and sons ([Pradyumna](characters/pradyumna.md), Aniruddha) belong to the same stretch of [Kṛṣṇa-līlā](krishna-lila.md).

## The Puṇyaka-vrata — Satyabhāmā gives Krishna away and redeems him

- **The vow behind the theft** — in the Harivaṃśa the tree was fetched not merely to spite Rukmiṇī but to furnish **Satyabhāmā's Puṇyaka-vrata**, a woman's vow for winning and keeping a husband across lifetimes, which requires the Pārijāta; Nārada, again the instigator, expounds the rite to her (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva 78 "The History of the Puṇyaka Rite" ff.*).
- **The rite taught by Umā** — Nārada frames the vow with its mythic charter: **Umā (Pārvatī) first described the regulations of the Puṇyaka to an assembly of celestial ladies — Arundhatī and the others — in ages past**; Satyabhāmā then performs it under Nārada's direction (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva 78–82*).
- **The dāna of Kṛṣṇa** — the vow's astonishing core: to complete it Satyabhāmā must **give her husband away in gift (dāna)** — she formally bestows **Kṛṣṇa himself upon Nārada** — and then **redeem him** by giving in exchange his weight in gold and gems (the *tulā-dāna*, a balance-weighing) (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva 78 ff.*).
- **Wealth cannot outweigh the Lord** — heaped upon the scale, all Satyabhāmā's gold and jewels cannot balance Kṛṣṇa; the redemption succeeds only when the vow is completed in proper devotion, teaching that the Godhead is bought back not by treasure but by *bhakti* — the same lesson as the falling flowers (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva 78 ff.*).
  - *Variant (popular "Kṛṣṇa-tulābhāram"):* the best-known folk and South-Indian temple form has **Rukmiṇī tip the balance with a single Tulasī leaf** laid on the scale with love after all Satyabhāmā's gold has failed — the origin-tale behind the **tulābhāram** weighing-donation practised at Vaiṣṇava shrines. This exact "Tulasī-leaf" turn is a **later/regional elaboration** not spelled out in the Harivaṃśa Puṇyaka chapters. [folk] [regional]
- **Redemption and reward** — the vow duly finished, Kṛṣṇa is redeemed and Satyabhāmā secures the fruit of the Puṇyaka: union with her Lord in birth after birth (*Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva 78–82*).

## Afterlife — *kāvya*, drama, and dance

- **Telugu *prabandha*** — **Nandi Timmana's *Pārijātāpaharaṇamu*** ([scholarly] early 16th century CE), composed at the court of **Kṛṣṇadevarāya** of Vijayanagara, is the classic literary retelling; it made the sulking-heroine Satyabhāmā a byword and the "toe-kick" of the offended queen a stock poetic image (*Telugu kāvya tradition*). [scholarly]
- **Bhāmākalāpam and dance-drama** — Satyabhāmā's pride, quarrel, and reconciliation are the heart of **Bhāmākalāpam**, the celebrated **Kuchipudi** dance-drama traditionally ascribed to **Siddhendra Yogi** ([scholarly] c. 17th century CE); the Pārijāta theme likewise animates **Yakṣagāna**, **Kathakaḷi**, and **Bhāgavata Meḷa** stagings across the South (*performance tradition*). [regional]
- **Wider retellings** — the episode recurs as *Pārijāta-haraṇa* / *Pārijātāpaharaṇa* across Sanskrit and vernacular *kāvya*, and is a favourite subject of Rajput, Pahari, and Deccani miniature painting (Kṛṣṇa and Satyabhāmā on Garuḍa bearing the flowering tree, Indra in pursuit). [scholarly]

## Where the texts differ — quick catalog

- *Variant (Harivaṃśa, Viṣṇu Parva — the master version):* the **longest and only fully developed** telling — the Nārada intrigue, the envoy, the multi-chapter war with Indra, and the whole **Puṇyaka-vrata** sequence (the dāna and redemption of Kṛṣṇa) are unique to or fullest in it. [scholarly]
- *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5.30–31):* a compact retelling welding the theft to the Naraka episode and the Amarāvatī visit; fixes Indra's condition (the tree stays only while Kṛṣṇa is on earth) but **omits the Puṇyaka**. [scholarly]
- *Variant (Bhāgavata X.59):* the most **devotionalized and briefest** — a token war, Indra's willing defeat, the tree planted at Dwārakā; the theology of Kṛṣṇa's supremacy over Indra is the payload, the martial detail pared away. [late text]
- *Variant (folk/temple — Tulasī-leaf tulābhāram):* the Rukmiṇī-outweighs-gold-with-one-Tulasī-leaf ending, the falling-flowers motif, and the tulābhāram donation are **popular and regional overlays** onto the older Puṇyaka core. [folk] [regional]
