# Shodasarajakiya (The Sixteen Kings)

> Where this sits: a deep-dive into the **Ṣoḍaśarājakīya** ("Account of the Sixteen Kings"), the celebrated consolation-inset of the *Mahābhārata* in which Vyāsa (and, in a parallel frame, Nārada) recite the deaths of sixteen mighty *cakravartins* of old to teach grief's vanity — even the greatest and most righteous die. Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Cosmic slot: a **cross-yuga catalogue** spanning the deep Solar and Lunar dynastic past, recited within the [Droṇa Parva](mahabharata/07-drona-parva.md) of the Kurukṣetra war (Dvāpara→Kali junction).

The **Ṣoḍaśarājakīya** (also *Ṣoḍaśa-rājika*, "the Sixteen-King [hymn]") is a compact **index of great kings** woven into the *Mahābhārata* as a teaching against grief. After the slaying of [Abhimanyu](mahabharata/07-drona-parva.md) (and, in the recapped versions, in answer to other sorrows), the bereaved are consoled with the reminder that sixteen of the most powerful, generous, and dharmic emperors who ever lived — men who performed countless sacrifices, conquered the whole earth, and gave away mountains of gold and cattle — **all nonetheless died**; therefore grief for any single death is vain (*"na cāsīt na bhaviṣyati"* — they were and are no more, and so shall it be for all). It is the tradition's great **mortality-list**, tying together in a few verses many kings the corpus treats individually elsewhere. The core block stands in **Droṇa Parva 55–72**; a famous shorter recap (the **Sṛñjaya / Nārada** version) appears in the **Śānti Parva** strata, and the motif echoes again in the Aśvamedhika frame.

---

## The frame — why the kings are recited

- **The consolation to Yudhiṣṭhira** - Crushed by the death of his nephew [Abhimanyu](mahabharata/07-drona-parva.md) on Day 13 of the war, **Yudhiṣṭhira** is consoled by **Vyāsa**, who recites the deaths of sixteen supreme kings of antiquity so that the king will see that **death spares no one, however mighty or righteous**, and that mourning is therefore fruitless (*Mahābhārata, Droṇa Parva ~55–72*). [scripture]
- **The Sṛñjaya recap (Nārada)** - In a closely parallel telling, the sage **Nārada** (with **Parvata**) consoles **King Sṛñjaya** over the death of his son **Svarṇaṣṭhīvin** ("the boy who voided gold") by reciting the same roll of sixteen mighty kings who died despite their greatness — the version most often quoted as *the* Ṣoḍaśarājakīya (*Mahābhārata, Droṇa Parva / Śānti Parva 29*). [scripture]
  - *Variant (frame & placement):* the catalogue's verses recur in the **Śānti Parva** (Bhīṣma's instruction, ~ch. 29) and are alluded to in the **Aśvamedhika** strata; the count of "sixteen" is fixed by title, but the **exact roster varies slightly between recensions**, some lists naming Paurava or Gaya where another names Bhagīratha. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The refrain** - Each king's stanza closes on the same drumbeat — *he too died* — building the cumulative argument that lamentation is powerless against the universal law of death (*Droṇa Parva*). [scripture]

## The sixteen kings (the standard roster)

*The order and inclusion differ by recension; the following follows the common Droṇa-Parva sequence. Each was a sacrificer, conqueror, and giver beyond reckoning — and each died.*

- **Marutta** - The Kārta-virya-line emperor (son of Avikṣit) whose **golden sacrifice** was so opulent that the gods themselves served as attendants and Indra drank his Soma; his largesse became proverbial — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva; cf. Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa*). [scripture]
- **Suhotra** - A Paurava (Lunar-line) emperor over whom the sky once **rained gold**, who covered the earth with sacrificial stakes and gave away countless cattle and gold — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*).
- **Bṛhadratha / Paurava** - A world-ruler (in some lists the Aṅga king **Bṛhadratha**, in others simply **Paurava**) of immense sacrificial generosity, lord of the earth bounded by the seas — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*). [disputed]
- **[Śibi](shibi.md)** - Son of Uśīnara, the king who **cut his own flesh to ransom a dove** from a hawk and so won imperishable fame, ruler of the whole earth — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*; full episode in [Śibi](shibi.md)). [scripture]
- **Rāma Dāśarathi** - The Ikṣvāku prince **Rāma**, slayer of Rāvaṇa, whose reign (*Rāma-rājya*) is the very emblem of perfect dharmic rule, righteous beyond all — yet he too died (*Droṇa Parva*; cf. [Rāmāyaṇa](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md)). [scripture]
- **Bhagīratha** - The Ikṣvāku ascetic-king who by titanic penance **brought Gaṅgā down from heaven** to redeem the sons of Sagara, gave away a hundred thousand maidens decked in gold — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva; cf. Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa*).
- **Dilīpa** - The Solar-line emperor (Bhagīratha's forebear in some lists), of such sacrificial splendour that the sacrificial post (*yūpa*) and the gods' chariots filled his realm, giver of unnumbered cattle — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*).
- **[Māndhātṛ](mandhata.md)** - The motherless *cakravartin* "born from his father's side," suckled by Indra, conqueror of the seven continents and proverbial yardstick of kingship — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*; full treatment in [Māndhātṛ](mandhata.md)). [scripture]
- **Yayāti** - The great Lunar-dynasty emperor who exchanged old age for his son's youth, lord of the whole earth and supreme sacrificer, who ascended to and fell from heaven — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*; cf. [Yayāti](yayati.md)).
- **Ambarīṣa** - The emperor (here the Solar *cakravartin*, distinguished from the Sudarśana-devotee son of Nābhāga) under whom dwelt a thousand thousand kings, lord of vast sacrifices and gifts — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*; on the two Ambarīṣas see [Ambarīṣa & Durvāsā](ambarisha-durvasa.md)). [scholarly]
- **Śaśabindu** - The Yādava (Lunar-line) emperor of **ten thousand wives** and a hundred thousand sons, performer of a hundred horse-sacrifices, who gave away cattle past counting — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*).
- **Gaya** - The royal sacrificer at whose **Gayā-yajña** the leavings alone fed multitudes and whose generosity gave the sacred *tīrtha* of Gayā its name and sanctity — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva; cf. Vāyu Purāṇa, Gayā-māhātmya*). [scripture] [regional]
- **Rantideva** - The Cāndravaṃśa king of legendary hospitality at whose kitchens **two thousand cattle were dressed daily** for guests and from whose ablutions arose the river **Carmaṇvatī (Chambal)** — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva; cf. Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21*). [scripture]
- **Bharata** - The son of **Duṣyanta and Śakuntalā**, eponym of **Bhārata-varṣa**, the conqueror who performed the **Aśvamedha** scores of times and bound thousands of horses and elephants to his sacrificial posts — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*; cf. [Śakuntalā](mahabharata-minor-episodes.md)).
- **[Pṛthu](prithu-prithvi.md)** - The first consecrated sovereign, **Pṛthu Vainya**, who **milked the Earth** (Pṛthvī) for all crops and goods and made her arable, the archetype of righteous rule — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*; full treatment in [Pṛthu & Pṛthvī](prithu-prithvi.md)). [scripture]
- **Sagara** - (In many recensions) the Ikṣvāku emperor of **sixty thousand sons**, performer of the horse-sacrifice whose lost horse led to the digging of the ocean-bed (the *sāgara*) — yet he died (*Droṇa Parva*; cf. Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa). [disputed]
  - *Variant (rosters):* where one recension names **Sagara** or **Gaya**, another substitutes **Pṛṣadhra**, **Aṅga**, **Nṛga**, or **Nimi**; the **fixed number is sixteen**, but the precise membership is fluid across manuscripts. The figures most consistently present are Marutta, Suhotra, Śibi, Rāma, Bhagīratha, Māndhātṛ, Yayāti, Ambarīṣa, Śaśabindu, Gaya, Rantideva, Bharata, and Pṛthu. [scholarly] [disputed]

## Themes & significance

- **The mortality-argument** - The Ṣoḍaśarājakīya is the *Mahābhārata*'s most concentrated statement of *anityatā* (impermanence) in royal-heroic terms: it does not deny the kings' greatness — it **insists on it**, precisely so the conclusion ("yet they died") strikes harder. The rhetorical form is **a fortiori**: if *these* could not escape death, no one can, so grief is vain. [scripture]
- **A compact index of the corpus** - The list functions as the tradition's own **cross-reference table of ideal kings**, gathering into a single recitation figures otherwise scattered across the Purāṇas and the Rāmāyaṇa — each named here in a couplet, each treated at length elsewhere in this knowledge base ([Māndhātṛ](mandhata.md), [Pṛthu](prithu-prithvi.md), [Śibi](shibi.md), Yayāti, Ambarīṣa, Bharata). [scholarly]
- **Sacrifice, conquest, gift** - Every stanza measures a king by the same triad — **yajña (sacrifice), digvijaya (world-conquest), and dāna (giving)** — codifying the threefold ideal of the *cakravartin* that the dharma-literature holds up as the standard of kingship. [scholarly]
- **The two clocks** - The sixteen belong to **scriptural time** — the deep dynastic past of the Solar and Lunar lines, immemorial antiquity with no historical-clock dates — while the **recitation itself is fixed on the historical-narrative clock** of the Kurukṣetra war, at the Dvāpara→Kali junction (traditionally 3102 BCE). The inset thus reaches back across many *yugas* from within the war's narrative present. [scholarly]
- **Recension fluidity** - That the **roster shifts while the number sixteen holds** is itself instructive: "the sixteen kings" is a *formula* — a slot for "the greatest emperors of old" — into which different transmitters poured slightly different names, all drawn from the shared stock of paradigmatic *cakravartins*. [disputed] [scholarly]

## Sources

See [Droṇa Parva](mahabharata/07-drona-parva.md).

- **Mahābhārata (Ganguli translation), Droṇa Parva, "Sections" on the Sixteen Kings (Pratijna / Drona-vadha strata)** — English numbering of the Sṛñjaya–Nārada dialogue and the roll of kings.
