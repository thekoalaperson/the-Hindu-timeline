# Vyuṣitāśva and Bhadrā — sons begotten by the dead king

> Where this sits: a Candravaṃśa (Lunar-dynasty) deep-past *upākhyāna*, cited by **Kuntī** to her husband **Pāṇḍu** inside the Mahābhārata's [Ādi Parva](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md) (Sambhava Parva) to answer his plea that she raise up children for his cursed, childless line. Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Cosmic slot: Vyuṣitāśva reigns in the deep dynastic past of the 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga; the *telling* sits at the Dvāpara–Kali cusp, before the Pāṇḍavas are born. Keep the **two clocks** distinct — the king belongs to remote Paurava antiquity, the recitation to the eve of Kali.

The **Vyuṣitāśva–Bhadrā** episode is one of the Mahābhārata's compact charter-tales on **offspring beyond the body** — the doctrine that a wife may lawfully raise sons *for* her husband when he cannot beget them himself. It is embedded in the tense marital dialogue of the [Ādi Parva](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md): [Pāṇḍu](characters/pandu.md), barred from fatherhood by the [Kindama curse](characters/pandu.md), urges [Kuntī](characters/kunti.md) to conceive by a Brahmana of high merit; Kuntī, unwilling to be sent to another man, counters with this precedent — a dead king who, from beyond the grave, begets **seven sons** on his grieving widow — and asks Pāṇḍu instead to raise children on her by his own **ascetic power**. The tale is thus not a *niyoga*-by-a-stranger story at all but its opposite: an argument that the husband's own generative claim survives even death, if a chaste wife wills it. It never appears elsewhere in this corpus; this file is its canonical home.

## The frame — Pāṇḍu's plea and Kuntī's answer

- **Pāṇḍu's crisis** — cursed by the deer-sage [Kindama](characters/pandu.md) to die in the very act of union, Pāṇḍu is barred from begetting heirs; anguished at the prospect of a sonless line and the ancestral debt it leaves unpaid, he presses Kuntī to obtain offspring for him. (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, Sambhava; Ganguli §CXX)
- **The discourse on the ancient liberty of women** — Pāṇḍu grounds his request in *āpaddharma* (dharma in extremity) and archaic custom: he argues that in olden times women were **unconfined**, roaming at will and unbound to a single husband, and that this was not accounted sinful; the strict rule of marital fidelity, he says, was fixed only later. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXX)
- **Śvetaketu's reform** — Pāṇḍu recounts how **Śvetaketu**, son of Uddālaka Āruṇi, outraged to see a Brahmana lead his mother away by the hand in his father's presence, **instituted the marriage-bond** obliging a wife's chastity — proof, to Pāṇḍu, that the confinement of women is a human ordinance, not an eternal law, and may yield before the higher duty of continuing a line. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXX) [the classic Mahābhārata charter for niyoga]
- **Kuntī's refusal to go to another** — Kuntī answers, *"O virtuous one, it behoves you not to say so unto me"* — she declines to be sent to a stranger's bed; instead she offers a counter-precedent and a counter-proposal. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI)
- **The precedent she chooses** — Kuntī tells Pāṇḍu the "ancient history" of **Vyuṣitāśva and Bhadrā**, in which a *dead* king fathered sons on his widow, and closes by urging Pāṇḍu himself to do the like by tapas rather than delegate her womb to another man. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI)

## The tale — Vyuṣitāśva of the Puru line

- **The righteous Puru king** — in ancient days there lived in the **race of Puru** a king named **Vyuṣitāśva**, virtuous, austere, and mighty; he performed great sacrifices at which **Indra** and the ṛṣis attended in person. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI)
  - *Variant (lineage):* the epic and its Ganguli rendering place him **in the Puru (Paurava / Lunar) line**; some reference works name his father as **Śaṅkhaṇa**. The two are compatible — the Pauravas are a branch of the [Candravaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md). [scholarly]
- **Conqueror of all directions** — through the **Aśvamedha** (horse-sacrifice) and his own strength he subdued the kingdoms of every quarter, and "protected every class of his subjects as a father protects his own begotten sons." (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI)
- **Bhadrā, unrivalled on earth** — his beloved queen was **Bhadrā**, daughter of **Kakṣīvat**, a woman without equal in beauty; the two loved each other so deeply that the king was seldom apart from her. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI)
  - *Genealogical resonance:* the Kakṣīvat named as Bhadrā's father is commonly identified with the Ṛgvedic seer **Kakṣīvat**, son of the blind ṛṣi [Dīrghatamas](dirghatamas.md) — making Bhadrā a granddaughter of the *Asya Vāmasya* poet. The epic itself gives only the patronymic *Kākṣīvatī*; the identification is a scholarly inference, not a textual claim. [scholarly / disputed]
- **Death by excess** — his very devotion undid him: **sexual excess brought on phthisis (consumption)**, and within a few days the king died — childless. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI) [the tale's moral hinge — intemperate love as the cause of an empty throne]
- **Bhadrā's lament** — the widowed, sonless queen was plunged into grief; embracing the corpse, she wept bitterly and declared her wish to follow her lord in death, mourning that a wife without offspring is as one already fallen. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI)
- **The incorporeal voice** — as she clung to the body an **incorporeal voice** addressed her: *"Rise up, O Bhadrā… I grant thee this boon. I will beget offspring upon thee."* It bade her lie down beside the corpse, after her ritual bath, on the **eighth or the fourteenth night** of the lunar fortnight. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI)
  - *Note on the speaker:* the text presents the voice as issuing from the dead king himself — the husband's generative will speaking from beyond death — not as a separate deity; the sons are reckoned his own, born of his own body. [the point on which the whole argument turns]
- **The seven sons** — the chaste Bhadrā did as directed, and **"the corpse of her husband begat upon her seven children"** — **three Śalvas and four Madras** — the eponymous progenitors of the Śalva and Madra peoples. (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI)
  - *Variant (spelling / count):* the sons are transmitted as **three Śalvas (Salwas) and four Madras**; some printings read "Satvas" for Śalvas. The Madra people recur elsewhere in the epic as the line of Pāṇḍu's second queen [Mādrī](characters/madri.md) — a quiet resonance in Kuntī's choice of exemplum. [scholarly]

## What Kuntī argues from it

- **The closing appeal** — Kuntī ends the tale by turning it on Pāṇḍu: *"O bull of the Bharatas, do thou also beget offspring upon me, like the illustrious Vyuṣitāśva, by the exercise of that ascetic power which thou possessest."* (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §CXXI)
- **The precise claim** — the argument is *not* the standard niyoga plea (a wife bearing by a summoned man). It is the **stronger, narrower** claim that an **incapacitated — even dead — husband remains the true father**, and that a chaste wife's devotion plus the husband's own spiritual power can raise up sons *for him* without recourse to another. Kuntī uses it to keep the begetting **within the marriage**. [the file's central point]
- **How it lands** — Pāṇḍu, lacking such tapas and bound by the curse, cannot fulfil Kuntī's counter-offer directly; the deadlock is broken only when Kuntī later discloses the secret she has held since girlhood — the **mantra of invocation** given her by [Durvāsā](characters/kunti.md), by which any deva she summons must beget a child upon her. Vyuṣitāśva's precedent thus prepares the ground for the divine paternity of the elder Pāṇḍavas — Yudhiṣṭhira (Dharma), Bhīma (Vāyu), Arjuna (Indra). (Ādi Parva; Ganguli §§CXXI–CXXIII) See [Kuntī](characters/kunti.md) and [Pāṇḍu](characters/pandu.md).
- **Why Kuntī, not Pāṇḍu, tells it** — the dialogue's structure matters: Pāṇḍu supplies the *doctrine* (Śvetaketu, the ancient freedom of women), Kuntī supplies the *exemplum* and bends it toward fidelity. The tale is therefore filed as **Kuntī's precedent-tale to Pāṇḍu**, correcting the common misattribution that reverses the two speakers. [disambiguation]

## Reading — kṣetra, niyoga, and the two clocks

- **The doctrine of the "field" (kṣetra)** — the episode dramatises the epic's law that a son belongs to the **owner of the field (kṣetra = the wife/womb)**, not necessarily to the physical begetter; here the field and the seed-claim both remain the dead husband's, which is why the seven sons are unambiguously Vyuṣitāśva's. This is the same *kṣetraja* logic that makes [Pāṇḍu](characters/pandu.md) and his brothers the sons of Vicitravīrya though begotten by Vyāsa, and that underlies the whole niyoga institution. See [Varṇa, Āśrama & the Saṃskāras](../05-concepts-and-dharma/varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md). [scholarly]
- **The ancestral debt** — the urgency behind both Pāṇḍu's plea and Kuntī's exemplum is the **pitṛ-ṛṇa**, the debt to the ancestors discharged only by a son who continues the line and the *śrāddha* offerings; a sonless death imperils the forefathers. See [The Three Debts](../05-concepts-and-dharma/pancha-maha-yajnas-and-the-three-debts.md) and [Śrāddha & Pitṛ-rites](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md). [scholarly]
- **Ascetic power as generative power** — that a *dead* body could beget presupposes the pervasive epic premise that accumulated **tapas** and boons override ordinary biology; the same premise animates the divine-invocation births that follow. See [Tapas, Boons & Curses](../05-concepts-and-dharma/tapas-boons-and-curses.md). [scholarly]
- **A darker cousin-tale** — set beside the [Gālava–Mādhavī](galava-madhavi.md) *upākhyāna* (a woman's womb rented to king after king for horses), Vyuṣitāśva–Bhadrā runs the opposite way: there the woman is currency passed among men; here a chaste widow's devotion keeps the paternity with her *own* dead husband. The two are the epic's paired meditations on women, wombs, and lineage. [scholarly]
- **Scriptural clock** — Vyuṣitāśva's reign belongs to the **remote Paurava past** of the 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga; Kuntī tells it in the **late [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md)**, shortly before Kali begins (traditionally 3102 BCE). The tale is a moral charter, not a datable event.
- **Academic clock** — the Ādi Parva reached its form in the epic's long composition window, **[scholarly] c. 400 BCE – 400 CE**; the Śvetaketu passage and this exemplum are read as the Mahābhārata **theorising the shift from an archaic, looser kinship regime to codified marital fidelity and controlled *niyoga*** — the text arguing with its own social memory. [scholarly]
