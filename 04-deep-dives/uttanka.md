# Uttaṅka — the Quest for the Earrings

> Cosmic address: a *Vaivasvata-Manvantara* tale of the present [Mahāyuga's](../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md) Dvāpara→Kali cusp, framed inside the *Mahābhārata* and told in two widely separated places: the **Paushya sub-parva of the [Ādi Parva](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md)** (the great opening frame, just before Janamejaya's snake-sacrifice) and the **Uttaṅkopākhyāna of the [Āśvamedhika Parva](mahabharata/14-ashvamedhika-parva.md)** (the Anugītā stratum, after Kurukṣetra).
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · See also: [Mahābhārata overview](mahabharata/README.md) · [Devas, Asuras & the Lokas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md) (Nāgaloka, Indra, Agni) · [Kṛṣṇa-līlā](./krishna-lila.md) (the Viśvarūpa darśana) · [Sūrya/Solar & Lunar lineages](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) (King Saudāsa) · [Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha](./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) (Vasiṣṭha's curse on Saudāsa)

The tale of **Uttaṅka** (Utaṅka) — a brāhmaṇa disciple whose ordeal to fetch a queen's celestial **earrings (kuṇḍala)** as his teacher's fee (*gurudakṣiṇā*) leads him through a dung-eating god, the theft of the jewels by the serpent-king **Takṣaka**, and a fiery descent into the subterranean **Nāgaloka** — is told **twice** in the *Mahābhārata* with notably different casts. Its structural importance is huge: the **Paushya** telling is the literal hinge of the epic's frame-story, because Uttaṅka's grudge against Takṣaka is what goads King **[Janamejaya](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md)** into the **snake-sacrifice (sarpa-satra)** at which Vyāsa's pupil Vaiśampāyana first recites the whole *Mahābhārata*. The later **Āśvamedhika** telling re-versions the same quest with a different guru and king, and grafts on the famous **Uttaṅka–Kṛṣṇa Viśvarūpa** episode and the etiology of the desert rain-clouds called **Uttaṅka-megha**. Variant policy here is essential: the two accounts disagree on almost every proper name.

---

## Where it sits in the timeline

- **Frame** - A late-[Dvāpara](../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md) / early-Kali episode of the present Mahāyuga, embedded in the *Mahābhārata*. The Paushya telling is a **flashback** narrated within Janamejaya's court-frame (the action precedes Janamejaya's reign by a generation or so), while the Āśvamedhika telling is set in the immediate **post-war** period when Kṛṣṇa still lives. [scripture]
- **Narrative occasion (Ādi Parva)** - Told in the **Paushya** (Pauṣya) sub-parva, the third minor parva of the Ādi Parva, as part of the long preamble (Sauti's recitation to the ṛṣis at Naimiṣāraṇya) that explains *why* Janamejaya undertook the sarpa-satra (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva, Paushya Parva, ~1.3 Critical Edition).
- **Narrative occasion (Āśvamedhika Parva)** - Told as the **Uttaṅkopākhyāna** ("the tale of Uttaṅka"), an *upākhyāna* attached to the Anugītā section after the great war (*Mahābhārata*, Āśvamedhika Parva, ~14.52–58 Critical Edition).
- **Antiquity** - The Paushya version is reckoned the **older** core; the Āśvamedhika version is generally treated as a **later retelling/doublet** that bolts the quest onto a Kṛṣṇa-theophany — note the change of guru (Veda → Gautama) and king (Pauṣya → Saudāsa). [scholarly]

## Who Uttaṅka is

- **A brāhmaṇa disciple of impeccable obedience** - Uttaṅka is defined by his absolute, almost extreme, fidelity to his guru — the very virtue the story tests and rewards. He is repeatedly described as a great ascetic (*maharṣi*) of fierce *tapas* (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva).
- **Lineage / school** - In the Paushya telling he is pupil of **Veda**, himself a pupil of **Āyoda Dhaumya** (Apoda Dhaumya). In the Āśvamedhika telling he is reckoned **of the Bhṛgu race** and is the pupil of **Gautama** (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi 1.3; Āśvamedhika 14.52–58).
  - *Variant (guru's identity):* the two principal versions name **different gurus** — **Veda** (Paushya) vs. **Gautama** (Āśvamedhika). This is the single largest divergence and the reason the two are often regarded as independent recensions of one motif. [scholarly]
  - *Note — "two Uttaṅkas":* because the names of guru, king and queen all differ, some commentators treat these as **two distinct sages both named Uttaṅka**; the mainstream view treats them as **one figure, two retellings**. [disputed]

## The guru's-fee demand (the trigger)

- **Veda's wife sets the fee (Paushya version)** - After Uttaṅka completes his studies and asks what *gurudakṣiṇā* to render, Veda leaves it to his wife. She demands **the earrings (kuṇḍalas) worn by the queen of King Pauṣya (Pushya)**, to be brought within a set time (often "four days," for a coming ritual) (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva).
  - *Variant (the wife's motive):* in some tellings Veda's wife harbors **resentment** because Uttaṅka had, in strict celibate propriety, **declined to lie with her during her fertile season** while serving the household in Veda's absence — so the impossible errand is partly spite. [variant]
- **Ahalyā / Gautama's wife sets the fee (Āśvamedhika version)** - Here Uttaṅka has served **Gautama** a hundred years; aged and bent under firewood, he collapses, and the compassionate guru **restores his youth**, gives him his **daughter** in marriage, and dismisses him. For the fee, Gautama defers to his wife (named **Ahalyā** in the encyclopaedic summaries), who demands **the divine earrings of Queen Madayantī (Madayanti), wife of King Saudāsa** (*Mahābhārata*, Āśvamedhika Parva 14.52ff).
  - *Variant (the queen and king):* **Paushya version** → an **unnamed queen of King Pauṣya**; **Āśvamedhika version** → **Madayantī, wife of King Saudāsa (Kalmāṣapāda / Mitrasaha)**. The popular short retellings frequently **conflate** the two, calling the donor "Queen Madayantī" even in the Pauṣya frame. [disputed]

## The road to the earrings

- **The bull-rider and the dung (Paushya version)** - On the road Uttaṅka meets a **gigantic man seated on a huge bull**, who bids him **eat the bull's dung and drink its urine**. Uttaṅka hesitates; reassured that **his guru Veda had once done the same**, he consumes a little — and is thereby (unknowingly) made proof against death on the quest (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva).
  - *Allegorical key (given later by Veda):* the **giant = Indra**, the **bull = Airāvata** (Indra's elephant-mount), and the **dung/urine = amṛta** (the nectar of immortality) — so Uttaṅka had in fact swallowed the elixir. This decoding is the Paushya version's signature flourish. [scripture]
- **King Pauṣya and the curse-duel (Paushya version)** - Uttaṅka reaches **King Pauṣya**, who sends him to the inner apartments for the queen. A side-episode follows: over a perceived insult (an unclean meal / a hair found in the food), Uttaṅka and Pauṣya **trade curses** — Uttaṅka cursing the king to **blindness**, Pauṣya cursing Uttaṅka to **childlessness** — then both, recognizing fault, **retract** their curses (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva). [scripture]
- **The cannibal-king's leave (Āśvamedhika version)** - The Āśvamedhika road is darker: to reach Madayantī, Uttaṅka must first confront her husband **Saudāsa**, who at that time is the **man-eating Rākṣasa Kalmāṣapāda** — cursed into cannibalism by **[Vasiṣṭha](./vishwamitra-vasishtha.md)** (in the wider epic, over the Viśvāmitra–Vasiṣṭha feud). Caught by the ogre-king, Uttaṅka explains his sacred errand and **pledges to return** to be eaten after discharging it; Saudāsa, honoring the duty, **lets him go** and sends him to his queen (*Mahābhārata*, Āśvamedhika 14.53–54). [scripture]
- **The queen's warning** - The queen (Madayantī, or Pauṣya's queen) hands over the earrings but **warns** that they are **celestial** and **coveted by Takṣaka / the Nāgas** (and, in the Āśvamedhika version, by **Yakṣas, Rākṣasas and even the gods**), so he must guard them and **never let them touch the ground** (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva; Āśvamedhika 14.54).
  - *Variant (the token of consent):* in the Āśvamedhika version Madayantī first demands a **token proving the cursed king's consent** before she will part with the earrings, which Uttaṅka supplies. [variant]
  - *Property of the earrings:* they are said to make the wearer **free from hunger, thirst, fear and the ravages of poison/age** — which is why gods and Nāgas alike covet them. [scripture]

## The theft by Takṣaka

- **Takṣaka the Nāga-king** - **Takṣaka**, one of the great serpent-kings (a son of Kaśyapa and Kadrū, brother to Vāsuki/Śeṣa in the Nāga genealogy), is the recurring antagonist of the whole Ādi-Parva frame — the same serpent who will later **kill King Parīkṣit** with his bite (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya & Āstīka Parvas).
- **The snatch (Paushya version)** - Carrying the earrings home, Uttaṅka **sets them down** to perform ritual ablutions / to eat by a stream. **Takṣaka, disguised as a naked mendicant (kṣapaṇaka)**, seizes the moment, **grabs the earrings, resumes serpent form, and dives into a hole** in the earth leading down to Nāgaloka (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva).
- **The fall (Āśvamedhika version)** - Here Uttaṅka, climbing a **tree** to pluck fruit, ties the earrings (knotted in **deerskin**) to a branch; the knot **slips**, the earrings **fall to the ground**, and a **serpent immediately seizes them and vanishes into an ant-hill (valmīka)** (*Mahābhārata*, Āśvamedhika 14.55).
  - *Variant (manner of loss):* **theft by a disguised Takṣaka** (Paushya) vs. **accidental fall + seizure by a snake into an ant-hill** (Āśvamedhika) — the moral shifts from "guard against the enemy" to "heed the warning about the ground." [scholarly]

## The descent into Nāgaloka

- **The digging and Indra's vajra** - Uttaṅka **digs** after the serpent — frantically with a stick — for a long span (the Āśvamedhika version specifies **thirty-five days**). **Indra**, watching the doomed labor, **sends his vajra (thunderbolt)** into the stick/earth to **carve a tunnel** down into the serpent-world (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva; Āśvamedhika 14.55–56).
- **The cosmic loom-vision (Paushya version only)** - In Nāgaloka Uttaṅka beholds an allegorical tableau which he praises in hymns:
  - **two women** at a **loom** weaving with **black and white threads**;
  - a **wheel with twelve spokes** turned by **six youths**;
  - a **man with a horse** standing by.
  Uttaṅka extols them; the man bids him **blow into the horse** (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva).
  - *Allegorical decoding (given afterward by Veda):* the **two weavers = Dhātṛ & Vidhātṛ** (Ordainers/creation); the **black & white threads = night and day**; the **twelve-spoked wheel = the year** (its 360 spokes/segments = the days); the **six youths = the six seasons (ṛtu)**; the **man = Indra**, the **horse = Agni** (the fire-god) — a compact almanac of cosmic time. [scripture]
- **The horse of fire (both versions)** - The horse is **Agni** in disguise (in the Āśvamedhika telling, expressly because Agni was **Gautama's own guru** and so aids his disciple's disciple). At the rider's word Uttaṅka **blows into the horse** (its mouth/ear/hindquarters, per recension); **flames and smoke pour from every pore** of the animal and **flood Nāgaloka with fire**, choking the serpents in their holes (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva; Āśvamedhika 14.56–57).
- **The serpents yield** - Terrified, the Nāgas surrender. In the Paushya version **Takṣaka himself** gives back the earrings; in the Āśvamedhika version the serpents **"headed by Vāsuki"** worship Uttaṅka and **return the jewels, begging pardon** (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva; Āśvamedhika 14.57).
  - *Variant (who returns the earrings):* **Takṣaka** (Paushya) vs. **Vāsuki and the assembled Nāgas** (Āśvamedhika). [scholarly]

## The return and the fees discharged

- **Racing the deadline (Paushya version)** - With the ritual-deadline almost expired, the **horse (Agni)** carries Uttaṅka back to Veda's hermitage **before sunset**; he delivers the earrings to the guru's wife in time, and Veda then **explains all the omens** (bull, dung, weavers, wheel, horse) Uttaṅka had met (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya Parva). The fee is discharged; Uttaṅka takes leave.
- **The Kṛṣṇa theophany (Āśvamedhika version only)** - Returning across the desert, Uttaṅka meets **[Kṛṣṇa](./krishna-lila.md)**, who tells him the **Kurukṣetra war is over** and the Kurus largely destroyed. Enraged that the all-powerful Kṛṣṇa "let it happen," Uttaṅka makes to **curse him**; Kṛṣṇa stays him, expounds the **necessity of the war for dharma**, and reveals his cosmic **Viśvarūpa (Universal Form)** to the sage — a post-war echo of the Gītā theophany (*Mahābhārata*, Āśvamedhika 14.53–54). [scripture]

## Uttaṅka-megha — the desert rain-clouds (Āśvamedhika only)

- **The boon of water** - Awed, Uttaṅka asks a boon: that **water be available to him whenever he is thirsty** in the waterless Maru desert. Kṛṣṇa grants it (*Mahābhārata*, Āśvamedhika 14.54).
- **The Caṇḍāla and the rejected amṛta** - Later, parched, Uttaṅka remembers the boon. **Indra appears disguised as a Caṇḍāla (outcaste) hunter** carrying water (in fact **amṛta**) in a leather skin, and offers it. Repelled by the giver, Uttaṅka **refuses** — thrice. The Caṇḍāla vanishes; Kṛṣṇa appears and explains that **Indra had been told to give amṛta only if Uttaṅka would accept it from a Caṇḍāla** — a test of equanimity that Uttaṅka **failed** by his caste-pride (*Mahābhārata*, Āśvamedhika 14.54). [scripture]
- **Uttaṅka-megha ("Uttaṅka's clouds")** - To honor the boon nonetheless, Kṛṣṇa promises that **rain-clouds will rise at Uttaṅka's wish** to slake him in the desert. These rare desert downpours are thereafter called **Uttaṅka-megha**, "the clouds of Uttaṅka" — an etiology for stray rain over the arid Marwar/Maru lands (*Mahābhārata*, Āśvamedhika 14.54).
  - *Variant emphasis:* later retellings read the Caṇḍāla episode as a **humility / non-discrimination lesson** (true amṛta refused through pride), paralleling other "god-in-disguise tests" such as [Ambarīṣa & Durvāsā](./ambarisha-durvasa.md). [scholarly]

## The Janamejaya link — the snake-sacrifice (Paushya / Āstīka frame)

- **Uttaṅka's grudge becomes the epic's engine** - After his ordeal, Uttaṅka **bears an undying hatred of Takṣaka**. Learning that **Takṣaka had killed King [Parīkṣit](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md)** (Parīkṣit, Arjuna's grandson, doomed by the curse of the sage-boy Śṛṅgin and bitten by Takṣaka), Uttaṅka goes to Parīkṣit's son **King Janamejaya** at Hāstinapura and **incites him to vengeance** (*Mahābhārata*, Paushya & Āstīka Parvas).
- **The sarpa-satra (snake-sacrifice)** - Goaded, **Janamejaya undertakes the sarpa-satra**, a great fire-rite into which serpents are drawn and burned, aimed above all at **Takṣaka**. The mantras drag Takṣaka toward the flames (*Mahābhārata*, Āstīka Parva).
- **Āstīka halts it; the epic begins** - The sacrifice is **stopped by the young brāhmaṇa Āstīka** (son of Jaratkāru and the Nāga-woman Manasā/Jaratkāru), who wins a boon and saves the surviving serpents — **Takṣaka clinging in mid-air is spared**. It is at **this very sattra** that **Vaiśampāyana**, pupil of Vyāsa, first **recites the *Mahābhārata*** to Janamejaya — so Uttaṅka's quest is, structurally, the **spark that lights the entire epic's frame** (*Mahābhārata*, Āstīka Parva). [scripture]
  - *Variant attribution:* some summaries credit the **incitement of Janamejaya to Uttaṅka**, others foreground the king's own grief and the **ministers'** counsel; the epic gives Uttaṅka the catalytic role in the Paushya frame. [variant]

## Other Uttaṅka episodes (same sage, separate tales)

- **Uttaṅka and the demon Dhundhu (Vana Parva)** - In a distinct episode, the asura **Dhundhu** (son of **Madhu & Kaiṭabha**, boon-protected by Brahmā) lies buried beneath the sands of the **Ujjālaka / Maru desert** near Uttaṅka's hermitage, breathing fire and earthquakes. Uttaṅka performs *tapas* to **Viṣṇu**, who foretells that the Ikṣvāku king **Kuvalāśva** (Kulavalāśva) will slay it. Kuvalāśva and his sons kill Dhundhu and the king earns the title **Dhundhumāra** ("Slayer of Dhundhu") (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva, Mārkaṇḍeya-samāsya, ~3.192–195). Links the [Solar (Ikṣvāku) line](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) to Uttaṅka. [scripture]
  - *Note:* this Uttaṅka of the Dhundhu tale is sometimes identified with, sometimes distinguished from, the earrings-Uttaṅka. [disputed]

## Variant accounts across the texts

- **Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva — Paushya (Pauṣya) sub-parva (~1.3)** - The **older core**: guru **Veda** (pupil of Āyoda Dhaumya); fee = earrings of **King Pauṣya's queen**; the **bull-rider/dung = Indra/Airāvata/amṛta**; curse-duel with Pauṣya; **theft by disguised Takṣaka**; the **loom-and-wheel cosmic vision**; Agni-horse; earrings returned by **Takṣaka**; leads directly into the **Janamejaya sarpa-satra** that frames the epic.
- **Mahābhārata, Āśvamedhika Parva — Uttaṅkopākhyāna (~14.52–58)** - The **later doublet**: guru **Gautama** (Uttaṅka of the Bhṛgu race), youth restored, married to Gautama's daughter; fee (via **Ahalyā**) = earrings of **Madayantī**, wife of the cursed cannibal-king **Saudāsa/Kalmāṣapāda**; **accidental fall** of the earrings + serpent into an **ant-hill**; **thirty-five days** digging; Agni-horse (Agni as Gautama's guru); earrings returned by **Vāsuki and the Nāgas**; then the unique **Kṛṣṇa Viśvarūpa**, the **Caṇḍāla/amṛta test**, and the **Uttaṅka-megha** clouds. **No Janamejaya link here.**
- **Mahābhārata, Vana Parva (~3.192–195)** - The separate **Dhundhu/Dhundhumāra** episode, linking Uttaṅka to King Kuvalāśva of the Ikṣvākus. [scripture]
- **Popular & encyclopaedic conflations** - Modern retellings routinely **merge** the two earrings-versions — most often importing the name **"Madayantī"** (from Āśvamedhika) into the **Paushya** plot, or attaching the Janamejaya climax (from Paushya) to the **Gautama/Madayantī** version. The primary text keeps them **separate**. [disputed]
- **Differences at a glance:**
  - *Guru:* **Veda** vs. **Gautama**.
  - *King / queen:* **Pauṣya & unnamed queen** vs. **Saudāsa-Kalmāṣapāda & Madayantī**.
  - *Loss of jewels:* **theft by disguised Takṣaka** vs. **accidental fall, seized into an ant-hill**.
  - *Nāgaloka centerpiece:* **loom + 12-spoked wheel + Agni-horse** vs. **fiery Agni-horse** (no loom).
  - *Restorer of earrings:* **Takṣaka** vs. **Vāsuki & the Nāgas**.
  - *Sequel:* **Janamejaya sarpa-satra** vs. **Kṛṣṇa Viśvarūpa + Uttaṅka-megha**. [scholarly]

## Themes and significance

- **Frame-engine of the *Mahābhārata*** - More than a curiosity, the Paushya–Uttaṅka tale is the **causal trigger** of the epic's outer frame: Uttaṅka's vendetta against Takṣaka → Janamejaya's **sarpa-satra** → Vaiśampāyana's recitation of the *Mahābhārata*. The whole epic is, narratively, told *because of* this serpent-feud.
- **Obedience and the perils of the path** - Uttaṅka is the paragon of the disciple who will brave death, deception and the underworld to discharge a guru's fee — the *gurudakṣiṇā* ideal pushed to its extreme; compare the loyalty/sacrifice motifs around [Ekalavya](./ekalavya.md) and [Karṇa](./karna.md).
- **Hidden amṛta, hidden gods** - The **dung-as-amṛta** and the **Caṇḍāla-as-Indra-with-nectar** motifs both teach that the **divine and the immortalizing arrive in repellent disguise**, and that pride/squeamishness forfeit grace — a lesson shared with [Ambarīṣa & Durvāsā](./ambarisha-durvasa.md) and the god-in-disguise tests across the corpus.
- **A clock in the underworld** - The **loom-and-wheel vision** is a compact cosmology of **time** — day/night threads, the twelve-spoked year, the six seasons — set, strikingly, inside the serpent-world; it ties this episode to the timeline's central concern, the [structure of cosmic time](../00-time-and-cosmology/README.md).
- **Serpent ambivalence** - Takṣaka and the Nāgas are thieves and killers here, yet the tale ends (in the Āstīka frame) by **sparing the serpents** — the *Mahābhārata*'s characteristic refusal to let even the snake-sacrifice run to genocide. Cross-link the broader Nāga material under [Devas, Asuras & the Lokas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md).
- **Kṛṣṇa's second theophany** - The Āśvamedhika Viśvarūpa is a deliberate **post-war reprise** of the Bhagavad-Gītā's vision, here granted to an outsider-sage who would presume to judge God — reinforcing the epic's theodicy of the war as dharma. See [Kṛṣṇa-līlā](./krishna-lila.md).
