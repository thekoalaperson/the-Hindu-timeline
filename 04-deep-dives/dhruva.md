# Dhruva — the Steadfast Pole Star

> Cosmic address: Shveta-Varaha Kalpa → 1st (Svāyambhuva) Manvantara → the dawn of human dynastic prehistory, in the Uttānapāda line of Svāyambhuva Manu. Set at the "first morning" of Brahmā's current day. Cross-link: [Manvantara 1 — Svāyambhuva](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-01-svayambhuva.md).
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Up to [README](../README.md)

**Dhruva** ("the fixed, immovable, eternal one") is the boy-prince whose extreme tapas wins him a personal vision of Viṣṇu and the **immutable polar seat — Dhruva-loka / Dhruva-pada**, the still point of the turning sky around which all stars, planets and the seven sages (saptarṣi) wheel. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa gives the seat a full Kalpa (VP 1.12.93); the Bhāgavata places it beyond even those who live for a Kalpa (BhP 4.9.20–21). He is the archetypal child-devotee (bāla-bhakta), the model of resolve born of a wound — slighted by a proud stepmother, he turns humiliation into the single most famous penance of a small boy in the Purāṇic corpus. His core story is told in the **Viṣṇu Purāṇa (1.11–12)** and, in its most elaborate form, in the **Bhāgavata Purāṇa (Canto 4, ch. 8–12)**; a shorter version, with no stepmother and with Brahmā as the giver of the seat, stands in the Harivaṃśa and several other Purāṇas.

*Verse numbers: Viṣṇu Purāṇa (VP) cited by the critical edition (Pathak, via GRETIL); the Bombay vulgate runs one verse higher in parts of 1.11–1.12 (e.g. vulgate 1.11.31 = crit. 1.11.30). Bhāgavata (BhP) cited by the standard (Vedabase/BBT) numbering.*

## The household: Uttānapāda's two queens

- **The line** - Brahmā → Svāyambhuva Manu → **Uttānapāda** (the younger of Manu's two sons by Śatarūpā; Priyavrata is the elder) → Dhruva. Uttānapāda has two queens (BhP 4.8.7–8; VP 1.11.1–3; younger son: Liṅga P. 1.5.17). See [Manvantara 1 — Svāyambhuva](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-01-svayambhuva.md).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 2.7; Brahma P. 2.7; Brahmāṇḍa P. 1.36.84–85):* Uttānapāda was **adopted by the Prajāpati Atri** (Manu gave him to Atri as a son).
- **Sunīti, the neglected queen** - Dhruva's mother **Sunīti** ("good conduct"), the chief queen (mahiṣī) but not much loved by the king (VP 1.11.3; BhP 4.8.8). The Liṅga calls her the elder wife (agrajā, Liṅga P. 1.62.4).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 2.7–9; Brahma P. 2.7–9; Matsya P. 4.34–36; Brahmāṇḍa P. 1.36.87–89):* Dhruva's mother is **Sūnṛtā**, daughter of Dharma, and she bears Uttānapāda four sons — Dhruva, Kīrtimat, Āyuṣmat and Vasu (Brahma P. 2.9; Brahmāṇḍa P. 1.36.89–90, which adds two daughters). The names vary: the Harivaṃśa reads Ayasmat and Ayaspati for the last two (Harivaṃśa 2.9), the Matsya Apasyati and Apasyanta (Matsya P. 4.35). No rival queen appears. The VP's closing verses may join the two names: "dhruvasya jananī ceyaṃ sunītir nāma sūnṛtā" (VP 1.12.99). Dutt renders "Suniti, who is otherwise named Sunritā"; *sūnṛtā* can also be read as an epithet, "kindly-spoken" [translator's reading].
- **Suruci, the favoured queen** - The proud, beloved **Suruci** ("good taste/pleasure"), mother of **Uttama**, Dhruva's half-brother and the king's favourite (VP 1.11.2; BhP 4.8.8–9).
- **Dhruva's age** - A small child. The VP's sages call him "four or five years old" (catuḥpañcābda, VP 1.11.33), and the phantom Sunīti says five (VP 1.12.17). The Bhāgavata says five (BhP 4.8.65, 4.11.28, 4.12.23, 4.12.42) and once "five or six" (ṣaṭ-pañca-varṣa, BhP 4.12.43). *(Opens at the dawn of human dynastic time, very early in the Svāyambhuva Manvantara.)*
  - *Variant (Liṅga P. 1.62.5):* he is **seven** years old.
  - *Variant (Brahmāṇḍa P. 1.36.91):* the penance is dated to the **first Tretā-yuga** of the Manvantara.

## The slight: pushed from the royal lap

- **The lap incident** - Dhruva sees Uttama seated on the lap of their father, who sits on the royal throne, and wants to climb up too; with Suruci watching, the king does not welcome him (VP 1.11.4–5; BhP 4.8.9).
  - *Variant (Liṅga P. 1.62.5–6):* Dhruva is already sitting on his father's lap when Suruci **shakes him off** and puts her own son there.
- **Suruci's cutting words** - Suruci humiliates the child, so that the king can hear: he was not born from *her* womb, so the throne and the king's lap are not for him (VP 1.11.6–10; BhP 4.8.10–12). In the Bhāgavata she adds a taunt: let him do tapas, win the favour of Nārāyaṇa, and so be **reborn from her womb** (BhP 4.8.13). The VP has no such advice. Her sneer becomes, ironically, the literal blueprint of his destiny.
- **Sunīti's consolation** - Weeping and angry, Dhruva runs to his mother. Sunīti tells him not to wish others harm and to take refuge at the feet of the Lord. Brahmā won his post and Svāyambhuva Manu his success by worshipping Him, and she knows no one else who can remove his grief (BhP 4.8.17–23).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu P. 1.11.15–23):* Sunīti grieves that Suruci spoke the truth: each person enjoys the fruit of merit from former births, so he should not resent it but be virtuous, friendly and kind, and gather merit. She does not mention Viṣṇu. Viṣṇu enters the VP only through the sages (VP 1.11.42–48).
  - *Variant (Liṅga P. 1.62.8–10):* Sunīti tells him to win a fixed station (dhruvaṃ sthānam) by his own power.
- **Dhruva's vow** - The four- or five-year-old resolves to win, by his own deeds, a station **higher than any ever attained**, one that even his father never reached; let Uttama have the throne (VP 1.11.24–28). In the Bhāgavata he tells Nārada he wants a place higher than any in the three worlds, never held by his forefathers or by anyone else (asmat-pitṛbhir … anyair apy anadhiṣṭhitam, BhP 4.8.37). He leaves the palace alone (VP 1.11.29; BhP 4.8.24).

## The counsel of Nārada (or the seven sages)

- **Nārada's testing and instruction** - In the Bhāgavata the wandering sage **Nārada** meets the boy and marvels at such kṣatriya pride in a child (BhP 4.8.25–27). He first tries to dissuade him: such tapas is hard even for yogīs, so go home and try when grown (BhP 4.8.28–34). Then, won over, he directs him to **Madhuvana** on the Yamunā (BhP 4.8.40–42), teaches him breath-control and a meditation on Viṣṇu's form (BhP 4.8.43–52), and initiates him into worship of Vāsudeva (BhP 4.8.53–61).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu P. 1.11.30–56, 1.12.6):* there is **no Nārada**. In a grove outside the city Dhruva finds **seven sages** (sapta munīn, VP 1.11.30), "Marīci and the others" (VP 1.12.6). Each of them in turn urges him to worship Govinda/Viṣṇu, and the Sanskrit names the speakers: Marīci, Atri, Aṅgiras, Pulastya, Kratu, Pulaha and Vasiṣṭha (VP 1.11.42–48; the vulgate puts Pulaha before Kratu). Together they then teach him the mantra (VP 1.11.51–54). Both the critical edition and the vulgate read the seven sages here.
  - *Variant (Liṅga P. 1.62.11–21):* the boy's guru is **Viśvāmitra**, who teaches "oṃ (praṇava) namo 'stu vāsudevāya" (Liṅga P. 1.62.20) and calls Keśava "born from the right side of Śambhu" (Liṅga P. 1.62.18) [Śaiva framing]. At the close Viṣṇu gives the fuller form, with praṇava, namaḥ and "bhagavat", and the chapter credits Dhruva's success to the "twelve-syllable knowledge" (dvādaśākṣaravidyā, Liṅga P. 1.62.37–40). The narrator is Mārkaṇḍeya (Liṅga P. 1.62.2).
- **The mantra** - Nārada gives the twelve-syllable mantra **"Oṃ Namo Bhagavate Vāsudevāya"** (BhP 4.8.54), with a meditation on Viṣṇu's four-armed, dark-blue, lotus-eyed form held in the heart (BhP 4.8.45–51). This is one of the canonical givings of the dvādaśākṣara mantra.
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu P. 1.11.53–56):* the formula the sages teach is "**hiraṇyagarbha-puruṣa-pradhāna-vyakta-rūpiṇe oṃ namo vāsudevāya śuddha-jñāna-svabhāvine**" (VP 1.11.54). It is not a first giving: **Svāyambhuva Manu**, Dhruva's grandfather, had chanted it before and won from Janārdana prosperity rare in the three worlds (VP 1.11.55–56). A verse the critical edition marks as an addition credits Dhruva's success to the "greatness of the twelve-syllable [mantra]" (VP 1.12.96*, vulgate 1.12.97) [interpolation].
- **Nārada visits the king** - After Dhruva sets out for Madhuvana, Nārada goes to Uttānapāda. The king is ashamed that, ruled by his wife, he drove away a five-year-old son, and Nārada assures him that the Lord protects the boy and he will soon return (BhP 4.8.63–70). The scene is absent from the VP.

## The great penance in Madhuvana

The graduated, ever-intensifying austerity is the heart of the legend — a child out-performing the rishis.

- **Madhuvana** - The VP explains the name: the forest was held by the daitya **Madhu**, and later Śatrughna killed Madhu's son Lavaṇa and founded **Mathurā** there (VP 1.12.2–5). The Bhāgavata calls it a sacred forest where the Lord always dwells (BhP 4.8.42, 4.8.62).
- **The escalating fast (Bhāgavata)** - Month by month Dhruva reduces his sustenance (BhP 4.8.71–77):
  - first month: fruits and berries every third day;
  - second month: dry grass and leaves every sixth day;
  - third month: water every ninth day;
  - fourth month: air every twelfth day;
  - fifth month: no breath at all, standing on **one leg**, motionless as a post, his mind fixed on the Supreme.

  The Bhāgavata reckons the whole penance at **six months** (māsair ahaṃ ṣaḍbhir, BhP 4.9.30). At BhP 4.12.43 the Sanskrit says only "in a few days" (ahobhir alpaiḥ), which BBT renders "six months" [translator's gloss].
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu P. 1.12.5–11):* there is no month-by-month fast. Dhruva meditates on Viṣṇu within himself until Hari pervades him. When he stands on his **left foot**, half the earth bends; on his right, the other half. The pressure of his big toe makes the earth, its mountains, rivers and oceans shake, and the gods are agitated.
  - *Variant (Liṅga P. 1.62.22):* he lives on greens, roots and fruits for **one year**, chanting the mantra.
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 2.10; Brahma P. 2.10; Matsya P. 4.36; Agni P. 18.3):* he performs tapas for **three thousand divine years**, "seeking great fame"; the Brahmāṇḍa P. gives **ten thousand** divine years, fasting (Brahmāṇḍa P. 1.36.90–91).
- **The worlds suffocate** - In the Bhāgavata, as he holds Brahman in meditation the three worlds tremble (BhP 4.8.78). As Dhruva stands on one leg, his big toe presses down half the earth, which rocks like a boat carrying an elephant (BhP 4.8.79). When, meditating with undivided mind on the Self of the universe, he closes the gates of his body and stops his breath, the **breathing of the whole universe is choked**, and the gods and world-guardians run to Hari (BhP 4.8.80–81). BBT renders that he became "practically one in heaviness with Lord Viṣṇu" [translator's gloss].
- **The gods' appeal** - The devas beg Viṣṇu for relief. In the Bhāgavata they ask only to be saved from the suffocation, and the Lord promises to stop the boy (BhP 4.8.81–82). In the VP, after their attempts to break his trance fail, the terrified gods suspect he wants **Indra's or Sūrya's place**, or Kubera's, Varuṇa's or Soma's. Viṣṇu assures them he seeks none of these and sends them home (VP 1.12.31–40).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu P. 1.12.12–30):* before the petition, the **Yāma gods with Indra** try to break his meditation. **Kūṣmāṇḍas** take many forms, and an illusory (māyāmayī) **Sunīti** weeps before him, begging him to give up so cruel a tapas at five years old. When that fails, rākṣasas with flaming weapons and howling jackals threaten to kill and eat him. Fixed on Govinda, the boy does not even perceive them. The Bhāgavata has no such episode.
  - *Variant (Liṅga P. 1.62.23–27):* vetālas, rākṣasas, lions and other beasts come to bewilder him, and a **piśācī in Sunīti's form** weeps over him; he keeps chanting and does not look.

## Viṣṇu's darśana and the boon

- **The Lord appears** - Viṣṇu comes to Madhuvana on Garuḍa (BhP 4.9.1). When the form Dhruva held in meditation suddenly vanishes, he opens his eyes and finds the Lord standing before him (BhP 4.9.2). In the VP, Dhruva hears Viṣṇu offer a boon, opens his eyes, and sees before him the Hari he had seen in meditation (VP 1.12.41–45).
- **The Dhruva-stuti** - Overwhelmed and unable to frame praise, Dhruva is touched by Viṣṇu with his **conch (śaṅkha)**. Vedic understanding floods him, and he pours forth the celebrated **Dhruva-stuti** (BhP 4.9.4–17; VP 1.12.46–75). In the Bhāgavata the conch touches his cheek (kapole, BhP 4.9.4; BBT renders "forehead" [translator's gloss]). In the VP, Dhruva first asks, as a boon, for the power to praise, and the Lord touches him with the tip of the conch (VP 1.12.48–51). The VP's hymn reworks the Puruṣa-sūkta (VP 1.12.58–65). The conch-touch granting speech appears in both texts and in the Liṅga (Liṅga P. 1.62.31).
- **The boon — the polar seat** - Viṣṇu grants **Dhruva-pada / Dhruva-loka**, a luminous, **immovable celestial station**:
  - **Viṣṇu Purāṇa:** above the three worlds, the support of all stars and planets (sarvatārāgrahāśrayaḥ), above the sun, the moon, the planets, all the nakṣatras, the saptarṣi and the gods who travel in vimānas (VP 1.12.90–92). Some gods last a caturyuga, some a manvantara, but Dhruva's seat is given for a **Kalpa** (VP 1.12.93).
  - **Bhāgavata:** a shining world that no one has held before. The planets, nakṣatras and stars circle it like oxen yoked around a threshing post, and Dharma, Agni, Kaśyapa, Śukra and the forest-dwelling sages, with the stars, circle it keeping it on their right. It stands beyond those who live for a Kalpa (parastāt kalpa-vāsinām) (BhP 4.9.20–21). BBT renders this as surviving "even after the dissolution" [translator's gloss]. Above all, it is Viṣṇu's own abode, from which none returns (BhP 4.9.25).

  The North Star (**Dhruva-tārā**, the pole star) is thereby a *person* — the steadfast bhakta.
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 2.11; Brahma P. 2.11; Matsya P. 4.37):* the giver is **Brahmā**, not Viṣṇu. Pleased, he grants Dhruva a station "equal to his own", immovable, in front of the saptarṣi. In the Brahmāṇḍa, Brahmā gives him the highest station of the lights, lasting until the dissolution of beings and free of rising and setting (Brahmāṇḍa P. 1.36.92–93).
  - *Variant (Agni P. 18.2–4):* the brief Agni summary keeps Suruci and Sunīti and the three thousand divine years, but has **Hari** grant the fixed station "before the sages".
  - *Variant (Liṅga P. 1.62.35–38):* Viṣṇu calls the polar seat "this supreme station of mine", which he says he once obtained from **Śaṅkara** by tapas (Liṅga P. 1.62.36–37) [Śaiva framing].
  - *Note (Viṣṇu P. 1.12.97–98; Harivaṃśa 2.12–13; Agni P. 18.4–5):* seeing his glory, **Uśanas** (Śukra), preceptor of gods and asuras, sings a verse: "Oh, the power of his tapas! The seven sages stand with Dhruva placed before them."
- **A reign first** - In the Bhāgavata, Viṣṇu also decrees that, once his father retires to the forest, Dhruva will rule the whole earth for **36,000 years** without ageing, perform great sacrifices, and remember the Lord at death (BhP 4.9.22–24; the reign fulfilled BhP 4.12.13).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu P. 1.12.83–88):* the VP gives a **past-life cause** instead. In a former birth Dhruva was a devout brāhmaṇa who served his parents. He befriended a splendid prince, longed for royal fortune, and so was born a prince in Uttānapāda's house. The VP has no earthly reign, Yakṣa war or later life. Its account ends with Dhruva in his station (VP 1.12.96), apart from a starred passage, absent from the constituted text, that briefly tells of his return home (VP 1.12.95*33) [interpolation]. That passage closely echoes the Bhāgavata's homecoming, down to the king trusting "what the devarṣi said" (compare BhP 4.9.37–43).
- **Dhruva's regret** - In the Bhāgavata the boon becomes a matter of regret. In the hymn Dhruva already calls the worship of God for worldly gain folly and asks only for the company of devotees (BhP 4.9.9–11). The Lord grants the station anyway, because He knows the desire in his heart (BhP 4.9.19). Dhruva goes home displeased. He laments that the gods, afraid of falling from their posts, clouded his mind (BhP 4.9.32), and that, having pleased the Lord, he asked for perishable things, "like a poor man asking an emperor for broken rice" (BhP 4.9.27–35).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu P. 1.12.81–82, 1.12.89–92):* there is no such turn. Dhruva explicitly asks for the highest, imperishable station because of Suruci's taunt, and receives it.

## Return, reign, and the war with the Yakṣas

*(This section follows the Bhāgavata alone; the Viṣṇu Purāṇa has none of it.)*

- **Homecoming** - Uttānapāda, who trusts Nārada's word, rides out to meet his son with both queens and Uttama. He embraces him, and Suruci herself blesses him (BhP 4.9.37–53). In due course the king consecrates Dhruva and retires to the forest (BhP 4.9.66–67).
- **Marriages** - Dhruva marries **Bhrami**, daughter of the Prajāpati Śiśumāra, and **Ilā**, daughter of Vāyu (BhP 4.10.1–2).
- **Death of Uttama** - The still-unmarried **Uttama** (akṛtodvāha), out hunting in the mountains, is **slain by a mighty Yakṣa** (puṇya-jana), and his mother "follows his path" (BhP 4.10.3). The Lord had foretold how: searching the forest for her son, Suruci would perish in a **forest fire** (BhP 4.9.23).
- **Dhruva's war on the Yakṣas** - Enraged, Dhruva drives north, into the quarter frequented by Rudra's followers, and finds the Yakṣa city, full of Guhyakas, in a Himālayan valley (BhP 4.10.4–5; BBT instead places Śiva's followers in the city; BBT names it **Alakāpurī** [translator's gloss]). He slaughters the Yakṣas in battle. When their sorcery fills the sky with storms, monsters and a rising sea, sages encourage him, and he fixes the **Nārāyaṇa-astra** to his bow; the illusions vanish (BhP 4.10.6–4.11.5).
- **Manu's intervention** - When Dhruva begins killing **innocent Yakṣas** for one offender's crime, his grandfather **Svāyambhuva Manu** comes with sages and stays his hand (BhP 4.11.6–35). Collective vengeance is adharma (BhP 4.11.7–9); birth and death are ruled by the Lord as Time, so the Yakṣas did not truly kill his brother (BhP 4.11.18–24); anger is the foremost enemy on the spiritual path (BhP 4.11.31–32); and he must pacify Kubera (BhP 4.11.33–34).
- **Kubera's boon** - **Kubera**, lord of the Yakṣas, then appears, praises Dhruva for giving up his enmity, and offers a boon. Dhruva asks only for **unbroken remembrance of Hari** (BhP 4.12.1–9).

## The final ascent

*(Bhāgavata, except where noted.)*

- **Renunciation and the celestial chariot** - After 36,000 years of rule Dhruva hands the throne to his son (BhP 4.12.13–14) and retires to **Viśālā** (BhP 4.12.16; BBT "Badarikāśrama" [translator's gloss]). There, absorbed in meditation, he sees a vimāna descend. Viṣṇu's attendants **Nanda and Sunanda** come to bear him to Viṣṇu's abode (BhP 4.12.19–27).
- **Conquering Death** - As he is about to board, Dhruva sees personified **Death (Mṛtyu/Antaka)** approach, sets his foot on Death's head, and mounts the vimāna (BhP 4.12.30).
- **Sunīti exalted** - Seated in the vimāna, he remembers his mother **Sunīti**: how can he go and leave her behind? Nanda and Sunanda show him that she is already going ahead of him in another vimāna (BhP 4.12.32–33). He passes the saptarṣi and reaches Viṣṇu's abode, around which all the luminaries circle (BhP 4.12.34–39).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu P. 1.12.94):* Viṣṇu promises that **Sunīti**, too, will dwell **near Dhruva as a star in a vimāna** for as long as he does. In the VP's closing verses Uśanas praises the mother who won the highest place by bearing Dhruva (VP 1.12.99–100).
  - *Variant (Liṅga P. 1.62.36, 1.62.39):* the gods, Gandharvas, Siddhas and sages **install Dhruva with his mother** in the polar station.
- **Nārada's verses and the phala-śruti** - Nārada sings Dhruva's glory at the Pracetās' sacrifice (BhP 4.12.40–43). Hearing the story brings wealth, fame and long life, and it is to be recited morning and evening (BhP 4.12.45–52; compare VP 1.12.95, 1.12.101–102).

## Descendants — the line toward Vena and Pṛthu

- **Dhruva's sons (Bhāgavata)** - By Bhrami, **Kalpa** and **Vatsara**; by Ilā, **Utkala** and a daughter (BhP 4.10.1–2).
- **Utkala passes over** - When Dhruva leaves for the forest, his son **Utkala**, unattached from birth, does not want the throne. The ministers take him for a simpleton and crown his younger brother **Vatsara** (BhP 4.13.6–11).
- **Down to Vena and Pṛthu (Bhāgavata)** - The line runs **Vatsara** (and Svarvīthi) → **Puṣpārṇa** → **Vyuṣṭa** → **Sarvatejā** → **Cākṣuṣa** (the sixth Manu) → **Ulmuka** → **Aṅga**. Aṅga's wife is **Sunīthā**, Death's daughter; their son is the tyrant **Vena**, and from Vena comes the first king **Pṛthu** — see [Pṛthu & the Milking of the Earth](prithu-prithvi.md) (BhP 4.13.12–20, 4.13.39).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu P. 1.13.1–11):* the VP gives a different line. Dhruva's sons are **Śiṣṭi** and **Bhavya**. Śiṣṭi and Succhāyā have five sons: Ripu, Ripuñjaya, Vipra, Vṛkala and Vṛkatejas. Ripu and Bṛhatī have **Cākṣuṣa**, who fathers **Manu** (Cākṣuṣa) on Puṣkariṇī. Manu has ten sons by Naḍvalā, among them **Kuru**; Kuru's sons by Āgneyī include **Aṅga**, and Aṅga and **Sunīthā**, Death's eldest daughter, have **Vena**. The Agni P. summary has the same line, with Ūru for Kuru (Agni P. 18.6–11).
    - *Śambhu, wife or grandson? [disputed]:* Both Sanskrit editions consulted read VP 1.13.1 as "dhruvāc chiṣṭiṃ ca bhavyaṃ ca **bhavyāc** chaṃbhur vyajāyata", which literally makes Śambhu a son of Bhavya. The parallel verses read "**dhruvāc** chambhur vyajāyata" (Agni P. 18.6; Brahma P. 2.14; Harivaṃśa 2.14), "Śambhū bore [them] to Dhruva", and Dutt's VP translation follows that sense: "his spouse Shambhu gave birth to two sons" [translator's reading].
  - *Variant (Matsya P. 4.38–40):* Dhruva's son **Śiṣṭa** is born of **Dhanyā**, a daughter of Manu; the line then runs through Ripuñjaya to Cākṣuṣa Manu.
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 2.14):* by Śambhū, Dhruva fathers **Śliṣṭi** and a second son, **Mānya** (śliṣṭaṃ ca mānyaṃ ca). Śliṣṭi's five sons by Succhāyā are then those of the VP: Ripu, Ripuñjaya, Vipra, Vṛkala and Vṛkatejas.
    - A passage the critical edition relegates to its apparatus inserts extra generations, Prācīnagarbha, Udāradhī (Indra in a former birth) and Divaṃjaya, before Ripu and Ripuñjaya (Harivaṃśa 2.14*39) [interpolation].

## Significance, astronomy & cross-links

- **The model child-devotee** - Dhruva and **Prahlāda** are the twin paragons of bāla-bhakti in the Purāṇas; both children, both saved by single-minded surrender to Viṣṇu/Nṛsiṃha. See [Prahlāda & Narasiṃha](prahlada-narasimha.md) and [devas, asuras & the lokas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md).
- **Dhruva-loka in cosmography** - Dhruva-loka sits at the very top of the wheeling heavens.
  - **Viṣṇu Purāṇa:** Dhruva is one lakh yojanas above the saptarṣi and is the **pivot (meḍhī)** of the whole wheel of lights (VP 2.7.9–10). The star-body of Hari is shaped like a **śiśumāra** (a porpoise or dolphin). Dhruva sits in its **tail**, the sun, moon, planets and stars are bound to him by cords of wind, and Janārdana in its heart supports it (VP 2.9.1–6).
  - **Bhāgavata (5th Canto):** Dhruva-loka is **thirteen lakh yojanas** above the saptarṣi, "the supreme abode of Viṣṇu". There Dhruva sustains those who live for a Kalpa (BhP 5.23.1). The lights circle him like oxen around a threshing post (BhP 5.23.2–3), and he is at the tip of the śiśumāra's tail (BhP 5.23.4–5).

  The Liṅga likewise says Auttānapādi turns like the hub of a potter's wheel, leading the planets, his fixity a gift of Viṣṇu's grace (Liṅga P. 1.54.28–31). See [00-time-and-cosmology](../00-time-and-cosmology/README.md).
- **In the epic** - The Mahābhārata knows **Dhruva Auttānapādi** as a star of the northern sky. Viśvāmitra is said to blaze "in the midst of Dhruva, son of Uttānapāda, and the brahmarṣis", in the northern quarter (MBh 13.3.15). The pole star is also watched for omens: before the Kurukṣetra war "Dhruva, blazing, moves terribly *apasavyam*" (MBh 6.3.16), which Ganguli renders "wheeleth towards the right". A full Mahābhārata telling of the lap-and-penance legend was not found (see Sources).
- *[scholarly]* **Precession note:** because of the precession of the equinoxes, the actual celestial pole drifts. In the centuries when the epics and early Purāṇas were redacted (c. 400 BCE–400 CE), **Polaris** stood roughly ten degrees or more from the true pole. So the *personification* of a fixed pole-deity predates Polaris's close alignment, and earlier "pole stars" (e.g. Thuban, c. 3rd millennium BCE) once held the seat. The myth fixes in story what the sky only approximates [scholarly].
- *[folk]* Across South Asia Dhruva is the standard moral exemplar for children — steadfastness, the dignity of the slighted, and devotion outweighing birth-rank; "Dhruva-tārā" (the Pole Star) is proverbial for the constant guide.
