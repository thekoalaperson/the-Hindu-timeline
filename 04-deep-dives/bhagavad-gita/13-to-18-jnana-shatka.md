# Gita Ch.13-18 — The Jnana Shatka in Detail (incl. the Charama Shloka)

> Where this sits: the third and final sextet (*ṣaṭka*) of the [Bhagavad Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md) — chapters 13–18, the **Jñāna-ṣaṭka** ("knowledge sextet"). In the Vaiṣṇava *tat-tvam-asi* mapping of the three ṣaṭkas, this set unpacks **"asi"** (the union of jīva and Lord). Spoken by [Kṛṣṇa](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) to Arjuna at Kurukṣetra in the [Dvāpara Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) of our 28th Mahāyuga.
>
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](../) · Up: [The Bhagavad Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md)

The final six chapters move from devotion (the Bhakti-ṣaṭka, 7–12) into systematic metaphysics and the practical question of liberation. They analyze the body and its knower (13), the three strands of nature and the one who transcends them (14), the cosmic tree and the three "persons" with the Supreme above them (15), the two moral constitutions of beings (16), the three grades of faith (17), and finally the **grand synthesis** of Ch.18 — the longest chapter — which resolves renunciation, duty, and surrender into the climactic *charama-śloka*, 18.66. Two clocks throughout: the Gītā's **in-narrative** Dvāpara setting (war traditionally c. 3102 BCE) vs. **academic** dating of composition c. 2nd c. BCE – 2nd c. CE [scholarly]. Verse-numbering note: Chapter 13 has **34 or 35 verses** depending on recension (some texts add an opening Arjuna question, *prakṛtiṃ puruṣaṃ caiva…*, shifting all numbers by one).

## Ch. 13 — Kṣetra-Kṣetrajña-Vibhāga Yoga (The Field and Its Knower)

- **The opening distinction** - The body (and by extension mind, intellect, ego, senses, and all of material nature attached to the person) is the **kṣetra** ("field"); the conscious self that knows it is the **kṣetrajña** ("knower of the field"). Krishna declares: "Know Me to be the knower of the field in *all* fields" — the supreme Self as the universal *kṣetrajña*. (Gītā 13.1–3)
- **Inventory of the field** - The kṣetra is itemized: the five great elements, ego (*ahaṅkāra*), intellect (*buddhi*), the unmanifest (*avyakta*), the ten senses + mind, the five sense-objects, plus desire, hatred, pleasure, pain, the aggregate body, sentience, and firmness — the whole psychophysical apparatus. (Gītā 13.5–6)
- **The 20 marks of true knowledge** (*jñāna*) - Not information but virtue: humility, unpretentiousness, non-violence, forbearance, uprightness, service to the teacher, purity, steadfastness, self-control, dispassion, absence of egoism, even-mindedness, unswerving devotion to the Lord, solitude, constancy in self-knowledge — all this is "knowledge," the rest is ignorance. (Gītā 13.7–11)
- **The Knowable** (*jñeya*) - Brahman, "beginningless," is described in paradox: with hands and feet everywhere, eyes and faces everywhere; without senses yet illuminating the senses; unattached yet sustaining all; beyond and within all beings; far and near; undivided yet appearing divided in beings. (Gītā 13.12–18)
- **Prakṛti and Puruṣa** - Both are beginningless (*anādī*); prakṛti is the cause of body, organs, and action; puruṣa is the cause of the experience of pleasure and pain. Puruṣa seated in prakṛti experiences the guṇa-born qualities; attachment to the guṇas is the cause of rebirth in good and evil wombs. (Gītā 13.19–21)
- **The indwelling Witness** - Within this body dwells the supreme Puruṣa — the **Witness** (*upadraṣṭā*), Permitter (*anumantā*), Sustainer (*bhartā*), Experiencer (*bhoktā*), Great Lord (*maheśvara*), and Supreme Self (*paramātmā*). (Gītā 13.22)
- **Seeing rightly = liberation** - One who sees the Lord seated equally in all beings, the imperishable amid the perishable, sees truly; who sees prakṛti alone as the doer and the Self as non-doer, sees truly. He does not destroy the Self by the self, and reaches the supreme. (Gītā 13.27–31)
  - *Cross-ref:* the Sāṅkhya prakṛti/puruṣa dualism here is the same scaffolding used in the [Yoga Sūtras of Patañjali](../yoga-sutras-of-patanjali.md) and Ch.2's Sāṅkhya Yoga.

## Ch. 14 — Guṇatraya-Vibhāga Yoga (The Three Guṇas)

- **The three strands that bind** - Prakṛti's three **guṇas** ("strands/qualities") bind the imperishable embodied Self to the body: **sattva** (purity, luminosity — binds by attachment to happiness and knowledge), **rajas** (passion, of the nature of craving and attachment — binds by attachment to action), **tamas** (darkness, born of ignorance — binds by heedlessness, indolence, and sleep). (Gītā 14.5–9)
- **The guṇas in flux** - They mutually overpower one another; when sattva prevails the body's gates shine with knowledge; rajas brings greed, restless activity; tamas brings darkness, inertia, delusion. (Gītā 14.10–13)
- **Death and the guṇas** - Dying in sattva → birth among the pure/wise; in rajas → birth among the action-attached; in tamas → birth in deluded wombs. The fruits: sattva → purity; rajas → pain; tamas → ignorance. (Gītā 14.14–18)
- **Transcending the guṇas** - When the seer perceives no agent other than the guṇas and knows That which is beyond them, he attains the Lord's nature (*mad-bhāva*). Crossing the three guṇas, the embodied one is freed from birth, death, old age, and sorrow, and attains immortality (*amṛtam aśnute*). (Gītā 14.19–20)
- **The marks of the guṇātīta** ("one beyond the guṇas") - Arjuna asks for the signs (14.21). Krishna's portrait: he neither hates illumination, activity, and delusion when present nor longs for them when absent; he sits unconcerned, "unmoved by the guṇas," knowing "the guṇas alone act"; equal in pleasure/pain, in a clod, stone, and gold; the same toward friend and foe; equal in honor and dishonor, praise and blame; abandoning all undertakings (*sarvārambha-parityāgī*). (Gītā 14.22–25)
- **The bhakti bridge** - The means to transcend the guṇas is unswerving devotion: "He who serves Me with unfailing *bhakti-yoga*, crossing the guṇas, becomes fit for becoming Brahman" — Krishna declares Himself the basis of Brahman, of immortality, of the eternal dharma. (Gītā 14.26–27) This verse links the Jñāna-ṣaṭka back to the [Bhakti-ṣaṭka](../bhagavad-gita.md).

## Ch. 15 — Puruṣottama Yoga (The Supreme Person)

This is the shortest chapter (20 verses) yet, in Vaiṣṇava reckoning, the doctrinal crown: it names **Puruṣottama** explicitly and is recited daily before meals in many traditions.

- **The inverted cosmic tree** - Saṃsāra is figured as an eternal **aśvattha** (peepal / sacred fig) tree growing *upside-down*: roots above (in Brahman/the Lord), branches below (the worlds), its leaves the Vedic hymns (*chandāṃsi*). One who knows it is a "knower of the Veda." (Gītā 15.1)
  - *Sanskrit (15.1):* **ūrdhva-mūlam adhaḥ-śākham aśvatthaṃ prāhur avyayam | chandāṃsi yasya parṇāni yas taṃ veda sa veda-vit ||** ("They speak of an imperishable aśvattha with roots above and branches below, whose leaves are the hymns; who knows it knows the Veda.")
  - *Note:* the image inverts the **Kaṭha Upaniṣad** 6.1 (*ūrdhva-mūlo'vāk-śākha*) [cross-ref [Nachiketa / Kaṭha Upaniṣad](../nachiketa-katha-upanishad.md)]; the "downward" secondary roots are bound by actions in the world of men. The tree's true form, end, beginning, and basis are not perceived from within saṃsāra. (Gītā 15.2–3)
- **The axe of detachment** - This firmly-rooted tree is to be cut down with the "strong axe of non-attachment" (*asaṅga-śastreṇa dṛḍhena*); then one seeks that primal goal from which there is no return, surrendering to the primeval Puruṣa. (Gītā 15.3–4)
- **The descent of the jīva** - An eternal fragment (*sanātanaḥ aṃśaḥ*) of the Lord becomes the living soul, drawing to itself the five senses and the mind; entering and leaving bodies it carries them like wind carrying scents. (Gītā 15.7–8)
- **The Lord as cosmic sustainer** - Krishna is the light of sun, moon, and fire; He enters the earth and sustains beings by His power; becoming the sap-giving moon He nourishes plants; as the digestive fire (*vaiśvānara*) in living bodies He digests the four kinds of food; He is seated in all hearts as memory, knowledge, and their loss; He alone is to be known by all the Vedas, is the author of Vedānta and knower of the Veda. (Gītā 15.12–15)
- **The three Puruṣas** - The chapter's metaphysical core distinguishes:
  - **Kṣara Puruṣa** ("perishable") - all beings as bodies bound in the changing world. (Gītā 15.16)
  - **Akṣara Puruṣa** ("imperishable") - the unchanging, called *kūṭastha* ("standing on the peak / immovable"). (Gītā 15.16)
  - **Puruṣottama** ("the Supreme Person") - the highest Self, the imperishable Lord (*īśvara*) who pervades and sustains the three worlds; being beyond the perishable and higher even than the imperishable, He is celebrated in Veda and world as **Puruṣottama**. (Gītā 15.17–18)
- **The fruit of knowing Puruṣottama** - "He who, undeluded, thus knows Me as Puruṣottama, knows all and worships Me with his whole being." Krishna calls this the "most secret teaching" (*guhyatamaṃ śāstram*); knowing it, one becomes wise and accomplished. (Gītā 15.19–20)
  - *Sectarian note:* Madhva and the Sri-Vaiṣṇava lineages treat 15.16–18 as decisive proof-text that the Supreme is a distinct *third* reality (a personal Lord), not merely the akṣara/Brahman of the formless reading. Advaita reads Puruṣottama as the non-dual Brahman appearing as the witness of both kṣara and akṣara. [disputed]

## Ch. 16 — Daivāsura-Sampad-Vibhāga Yoga (Divine and Demonic Natures)

- **The divine endowment** (*daivī sampad*) - A 26-item roster of virtues born for liberation: fearlessness, purity of heart, steadfastness in knowledge-yoga, charity, self-restraint, sacrifice, study, austerity, uprightness, non-violence, truth, absence of anger, renunciation, tranquility, absence of malice, compassion, freedom from greed, gentleness, modesty, steadiness, vigor, forgiveness, fortitude, purity, absence of hatred and pride. (Gītā 16.1–3)
- **The demonic endowment** (*āsurī sampad*) - Six (and their offshoots): ostentation, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness, and ignorance — born for bondage. (Gītā 16.4)
- **The demonic mentality** - Krishna gives an extended psychological portrait (often read as the most caustic passage in the Gītā): the *āsurī* know neither right action nor restraint; deny the world has a moral foundation or a God; act from lust; bound by a hundred snares of hope and craving; amass wealth by unjust means; think "this I have gained, that I shall gain," "I am lord, enjoyer, successful, mighty"; self-conceited, performing sacrifices in name only for show. (Gītā 16.7–18)
- **Their fate** - Such cruel haters Krishna "hurls perpetually into demonic wombs"; falling birth after birth into ever-lower states, they reach the most degraded condition. (Gītā 16.19–20)
- **The three gates to hell** - The single most-cited verse of the chapter: lust (*kāma*), anger (*krodha*), and greed (*lobha*) are the threefold gate of self-destructive hell (*trividhaṃ narakasyedaṃ dvāraṃ nāśanam ātmanaḥ*); one should abandon all three. (Gītā 16.21)
- **Released by abandoning the gates** - The man freed from these three gates of darkness works for his own good and attains the supreme goal. (Gītā 16.22)
- **Scripture as the standard** (*śāstra-pramāṇa*) - The chapter closes by making revealed scripture the authority (*śāstra*) for deciding what should and should not be done; he who acts on impulse, casting aside scripture, attains neither perfection, happiness, nor the supreme goal. (Gītā 16.23–24)

## Ch. 17 — Śraddhā-traya-Vibhāga Yoga (The Three Kinds of Faith)

- **The question of unguided faith** - Arjuna asks: those who worship with faith but ignore scriptural rule — what is their standing? Krishna answers that **faith (*śraddhā*) itself is threefold**, conforming to a person's innate constitution (*sattvānurūpā*): "a person is made of his faith — as is his faith, so is he" (*yo yac-chraddhaḥ sa eva saḥ*). (Gītā 17.1–3)
- **Faith by guṇa** - Sāttvika persons worship the gods (*devas*); rājasika worship yakṣas and rākṣasas; tāmasika worship ghosts and spirits (*pretas/bhūtas*). Those who practice severe, ostentatious, ego- and lust-driven austerity, tormenting the body and the indweller within, are of demonic resolve. (Gītā 17.4–6)
- **Food in three modes** - Even diet reflects the guṇas: **sāttvika** food — juicy, smooth, firm, heart-pleasing, promoting life, vigor, health, joy (17.8); **rājasika** — bitter, sour, salty, hot, pungent, dry, burning, causing pain and disease (17.9); **tāmasika** — stale, tasteless, putrid, leftover, impure (17.10).
- **Sacrifice, austerity, charity in three modes** -
  - *Yajña:* sāttvika = offered by rule, without desire for fruit, as a duty; rājasika = for show and result; tāmasika = without faith, rule, food-offering, mantra, or gift. (Gītā 17.11–13)
  - *Tapas* (austerity) is analyzed by **body** (worship, purity, non-violence), **speech** (truthful, beneficial, non-agitating words + study), and **mind** (serenity, gentleness, silence, self-control). Each then graded sāttvika/rājasika/tāmasika. (Gītā 17.14–19)
  - *Dāna* (charity): sāttvika = given at the right place/time to a worthy person without expecting return; rājasika = given grudgingly or for a return; tāmasika = given at the wrong place/time, to the unworthy, with disrespect. (Gītā 17.20–22)
- **Oṃ Tat Sat** - The threefold designation of Brahman, used since creation to consecrate Vedic acts: **Oṃ** — uttered to begin acts of sacrifice, charity, and austerity; **Tat** — "That," uttered by seekers of liberation when performing such acts *without* desire for fruit; **Sat** — "the Real/the Good," denoting reality, goodness, an auspicious act, and steadfastness in sacrifice/austerity/charity. Anything done **without faith** (*aśraddhayā*) is "*asat*" — barren here and hereafter. (Gītā 17.23–28)

## Ch. 18 — Mokṣa-Sannyāsa Yoga (The Grand Synthesis)

The longest chapter (78 verses); Krishna himself calls his teaching now complete and reviews the whole Gītā through the lens of the three guṇas before the climactic surrender.

- **Sannyāsa vs. tyāga** - Arjuna asks the difference. Krishna: **sannyāsa** = the giving-up of desire-prompted (*kāmya*) actions; **tyāga** = the relinquishing of *the fruits* of all actions. He sides decisively with tyāga as the practicable ideal — actions of sacrifice, charity, and austerity are *not* to be abandoned but performed *while* abandoning attachment and fruit. (Gītā 18.1–9)
- **The three kinds of tyāga** -
  - *Sāttvika tyāga:* duty done because it ought to be done, abandoning attachment and fruit. (18.9)
  - *Rājasika tyāga:* abandoning duty out of fear of bodily trouble — yields no fruit of relinquishment. (18.8)
  - *Tāmasika tyāga:* abandoning an obligatory duty out of delusion. (18.7)
  - The true *tyāgī* neither hates disagreeable action nor clings to agreeable; embodied beings cannot abandon actions entirely, so "he who relinquishes the fruit of action is the true relinquisher." (Gītā 18.10–11)
- **The five factors of action** - Sāṅkhya's analysis of every deed: the body (*adhiṣṭhāna*), the agent (*kartā*), the various organs, the manifold efforts, and **daiva** (divine providence / fate) as the fifth. He who sees the Self as the sole agent, due to untrained understanding, sees wrongly. (Gītā 18.13–16)
- **Knowledge, action, agent — each in three modes** - Krishna grades *jñāna* (sāttvika sees the one undivided in all; tāmasika clings to one effect as if it were all), *karma*, and *kartā* (agent) by guṇa, and likewise **buddhi** (intellect), **dhṛti** (firmness), and even **sukha** (happiness — sāttvika happiness is "like poison at first, nectar in the end," born of self-knowledge). (Gītā 18.18–39)
- **Varṇa by guṇa (svabhāva), not merely birth** - Krishna assigns the duties of the four *varṇas* according to the guṇas born of one's nature (*svabhāva-jaiḥ guṇaiḥ*): brāhmaṇa (serenity, restraint, austerity, purity, knowledge), kṣatriya (heroism, vigor, leadership, generosity), vaiśya (agriculture, cattle, trade), śūdra (service). Worshiping the Lord through one's own ordained work, a person attains perfection. (Gītā 18.41–46)
  - *Svadharma over para-dharma:* "Better one's own duty though imperfect than another's well-performed" (*śreyān svadharmo viguṇaḥ…*, echoing 3.35); one should not abandon the work to which one is born even if flawed, for all undertakings are clouded by some fault as fire by smoke. (Gītā 18.47–48) [Vaiṣṇava bhakti commentaries stress the *guṇa/svabhāva* basis; the verse is also widely cited in modern debates over caste-by-birth vs. caste-by-quality — flag as contested. [disputed]]
- **The ascent to Brahman and then to bhakti** - The disciplined, dispassionate one, fixed in solitude and meditation, "becomes fit to become Brahman" (*brahma-bhūyāya kalpate*); having become Brahman, serene, he attains supreme **devotion** (*parā bhakti*) to the Lord; by devotion he knows the Lord in truth and then *enters* Him. (Gītā 18.50–55) — knowledge culminates in devotion, not the reverse.
- **"Surrender all acts to Me"** - Mentally renouncing all actions to the Lord, taking refuge in *buddhi-yoga*, one should constantly think of Him. By His grace one crosses all obstacles; refusing out of egoism ("I will not fight"), one's nature will compel one anyway. The Lord dwells in the heart of all beings, whirling them by His *māyā* as if mounted on a machine (*yantra*). (Gītā 18.56–61)
- **The penultimate counsel** - "Take refuge in Him alone with your whole being; by His grace you shall attain supreme peace and the eternal abode." (Gītā 18.62)

### The Charama Śloka — 18.66

- **The verse** - After declaring he has imparted "knowledge more secret than the secret" and bidding Arjuna reflect and act as he wishes (18.63), and then giving the "most secret of all" (*sarva-guhyatamam*) teaching (18.64) — "fix your mind on Me, be My devotee, sacrifice to Me, bow to Me" (18.65) — Krishna delivers the supreme final word:
  - *Sanskrit (18.66):* **sarva-dharmān parityajya mām ekaṃ śaraṇaṃ vraja | ahaṃ tvāṃ sarva-pāpebhyo mokṣayiṣyāmi mā śucaḥ ||**
  - *Translation:* "Abandoning all dharmas, take refuge in Me alone; I will liberate you from all sins — do not grieve."
- **Why "charama" ("final/ultimate") śloka** - For the Sri-Vaiṣṇava (Viśiṣṭādvaita) tradition this is *the* culminating instruction of the whole Gītā, foundational to the doctrine of **prapatti / śaraṇāgati** (loving self-surrender). It is one of the three core *rahasya* mantras ("*charama-śloka*") expounded by [Rāmānuja](../bhagavad-gita.md) and the ācāryas, alongside the Tirumantra and the Dvaya. (Sri-Vaiṣṇava tradition) [late-text commentary tradition]
- **The Sri-Vaiṣṇava (Rāmānuja) reading** - "Abandoning all dharmas" means giving up reliance on *upāyas* (means/works such as karma- and jñāna-yoga as independent paths to mokṣa), and instead taking the Lord Himself (Nārāyaṇa/Viṣṇu) as the *sole* means and goal. Surrender (*prapatti*) is the act of a real, eternally-distinct soul casting its entire burden on a personal Lord, who then performs the liberating; "abandon" targets the *anxiety/agency* over salvation, not one's ordained duties (which 18.5–9 expressly forbade abandoning). The soul remains real and dependent (*śeṣa*) on the Lord (*śeṣī*). (Rāmānuja, *Gītā-bhāṣya*) [disputed]
  - *Madhva (Dvaita) variant:* likewise dualistic — surrender to Viṣṇu as the wholly independent Supreme; the jīva is eternally distinct and graded. (Madhva commentary) [disputed]
- **The Advaita (Śaṅkara) reading** - "Abandon all dharmas" (and a-dharmas) means transcending the entire dual order of merit/demerit at the summit of realization; the ultimate "refuge in Me" is the *recognition* that the witnessing Self is none other than the non-dual Brahman that one *is*. Surrender here is not to an external Lord but the dissolution of the agentive ego into the Self — the consummation of jñāna-yoga. (Śaṅkara, *Gītā-bhāṣya*) [disputed]
- **Why both endure** - Both readings are textually grounded and have coexisted for well over a millennium: Viśiṣṭādvaita reads the verse as devotional surrender to a distinct, personal Lord; Advaita reads it as non-dual self-recognition. The verse's "mām ekam" ("Me alone") is the contested fulcrum — a personal Lord for Rāmānuja/Madhva, the inmost non-dual Self for Śaṅkara. [scholarly]
- **Guarding the teaching** (18.67–68) - Krishna warns this is not to be told to the undisciplined, the non-devout, or the scoffer; he who teaches this supreme secret to devotees performs the highest devotion and comes to Him.

### Closing frame of the Gītā

- **Arjuna's resolution** - Asked whether his delusion is dispelled, Arjuna answers: "My delusion is destroyed, I have regained memory (*smṛti*) by Your grace; I stand firm, free of doubt — I will do Your word" (*kariṣye vacanaṃ tava*, 18.73). The teaching's purpose — moving Arjuna from despondency (Ch.1) to resolved action — is fulfilled.
- **Sañjaya's epilogue** - The narrator Sañjaya, who has relayed the whole dialogue to blind Dhṛtarāṣṭra by Vyāsa's gift of divine sight, marvels at the "wondrous dialogue," recalls the *Viśvarūpa*, thrills again and again, and pronounces the famous benediction: "Wherever there is Krishna, lord of yoga, and Pārtha the archer, there are fortune, victory, prosperity, and sound morality" (*yatra yogeśvaraḥ kṛṣṇo… tatra śrīr vijayo bhūtir dhruvā nītir matir mama*, 18.78) — the final verse of the Gītā. (Gītā 18.74–78)
