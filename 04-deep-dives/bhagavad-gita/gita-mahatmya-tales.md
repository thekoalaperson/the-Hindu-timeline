# Gītā-Māhātmya tales — the parables of the eighteen chapters

> Cosmic address: a *phala-śruti* appendix to the [Bhagavad Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md), preserved not in the Gītā itself but in the [Padma Purāṇa](../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/padma-purana.md) (Uttara-khaṇḍa). Framed as a timeless Śaiva dialogue — [Śiva](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) narrating to Pārvatī — about a text spoken in the [Dvāpara Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) and recited across the [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md).
>
> Parent: [The Bhagavad Gītā (folder hub)](README.md) · Up: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md)

The **Gītā-Māhātmya** ("Glorification of the Gītā") is an eighteen-chapter *phala-śruti* — a "fruit-of-hearing" text — that supplies one deliverance-parable for each of the Gītā's eighteen *adhyāyas*. Its argument is not doctrinal but soteriological-by-anecdote: reciting, hearing, or even *accidentally* overhearing a given chapter (or a single verse, or half a verse) wipes out heaped-up sin and lifts the wretch — sinful brahmin, courtesan, ghost, goat, swan, elephant — straight to Viṣṇu's abode. It is the source of the eight-verse invocatory "Gītā-Māhātmya" (*gītā-śāstram idaṃ puṇyam…*) printed before the Gītā in most editions, but the eighteen *stories* below are its distinctive core. This file catalogs those eighteen tales.

## The frame and the two clocks

- **The nested narration** — Pārvatī asks Śiva to relate the greatness of the Gītā; Śiva answers by *quoting* an inner dialogue between Viṣṇu and Lakṣmī, in which Viṣṇu declares the Gītā to be his own heart (*gītā me hṛdayaṃ pārtha*) and tells the tales. So the text is doubly framed: Śiva→Pārvatī enclosing Viṣṇu→Lakṣmī. (Padma Purāṇa, Uttara-khaṇḍa, Gītā-Māhātmya, opening)
- **Scriptural clock** — the Gītā the tales praise is spoken at the close of Dvāpara (war traditionally c. 3102 BCE); the tales themselves are set in an unfixed "recitation age," their power meant for the whole Kali Yuga in which hearers live. The Śiva–Pārvatī frame is a **recurring/timeless** Śaiva teaching-scene, not pinned to one yuga.
- **Academic clock** — the Gītā-Māhātmya is a **late Purāṇic appendix**, far later than the Gītā it eulogizes; the Padma Purāṇa's strata are dated broadly c. 4th–15th c. CE, with this *māhātmya* among the later devotional accretions. [scholarly] [late text]
- **Textual placement** — the eighteen chapters are cited as **Padma Purāṇa, Uttara-khaṇḍa 175–192** (one adhyāya per Gītā chapter). Khaṇḍa-and-chapter numbering varies by recension and printed edition, so the tales are best cited at the *māhātmya*-chapter level (Gītā-Māhātmya 1–18) rather than by a fixed verse. [disputed] [regional]
- **Genre note** — this is *phala-śruti* (merit-catalog), the same didactic machinery that frames the [Śiva Purāṇa māhātmya tales](../../90-literature-corpus/shiva-purana-mahatmya-tales.md), the [Kārtika-māhātmya tales](../kartika-mahatmya-tales.md), and the [Brahmottara-khaṇḍa vrata tales](../brahmottara-khanda-vrata-tales.md); read as devotional persuasion, not history. [folk]

## The eighteen deliverance-tales — Adhyāyas 1–9

- **Adhyāya 1 (Arjuna-viṣāda) — Suśarman the sinful brahmin** — Suśarman, a brahmin of low conduct who lived by selling leaves and indulged wine, meat, and vice, dies and after hells is reborn a **bullock** (ox) yoked to an oil-presser. When the exhausted beast collapses, a **courtesan** who happens to be present transfers to it the merit she had unknowingly earned — by overhearing a caged **parrot** recite the Gītā's first chapter. The bullock recovers past-life memory (*jāti-smara*); the courtesan and the parrot (itself a fallen, proud brahmin of a former birth) share merit, and all reach Vaikuṇṭha. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 1)
- **Adhyāya 2 (Sāṅkhya) — Devaśarman and the goatherd Mitravat** — the brahmin **Devaśarman**, hungry for the highest knowledge, is sent to the goatherd **Mitravat** in a forest (Sauvīra/Sowpur country); through a linked series of encounters — a tiger, a she-goat that refuses to flee, a monkey-king, and the sage Sukarman meditating at a Śiva shrine — Devaśarman learns that steady performance of one's own duty ripens spontaneously into wisdom, the lesson of Chapter 2. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 2)
- **Adhyāya 3 (Karma-yoga) — the brahmin Jaḍa** — **Jaḍa**, a brahmin who abandoned his rites for trade and vice, is murdered by robbers on the road and becomes a wandering **preta** (ghost). His dutiful son, performing rites at Kāśī (Vārāṇasī), recites the third chapter; its merit releases Jaḍa and the ancestral line from the ghost-state up to Vaikuṇṭha — a dramatization of the chapter's insistence on doing one's obligatory action. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 3)
- **Adhyāya 4 (Jñāna-yoga) — the two apsaras turned to trees** — two celestial girls sent by Indra to break the penance of the ascetic **Satyatapas** (at Chinnapāpa on the Godāvarī) are cursed to become **badarī trees** until "the coming of Bharata." Ages later the saint **Bharata** of Vārāṇasī, absorbed in study of the fourth chapter, passes by; his mere remembrance of Chapter 4 lifts the curse and grants the two girls heaven. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 4)
- **Adhyāya 5 (Karma-sannyāsa) — Piṅgala and Aruṇā** — the brahmin musician **Piṅgala**, favored by a king, marries the low-born **Aruṇā**; jealous Aruṇā murders him. Both suffer hells and are reborn — he a **vulture**, she a **she-parrot**. Their carcasses fall into a river on whose bank a brahmin is reciting the fifth chapter; hearing it, both are absolved and delivered. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 5)
- **Adhyāya 6 (Dhyāna-yoga) — Raikya and King Jñānaśruti** — King **Jñānaśruti** of Pratiṣṭhāna-pura on the Godāvarī, humbled by a hint that the cart-driving sage **Raikya** outranks him in wisdom, journeys to find him; Raikya, a *guṇātīta* recluse, at first refuses the king's gifts, teaching that true dhyāna transcends possessions. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 6)
  - *Cross-ref:* this reworks the **Raikva–Jānaśruti** narrative of the [Chāndogya Upaniṣad](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md) 4.1–3 (the *saṃvarga-vidyā*), here re-tooled as a Gītā-phala-śruti. [scholarly]
- **Adhyāya 7 (Jñāna-vijñāna) — Śaṅkukarṇa the merchant-serpent** — the rich merchant **Śaṅkukarṇa**, dying of snakebite while chasing a fourth marriage, is reborn a **serpent** guarding his own buried hoard. He appears in his sons' dreams; when they recite the seventh chapter over the treasure, the serpent sheds its form, becomes their father again, hands over the wealth, and ascends to Viṣṇu-loka, the sons then funding yajñas and charity. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 7)
- **Adhyāya 8 (Akṣara-brahma) — Bhāvaśarman and the tree-demons** — the debauched brahmin **Bhāvaśarman**, husband of a courtesan and addicted to meat and wine, is reborn a **palm tree**; the swindling couple **Kuśibala and Kumati** become **brahma-rākṣasas** lodged in the same trees. A learned brahmin's recitation of the eighth chapter liberates all four at once. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 8)
- **Adhyāya 9 (Rāja-vidyā) — the goat that spoke** — at a sacrifice arranged by the brahmin Mādhava at Māhiṣmatī, the tethered **goat** about to be immolated suddenly speaks, revealing it was a brahmin cursed to goathood by the goddess Caṇḍikā. It recounts how King **Candraśarman** of Kurukṣetra had once been shielded — during a black-magic rite in which the demons **Pāpa** ("Sin") and **Nindā** ("Slander") were loosed on him — by a brahmin's recitation of the ninth chapter, which turned the attack aside. The goat-sacrifice is halted; the tale doubles as a caution against blood-offering. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 9)

## The eighteen deliverance-tales — Adhyāyas 10–18

- **Adhyāya 10 (Vibhūti) — the swan and the lotus-woman** — a **swan** crossing a lotus thicket is blackened by sixty-odd bees clinging to it. The thicket had been **Sarojavadanā**, a brahmin wife who neglected her husband for a caged **myna**; cursed into myna, then apsara, then (by Durvāsas' curse) into the lotus-clump, with the bees her fellow-fallen. A muni's recitation of the tenth chapter redeems the lotus, the bees, and the swan together. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 10)
- **Adhyāya 11 (Viśva-rūpa-darśana) — Sumanda and the rākṣasa** — the muni **Sumanda** (var. *Sunandana*), passing through Meghaṅkara on the river Praṇītā, meets a man-eating **rākṣasa** — a farmer cursed for turning away a traveler in distress. Sumanda's recitation of the eleventh chapter frees the demon and its many victims, who rise to Viṣṇu-loka in celestial cars. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 11)
- **Adhyāya 12 (Bhakti) — Siddhasamādhi and the stolen horse** — a young prince praying at the Mahā-Lakṣmī temple of Kolhāpura for means to complete his dead father's Aśvamedha is directed to the ascetic **Siddhasamādhi**, whose ceaseless recitation of the twelfth chapter has won him powers; he recovers the stolen sacrificial horse and restores the dead king to life. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 12)
- **Adhyāya 13 (Kṣetra-kṣetrajña) — Durācārā the fallen wife** — **Durācārā** ("the ill-conducted"), wife of the brahmin Hari-dīkṣita on the Tuṅgabhadrā, dies by a tiger, endures hells, and is reborn a diseased **cāṇḍālī**; at the shrine of Jambhaka-devī a brahmin recites and expounds the thirteenth chapter, granting her purification and Viṣṇu's realm. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 13)
- **Adhyāya 14 (Guṇatraya-vibhāga) — the bitch and the hare** — King **Vikrama-betāla** of Siṃhala-dvīpa hunts **hares** that flee across a boundary into the āśrama of the muni Vatsa, where the fourteenth chapter is recited daily; the hares and the pursuing **hounds** — karmic forms of a murdered wife and her brahmin killers, **Keśava and Vilobhana** — are lifted out of the round of rebirth by the chapter's ambient power. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 14)
- **Adhyāya 15 (Puruṣottama) — King Narasiṃha and the half-verse** — the kindly King **Narasiṃha** of the Gauḍa country, hunting, finds **half a verse** of the fifteenth chapter written on a scrap fallen in a rock-fissure; merely having it read aloud sends a karma-bound **horse** to heaven, and the king who then reads it wins salvation for himself, his ministers, and his son — the chapter recited daily before meals in living practice. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 15)
- **Adhyāya 16 (Daivāsura-sampad) — Khaḍgabāhu's mad elephant** — King **Khaḍgabāhu** of Saurāṣṭra owns the uncontrollable rogue elephant **Amardana**; where mahouts, chains, and goads fail, a humble brahmin quiets the beast with a touch, crediting his constant recitation of the sixteenth chapter, whose divine temperament (*daivī sampad*) tames the demonic. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 16)
- **Adhyāya 17 (Śraddhātraya-vibhāga) — Duḥśāsana reborn the elephant** — Khaḍgabāhu's servant **Duḥśāsana**, thrown and killed by that same elephant, is himself reborn as an elephant, sold to the king of Mālava; when it falls mortally ill, a brahmin's recitation of the seventeenth chapter first heals it and then lets it die serenely into mokṣa. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 17)
- **Adhyāya 18 (Mokṣa-sannyāsa) — a brahmin becomes Indra** — **Indra**, warned that a mortal's relentless recitation of the eighteenth (final) chapter is generating enough merit to unseat him, goes down in brahmin disguise to Kālikā-grāma to witness it; the reciter, offered Indra's very throne, spurns it and chooses instead **Viṣṇu-sāyujya** (absorptive union), proving the eighteenth chapter the crown of the whole. (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya, adhyāya 18)

## Recurring motifs

- **Merit-transfer (*puṇya-dāna*)** — deliverance rarely comes to the sinner directly; a bystander (courtesan, son, muni, king) donates borrowed merit, often earned by *accidental* hearing. The Gītā's grace is figured as contagious and transferable. (tales 1, 3, 5)
- **Accidental / partial efficacy** — a parrot's rote recitation (1), a half-verse in a fissure (15), a chapter merely recited *nearby* (14) all suffice; the text insists the Gītā works below the threshold of intention. [folk]
- **Animal and demon rebirths as the wage of sin** — bullock, serpent, palm-tree, goat, swan, vulture, hare, elephant, brahma-rākṣasa, preta: the fallen are re-embodied down the ladder of beings, then hoisted back by recitation. (tales 1, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 14, 17)
- **Jāti-smara (recovered past-life memory)** — the moment of release is typically marked by the beast remembering its former human birth (1, 7, 9). (Padma Purāṇa, Gītā-Māhātmya)
- **Structural claim** — some editions state that the *māhātmya's* eighteen chapters are back-weighted, the later tales glossing the "saving power" of the Gītā's earlier chapters; the internal ordering-claims are inconsistent across recensions. [disputed]
