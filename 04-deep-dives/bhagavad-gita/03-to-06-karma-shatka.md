# Gītā Ch.3–6 — The Karma Ṣaṭka in Detail (Karma, Jñāna-Karma-Sannyāsa, Karma-Sannyāsa, Dhyāna)

> Where this sits: a deep dive into the **second half of the Karma-ṣaṭka** (sextet 1–6) of the [Bhagavad Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md) — chapters 3 (Karma), 4 (Jñāna-Karma-Sannyāsa), 5 (Karma-Sannyāsa) and 6 (Dhyāna). For the whole-text frame, the 18-chapter map, and the Viśvarūpa, see the [parent Gītā file](../bhagavad-gita.md). Spoken by [Kṛṣṇa](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) to **Arjuna** at Kurukṣetra during the [Dvāpara Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) of our 28th Mahāyuga.
>
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](../) · Section: [bhagavad-gita](./README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md)

These four chapters are the working core of the **Karma-ṣaṭka**. Chapter 2 (Sāṅkhya) planted the seed of *niṣkāma-karma* (action without attachment to fruit, 2.47); chapters 3–6 unfold its full apparatus: *why* action is unavoidable, *how* it becomes sacrifice (*yajña*) and world-sustenance (*loka-saṅgraha*), how knowledge dissolves the bondage of action, how outer renunciation and inner action are reconciled, and finally the practical **meditation manual** that turns the doctrine into discipline. In the Vaiṣṇava *tat-tvam-asi* reading, this whole sextet explores **tvam** — the nature of the embodied self (*jīva*) before chapters 7–12 turn to **tat** (the Lord). The two layers of dating apply throughout: the *in-narrative* setting is the eve of [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) (war traditionally c. 3102 BCE), while text-critical scholarship places composition c. 2nd c. BCE – 2nd c. CE. [scholarly]

## Chapter 3 — Karma Yoga ("The Yoga of Action") · 43 verses

The chapter answers Arjuna's confusion at 3.1–2: if knowledge (*buddhi*) is superior, why push him into the "terrible work" of war? Kṛṣṇa's reply is that action is structurally inescapable and, rightly performed, *is* the path.

- **Arjuna's opening objection** - "If you hold knowledge superior to action, why urge me to this dreadful deed?" — Arjuna mistakes Kṛṣṇa's praise of *buddhi-yoga* (Ch.2) for a recommendation to abstain. (Gītā 3.1–2)
- **The two disciplines (3.3)** - Kṛṣṇa names two ancient paths: **jñāna-yoga** (the *Sāṅkhyas'* path of knowledge) and **karma-yoga** (the *yogins'* path of action) — not rivals but two doors. (Gītā 3.3)
- **No one rests action-free (3.4–5)** - One does not reach *naiṣkarmya* (action-transcendence) merely by abstaining from works; "no one can remain even for a moment without performing action" (*na hi kaścit kṣaṇam api jātu tiṣṭhaty akarmakṛt*, 3.5) — the *guṇas* of *prakṛti* compel activity. (Gītā 3.4–5)
- **The hypocrite condemned (3.6)** - One who restrains the organs of action but dwells mentally on sense-objects is a *mithyācāra* — a self-deceiving hypocrite; outer stillness with inner craving is worthless. (Gītā 3.6–7)
- **"Do your allotted work" (3.8)** - *niyataṃ kuru karma tvaṃ* — perform the prescribed action; even bodily survival is impossible by inaction. (Gītā 3.8)

### Action as yajña (sacrifice) — the cosmic reciprocity

- **Work as sacrifice (3.9)** - All action other than that done **as yajña** (*yajñārthāt karmaṇaḥ*) binds the world; do your work as offering, free of attachment, and you are unbound. The verse universalizes the Vedic ritual of [yajña](../../90-literature-corpus/yajnas-and-vedic-rituals.md) into an inner attitude toward all action. (Gītā 3.9)
- **The wheel of sacrifice (3.10–16)** - Prajāpati created beings together with *yajña*, saying "by this shall you multiply" (3.10); humans nourish the **devas** by sacrifice, the devas send rain, rain yields food, food sustains beings — a closed loop. One who eats without first feeding this cycle "eats sin" (*sa stenaḥ* — is a thief, 3.12); the one who does not turn the wheel "lives in vain" (3.16). (Gītā 3.10–16)
- **The self-content sage exempt (3.17–18)** - The one who delights only in the Self (*ātma-rati*) has no duty to perform and no gain or loss in any being — but this is the *exception*, not Arjuna's case. (Gītā 3.17–18)

### Loka-saṅgraha — acting for the holding-together of the world

- **Act detached, reach the Supreme (3.19)** - "Therefore, ever unattached, perform the work that must be done; performing action without attachment, one attains the Supreme." (Gītā 3.19)
- **Janaka as exemplar (3.20)** - **King Janaka** and others reached perfection *through action itself* (*karmaṇaiva hi saṃsiddhim*) — the *jñānin*-king who ruled while liberated, the Gītā's standing proof that engagement and realization coexist. (Gītā 3.20)
- **The leader sets the standard (3.21)** - *yad yad ācarati śreṣṭhas* — "whatever the great one does, the rest follow; the world models itself on his standard." The exemplary motive for the realized to keep working. (Gītā 3.21)
- **Kṛṣṇa himself as model (3.22–24)** - Kṛṣṇa says he has nothing in the three worlds to gain, yet he remains in action; were he to cease, "these worlds would perish" (*utsīdeyur ime lokāḥ*) and he would be the author of confusion (*saṅkara*) and the destroyer of beings. (Gītā 3.22–24)
- **The defining loka-saṅgraha verse (3.25)** - The ignorant act with attachment to results; the wise should act *equally diligently but unattached*, "desiring the holding-together of the world" (*kuryād vidvāṃs tathāsaktaś cikīrṣur loka-saṅgraham*). The realized must not unsettle the minds of the unwise by abandoning works; let them inspire by example instead (3.26). (Gītā 3.25–26)
  - *On rendering loka-saṅgraha:* Śaṅkara reads it as "preventing people from straying" (preservation of moral order); modern commentators (e.g. Tilak, Gandhi) read it more activistically as "world-welfare / social service." Both senses are textually defensible. [scholarly]

### Agency, svadharma, and the enemy named

- **The guṇas are the real agents (3.27–29)** - Actions are wrought entirely by the *guṇas* of *prakṛti*; one deluded by ego thinks "I am the doer" (*ahaṃkāra-vimūḍhātmā kartāham iti manyate*, 3.27). The knower of the divisions of *guṇa* and *karma* stays unattached, knowing "the guṇas act among the guṇas." (Gītā 3.27–29)
- **Surrender the work to Me (3.30)** - *mayi sarvāṇi karmāṇi saṃnyasya* — renounce all actions into Kṛṣṇa, freed of longing and ego-sense, and fight without feverish grief. The first explicit hinge toward the *bhakti* solution. (Gītā 3.30)
- **Svadharma over paradharma (3.35)** - "Better one's own duty though imperfect than another's duty well performed; better to die in one's own dharma — another's dharma brings danger" (*śreyān svadharmo viguṇaḥ… svadharme nidhanaṃ śreyaḥ paradharmo bhayāvahaḥ*). Echoed almost verbatim at 18.47. (Gītā 3.35)
- **The enemy is desire (3.36–43)** - Arjuna asks what *impels* a person to sin "as if by force." Kṛṣṇa names it: **kāma** (desire) born of *rajas*, and its twin **krodha** (anger) — "the all-devouring, ever-sinful enemy" (*mahāśano mahāpāpmā*, 3.37). Desire veils wisdom as smoke veils fire, dust a mirror, the womb the embryo (3.38). It sits in the senses, mind, and intellect (3.40). The cure: master the senses, slay desire — climbing the ladder *senses > mind > intellect > the Self beyond intellect* (3.42), and "by the Self restrain the self" to kill this hard-to-conquer foe (3.43). (Gītā 3.36–43)

## Chapter 4 — Jñāna-Karma-Sannyāsa Yoga ("Renunciation of Action in Knowledge") · 42 verses

The chapter grounds the teaching in an ancient lineage, gives the Gītā's central **incarnation theology**, and shows how *jñāna* (knowledge) converts action into liberation — culminating in renunciation *through* knowledge rather than abstention.

- **The eternal lineage (4.1–3)** - Kṛṣṇa says he taught this "imperishable yoga" first to **Vivasvān** (the sun-god), who told **Manu**, who told **Ikṣvāku** — the royal-sage succession (*rāja-ṛṣi paramparā*). Lost over long time, he now restores it to Arjuna as "friend and devotee," for it is the "supreme secret." (Gītā 4.1–3)
  - *Chronology note:* placing the first teaching with Vivasvān and Manu sets the doctrine in the dawn of the current [Vaivasvata Manvantara](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md) — an internal-scripture claim of immemorial antiquity, not a text-critical date. [scholarly]
- **Arjuna's chronology puzzle (4.4)** - "Your birth was recent, Vivasvān's ancient — how could you have taught him at the beginning?" The setup for the avatāra doctrine. (Gītā 4.4)
- **The many births of God (4.5–6)** - "Many births of mine have passed, and yours too, Arjuna; I know them all, you do not" (4.5). Though birthless and the Lord of beings, he takes birth by his own *māyā*, governing his own *prakṛti* (*ātma-māyayā… sambhavāmy ātma-māyayā*, 4.6). (Gītā 4.5–6)

### The avatāra verse — yadā yadā hi dharmasya (4.7–8)

- **The core declaration** - The single most-quoted statement of [avatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) theology: God descends in every age, whenever righteousness wanes, to restore *dharma*. The two verses (4.7 then 4.8) form one doctrine. (Gītā 4.7–8)
- **Sanskrit (Devanāgarī):**

  > यदा यदा हि धर्मस्य ग्लानिर्भवति भारत ।
  > अभ्युत्थानमधर्मस्य तदात्मानं सृजाम्यहम् ॥ ४.७ ॥
  > परित्राणाय साधूनां विनाशाय च दुष्कृताम् ।
  > धर्मसंस्थापनार्थाय सम्भवामि युगे युगे ॥ ४.८ ॥

- **IAST transliteration:**

  > *yadā yadā hi dharmasya glānir bhavati bhārata |*
  > *abhyutthānam adharmasya tadātmānaṃ sṛjāmy aham || 4.7 ||*
  > *paritrāṇāya sādhūnāṃ vināśāya ca duṣkṛtām |*
  > *dharma-saṃsthāpanārthāya sambhavāmi yuge yuge || 4.8 ||*

- **Translation** - "Whenever there is a decline of dharma, O Bhārata, and a rise of adharma, then I send forth (manifest) Myself (4.7). For the protection of the good, the destruction of the wicked, and the establishment of dharma, I am born age after age (*yuge yuge*) (4.8)." (Gītā 4.7–8)
- **Doctrinal load** - The verse is the textual charter for the [Daśāvatāra](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) and the recurring-descent model: divine intervention is *periodic and dharma-triggered*, not arbitrary. *sṛjāmy aham* ("I create/emit Myself") implies the avatāra is a self-projection of an unborn Lord, not an ordinary birth. (Gītā 4.7–8)
  - *Variant reading "yuge yuge":* commonly "in every age/cyclically"; some read it distributively ("in each yuga as needed"), harmonizing with the four-[yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md) avatāra-by-yuga schemes elsewhere (e.g. the Bhāgavata's *yuga-avatāras*). [scholarly]
- **Knowing this confers liberation (4.9–11)** - Whoever truly knows the divine nature of his birth and acts is not reborn but comes to Kṛṣṇa (4.9). "As they approach Me, so I reward them; men follow My path in all ways" (*ye yathā māṃ prapadyante…*, 4.11) — a verse of religious inclusivism. (Gītā 4.9–11)

### Action, inaction, and the fire of knowledge

- **The varṇa-by-guṇa note (4.13)** - "The fourfold order (*cātur-varṇya*) was created by Me according to guṇa and karma" — Kṛṣṇa as author of the classes, yet himself their non-doer (*akartāram*). Foreshadows 18.41–44. (Gītā 4.13)
- **The riddle of action-in-inaction (4.16–18)** - "Even the wise are confused about what is action and what is inaction" (4.16). The illumined "sees inaction in action, and action in inaction" (*karmaṇy akarma yaḥ paśyed akarmaṇi ca karma yaḥ*, 4.18) — the detached doer is inwardly actionless though outwardly busy; the inert idler still generates karma. (Gītā 4.16–18)
- **The unattached doer (4.19–23)** - One whose undertakings are free of desire-and-purpose, whose karma is burnt in the fire of knowledge (*jñānāgni-dagdha-karmāṇam*), is called wise (4.19); content, dependent on nothing, he does nothing though fully engaged (4.20–22); for the liberated, "his entire karma dissolves" (4.23). (Gītā 4.19–23)
- **Brahman as the whole sacrifice (4.24)** - *brahmārpaṇaṃ brahma haviḥ* — the act of offering is Brahman, the oblation is Brahman, offered by Brahman into the fire of Brahman; the goal of one absorbed in *brahma-karma* is Brahman itself. (Recited as a meal-blessing in many traditions.) (Gītā 4.24)
- **Many kinds of yajña (4.25–30)** - A catalog of sacrifices — to the gods, of the senses into restraint, of the breath into *prāṇāyāma*, of wealth, austerity, study, knowledge — establishing that *yajña* is a flexible inner spectrum, not only ritual fire. "Knowledge-sacrifice is superior to material sacrifice" (4.33). (Gītā 4.25–33)
- **Approach the teacher (4.34)** - Knowledge is gained by *praṇipāta* (humble approach), *paripraśna* (questioning), and *sevā* (service) to those who have seen the truth (*tattva-darśinaḥ*). (Gītā 4.34)
- **The fire that burns all karma (4.36–38)** - "Even the worst sinner crosses all evil by the boat of knowledge" (4.36); "as kindled fire reduces fuel to ash, the fire of knowledge reduces all karma to ash" (*jñānāgniḥ sarva-karmāṇi bhasmasāt kurute*, 4.37); "nothing in the world is as purifying as knowledge" (4.38). (Gītā 4.36–38)
- **Cut doubt with the sword of knowledge (4.41–42)** - The chapter closes urging Arjuna to slay the doubt in his heart with the sword of knowledge (*jñāna-asinā*) and "arise!" (*uttiṣṭha*) — pivoting from theory back to the battlefield. (Gītā 4.41–42)

## Chapter 5 — Karma-Sannyāsa Yoga ("Renunciation of Action") · 29 verses

Arjuna, hearing both *renunciation* (Ch.4) and *action* (Ch.3) praised, asks Kṛṣṇa to settle which is better. The chapter's work is the **reconciliation**: outer renunciation and selfless action lead to the same goal, but action is the easier and superior route, and the true renunciation is *inner*.

- **Arjuna's "which is better?" (5.1)** - "You praise renunciation of actions (*sannyāsa*) and also yoga (*karma-yoga*) — tell me decisively which is the better." (Gītā 5.1)
- **Kṛṣṇa's verdict (5.2)** - Both lead to the highest good, "but of the two, the **yoga of action** (*karma-yoga*) excels renunciation of action" (*karma-saṃnyāsāt karma-yogo viśiṣyate*). (Gītā 5.2)
- **The true sannyāsin (5.3)** - The real renouncer is one who "neither hates nor desires" (*nirdvandva*) — free of the pairs of opposites; such a one is "easily freed from bondage," whatever his outer mode of life. Renunciation is redefined as an inner state. (Gītā 5.3)
- **Sāṅkhya and Yoga are one (5.4–5)** - Only "children" (*bālāḥ*) say *Sāṅkhya* (knowledge-renunciation) and *Yoga* (action) are different; the one truly established in either gains the fruit of both. "The state reached by the men of knowledge is also reached by the men of action; he who sees that the two are one, sees truly" (5.5). (Gītā 5.4–5)
- **Renunciation is hard without yoga (5.6)** - Mere outer renunciation without *karma-yoga* brings only sorrow; the yogi purified by action "swiftly attains Brahman." (Gītā 5.6)
- **"I do nothing at all" (5.7–9)** - The yogi pure of self, master of the senses, whose self is the Self of all beings, is untainted though acting (5.7). The knower thinks "I do nothing at all" (*naiva kiṃcit karomīti yukto manyeta tattva-vit*) even while seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, eating, walking, sleeping, breathing — knowing the senses merely move among sense-objects (5.8–9). The classic statement of detached agency. (Gītā 5.7–9)
- **The lotus-leaf simile (5.10)** - One who acts offering all to Brahman, abandoning attachment, is untouched by sin "as a lotus leaf is untouched by water" (*padma-patram ivāmbhasā*). The defining image of engaged-yet-unstained action. (Gītā 5.10)
- **Action for self-purification (5.11)** - Yogis act with body, mind, intellect, and senses, free of attachment, *only for self-purification* (*ātma-śuddhaye*). (Gītā 5.11)
- **Peace by surrendering fruit (5.12)** - The yogi who abandons the fruit of action attains lasting peace (*śāntim*); the unsteady, attached to results, is bound. (Gītā 5.12)
- **The city of nine gates (5.13–15)** - Mentally renouncing all actions, the embodied self rests serene in "the city of nine gates" (*nava-dvāre pure*, the body), neither acting nor causing action (5.13). The Lord creates neither agency nor actions nor their fruit-union for beings — "*prakṛti* alone operates" (5.14); the all-pervading Self takes on no one's sin or merit — knowledge is veiled by ignorance, and beings are deluded (5.15). (Gītā 5.13–15)
- **Knowledge as sun (5.16–21)** - Knowledge destroys ignorance "like the sun" revealing the Supreme (5.16); the wise see with equal eye (*sama-darśinaḥ*) a learned brāhmaṇa, a cow, an elephant, a dog, and an outcaste (5.18) — the equality-vision (*brahma-sama*). Those whose minds rest in this evenness "have conquered the world even here" (5.19). Inner joy, inner light, the yogi becomes Brahman and attains *brahma-nirvāṇa* (5.24). (Gītā 5.16–24)
- **Bridge to meditation (5.27–28)** - The chapter ends pre-figuring Ch.6: shutting out external contacts, fixing the gaze between the brows, balancing *prāṇa* and *apāna* in the nostrils, the sage controlling senses, mind, and intellect, with liberation as sole aim, "is ever free." The seated-meditation transition. (Gītā 5.27–29)

## Chapter 6 — Dhyāna / Ātma-Saṃyama Yoga ("The Yoga of Meditation") · 47 verses

The most *practical* chapter of the Karma-ṣaṭka — a hands-on **meditation manual**: who qualifies, where and how to sit, how to steady the runaway mind, and what becomes of the one who tries and fails (*yoga-bhraṣṭa*).

- **The true renouncer acts (6.1)** - "He who does the work he ought to do without depending on its fruit is the *sannyāsin* and the *yogin* — not he who lights no fire and does no work." The Karma-ṣaṭka's thesis restated: renunciation is of fruit, not of action. (Gītā 6.1)
- **Renunciation = yoga (6.2)** - "Know that what they call renunciation (*sannyāsa*) is yoga; no one becomes a yogin who has not renounced selfish purpose (*saṃkalpa*)." (Gītā 6.2)
- **Action then stillness (6.3)** - For the aspirant climbing toward yoga, *action* is the means; for one who has attained, *stillness* (*śama*) is the means — a staged path. (Gītā 6.3)
- **Self the friend, self the enemy (6.5–6)** - "Let one lift oneself by oneself; let one not degrade oneself, for the self alone is the friend of the self and the self alone is its enemy" (*uddhared ātmanātmānaṃ… ātmaiva hy ātmano bandhuḥ*). The self conquered is a friend; unconquered, an enemy. (Gītā 6.5–6)
- **Equanimity of the yogi (6.7–9)** - The self-mastered is serene in cold and heat, pleasure and pain, honor and dishonor (6.7); content in knowledge, even-minded toward a clod, a stone, and gold (6.8); equal toward friend and foe, the righteous and the sinful alike (6.9). (Gītā 6.7–9)

### The meditation manual proper (6.10–15)

- **Seat and setting (6.11)** - In a clean spot, set a firm seat (*sthiram āsanam*), neither too high nor too low, covered with cloth, deerskin, and *kuśa* grass, one upon the other. (Gītā 6.11)
- **Posture and gaze (6.12–13)** - Sitting there, making the mind one-pointed, controlling thought and senses, let one practice yoga for self-purification; hold the body, head, and neck erect and still, gazing at the tip of the nose (*nāsikāgra*) without looking around. (Gītā 6.12–13)
- **Inner discipline (6.14–15)** - Serene, fearless, firm in the vow of celibacy (*brahmacāri-vrate*), the mind controlled and fixed on Kṛṣṇa as the supreme goal — thus ever uniting the self, the yogi attains the peace that culminates in *nirvāṇa* and abides in Him. (Gītā 6.14–15)
- **Moderation in all things (6.16–17)** - Yoga is not for the over-eater or the faster, nor for the over-sleeper or the always-wakeful (6.16); for the moderate in food and recreation, in effort, sleep, and waking, yoga becomes the destroyer of sorrow (*yukta-āhāra-vihārasya… yogo bhavati duḥkha-hā*, 6.17). (Gītā 6.16–17)
- **The steady flame simile (6.18–19)** - When the disciplined mind rests in the Self alone, free of all craving, one is "united"; "as a lamp in a windless place does not flicker" (*yathā dīpo nivāta-stho neṅgate*) is the image of the yogi of controlled mind. (Gītā 6.18–19)
- **The summit — seeing the Self in all (6.20–23)** - Where the mind, stilled, beholds the Self by the Self and is content; where one knows the boundless bliss grasped by intellect beyond the senses; gaining which, no greater gain is conceived, and no sorrow can shake — "let this severance from union-with-pain be known as **yoga**" (*taṃ vidyād duḥkha-saṃyoga-viyogaṃ yoga-saṃjñitam*, 6.23). (Gītā 6.20–23)

### Taming the restless mind, and the fate of the falterer

- **The mind is restless (6.33–34)** - Arjuna protests: this yoga of evenness seems unattainable because "the mind is restless, turbulent, strong, and obstinate (*cañcalaṃ… pramāthi balavad dṛḍham*); to control it seems as hard as to control the wind" (6.34). (Gītā 6.33–34)
- **By practice and dispassion (6.35)** - Kṛṣṇa agrees the mind is hard to curb, "but it can be controlled by **practice** (*abhyāsa*) and **dispassion** (*vairāgya*)" — the same twin remedy as [Yoga Sūtra 1.12](../yoga-sutras-of-patanjali.md). The unrestrained find yoga hard; the self-controlled who strive can attain it (6.36). (Gītā 6.35–36)

#### The yoga-bhraṣṭa — the one who falls from yoga (6.37–45)

- **Arjuna's fear (6.37–39)** - "What of the one of faith who fails to control himself, whose mind wanders from yoga and who does not reach perfection? Does he, fallen from both (worldly success and yoga), perish like a riven cloud (*chinnābhram iva naśyati*), rootless and bewildered? Dispel this doubt, for none but you can." (Gītā 6.37–39)
- **Kṛṣṇa's reassurance (6.40)** - "Neither here nor hereafter is there destruction for him; **no doer of good ever comes to grief** (*na hi kalyāṇa-kṛt kaścid durgatiṃ tāta gacchati*)." The Gītā's great comfort on spiritual effort never being wasted. (Gītā 6.40)
- **Two rebirth tracks for the fallen yogi:**
  - *Track one — the worldly householder rebirth (6.41):* Having fallen from yoga, he attains the worlds of the righteous (*puṇya-kṛtāṃ lokān*), dwells there countless years, then is reborn into the house of the **pure and prosperous** (*śucīnāṃ śrīmatāṃ gehe*). (Gītā 6.41)
  - *Track two — the yogi-family rebirth (6.42):* Or — rarer and more fortunate — he is born into a family of **wise yogis** (*yoginām eva kule… dhīmatām*); such a birth is "very hard to obtain in this world" (*durlabhataraṃ loke janma*). (Gītā 6.42)
- **Recovery of past attainment (6.43–44)** - In the new birth he recovers the *buddhi-saṃyoga* — the mental discipline earned in his former body — and strives onward toward perfection (6.43); "carried on by that very former practice (*pūrvābhyāsena*) even against his will" (*avaśo'pi*), the inquirer is drawn beyond mere ritual scripture (6.44). Spiritual progress is *cumulative across lives*. (Gītā 6.43–44)
- **Perfection across many births (6.45)** - "Striving diligently, the yogi, purified of sin, **perfected through many births** (*aneka-janma-saṃsiddhas*), then reaches the supreme goal" (*paraṃ gatim*). (Gītā 6.45)
- **The yogi is the highest (6.46)** - "The yogi is greater than the ascetic (*tapasvin*), greater than the man of knowledge (*jñānin*), greater than the man of action (*karmin*); therefore, Arjuna, be a yogi." (Gītā 6.46)
- **The closing verse — devotion crowns it (6.47)** - "Of all yogis, he who worships Me with faith, his inner self absorbed in Me (*mad-gatena antar-ātmanā*), is held by Me to be **the most devout (*yukta-tamaḥ*)**." The Karma-ṣaṭka ends by pointing forward to the Bhakti-ṣaṭka — meditation perfected becomes loving union with the personal Lord. (Gītā 6.47)
