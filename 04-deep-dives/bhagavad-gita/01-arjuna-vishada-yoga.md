# Gita Ch.1 — Arjuna-Vishada Yoga (The Yoga of Arjuna's Despair)

> Where this sits: the **first of 18 chapters** (*adhyāyas*) of the [Bhagavad Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md), embedded in the [Bhīṣma Parva](../mahabharata/06-bhishma-parva.md) of the [Mahābhārata](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md). 47 verses. Spoken on the first morning of the Kurukṣetra war, [Dvāpara Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) of our 28th Mahāyuga, war traditionally placed c. 3102 BCE on the eve of [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md).
>
> Parent: [Bhagavad Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md) · Section: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md)

Chapter 1 contains no philosophy and no answer — it poses the **problem the rest of the Gītā exists to solve**. Titled *Arjuna-viṣāda-yoga* ("the yoga of Arjuna's despondency/grief"), it moves from martial pageantry (conches, the two arrayed hosts) through Arjuna's request to survey the foe, into the full collapse of a warrior who suddenly sees the war as kin-slaughter. The chapter ends with Arjuna throwing down his bow Gāṇḍīva and sinking onto the chariot seat. The naming of every chapter as a "yoga," even this one of breakdown, signals the traditional reading that despair itself can become a doorway when surrendered to a teacher.

## Frame & speakers

- **The opening question (1.1)** - The blind king **Dhṛtarāṣṭra** asks his bard **Sañjaya**: "Gathered on the field of dharma (*dharma-kṣetre kuru-kṣetre*), eager to fight, what did my sons and the sons of Pāṇḍu do?" — the Gītā's only verse Dhṛtarāṣṭra speaks, and the word *māmakāḥ* ("my people") vs. *pāṇḍavāḥ* exposes his partisan blindness. (Gītā 1.1)
- **Sañjaya as narrator** - All 46 remaining verses of Ch.1 are Sañjaya's report, granted *divya-cakṣus* (divine sight) by [Vyāsa](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md) to relay the distant battlefield to the blind king. The entire Gītā is thus a vision-within-a-recitation. (Mahābhārata, Bhīṣma Parva)
- **"Dharma-kṣetra"** - Commentators stress the very first word: Kurukṣetra is a *field of dharma*, a tīrtha where right action is amplified — already loading the scene morally before a word of teaching. (Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja on 1.1)

## The Kaurava survey & Duryodhana's speech (1.2-11)

- **Duryodhana approaches Droṇa (1.2-3)** - Seeing the Pāṇḍava army marshalled by **Dhṛṣṭadyumna** (Drupada's son, born to kill Droṇa), Duryodhana goes to his teacher Droṇa and nervously recites the enemy's strength — the speech of a man reassuring himself. (Gītā 1.2-3)
- **Pāṇḍava warrior roll-call (1.4-6)** - Duryodhana names the great bowmen "equal to Bhīma and Arjuna": **Yuyudhāna** (Sātyaki), **Virāṭa**, the *mahā-ratha* **Drupada**, **Dhṛṣṭaketu**, **Cekitāna**, the king of Kāśī, **Purujit**, **Kuntibhoja**, **Śaibya**, **Yudhāmanyu**, **Uttamaujas**, **Abhimanyu** (Saubhadra), and the sons of Draupadī. (Gītā 1.4-6)
- **Kaurava warrior roll-call (1.7-9)** - For his own side he names **Bhīṣma**, **Karṇa**, **Kṛpa**, **Aśvatthāmā**, **Vikarṇa**, and Somadatta's son (Bhūriśravas) — "many heroes ready to give up their lives for me." (Gītā 1.7-9)
- **The two armies measured (1.10)** - Duryodhana's much-debated line: *aparyāptaṃ tad asmākaṃ balaṃ bhīṣmābhirakṣitam / paryāptaṃ tv idam eteṣāṃ balaṃ bhīmābhirakṣitam* — read either "**our** force guarded by Bhīṣma is **unlimited/insufficient**, **theirs** guarded by Bhīma is **limited/sufficient**." (Gītā 1.10)
  - *Variant (interpretive split):* *aparyāpta* can mean "immeasurable" (so: our army is vast, theirs is small) **or** "inadequate" (so: our army is insufficient, theirs is adequate). Śaṅkara and many take the first; some read Duryodhana as unconsciously voicing his own doom. [disputed]
- **Guard Bhīṣma (1.11)** - Duryodhana orders all divisions to protect Bhīṣma at every front — making the grandsire the linchpin of the Kaurava battle plan. (Gītā 1.11)

## The conch-roll-call (1.12-19)

- **Bhīṣma's lion-roar (1.12)** - To cheer Duryodhana, the aged Bhīṣma roars like a lion and blows his conch — the first sound, from the Kaurava side. (Gītā 1.12)
- **The Kaurava din (1.13)** - Conches, kettledrums, tabors, trumpets and horns burst out together in a tumultuous roar (*sa śabdas tumulo'bhavat*). (Gītā 1.13)
- **The divine chariot (1.14)** - From the white-horsed great chariot, **Mādhava (Kṛṣṇa)** and **Pāṇḍava (Arjuna)** blow their celestial conches — the Pāṇḍava answer. (Gītā 1.14)
- **The five named conches (1.15-16)** - Sañjaya names each by owner: (Gītā 1.15-18)
  - **Pāñcajanya** — Kṛṣṇa (Hṛṣīkeśa). Viṣṇu's conch, made from the bones of the demon **Pañcajana** whom Kṛṣṇa slew; symbolically the primeval sound, the five elements. (Gītā 1.15)
  - **Devadatta** ("God-given") — Arjuna (Dhanañjaya). (Gītā 1.15)
  - **Pauṇḍra** — Bhīma (Vṛkodara, "wolf-belly"), "the great conch" (*mahā-śaṅkha*). (Gītā 1.15)
  - **Anantavijaya** ("endless victory") — Yudhiṣṭhira, son of Kuntī, the king. (Gītā 1.16)
  - **Sughoṣa** ("sweet-sounding") — Nakula. (Gītā 1.16)
  - **Maṇipuṣpaka** ("jewel-bracelet/flower") — Sahadeva. (Gītā 1.16)
- **The allied conches (1.17-18)** - **Kāśya** (king of Kāśī, supreme archer), **Śikhaṇḍī** (*mahā-ratha*), **Dhṛṣṭadyumna**, **Virāṭa**, the unconquered **Sātyaki**, **Drupada**, the sons of Draupadī, and **Abhimanyu** (Saubhadra) each blow their conches — naming the inner ring of Pāṇḍava champions. (Gītā 1.17-18)
- **The earth-rending sound (1.19)** - The Pāṇḍava blast tears through earth and sky, shattering the hearts (*hṛdayāni vyadārayat*) of Dhṛtarāṣṭra's sons — the psychological turning of the tide before a single arrow. (Gītā 1.19)

## The survey between the armies (1.20-25)

- **Arjuna lifts his bow (1.20)** - Seeing the Kauravas arrayed, the ape-bannered (*kapi-dhvaja*, Hanumān on his flag) Arjuna takes up his bow as missiles are about to fly, and addresses Hṛṣīkeśa. (Gītā 1.20)
- **"Drive between the two armies" (1.21-22)** - Arjuna's request: *senayor ubhayor madhye rathaṃ sthāpaya me'cyuta* — "Place my chariot, O Acyuta, in the middle of the two armies, that I may behold those standing eager to fight, with whom I must war in this enterprise." (Gītā 1.21-22)
- **The reason (1.23)** - He wishes to survey those assembled to do battle, pleasing the "evil-minded son of Dhṛtarāṣṭra" (Duryodhana). (Gītā 1.23)
- **Kṛṣṇa halts the chariot (1.24-25)** - Hṛṣīkeśa drives the splendid chariot into the gap **before Bhīṣma, Droṇa and all the kings** and says: *paśyaitān samavetān kurūn iti* — "**Behold these Kurus gathered together**." The teacher of the universe positions Arjuna so that he sees not "the enemy" but **his own family**. (Gītā 1.24-25)

## Arjuna sees his kin & the collapse (1.26-30)

- **The fatal recognition (1.26-27)** - Standing between the armies, Arjuna sees **fathers, grandfathers, teachers, uncles, brothers, sons, grandsons, comrades, fathers-in-law and friends** in **both** hosts. Seeing all these *bandhūn* (kinsmen) arrayed, he is overcome with deep pity (*kṛpayā parayāviṣṭaḥ*) and despairs. (Gītā 1.26-28)
- **The first words of despair (1.28-29)** - *dṛṣṭvemaṃ sva-janaṃ kṛṣṇa yuyutsuṃ samupasthitam* — "Seeing my own people, O Kṛṣṇa, present and eager to fight, my limbs give way (*sīdanti mama gātrāṇi*), my mouth dries up, my body trembles (*vepathuś ca śarīre me*), my hair stands on end (*roma-harṣaś ca jāyate*)." (Gītā 1.28-29)
- **Gāṇḍīva slips; the body fails (1.30)** - *gāṇḍīvaṃ sraṃsate hastāt tvak caiva paridahyate* — "**Gāṇḍīva slips from my hand**, my skin burns all over; I cannot stand steady, my mind reels (*bhramatīva ca me manaḥ*)." The four classic symptoms — trembling, sweating, horripilation, the dropping bow — are a clinical portrait of acute crisis. (Gītā 1.30)
- **The bow Gāṇḍīva** - Arjuna's divine bow, given by Varuṇa via Agni at the Khāṇḍava burning; its slipping is the chapter's central image — the warrior's identity literally falling from his grip. (cross-ref [Mahābhārata Ādi Parva](../mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md))

## The catalog of Arjuna's arguments against war (1.31-46)

Arjuna's refusal is not cowardice but a **moral case**, argued on five interlocking grounds. Tradition reads it as *dharma-saṅkaṭa* (a collision of duties) — kṣatriya-dharma to fight vs. kula-dharma not to destroy family.

- **1. Ill omens & no good from victory (1.31-32)** - He sees adverse omens (*nimittāni viparītāni*) and "no good in slaying my own people in battle"; he desires neither victory, nor kingdom, nor pleasures. (Gītā 1.31-32)
- **2. Kingdom is worthless without those for whom it is sought (1.32-34)** - Those for whose sake one wants kingdom and pleasures — teachers, fathers, sons, grandfathers, uncles, in-laws — are the very ones "standing here risking life and wealth." What joy in a realm bought with their blood? (Gītā 1.33-34)
- **3. Refusal to kill even if killed (1.35)** - *etān na hantum icchāmi ghnato'pi madhusūdana* — "I do not wish to kill them even if I am killed, not even for the kingship of the three worlds, much less for the earth." (Gītā 1.35)
- **4. Sin attaches to the slayer of aggressors (1.36-37)** - Even though the Kauravas are *ātatāyinaḥ* (aggressors whom śāstra permits killing), Arjuna argues that killing one's own kin (*sva-bāndhavān*) brings only sin (*pāpam evāśrayed asmān*); how could the Pāṇḍavas be happy after slaying their own people? (Gītā 1.36-37)
- **5. The kula-kṣaya (clan-destruction) argument (1.38-44)** - Arjuna's longest and most technical case, the doctrinal heart of the chapter: (Gītā 1.38-44)
  - **They are blinded, we are not (1.38-39)** - Though the Kauravas, blinded by greed, see no fault in destroying family (*kula-kṣaya-kṛtaṃ doṣam*), "why should we not turn back from this sin, we who **do** see the evil in destroying a clan?" (Gītā 1.38-39)
  - **Kula-kṣaya destroys eternal dharmas (1.40)** - *kula-kṣaye praṇaśyanti kula-dharmāḥ sanātanāḥ* — "In the ruin of a family, its ancient dharmas (*kula-dharmāḥ*) perish; when dharma perishes, adharma overwhelms the **whole** family (*kṛtsnam adharmo'bhibhavaty uta*)." (Gītā 1.40)
  - **Adharma corrupts the women → varṇa-saṅkara (1.41)** - *adharmābhibhavāt kṛṣṇa praduṣyanti kula-striyaḥ* — "When adharma prevails, the women of the family are corrupted; from the corruption of women, O Vārṣṇeya, arises **varṇa-saṅkara** (intermixture / confusion of social order)." (Gītā 1.41)
  - **Varṇa-saṅkara drags clan-destroyers & ancestors to hell (1.42)** - *saṅkaro narakāyaiva kula-ghnānāṃ kulasya ca* — "Confusion of order leads to hell for the destroyers of the family and for the family itself; and **their ancestors fall (*patanti pitaraḥ*), deprived of the offerings of *piṇḍa* (rice-balls) and *udaka* (water)**." The war severs the **śrāddha** rites by which the dead are sustained. (Gītā 1.42)
  - **Caste-dharmas and family-dharmas are wiped out (1.43)** - By these very crimes of the clan-destroyers, which produce *varṇa-saṅkara*, the **eternal caste-dharmas and family-dharmas (*jāti-dharmāḥ kula-dharmāś ca*) are abolished**. (Gītā 1.43)
  - **Hell for those whose family-dharma is destroyed (1.44)** - *utsanna-kula-dharmāṇāṃ manuṣyāṇāṃ janārdana / narake niyataṃ vāso bhavatīty anuśuśruma* — "We have heard (*anuśuśruma* — from tradition) that for people whose family-dharma is destroyed, dwelling in **hell is assured**." Arjuna invokes received tradition, not personal opinion. (Gītā 1.44)
- **The "great sin" lament (1.45)** - *aho bata mahat pāpaṃ kartuṃ vyavasitā vayam* — "Alas! We are resolved to commit a great sin, ready to kill our own kin out of greed for kingdom and pleasures." (Gītā 1.45)
- **Better to be killed unresisting (1.46)** - *yadi mām apratīkāram aśastraṃ śastra-pāṇayaḥ / dhārtarāṣṭrā raṇe hanyus tan me kṣemataraṃ bhavet* — "If the armed sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra were to slay me, **unarmed and unresisting**, in battle, that would be **better (*kṣemataram*) for me**." The total inversion of the warrior ethos. (Gītā 1.46)

## The collapse — dropping of Gāṇḍīva (1.47)

- **The chapter's closing image (1.47)** - *evam uktvārjunaḥ saṅkhye rathopastha upāviśat / visṛjya sa-śaraṃ cāpaṃ śoka-saṃvigna-mānasaḥ* — "Having spoken thus on the battlefield, Arjuna, **casting aside his bow and arrows**, sank down onto the seat of the chariot, his mind overwhelmed with grief (*śoka-saṃvigna-mānasaḥ*)." (Gītā 1.47)
- **The traditional colophon** - Each chapter closes with a colophon naming it; for Ch.1: *...śrīmad-bhagavad-gītāsu ... arjuna-viṣāda-yogo nāma prathamo'dhyāyaḥ* — "the first chapter, named the Yoga of Arjuna's Despair." Note even despair is dignified as a *yoga*. (manuscript colophon)
- **Why this is the hinge** - The thrown-down bow physically dramatizes *karma-sannyāsa* done for the **wrong** reason — abdication from attachment and grief, not from knowledge. The whole Gītā will teach the right relation to action that 1.47 gets backwards. (Śaṅkara, intro to Ch.2)

## Why Chapter 1 is "Sāṅkhya in the negative"

- **Arjuna reasons like a philosopher — to the wrong end** - His argument in 1.38-44 is a genuine *ethical syllogism* (clan-destruction → loss of dharma → corruption of women → varṇa-saṅkara → fall of ancestors → hell), but it is built on **attachment, grief and fear of consequence**, the very *rāga-dveṣa* the Gītā will dissolve. He performs analysis (the literal meaning of *sāṅkhya*, "enumeration/reasoning") in service of paralysis. [scholarly]
- **The mirror-image of Ch.2** - In Ch.2 (*Sāṅkhya Yoga*) Kṛṣṇa uses the **same analytical mode** — distinguishing the imperishable *ātman* from the perishable body (2.11-30) — to reach the **opposite** conclusion: that grief over death is itself the error. Ch.1 is the same intellect running backwards; Kṛṣṇa's first move (2.11, *aśocyān anvaśocas tvam* — "you grieve for those who should not be grieved for") is a direct rebuke of Ch.1's premise. [scholarly]
- **"Sāṅkhya in the negative"** - Commentators and modern readers describe Ch.1 this way: it contains the **raw materials of Sāṅkhya discrimination** (kṣatriya duty, consequence, dharma, the self facing death) arranged to produce *moha* (delusion) instead of clarity. The chapter is the disease for which Sāṅkhya (Ch.2) is the diagnosis and the rest of the Gītā the cure. [scholarly]
- **Wrong renunciation vs. right renunciation** - Arjuna proposes *sannyāsa* of action out of *viṣāda*; the Gītā (esp. Ch.3, 5, 18) insists true renunciation is of the **fruits of action**, not of action itself — making 1.47 the textbook case of "tāmasic/rājasic relinquishment" later defined in 18.7-8. (Gītā 18.7-9 read back onto 1.47)

## Interpretive variants on Chapter 1

- *Variant (Gandhi — allegorical):* In *Anāsaktiyoga* Gandhi reads the battlefield as the **inner war** in every heart and Arjuna's despair as the soul's recoil before moral struggle — not a literal endorsement of war. [modern] (cross-ref [Bhagavad Gītā § commentaries](../bhagavad-gita.md))
- *Variant (Tilak — activist):* In *Gītā-Rahasya* Arjuna's collapse is **false renunciation/inertia** to be overcome by *karma-yoga*; Kurukṣetra is a real *dharma-kṣetra* and refusal of just battle is itself adharma. [modern]
- *Variant (Śaṅkara — Advaita):* Arjuna's grief and delusion (*śoka-moha*) are the very *saṃsāra*-bondage rooted in false identification of the self with the body; the Gītā's teaching destroys this *avidyā*. (Śaṅkara, *Gītā-bhāṣya*, Ch.1 intro)
- *Variant (Rāmānuja — Viśiṣṭādvaita):* Arjuna's confusion is genuine *dharma-saṅkaṭa*; the resolution is surrender to the Lord, foreshadowing 18.66. (Rāmānuja, *Gītā-bhāṣya*)
- *Variant (ISKCON / Bhaktivedanta):* titles the chapter "Observing the Armies on the Battlefield of Kurukṣetra"; reads Arjuna's compassion as well-meaning but materially-conditioned (*kṛpaṇa*), to be corrected by transcendental knowledge. [modern]
