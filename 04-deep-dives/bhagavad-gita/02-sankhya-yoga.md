# Gita Ch.2 — Sāṅkhya Yoga (The Yoga of Knowledge)

> Where this sits: the second *adhyāya* (chapter) of the [Bhagavad Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md), spoken by [Kṛṣṇa](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) at Kurukṣetra in the [Dvāpara Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md). 72 verses — the longest doctrinal block before Ch. 18, and the chapter Gandhi called the Gītā in miniature.
>
> Parent: [bhagavad-gita.md](../bhagavad-gita.md) · Section: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md)

Chapter 2 is the **seed-chapter** (*bīja-adhyāya*) of the whole Gītā: nearly every later doctrine is first planted here. It opens with Arjuna's despair carried over from [Ch. 1](../bhagavad-gita.md) and Kṛṣṇa's rebuke, then unfolds in two great movements — **Sāṅkhya** (the metaphysics of the deathless self, 2.11–38) and **Yoga / Buddhi-yoga** (the discipline of desireless action, 2.39–72). The chapter-title *Sāṅkhya Yoga* refers not to Kapila's classical Sāṅkhya darśana but to "yoga via discriminative knowledge (*sāṅkhya* = reasoned analysis) of the eternal vs. the transient." It is the doctrinal hinge from which Karma-yoga (Ch. 3), Bhakti, and Jñāna all branch.

## Frame: the turn from despair to teaching (2.1–10)

- **Kṛṣṇa's rebuke** — Seeing Arjuna overcome with pity, eyes brimming, Kṛṣṇa asks whence this "un-Aryan" faintheartedness (*kaśmalam*) has come; it is unworthy, ignoble (*anārya-juṣṭam*), and bars heaven. "Yield not to impotence (*klaibyam*), Pārtha — it does not befit you; cast off this petty weakness of heart and arise." (Gītā 2.2–3)
- **Arjuna's counter-argument** — Arjuna protests that he cannot fight Bhīṣma and Droṇa, men worthy of worship; better to live by begging than slay these gurus; he does not even know which outcome is better, victory or defeat. (Gītā 2.4–6)
- **Formal surrender as disciple** — "My very being is stricken with the taint of faint-heartedness (*kārpaṇya-doṣa*); confused about dharma I ask you — tell me decisively what is best. **I am your disciple (*śiṣyas te'ham*); teach me, who have taken refuge in you.**" (Gītā 2.7) — the verse that converts a battlefield refusal into a teaching occasion and licenses everything that follows.
- **"I will not fight" (2.9)** — Arjuna declares *na yotsya* ("I shall not fight") and falls silent. Kṛṣṇa, "as if smiling" (*prahasann iva*, 2.10), begins the teaching — the smile marking the shift from grief-counseling to the gravest metaphysics.

## Movement I — Sāṅkhya: the imperishable ātman (2.11–30)

The teaching proper begins at **2.11**, traditionally counted by commentators as the Gītā's true opening verse (*aśocyān anvaśocas tvam* — "you grieve for those not to be grieved for, yet speak words of wisdom; the wise grieve neither for the living nor the dead").

- **2.12 — the eternity of all selves** — "**Never was there a time when I did not exist, nor you, nor these kings; nor shall any of us ever cease to be**" (*na tv evāhaṃ jātu nāsaṃ na tvaṃ neme janādhipāḥ…*). The keystone verse for the self's beginninglessness and endlessness; its plural ("I, you, all these kings") becomes the central battleground of the Vedānta schools (see [§ acharya readings](#how-the-ācāryas-read-212-and-247) below).
- **2.13 — the embodied self through aging** — As the *dehin* (embodied one) passes through childhood, youth, and age in one body, so it passes to another body at death; the steady are not deluded by this. The first statement of reincarnation in the Gītā.
- **2.14 — the dualities (dvandvas)** — Contacts of the senses with objects produce cold and heat, pleasure and pain; they come and go, impermanent (*anityāḥ*) — "endure them, Bhārata" (*titikṣasva*). Endurance (*titikṣā*) introduced as practice.
- **2.16 — the real and the unreal** — "**Of the unreal (*asat*) there is no being; of the real (*sat*) there is no non-being**" (*nāsato vidyate bhāvo nābhāvo vidyate sataḥ*) — the seers of truth have seen the conclusion (*anta*) of both. A foundational *sat-asat* discrimination (*viveka*) verse cited across Vedānta.
- **2.17 — the indestructible pervader** — "Know That to be indestructible (*avināśi*) by which all this is pervaded; none can cause the destruction of the imperishable."
- **2.18–19 — bodies perish, the dweller does not** — The bodies are said to end, but the embodied self is eternal, indestructible, immeasurable — "therefore fight" (2.18). He who thinks the self slays or is slain understands nothing: it neither slays nor is slain (2.19) — echoing **Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.19** almost verbatim (cf. [Naciketā / Kaṭha Upaniṣad](../nachiketa-katha-upanishad.md)).
- **2.20 — na jāyate mriyate vā** — "**It is never born, nor does it ever die; … unborn, eternal, everlasting, primeval — it is not slain when the body is slain**" (*na jāyate mriyate vā kadācin…ajo nityaḥ śāśvato'yaṃ purāṇo, na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre*). The single most-quoted verse on the deathless ātman; near-identical to **Kaṭha 1.2.18**.
- **2.22 — the garment simile** — "**As a man casts off worn-out garments and takes new ones, so the embodied self casts off worn-out bodies and enters others new**" (*vāsāṃsi jīrṇāni yathā vihāya…*). The classic image of reincarnation as change of clothing.
- **2.23–24 — the four elements cannot harm it** — "**Weapons do not cleave it, fire does not burn it, water does not wet it, wind does not wither it**" (*nainaṃ chindanti śastrāṇi, nainaṃ dahati pāvakaḥ…*). The self is uncleavable, unburnable, unwettable, undryable — eternal, all-pervading, stable, immovable.
- **2.25 — the unmanifest** — It is called unmanifest (*avyakta*), unthinkable (*acintya*), unchanging (*avikārya*); knowing it thus, you should not grieve.
- **2.27 — the certainty of death and rebirth** — "**For the born, death is certain; for the dead, birth is certain** (*jātasya hi dhruvo mṛtyur dhruvaṃ janma mṛtasya ca*); therefore grieve not over the inevitable." A consolation argument that even grants the materialist premise.
- **2.28 — beings unmanifest at both ends** — Beings are unmanifest in origin, manifest in the middle, unmanifest again at death — so why lament?
- **2.29 — the wonder of the self** — One sees it as a wonder, another speaks of it as a wonder, another hears of it as a wonder, yet even after hearing none knows it — its incomprehensibility (echoing **Kaṭha 1.2.7**).
- **2.30 — closing the Sāṅkhya argument** — The dweller in every body is ever-unslayable; "therefore you should not grieve for any being" — the metaphysical case for fighting is complete.

## Movement I (cont.) — the kṣatriya's svadharma (2.31–38)

Having argued from the eternal self, Kṛṣṇa now argues from **relative duty** — the same act justified on a second, "lower" level (the two-clock structure of the Gītā's ethics: absolute and social).

- **2.31 — svadharma of the warrior** — "Looking to your own duty (*svadharma*) you should not waver; for a kṣatriya there is no greater good than **a righteous war** (*dharmyād dhi yuddhāc chreyo'nyat kṣatriyasya na vidyate*)." The first appearance of *svadharma* in the Gītā — caste-specific duty (cf. its great restatement at 3.35 and 18.47, *śreyān svadharmo viguṇaḥ*).
- **2.32 — the open gate of heaven** — Happy the kṣatriyas (*sukhinaḥ kṣatriyāḥ*) who get such a war unsought, "an open door to heaven" (*svarga-dvāram apāvṛtam*).
- **2.33–34 — the cost of refusal** — If Arjuna refuses this righteous fight, he forfeits his own dharma and honor and incurs sin; people will recount his **everlasting disgrace** (*akīrti*), and for the honored, dishonor is worse than death.
- **2.35–36 — contempt of the warriors** — The great chariot-warriors will think he withdrew from fear; his enemies will mock his prowess with unspeakable words — "what is more painful than that?"
- **2.37 — the win-win of the kṣatriya** — "**Slain, you gain heaven; victorious, you enjoy the earth** (*hato vā prāpsyasi svargaṃ jitvā vā bhokṣyase mahīm*); therefore arise, Kaunteya, resolved to fight." A martial-ethical argument framed for the warrior audience.
- **2.38 — the bridge to Yoga** — "**Treating alike pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat** (*sukha-duḥkhe same kṛtvā lābhālābhau jayājayau*), engage in battle — thus you shall incur no sin." This verse pivots: fighting *with equanimity* removes karmic bondage. The word *same* ("equal") foreshadows *samatva* (2.48). With this, Sāṅkhya ends and Yoga begins.

## Movement II — Buddhi-yoga: desireless action (2.39–53)

- **2.39 — the announced transition** — "This wisdom (*buddhi*) has been declared to you in **Sāṅkhya** (analysis); now hear it in **Yoga** (application), by which, yoked, you will cast off the bondage of karma." This verse names the chapter's two halves explicitly and christens the path *buddhi-yoga* (yoga of discriminating intellect).
- **2.40 — no loss on this path** — "On this path no effort is lost, no obstacle prevails; even a little of this dharma protects from great fear" (*svalpam apy asya dharmasya trāyate mahato bhayāt*) — karma-yoga as risk-free spiritual capital.
- **2.41 — single-pointed resolve** — Here the resolute intellect (*vyavasāyātmikā buddhi*) is one-pointed; the irresolute have endlessly branching, manifold intellects — focus vs. dissipation.
- **2.42–44 — critique of ritualism (the flowery Veda)** — The unwise delight in the "flowery speech" (*puṣpitāṃ vācam*) of the karma-kāṇḍa Vedas, promising heaven and enjoyment as reward for rites; their minds, robbed of discrimination by craving pleasure and power, never attain the resolute *samādhi-buddhi*. A pointed relativizing of Vedic ritual in favor of inner discipline.
- **2.45 — beyond the three guṇas** — "The Vedas deal with the three guṇas; **be free of the three guṇas** (*nistraiguṇyo bhava*), Arjuna — free of dualities, ever fixed in *sattva*, beyond acquisition and preservation, self-possessed." First mention of the [three guṇas](../bhagavad-gita.md), elaborated in Ch. 14.
- **2.46 — the well and the flood** — "As much use as a well when water floods everywhere, so much use are all the Vedas to the illumined Brahmin" — knowledge of the Absolute subsumes the partial good of ritual.
- **2.47 — niṣkāma-karma (the cardinal verse)** — "**karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana / mā karma-phala-hetur bhūr mā te saṅgo'stv akarmaṇi**" — "**You have a right to action alone, never to its fruits; let not the fruits of action be your motive, nor let your attachment be to inaction.**" The single most famous verse of the Gītā and the charter of **niṣkāma-karma** (desireless action): one controls the *doing*, never the *outcome*; renounce neither action (inaction is wrong) nor fruit-anxiety (craving results is wrong) — only the *attachment* to fruit. See [acharya readings](#how-the-ācāryas-read-212-and-247).
- **2.48 — yoga as samatva (equanimity)** — "**yoga-sthaḥ kuru karmāṇi… siddhy-asiddhyoḥ samo bhūtvā samatvaṃ yoga ucyate**" — "Established in yoga, perform actions, abandoning attachment, even-minded in success and failure; **equanimity (samatva) is called yoga.**" The Gītā's first formal *definition* of yoga — not posture or trance but evenness of mind toward results.
- **2.49 — action is far inferior to buddhi-yoga** — Mere fruitive action (*sakāma karma*) is far below action done with the yoke of wisdom (*buddhi-yoga*); take refuge in the intellect — wretched are those who act for fruits (*phala-hetavaḥ*).
- **2.50 — yoga as kauśala (skill in action)** — "**buddhi-yukto jahātīha ubhe sukṛta-duṣkṛte… yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam**" — "One yoked in wisdom casts off here both good and evil deeds; therefore **strive for yoga — yoga is skill in action.**" A second definition: skill (*kauśala*) is acting from the right state of consciousness, so that action ceases to bind. (Read by many as: the "skill" *is* doing action without attachment so it produces no karmic residue.)
- **2.51 — the fruit of buddhi-yoga** — The wise, yoked in intellect, renounce the fruit born of action, are freed from the bondage of birth, and go to the realm beyond all ill (*anāmayaṃ padam*).
- **2.52–53 — the crossing and the goal** — When the intellect crosses beyond the "thicket of delusion" (*moha-kalilam*), one becomes indifferent to what has been and is yet to be heard (scripture as means, not end); when the intellect, bewildered by Vedic chatter, stands unmoving and steady in *samādhi*, then yoga is attained. This closes the doctrinal exposition and triggers Arjuna's next question.

## Movement II (cont.) — the sthitaprajña portrait (2.54–72)

Arjuna's question at **2.54** — "What is the description of the man of steady wisdom (*sthitaprajña*), established in *samādhi*? How does he speak, sit, walk?" — prompts the Gītā's first and most influential **character-portrait of the liberated being**, a passage Hindus across sects memorize and recite (often chanted before meals).

- **2.55 — the definition** — He abandons all desires of the mind and is content in the Self by the Self alone (*ātmany evātmanā tuṣṭaḥ*) — *then* he is called *sthitaprajña*. Satisfaction sourced internally, not externally.
- **2.56 — unshaken in the dualities** — Unshaken in sorrow, free of craving in pleasure, gone are attachment, fear, and anger (*vīta-rāga-bhaya-krodhaḥ*) — he is the sage of steady mind (*sthita-dhī muni*).
- **2.57 — neither rejoicing nor recoiling** — He who is without affection on any side, neither delighting in good fortune nor recoiling from bad, his wisdom is firmly set.
- **2.58 — the tortoise simile** — "**As a tortoise draws in its limbs (*kūrmo'ṅgāni*) from all sides, so when he withdraws his senses from sense-objects, his wisdom is firm.**" The signature image of voluntary, at-will sense-control (*pratyāhāra*) — withdrawal by mastery, not suppression.
- **2.59 — taste alone remains, until vision** — The objects fall away for the abstinent, but the *taste* (*rasa*) for them lingers; even the taste ceases for one who has seen the Supreme (*paraṃ dṛṣṭvā*). Realization, not mere abstention, uproots desire.
- **2.60 — even the wise are endangered** — The turbulent senses forcibly carry away the mind even of a striving man of discernment — a candid warning that the senses are perennially dangerous.
- **2.61 — the remedy: fixed on the Lord** — Restraining all senses, let him sit yoked, intent on Me (*mat-paraḥ*) — for one whose senses are mastered, wisdom is firm. (The first hint of devotional theism — *mat-paraḥ* — within the chapter.)
- **2.62–63 — the ladder of fall (the most-quoted causal chain)** — "**Brooding on sense-objects (*dhyāyato viṣayān*), attachment to them is born; from attachment desire (*kāma*); from desire anger (*krodha*); from anger delusion (*sammoha*); from delusion loss of memory (*smṛti-bhraṃśa*); from loss of memory destruction of the intellect (*buddhi-nāśa*); and with the intellect destroyed, he perishes (*praṇaśyati*).**" The Gītā's celebrated 7-step psychology of self-ruin — a cascade from idle dwelling-on to total collapse.
- **2.64–65 — the ascent: grace through self-mastery (prasāda)** — But one who moves among sense-objects with senses controlled, free of attraction and aversion (*rāga-dveṣa-viyuktaiḥ*), attains serenity (*prasāda*); and in that serenity all his sorrows are destroyed, for the intellect of the serene-minded (*prasanna-cetas*) soon becomes steady. The mirror-image ascent answering the 2.62–63 descent.
- **2.66 — no peace without yoga** — For the unyoked there is no wisdom, no meditation (*bhāvanā*); without meditation no peace; and for the peaceless, whence happiness? A tight chain of negations.
- **2.67 — the boat-on-water simile** — "**As the wind sweeps away a boat on the water, so even one wandering sense, to which the mind yields, carries away his wisdom.**" A single uncontrolled sense suffices to wreck the intellect — hence total sense-mastery is required.
- **2.69 — the inverted night and day** — "**What is night for all beings, therein the disciplined sage is awake; what is the waking-state of all beings, that is night for the seeing sage.**" (*yā niśā sarva-bhūtānāṃ tasyāṃ jāgarti saṃyamī…*) The famous inversion: the sage is "awake" to the eternal Self that the worldly sleep through, and "asleep" to the sense-world the worldly call life.
- **2.70 — the ocean-and-rivers simile** — "**As the waters enter the ocean, which, ever-filled, remains unmoved, so he into whom all desires enter attains peace — not the desirer of desires.**" (*āpūryamāṇam acala-pratiṣṭhaṃ samudram āpaḥ praviśanti…*) Peace comes from absorbing desires without being moved, not from satisfying them.
- **2.71 — the formula of peace** — Abandoning all desires, acting free of longing, without "mine" (*nirmama*) and without ego (*nirahaṅkāra*), he attains peace (*śāntim adhigacchati*).
- **2.72 — brāhmī sthiti / brahma-nirvāṇa (the closing crown)** — "**This is the brāhmī state (*brāhmī sthiti*), O Pārtha; attaining it none is deluded; established in it even at the last hour, one attains brahma-nirvāṇa.**" The chapter ends on the goal: the state of being established in Brahman, and *brahma-nirvāṇa* (extinction-in-Brahman / liberation), reachable even at the moment of death. The first use of *nirvāṇa* in the Gītā — a term it shares with, and re-reads against, Buddhism.

## How the ācāryas read 2.12 and 2.47

The Gītā is one leg of the *prasthāna-trayī*; each Vedānta founder wrote a *bhāṣya* on it, and 2.12 (plurality of selves) and 2.47 (action vs. knowledge) are the chapter's two great fault-lines. See [Acharyas & Sampradayas](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/README.md).

### On 2.12 — "never was there a time when I, you, these kings did not exist"

- **Śaṅkara (Advaita)** — Reads the plural ("I, you, all these kings") as **conventional / empirical (*vyāvahārika*)**, accommodating the apparent multiplicity of bodies; ultimately there is one non-dual Self (*Brahman = ātman*). The eternity asserted is of the *one* Self that wrongly *appears* as many through *upādhis* (limiting adjuncts). The verse proves the self's eternity, not a real plurality. [Advaita Vedānta]
- **Rāmānuja (Viśiṣṭādvaita)** — Reads the plural as **literal and real**: the many *jīvas* (Kṛṣṇa, Arjuna, the kings) are eternally distinct individual selves, each a real mode/attribute (*śeṣa*) of Brahman. His *Gītā-bhāṣya* (and at length his *Śrī-bhāṣya*) argues against Śaṅkara that scripture would not enumerate distinct persons if their distinction were finally unreal. [Viśiṣṭādvaita]
- **Madhva (Dvaita)** — Pushes hardest for **eternal, real, irreducible plurality** of selves, each forever distinct from Brahman and from one another (*pañca-bheda*, fivefold difference); the verse is direct proof against monism. [Dvaita]
- *Variant (Gauḍīya / Caitanya school, via Viśvanātha & Baladeva, glossed in ISKCON's Bhaktivedanta ed.):* the plural words *sarve* ("all") and *janādhipāḥ* ("rulers") "clearly indicate individual souls"; cited as scriptural refutation of *māyāvāda* (Śaṅkara's illusionism). [late text]
- *Variant reading of "I" (aham):* across all theistic schools, Kṛṣṇa's "I" is read as **Bhagavān** (the Supreme Person) eternally distinct in status from the *jīvas*, where Advaita reads "I" and "you" as the *same* one Self under different names.

### On 2.47 — "karmaṇy evādhikāras te" (right to action, not to fruit)

- **Śaṅkara (Advaita)** — "Your right is **for action alone (*karmaṇi eva*), not ever for the results** — and not for steadfastness in Knowledge (*na ca jñāna-niṣṭhāyām*)." For Śaṅkara, karma-yoga is **purificatory and preparatory**: desireless action cleanses the mind (*sattva-śuddhi*) and qualifies one for *jñāna*, which alone liberates. Action is *adhikāra* (entitlement) for the not-yet-realized; the realized renounce action and abide in knowledge. [Advaita]
- **Rāmānuja (Viśiṣṭādvaita)** — Karma-yoga done as **disinterested service offered to the Lord** is not merely preparatory but a valid, even self-sufficient, path; the fruit one relinquishes is *kaivalya*-style self-enjoyment, surrendering all to God (the inner controller, *antaryāmin*). Action without attachment, dedicated to Bhagavān, leads onward to *bhakti*. [Viśiṣṭādvaita]
- **Madhva (Dvaita)** — Action is to be performed as worship of Hari, with the firm knowledge that **the Lord, not the self, is the true agent and giver of fruit** (*īśvara* is *kartā* and *phala-dātā*); renouncing fruit means renouncing the false sense of independent doership and depending on Viṣṇu. [Dvaita]
- *Variant (Bhaktivedanta / Gauḍīya):* glosses *adhikāra* as "prescribed duty" and reframes the verse as the charter of **niṣkāma karma-yoga** — work done as devotional offering (*bhakti*) so that action produces no karmic reaction. [late text]
- *Modern reading (Tilak, Gandhi):* B.G. Tilak's *Gītā-Rahasya* makes 2.47 the textual heart of an **activist karma-yoga** (action as duty, energetically pursued, fruit surrendered) against the renunciatory Advaita reading; Gandhi reads it as the scriptural basis of *anāsakti-yoga* (the yoga of non-attachment) and selfless service. [scholarly] [modern]

## Why Chapter 2 matters (cross-links)

- **Seeds Karma-yoga** — 2.47–50 are unfolded in [Ch. 3 Karma Yoga](../bhagavad-gita.md) (action as *yajña*, *lokasaṅgraha*) and the *svadharma* of 2.31 returns at 3.35 and 18.47.
- **Seeds the guṇa-doctrine** — *nistraiguṇya* (2.45) is elaborated in Ch. 14 ([three guṇas](../bhagavad-gita.md)).
- **Seeds devotional theism** — *mat-paraḥ* (2.61) anticipates the Bhakti-ṣaṭka (Chs. 7–12).
- **Upaniṣadic ancestry** — 2.19–20 and 2.29 closely parallel the [Kaṭha Upaniṣad](../nachiketa-katha-upanishad.md) (1.2.18–19, 1.2.7); the chapter is in part a Gītā-recasting of Yama's teaching to Naciketā.
- **Yoga overlap** — the sense-withdrawal of 2.58 (tortoise) is *pratyāhāra*, formalized in the [Yoga Sūtras of Patañjali](../yoga-sutras-of-patanjali.md); the *sthitaprajña* anticipates the *jīvanmukta*.
- **The two clocks** — note the *scriptural* setting (Dvāpara, c. 3102 BCE, on the eve of [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)) vs. the *academic* dating of the Gītā's composition (c. 2nd c. BCE – 2nd c. CE). [scholarly]
