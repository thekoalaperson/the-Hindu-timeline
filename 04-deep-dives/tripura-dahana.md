# Tripura-dāhana — The Burning of the Three Cities

> Cosmic address: a first-tier *standalone Śaiva cosmic-warrior myth* in which [Śiva](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md) becomes **Tripurāntaka** ("ender of the three cities") and incinerates the three flying fortresses of the asura brothers **Tārakākṣa, Vidyunmālī, and Kamalākṣa** (sons of Tāraka, in the epic, Liṅga and Śiva Purāṇa tellings; the *Matsya* makes **Maya** himself the leader) with a *single* arrow — the entire pantheon collapsing into the components of his war-machine. Undated within the Manvantara frame; the texts set it in primordial deva-time after the asuras win their boon (the *Mahābhārata* places it after the Tārakāmaya war, 8.24.3–4).
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · See also: [Śiva myth-cycle](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) · [Samudra Manthana](samudra-manthana.md)

The **Tripura-dāhana** (also *Tripura-saṃhāra*, "destruction of the Tripura") is the signature demonstration of **Śaiva primacy**: a battle that the whole divine order cannot win until Śiva consents to fight, and which he then ends in a *single instant* with *one* arrow. Its theological argument is built into its imagery — every other god is reduced to a *part* of Śiva's chariot or weapon. Brahmā becomes his charioteer, Viṣṇu his arrow, the Earth his car, the Vedas his horses, and the "lord of mountains" (or, in the epic, the Year) his bow. The episode is one of the most-depicted in Śaiva art, generating the **Tripurāntaka-mūrti** iconographic type. The oldest layer is Vedic: the *Taittirīya Saṃhitā* (6.2.3) already has three asura forts of iron, silver and gold and a god-composed arrow loosed by Rudra, but no cosmic chariot. The fullest narratives are in the *Śiva Purāṇa* (Rudra-saṃhitā 5, Yuddha-khaṇḍa, chs. 1–12), the *Liṅga Purāṇa* (1.71–72), the *Matsya Purāṇa* (129–140), and the *Mahābhārata* — Karṇa Parva 8.24, where **Duryodhana** tells it to **Śalya** (as Mārkaṇḍeya had told it to Dhṛtarāṣṭra) to persuade him to be Karṇa's charioteer as Brahmā was Śiva's, and a shorter Droṇa Parva telling (7.173.52–58, Vyāsa to Arjuna). The *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* (7.10.53–71, Nārada to Yudhiṣṭhira) gives a markedly Vaiṣṇava version. Kālidāsa alludes to it.

---

## I. The Boon and the Building of the Three Cities

- **Vedic prototype** — The asuras had three forts (*puraḥ*): the lowest of **iron**, then **silver**, then **gold** (*ayasmayī … rajatā … hariṇī*). The gods could not take them and besieged them with the *upasad* rite; they fashioned an arrow — **Agni** its point (*anīka*), **Soma** its barb (*śalya*), **Viṣṇu** its shaft (*tejana*) — and chose **Rudra** to shoot it; Rudra first demanded to be "lord of animals" (*paśūnām adhipati*), then split the three forts and drove the asuras from the worlds. (*Taittirīya Saṃhitā* 6.2.3) [Vedic; aetiology of the *upasad* rite]
  - *Variant (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 3.4.4.3–5):* the iron fort is on earth, the silver in the mid-air, the golden in the sky; the **gods themselves** break them by means of the *upasads* — Rudra is not named.
- **Origin of the brothers** — After the asura **Tāraka** is slain by Skanda/Kārttikeya, his three sons **Tārakākṣa** (eldest), **Vidyunmālī** (middle) and **Kamalākṣa** (youngest), "inimical to the gods", undertake fierce penance to Brahmā. (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.1.7–24; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.71.8–10) The *Mahābhārata* sets their penance after the daityas' defeat in the Tārakāmaya war, without naming Skanda (8.24.3–5).
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 129.3–25):* the petitioner is **Maya** himself, defeated in the Tārakāmaya war, who does penance flanked by two other dānavas, **Vidyunmālī** and **Tāraka** (*Tārakākhya*); there is no Kamalākṣa, and Maya asks for the fortress.
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.53–54):* no boon and no named brothers — the asuras, beaten by the gods, take refuge with Maya, "the supreme teacher of the māyā-wielders", who builds them three cities of gold, silver and iron.
- **Brahmā's boon** — Brahmā refuses outright immortality ("there is no immortality for all", *nāsti sarvāmaratvam*). The brothers instead ask for three cities in which to roam the worlds, which will **unite into one** (*ekībhāvam*) after a thousand years; only the god who strikes the united cities with a *single arrow* can be their death. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.7–12; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.71.11–17; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.1.30–54; *Matsya Purāṇa* 129.22–25 has the refusal and the single-arrow clause only)
  - *Variant (Śiva Purāṇa 2.5.1.48–53):* the union is timed to midday at the **Abhijit** moment with the moon in **Puṣya**, at the end of a thousand years; the slayer — "the lord who embodies all the gods" — must sit in a wonderful, fully equipped chariot and pierce the cities with a single arrow (Shastri translation) — and the brothers add that Śiva, whom they worship, would never burn them.
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 129.31–32):* Maya plans the cities to be built, and to come together in the sky, under the **Puṣya** conjunction (*puṣyayogeṇa*); whoever reaches them at that conjunction can destroy them with one blow.
- **The architect Maya builds Tripura** — The asura-artificer **Maya** (Mayāsura) constructs the three *pura*: one of **gold** in **heaven (div)**, one of **silver** in the **sky (antarikṣa)**, one of **iron** on the **earth (bhūmi)**, each a hundred *yojanas* across. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.13–16; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.71.18–20; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.1.55–58, where Brahmā orders Maya; *Matsya Purāṇa* 129.30–34) The texts agree on the metal–world pairing, which already appears in the Vedic layer (*Taittirīya Saṃhitā* 6.2.3; *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* 3.4.4.3). The Shastri translation renders the third city "steel" (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.1.47, 55) [translator's gloss].
  - *Variant (which brother rules which city):* gold for **Tārakākṣa**, silver for **Kamalākṣa**, iron for **Vidyunmālī** (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.18; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.71.20–21; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.1.46–47, 57). The Droṇa Parva swaps the outer two: iron for Tārakākṣa, silver for Kamalākṣa, gold for Vidyunmālī (*Mahābhārata* 7.173.52–53). In the *Matsya*, Tārakākhya holds the iron city, Vidyunmālī the silver, and **Maya himself** the gold (*Matsya Purāṇa* 130.7–11).
- **The flying, mobile fortresses** — The three *pura* roam the three worlds and come together only at the appointed time; their mobility plus the single-arrow clause is what makes them effectively indestructible. Maya supplies by his māyā whatever any inhabitant wishes. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.10–12, 8.24.21–22; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.71.15–17; *Matsya Purāṇa* 131.6)
- **The pool that revives the dead** — A further protection makes the asuras unkillable in battle: a pool (*vāpī*) in which slain asuras, thrown in, rise again in the same form and dress. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.23–27)
  - *Variant (who makes the pool):* in the *Mahābhārata* it is a boon won from Brahmā by **Hari**, son of Tārakākṣa (8.24.23–25); in the *Matsya*, Maya creates it by māyā in the middle of the war (136.10–26); in the *Bhāgavata*, Maya throws the dead into a well of perfected nectar (*kūpa-rasa*, 7.10.59–60).
  - *Variant (how the pool is emptied):* **Viṣṇu in the form of a great bull** enters Tripura and drinks it dry (*Matsya Purāṇa* 136.63–65; the pool-guards report it at 137.11–15); **Viṣṇu as a cow with Brahmā as her calf** drink up the nectar while the deluded asuras watch (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 7.10.61–63).
- **Asura rule and the fall from dharma** — Empowered, the Tripura asuras oppress the worlds; overcome by greed and delusion they break the established order, scatter the gods, and lay waste the gods' groves, the sages' hermitages and the sacrificial posts. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.27–30) In the *Śiva Purāṇa* the oppression comes only as the gods' complaint — the worlds subdued, the sages' rites stopped, the sacrificial shares seized (2.5.2.3–5, 2.5.2.58–62) — while Śiva calls the Tripura rulers meritorious (2.5.3.1–3); greed and delusion are the epic's diagnosis.

## II. The Corruption of Dharma — Viṣṇu's Deluding Teacher

- **Why the cities cannot simply be attacked** — While the Tripura asuras remain dharmic and devoted to Śiva's liṅga-worship, they are protected: in the *Śiva Purāṇa* Śiva himself refuses to kill his devotees (2.5.3.6–7), in the *Liṅga* Viṣṇu reasons that Rudra-worshippers are freed of sin and cannot be slain (1.71.47–54, 66–71), and Viṣṇu's first attempt — an army of *bhūtas* raised from an *upasad* sacrifice — is burnt up "like moths in a fire". A stratagem is needed to first *corrupt* them. (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.71.28–34, 1.71.44–71; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.3.1–7, 2.5.3.24–48)
- **Viṣṇu generates a deluding teacher** — Viṣṇu emanates a māyā-man (*puruṣa … māyāmaya*) and a deluding scripture (*māyāmaya śāstra*) of sixteen lakh *granthas*, contrary to Śruti and Smṛti and without varṇa or āśrama, whose tenet is that heaven and hell are here alone; the teacher enters Tripura and the asuras abandon Vedic rites and Śiva. **Nārada** joins the deceit and gets himself "initiated", and Viṣṇu sends **Alakṣmī** (ill-fortune) into the cities while their Lakṣmī departs. (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.71.72–94; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.4.1–64, 2.5.5.59–63)
  - In the *Śiva Purāṇa* the teacher is shaven-headed, carries a broom of cloth and covers his mouth with a cloth for fear of harming living beings, preaches non-violence, and is named **Arihat/Arihan** with four disciples (2.5.4.2–36, 2.5.5.2–20) — a polemical Jain-coded figure; the gods also say the asuras "resorted to Buddha's religion" (2.5.6.28, Shastri translation). He and his disciples are afterwards sent to the desert to wait until the Kali age (2.5.4.20–21; 2.5.12.21–33). The translator's notes call him **Māyāmoha** [translator's gloss] — that is the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*'s name for Viṣṇu's deluder in a different deva–asura war (3.17.41–3.18). The Shastri translation also adopts the reading "sixteen lakhs" over printed "sixteen thousand" [editorial reading]. [scholarly: the figure belongs to the Purāṇic deluder type behind the later **Buddha-avatāra** of Viṣṇu]
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 131.19–47):* there is no deluding teacher — Maya sees an ominous dream of figures entering the cities, urges the asuras to keep truth and dharma and to worship Maheśvara, but, struck by fate and pervaded by **Alakṣmī**, they quarrel, despise brahmins and gurus, abandon purity and ravage the gods' groves.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 8.24.28–30):* the asuras fall through their own greed and delusion (*lobha-moha*); no deluder appears.
- **Connection to the [Mohinī](samudra-manthana.md)/deluder pattern** — This is the same Vaiṣṇava "deceive-to-win" device seen in the Samudra Manthana and the [Bhasmāsura](bhasmasura-and-vrikasura.md) tales — Viṣṇu wins by guile, Śiva by force. [scholarly: a recurring sectarian division of labor]

## III. Śiva's Cosmic Chariot — The Pantheon as Weapon-System

The most distinctive feature of the Purāṇic and epic myth: the gods cannot defeat the cities, so they *become* Śiva's war-machine. The texts themselves remark that Śiva could burn the worlds by mere thought, and that the apparatus is play (*līlā*) and a display of his glory. (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.95–97; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.9.41–44)

- **Half the gods' strength — "Mahādeva"** — Śiva says he cannot kill them alone and offers the gods half his own *tejas*; when they say they cannot bear it, he takes half the strength of all of them, becomes stronger than all, and "from then on Śaṅkara was known as **Mahādeva**". (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.57–63)
- **The Earth as chariot (ratha)** — The goddess **Earth (pṛthivī)**, with her mountains, forests and islands, becomes the body of the chariot, and **Mandara** is its axle; the great rivers, the quarters, the planets and stars make its other parts. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.68–70; *Matsya Purāṇa* 133.17, with Meru's peak as the seat) In the *Liṅga* and *Śiva Purāṇa* the chariot is built by Viśvakarman out of all the worlds: the sky is its frame (*puṣkara*), **Mandara** its interior, the rising and setting mountains its poles and **Mahāmeru** its seat. (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.1–12; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.8.5–14)
- **Sun and Moon as wheels (cakra)** — **Sūrya** and **Candra** become the two wheels; in the Purāṇas the right (sun) wheel has twelve spokes for the twelve Ādityas and the left (moon) wheel sixteen for the moon's digits. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.71; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.3–5; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.8.6–9; *Matsya Purāṇa* 133.18)
- **The Vedas as horses** — The four **Vedas** are yoked as the horses, and the syllable **Om** (*praṇava*) is the driver's goad. (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.13, 1.72.20; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.8.24, 2.5.8.27, 2.5.9.1; *Matsya Purāṇa* 133.31, 133.36; *Mahābhārata* 8.24.103) The reins, in the epic, are deeds, truth, austerity and wealth (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.75); in both the epic and the *Matsya* the harness straps (*yoktra*) are the moon-phase goddesses Sinīvālī, Kuhū, Rākā and Anumati (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.74; *Matsya Purāṇa* 133.36); in the *Liṅga* and *Śiva Purāṇa* the gods hold the reins (1.72.19; 2.5.8.24).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 8.24.74, 8.24.80–81):* the horses are the four world-guardians (lords of the gods, waters, the dead and wealth), while the Vedas run before the chariot and guard its wheels and rear; the gods' own summary then calls the four Vedas the horses (8.24.103). [internal inconsistency of the epic text]
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, vulgate passage after 7.173.56, App. 025):* the four Vedas are the horses, the Upavedas their bits, **Gāyatrī and Sāvitrī** the reins (*pragraha*) and Om the goad, with Brahmā as charioteer; the Earth with its oceans and forests is the chariot, the serpent **Śeṣa** its axle, sun and moon its wheels. [not in the critical text]
- **Brahmā as charioteer (sārathi)** — Śiva asks for a driver greater than himself; the gods can find none but **Brahmā**, who takes the reins. When Śiva mounts, the horses sink to the ground. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.94–111; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.19, 1.72.29–32; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.8.24, 2.5.9.5–6; *Matsya Purāṇa* 133.47–55)
- **Viṣṇu as the bull who lifts the chariot** — When the horses fall under Śiva's weight, **Viṣṇu takes the form of a great bull** and lifts the chariot from beneath, but soon he too sinks to his knees. (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.30–31; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.9.7–9; *Matsya Purāṇa* 136.57–59, where Brahmā first lifts it and Janārdana then leaves the arrow as a bull and holds it on his horns)
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, vulgate passage after 8.24.114, App. 004):* Nārāyaṇa leaves the arrow as a bull to lift the sinking chariot; Śiva, standing on the bull and the horse, splits the bull's hooves and removes the horse's teats — whence cattle have cloven hooves and horses no teats. [not in the critical text]
  - *Variant (Matsya Purāṇa 138.40–42):* Śiva stands with one foot on the back of the Ṛgveda-horse and one on the bull; under the pressure the horse's teats and the bull's teeth fall, and since then horses have no teats and cattle lack (upper) teeth.
- **The bow (dhanus) and bowstring** — The "lord of mountains" (*śailendra*) is the bow and the "lord of serpents" (*bhujaṅgādhipa*) its string, with **Sarasvatī** as its bell. (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.23–24; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.8.25–26) Neither text names the mountain here; in the *Liṅga* **Meru** is listed separately as the chariot's great umbrella (1.72.22), and the same verse joins **Kālarātri** and Indra's bow to the bow and string (1.72.23). Retellings commonly identify the bow as Meru [retelling gloss; no Sanskrit locus found for Meru as the bow]; Vāsuki as the string is attested in the epic vulgate (below).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 8.24.83; Matsya Purāṇa 133.38–39):* the bow is the **Year** (*saṃvatsara*) of six seasons and the unfailing string is **Kālarātri**, the Night of Doom — in the *Matsya*, Umā as Kālarātri.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, vulgate passage after 7.173.56, App. 025):* Mount **Mandara** is the bow and the serpent **Vāsuki** its string; **Meru** is the banner, and Gandhamādana and Vindhya the flagstaffs. [not in the critical text]
- **Viṣṇu as the arrow (bāṇa/śara)** — The arrow is a triad of **Viṣṇu, Agni and Soma** (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.84, 8.24.91; *Matsya Purāṇa* 133.40; the *Liṅga* lists Soma, Viṣṇu, Kālāgni and Vāyu as stationed in the arrow, 1.72.111), into which Śiva puts the fire of his own anger (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.86); the gods make the **wind** its feathers (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.92). The texts differ on the parts: in the *Liṅga* Viṣṇu is the arrow, **Soma** its barb (*śalya*), the **Kālāgni** its sharpness, its tip (*anīka*) born of poison and the winds its feathers (1.72.24–25); in the *Śiva Purāṇa* Viṣṇu is the arrow and **Agni** its head, with the winds as feathers (2.5.8.26–28); in the *Matsya* **Agni** is the head and **Soma** the barb (*śalya*), and the serpent-king **Vāsuki** pours his venom into it (133.41–42). These elaborate the Vedic arrow of Agni, Soma and Viṣṇu (*Taittirīya Saṃhitā* 6.2.3).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 7.173.52–58):* all three cities are in the sky (*divi*); Śiva stands like a pillar (*sthāṇu*) for a thousand years, and when the three cities meet in the sky he pierces them with a three-jointed, three-pointed arrow joined with the fire of time (*kālāgni*), Viṣṇu and Soma. The critical text describes no chariot.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, vulgate passage after 7.173.56, App. 025):* Viṣṇu is the arrow, **Agni** its barb (*śalya*), **Vāyu** its two feathers, and **Yama Vaivasvata** is set at its feathered butt (*puṅkha*). [not in the critical text]
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.65–66):* **Viṣṇu himself** furnishes Śiva's chariot, charioteer, banner, horses, bow, armour and arrows out of his own powers — dharma, knowledge, detachment, prosperity, austerity, learning and rites. There is no Brahmā-charioteer and no Vedas-as-horses.
- **Lord of the animals (Paśupati)** — Before he will strike, Śiva asks that the gods accept the status of his animals (*paśu*); when they hesitate he promises release to all who keep his **Pāśupata** vow, and so he becomes Paśupati. (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.34–43; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.9.13–24) This reworks Rudra's Vedic demand to be lord of animals (*Taittirīya Saṃhitā* 6.2.3); in the Droṇa Parva the gods, seeking his help, promise Rudra that the animals in all rites shall be his (*Mahābhārata* 7.173.55).
- **The bull banner** — Śiva sets his bull (*govṛṣa*) on the flagstaff; in the *Matsya* **Nandin** mounts it. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.78; *Matsya Purāṇa* 133.60)
- **The pramatha-gaṇas and the army** — Śiva's gaṇas (Bhṛṅgin, Nandin and countless others), **Vīrabhadra**, Mahākāla, Skanda, Gaṇeśa, Kālī and the Mothers, the world-guardians and the gods accompany him; Viṣṇu rides Garuḍa on his left and Indra the elephant on his right. (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.50–86; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.9.27–40) In the *Matsya* the gaṇas fight a full battle: Nandin cuts down Tārakākhya with his axe (138.43–46) and later kills Vidyunmālī with the spear Vidyunmālī had hurled at him (140.18–38). The killing-stroke on the cities is Śiva's alone.

## IV. The Single Arrow — Alignment and Destruction

- **The appointed union** — Śiva waits, bow drawn, for the moment when the three *pura* become one (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.115–117; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.101–104). In the Purāṇas it is the **Puṣya** conjunction (*Matsya Purāṇa* 129.31–32, 140.44; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.105–109, where Brahmā urges him to shoot before they part again; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.1.48–50, Abhijit with the moon in Puṣya). The *Bhāgavata* has Śiva loose the arrow at the **Abhijit** *muhūrta* (7.10.67).
  - *Variant (the worship of Gaṇeśa):* Śiva stands in the archer's *pratyālīḍha* posture for a hundred thousand years, but the cities will not come into line until a heavenly voice tells him to worship **Gaṇeśa**; once he does, they unite (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.10.2–14). In the *Liṅga*, the unworshipped Vināyaka threatens to obstruct the gods, and the gods and Śiva worship him before setting out (1.72.44–50).
  - *Variant (Tamil sthala tradition, Accirupākkam):* because the gods set out without worshipping Vināyaka, the axle of Śiva's chariot breaks on the way, and Gaṇeśa (worshipped there as Accumuri Vināyakar) restores it once Śiva prays to him. (temple tradition, per the Aatcheeswarar temple page on shivatemples.com) [regional / folk; sthala-purāṇa locus not verified]
- **The loosed arrow** — Yoking the arrow to the **Pāśupata** missile, Śiva looses it; it burns the three cities and all their asuras in a single blaze. (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.115–121; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.101, 1.72.113–115; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.10.20–36; *Matsya Purāṇa* 140.45–75) In the epic the burnt cities are hurled into the western ocean (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.120); in the *Matsya* the burning houses and ramparts fall into the ocean (140.70–75, 140.84), no quarter named; in the *Śiva Purāṇa* the ashes fall on the ocean-girt earth (2.5.10.28). The epic adds that Śiva then restrained his anger-born fire from burning the worlds (8.24.122).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 7.10.57–58, 7.10.67–68):* Śiva shoots twice. His first missile throws out fire-coloured shafts, like masses of rays streaming from the sun's disc, which hide the cities and kill the city-dwellers, but Maya revives them in his nectar well. Only after the well is drunk dry does Śiva, in his Viṣṇu-furnished chariot, burn the cities.
  - *Variant (the glance and the laugh — Liṅga Purāṇa 1.72.110–114):* at the Puṣya moment the all-seeing god merely **looks** at Tripura and it is ash at once. Soma, Viṣṇu, Kālāgni and Vāyu, stationed in the arrow, then beg him to loose it anyway for their sake, and, **laughing** (*hasan*), he shoots. The *Śiva Purāṇa* likewise has the gods remind him he could burn the three worlds with one glance and ask him to shoot "for our fame" (2.5.10.21–24). Śiva "as if laughing" (*hasann iva*) also asks for a charioteer in the epic (*Mahābhārata* 8.24.94). Tamil Śaiva tradition has Śiva burn the cities by a smile alone (the epithet "Sirittu puram eritta Perumān", "the lord who burnt the cities with a smile"); at Tiruvatikai Vīraṭṭānam the smile answers the gods' pride (see "The gods' awe" below). [regional / devotional; Tamil textual locus not verified]
- **The spared devotee — Maya and others** — Having loosed the arrow, Śiva cries out in grief that his devotee Maya will die; **Nandin** rushes into Tripura ahead of the arrow and warns Maya, "firm in devotion to Maheśvara", who escapes with his own house. Indra curses that house; Śiva gives Maya a dwelling where Dhruva shines. (*Matsya Purāṇa* 140.47–52, 140.75–82) In the *Śiva Purāṇa* nothing escapes the fire except Maya, and those who sought refuge in Śiva are saved (2.5.10.38–41); Maya then praises Śiva, asks only for unfailing devotion, and is sent to **Vitala** with his kin (2.5.12.1–20). Asuras who had kept up Rudra-worship attain the status of Śiva's gaṇas (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.116–117; *Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.10.44).
- **Tārakākṣa's last prayer** — Burning with his two brothers, Tārakākṣa remembers Śiva and prays to keep his mind on him in every birth. (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.10.30–33)
- **The gods' awe** — After the burning the gods stand silent and afraid before Śiva's blazing form and praise him; Śiva grants them a boon to come whenever they are in distress. (*Śiva Purāṇa* 2.5.11.5–39; *Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.117–122; *Mahābhārata* 8.24.123–124) The Śaiva-primacy point is made by the texts' remarks that he needed none of the apparatus (§III). [Śaiva-primacy motif]
  - *Variant (Tamil sthala tradition, Tiruvatikai):* each god serving as a part of the chariot grows proud of his own share in the coming victory; Śiva merely smiles, and the cities burn with that effortless smile, without the apparatus. (temple tradition of Tiruvatikai Vīraṭṭānam, one of the eight Vīraṭṭāna shrines, where the Tripura-saṃhāra is located; per the temple page on shivatemples.com) [regional / folk; sthala-purāṇa locus not verified] No version in which the gods' pride is humbled was found in Śiva Purāṇa 2.5.1–12, Liṅga 1.71–72, Matsya 129–140 or Mahābhārata 8.24.

## V. Tripurāntaka-mūrti and Cultic Afterlife

- **Tripurāntaka / Tripurāri iconography** — The deed crystallizes into a fixed icon: Śiva as an archer drawing the bow, usually four-armed, standing on or beside the cosmic chariot — the **Tripurāntaka-mūrti**. It appears early at Pattadakal (Bādāmi Cālukya) and Ellora's Kailāsanātha (per Wikipedia, *Tripurantaka*). It is also prominent in Cōḻa bronze and South Indian temple sculpture [art-historical; not in the cited page, locus not verified]. (Śilpa/Āgama tradition) [iconographic; specific Āgama prescriptions (*Kāraṇāgama* etc.) — locus not verified]
- **Epithets** — From this act Śiva bears the names **Tripurāntaka** (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.171, Brahmā's address; at 1.72.114 the arrow itself is *tripurāntakara*), **Tripurārdana** (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.113), **Tripurāri / Tripurārāti** (*Liṅga Purāṇa* 1.72.121), **Tripuraghna** (*Mahābhārata* 7.173.37; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 11.16.20, where Kṛṣṇa says "among archers I am the Tripura-slayer"), **Tripurahā / Purahā** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 4.17.13, 7.10.70), **Tripuradviṣ** and **Purāri** ("enemy of the city/cities"). (Purāṇic / kāvya / stotra usage)
- **Kālidāsa allusions** — Kālidāsa attests the myth's classical-age currency: the Gandharvas led by Viśvāvasu sing Śiva's Tripura deed (*tripurāvadāna*) as he travels to his wedding (*Kumārasaṃbhava* 7.48); kinnarīs sing the "Tripura-victory" (*tripuravijaya*) in the cloud's mountain landscape (*Meghadūta* 56, GRETIL numbering); and the epithets *purāri* (*Kumārasaṃbhava* 5.54), *puraśāsana* (*Kumārasaṃbhava* 7.30) and *tripuradviṣ* (*Raghuvaṃśa* 17.14) appear. (*Kālidāsa* corpus) [allusive]
- **Kārtika Pūrṇimā / Kārttikai Dīpam association** — The full moon of Kārtika is called **Tripurārī / Tripurī Pūrṇimā**, after Tripurāri, the foe of Tripurāsura, and is also called *Deva-Dīpāvalī* (per Wikipedia, *Kartik Purnima*). In Tamil tradition the lamp festival of **Kārttikai Dīpam** (Kārttikai month; the great beacon on Tiruvaṇṇāmalai) is popularly linked to the burning of Tripura as well. The same page mentions Kārttikai Dīpam and Tiruvaṇṇāmalai, but does not connect them to Tripura, and no primary locus was found for that link. (festival / Tamil Śaiva sthala and folk tradition) [regional / folk; locus not verified]
- **Contrast with Viṣṇu's mode** — As in [Gajendra Mokṣa](gajendra-moksha.md) (Viṣṇu rescues by grace) and the Manthana (Viṣṇu wins by Mohinī's guile), Tripura-dāhana is the Śaiva counter-paradigm: deliverance by overwhelming, instantaneous *force*. The *Bhāgavata* turns this round, counting the deed among Hari's acts, done through his māyā (7.10.71). [scholarly: sectarian patterning]
