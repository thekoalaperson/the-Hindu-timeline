# Brāhmaṇa legends catalog — ten Vedic prose tales

> Parent: [04 · Deep Dives](README.md) → [Master Index](../README.md). A **grouped catalog** of ten *ākhyāna* (prose legends) embedded in the ritual exposition of the **Brāhmaṇas** — the oldest sustained narrative layer in Sanskrit, older than the epics and Purāṇas that later re-tell many of these seeds. Each tale is a single node too small for its own file; together they map the story-reservoir of the [Aitareya](../90-literature-corpus/vedas/brahmanas/aitareya-brahmana.md), [Śatapatha](../90-literature-corpus/vedas/brahmanas/shatapatha-brahmana.md), [Pañcaviṃśa](../90-literature-corpus/vedas/brahmanas/panchavimsha-brahmana.md), and [Jaiminīya](../90-literature-corpus/vedas/brahmanas/jaiminiya-brahmana.md) Brāhmaṇas, plus the Ṛgvedic *anukramaṇī*-tradition of the *Bṛhaddevatā*.

Two clocks throughout. **Scripture clock:** apauruṣeya Veda "re-heard" and re-divided by Kṛṣṇa-Dvaipāyana Vyāsa at the Dvāpara–Kali junction of the current 28th Mahāyuga (c. **3102 BCE**); these legends sit in the deep ṛṣi-and-Manu past of the current (Vaivasvata) Manvantara. **Academic clock:** orally composed Brāhmaṇa prose, c. **900–600 BCE**, Iron-Age Kuru-Pañcāla and southern heartlands. [scholarly] These are *not* Purāṇic elaborations — they predate them by a millennium, and the Purāṇic forms are the derivatives.

The Śatapatha's [Videgha Māthava](../90-literature-corpus/vedas/brahmanas/shatapatha-brahmana.md) and Matsya-flood legends are treated at length in that text-profile (§5a) and are not repeated here; this file catalogs the ten that had no home.

## Celestial & cosmogonic legends

- **Atri and Svarbhānu — the eclipse before Rāhu** — The Āsura **Svarbhānu** ("own-light," a Dānava) strikes the **Sun** with darkness (*tamas*), so that the world is bewildered and cannot find where the sun stands. The ṛṣi **Atri**, alone able to do it, finds the hidden sun and restores it to the sky "by the fourth *brahman* (formula)" — the Atris uncovering the eye of the heavens. (Ṛgveda 5.40.5–9; the *sāman* origin at Pañcaviṃśa Brāhmaṇa 4.5.2) [scholarly]
  - *The Rāhu ancestry:* this is the **Vedic prototype of the eclipse myth** — Svarbhānu, the demon who "seizes" the sun with darkness, is the direct ancestor of the Purāṇic **Rāhu**, the severed asura-head who swallows Sūrya and Candra at eclipses. In the Ṛgveda the eclipse is *undone by a ṛṣi's mantra*, not by the gods; the Atri clan claims the recovery as its ritual charter. See [Atri & Anasūyā](atri-anasuya.md); the developed eclipse-demon belongs to the [Samudra-manthana](samudra-manthana.md) cycle (Rāhu–Ketu). [late text — for the Rāhu identification]

- **Dīrghajihvī the soma-licker** — **Dīrghajihvī** ("Long-tongue"), a demoness, keeps **licking up the gods' soma** with her long tongue before it can be pressed, robbing the sacrifice; **Indra** cannot catch or conquer her. He fashions a youth, **Sumitra Kutsa**, and sends him to seduce her; entangled with him she is caught and destroyed, and the soma is secured for the gods — an etiological *sāman* legend on why a particular chant "seizes" the pressing. (Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa 1.161–163) [scholarly — the fuller, "wilder" JB telling, ed. O'Flaherty]

## Trials of true brāhmaṇahood

- **Kavaṣa Ailūṣa and the Sarasvatī** — At a soma-session (*sattra*) on the banks of the **Sarasvatī**, the ṛṣis discover that **Kavaṣa Ailūṣa** is the son of a *dāsī* (slave-woman, Ilūṣā) and cast him out into the desert sands, refusing to let so base-born a man drink the river's water: "let him die of thirst." Alone in the waste, Kavaṣa "sees" a hymn to the **Waters** and praises them — and the **Sarasvatī herself flows out after him** and encircles him. The ṛṣis, seeing the river follow him, recognize the gods have chosen him, and take him back as a true seer. (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 2.19) [scholarly — a charter against birth-only reckoning of ṛṣi-hood]

- **Nābhānediṣṭha and Rudra's leavings** — **Nābhānediṣṭha**, a son of **Manu**, returns from his brahmacārin studies to find his brothers have divided Manu's estate without him ("we have apportioned you into our father"). Manu sends him to the **Aṅgirases**, who are stuck at a *sattra* unable to reach heaven; Nābhānediṣṭha teaches them the *viśvedeva*-verses that free them, and in fee they leave him the cattle on the sacrificial ground. As he gathers them a **dark man** (a form of **Rudra**) appears: "What is left on the sacrificial ground is mine." Nābhānediṣṭha appeals to Manu, who confirms the god's right; when the boy tells Rudra truthfully that his father upheld the claim, Rudra — pleased with the honesty — **gives the cattle back to him**. (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 5.14) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Ṛgvedic frame):* the same Nābhānediṣṭha is the ascribed ṛṣi of Ṛgveda 10.61–62, the "obscure" Aṅgiras hymns his teaching is tied to; genealogical texts fold him into the Manu-and-the-Manvas lineage of the [Solar/Lunar stem](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).

## Death undone — the revival tales

- **Vṛśa Jāna and the boy under the chariot (Tryaruṇa)** — The Ikṣvāku king **Tryaruṇa Traivṛṣṇa**, driving with his *purohita* **Vṛśa Jāna** at the reins, runs his chariot over a **brāhmaṇa boy**, killing him. King, charioteer, and horses each disclaim the guilt in turn (a formal apportioning of blood-guilt); the death is finally laid on the driver. The priest **Vṛśa** then "sees" a hymn to **Agni** and by its recitation **restores the dead boy to life** — the origin-legend of the Vārṣa *sāman*. (Pañcaviṃśa Brāhmaṇa 13.3.12; narrated in full at Bṛhaddevatā 5.14–23; the hymn is Ṛgveda 5.2) [scholarly]

- **The Gaupāyanas and Subandhu's recalled soul** — King **Asamāti Rāthaproṣṭha** dismisses his four **Gaupāyana** (Laupāyana) family-priests — the brothers **Bandhu, Subandhu, Śrutabandhu, Viprabandhu** — in favor of two Āsura sorcerer-brothers, **Kirāta and Ākuli**, who by witchcraft **kill Subandhu**, driving his life-breath (*asu/manas*) out of the body. The surviving brothers chant the **"recall of the departed spirit"** hymns over the corpse, summoning his mind back from Yama, from the sky, from the sun, from the four quarters — and **Subandhu revives**. (Ṛgveda 10.57–60, the *mano-nivartana* hymns; narrated at Bṛhaddevatā 7.83–102) [scholarly — one of the oldest "recall-of-the-soul" rites in the corpus]

## Indra's crimes and Indra's double

- **Indra and the Yatis — thrown to the salāvṛkas** — In a passage on expiation, the Brāhmaṇa records without flinching that **Indra gave the ascetic Yatis to the salāvṛkas** (hyenas / wild dogs) to be devoured — one of the canonical **"sins of Indra"** (alongside the slaying of Vṛtra, of the three-headed Triśiras Viśvarūpa, and the betrayal of Bṛhaspati). The deed is reported as a stain the god must answer for, not a triumph. (Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.28) [disputed — a deed the tradition itself flags as Indra's transgression]
  - *Variant (count spared):* tellings differ on whether Indra destroyed the Yatis wholesale or spared a remnant (often "three," or the youngest); the number is unstable across recensions. [disputed]

- **Kutsa Aurava, Indra's doppelgänger** — The ṛṣi **Kutsa Aurava** so exactly **resembles Indra** — same form, same braided lock of hair — that when Indra carries him up to heaven, bound to him by a single shared lock, even **Indrāṇī (Śacī)** cannot tell god from man and cannot say which is her husband. The ambiguity is the point: the myth ties the near-identity of the Ṛgvedic protégé Kutsa (Indra's charioteer-companion) to a *sāman* and to the danger of the indistinguishable double. (Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa 3.199–200) [scholarly]
  - *Note — name collision:* this **Kutsa Aurava** (Indra's twin-double) is distinct from the **Sumitra Kutsa** created to trap Dīrghajihvī above, and from the composer-ṛṣi Kutsa Āṅgirasa of the Ṛgvedic Kutsa hymns; the Brāhmaṇas reuse the name across unrelated figures.

## Ghosts, and the Asura priests who tricked Manu

- **Keśin Dārbhya and the ghost Kaupayeya** — The Pañcāla sage-king **Keśin Dārbhya** encounters the **preta (ghost) of Kaupayeya** — a spirit of the dead who reveals hidden ritual knowledge: which chant carries the sacrificer safely across to the world of the fathers, and the fate awaiting one who errs. The tale belongs to the Jaiminīya's marked interest in the **afterworld of requital** (the same theological soil as its Bhṛgu-vision), staging instruction of the living by the dead. (Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa 2.269–272) [scholarly — edition-dependent numbering; the JB survives only in partial manuscripts]

- **Manu's bull, and Kilāta & Ākuli** — **Manu** owns a **bull** in whom dwells an **Asura-and-Rakṣas-slaying voice**: wherever the bull bellows, demons are crushed. The two **Asura priests Kilāta and Ākuli**, unable to bear it, come to Manu offering to "sacrifice for you" — and trick him into offering up first the **bull** (the voice leaps into his wife **Manāvī**, who now crushes demons wherever she speaks), then **Manāvī herself** (the voice escapes into the **sacrifice and the sacrificial vessels**, where the Asuras can no longer reach it). A grim etiology of how the demon-slaying power came to reside in the rite itself. (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.1.4.14–17) [scholarly]
  - *Note — name collision:* the **Ākuli** here (an Asura priest paired with Kilāta) is a different figure from the **Ākuli** who, with **Kirāta**, murders Subandhu in the Gaupāyana tale above; both are "Asura sorcerer" names the tradition recycles.

## Why these ten sit together

- **The narrative Brāhmaṇas.** Nine of the ten come from the four most story-laden Brāhmaṇas — the Jaiminīya and Śatapatha (the "richest reservoirs of Vedic myth"), the Aitareya (the Hotṛ's book, home of Śunaḥśepa), and the Pañcaviṃśa (the Udgātṛ's book) — with the *Bṛhaddevatā* supplying the connected Ṛgvedic narrative frames. Each tale is an *arthavāda* — a legend told to explain *why* a chant, a rite, or a fee works.
- **Seeds, not derivatives.** Several are the earliest form of a story the Purāṇas later swell: Svarbhānu → Rāhu (eclipses); the "recall of the departed soul" → later *śrāddha* and revival motifs; Manu's demon-slaying voice → the later theology of the sacrifice as apotropaic. Cataloged here **as the seed**, with the derivative flagged.
- **Attribution over merger.** Where the Bṛhaddevatā, a Brāhmaṇa, and the Ṛgvedic *anukramaṇī* give different framings of the same event (Nābhānediṣṭha, the Gaupāyanas, Tryaruṇa), the variants are kept as nested bullets, never collapsed.
