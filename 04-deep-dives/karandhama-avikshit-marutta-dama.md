# Karandhama, Avīkṣit, Marutta and Dama — the Mārkaṇḍeya's royal cycle

> *Cosmic address: a four-generation dynastic saga embedded in the **bird-dialogues (dharma-pakṣin)** of the [Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/markandeya-purana.md), Cantos ~CXIX–CXXXVII (Pargiter), narrated by the four wise birds to **Jaimini**; the Vaivasvata-Manu genealogy section of the text, set in legendary royal antiquity of the present **[7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)** with no precise yuga-address. One continuous line — a sigh-born army, a bridegroom's shame, a universal emperor's Nāga war, and a son's rākṣasa-vengeance.*
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Kindred: [Kuvalayāśva & Madālasā](kuvalayashva-madalasa.md) · [Vipaścit in Hell](vipashchit-in-hell.md) · [Mārkaṇḍeya the sage](markandeya.md) · [the Vaivasvata king-tales](../03-lineages-vamsha/markandeya-vaivasvata-king-tales.md)

The **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa** devotes a long block of its royal genealogy (Pargiter's Cantos ~CXIX–CXXXVII) to a single descent of chakravartins — **Karandhama → Avīkṣit → Marutta → Nariṣyanta → Dama** — and treats each king's reign as an *upākhyāna* (embedded tale) rather than a bare name-list. Read end to end it is one of the Purāṇa's darkest arcs: it opens with a miracle (an army conjured from a king's own breath), turns on a study of wounded kṣatriya honour (a bridegroom so shamed by capture that he refuses his own crown), rises to the imperial grandeur of Marutta, and closes on a scene of ritual horror — a grieving son offering his father's murderer's **flesh and blood as the funeral libation**, feasting brāhmaṇas descended from rākṣasa stock. The tradition preserves the whole descent without softening the ending.

This file is the canonical home for the cycle; the sage [Mārkaṇḍeya](markandeya.md) and the bird-frame belong to the same textual world as [Kuvalayāśva & Madālasā](kuvalayashva-madalasa.md) and the [Vaivasvata king-tales](../03-lineages-vamsha/markandeya-vaivasvata-king-tales.md).

---

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — these kings sit in the present **Śveta-Vārāha Kalpa · 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara**, in the deep antiquity of the current Mahāyuga, well before the epic age. The Purāṇa itself gives them **no yuga-tag**; they are "legendary royal antiquity," dynastic ancestors of the Solar/Vaivasvata stock (see [Sūryavaṃśa & Candravaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)). Marutta is remembered across the corpus as one of the great emperors of old.
- **Academic / historical frame** — [scholarly] the Mārkaṇḍeya's core genealogical cantos are among the **older strata** of the text (Pargiter dated the base layers early, c. 3rd–5th century CE), with the whole Purāṇa compiled by roughly the 6th century CE. The famous Devī-Māhātmya is a *later* interpolation into the same book and does not touch this royal cycle.

*Never collapse the two: the "sigh-born army" and "universal monarch" are inside the yuga-clock of myth; the c. 3rd–6th-century dating is the historian's clock for the text that records them.*

## The line at a glance

- **Balāsva → Karandhama** — son of King Khaninetra (of the Vaivasvata line); won the surname **Karandhama** by a single feat of arms (Canto CXXI).
- **Avīkṣit (Avīkṣita)** — son of Karandhama; the shamed bridegroom who refused consecration; father of Marutta (Cantos CXXII–CXXVII).
- **Marutta** — son of Avīkṣit and the princess **Vaiśāliṇī / Vishālā**; the *cakravartin* and great sacrificer (Cantos CXXVIII–CXXXI).
- **Nariṣyanta** — son of Marutta; a lavish patron of brāhmaṇas, murdered in the forest (Canto CXXXII).
- **Dama** — son of Nariṣyanta by **Indrasenā** (daughter of Babhru); wed **Sumanā** of Daśārṇa; avenger of his father (Cantos CXXXIII–CXXXVI).

---

## Karandhama — the sigh-born army (Canto CXXI)

- **Balāsva, the king with no army** — the prince **Balāsva** (son of Khaninetra, himself a boon-son granted by Indra's favour) came to the throne and was set upon by hostile kings while lacking any standing host to meet them (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXI). Pargiter's canto-summary calls what follows a **"fanciful victory"** — the etymological legend behind the king's second name.
- **The host from his own breath** — in his extremity Balāsva **produced a whole army out of his own body/breath** — a phantom host exhaled into being — and with it routed his enemies. From this feat he took the name **Karandhama** (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXI). [The Purāṇa treats the episode as the origin-story of the name rather than a literal military campaign; hence Pargiter's "fanciful."]
- **Reading** — the motif inverts the ordinary kṣatriya order: instead of an army making the king, the king *is* the army, generating force from his own person — a fitting founder-miracle for a line whose later members will win and lose everything by personal prowess.

---

## Avīkṣit — the svayaṃvara capture and the shame (Cantos CXXII–CXXVII)

- **The abduction at the svayaṃvara** — Karandhama's son **Avīkṣit** grew into a king "equal to Indra in prowess, to the Sun in brightness, as forgiving as the earth." At the **svayaṃvara** of the princess **Vaiśāliṇī** (the princess of Vaiśālī / **Vishālā**) he seized her and carried her off by force, defeating the assembled suitor-kings (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXII). He is also credited with several other wives (Vara, Gaurī, Subhadrā, Līlāvatī, Vibhā, Maṅgavatī, Kumudvatī).
- **The counter-defeat and capture** — the humiliated suitor-kings **combined**, fell upon Avīkṣit, defeated him in turn, and **took him prisoner** — the pivot of his whole story (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXIII).
- **Karandhama's rescue** — his father **Karandhama** came to the field and **freed his captured son** (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXIV). The princess, for her part, is said to have **refused every rival suitor**, holding to Avīkṣit despite his disgrace.
- **The refusal of the crown** — this is the psychological heart of the cycle. So **ashamed of having been captured** was Avīkṣit that, when the time came, he **refused to be consecrated / anointed king** — a kṣatriya declining sovereignty itself because his honour had been stained (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXV–CXXVI). One strand adds that in his mortification he even **held back from completing the marriage** with the princess he had won.
- **The birth of Marutta** — the impasse is resolved across the closing Avīkṣit cantos: the princess perseveres (in one telling turning to **austerities** and winning an assurance from the gods), the union is at last consummated, and their son **Marutta** is born — the heir who *will* take the crown his father would not (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXVI–CXXVII). [The exact mechanics of the resolution vary in the summaries; the load-bearing facts are Avīkṣit's shame-driven refusal and Marutta's parentage.]

---

## Marutta — universal monarch and the Nāga war (Cantos CXXVIII–CXXXI)

- **Consecrated where his father refused** — **Marutta** was anointed king and became a **cakravartin (universal monarch)**, "a great sacrificer and a liberal benefactor to brāhmaṇas" (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXVIII–CXXIX). His reign is the dynasty's zenith.
- **The Nāga trouble** — the **Nāgas (serpent-folk)** gave great trouble in his realm and Marutta **marched against them** — but his father **Avīkṣit interposed on the Nāgas' behalf**, and a pitched battle was **averted by the intervention of the ṛṣis**, the Nāgas making **reparation** for the wrong (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXX). It is a rare Purāṇic case of the elder generation restraining the younger from a war of extermination. See [Nāgas & serpents](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md).
- **The great sacrifice** — Marutta's fame rests above all on his enormous **yajña**; the Purāṇa names the priest **Saṃvarta** among those who officiate for him (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXXI).
  - *Cross-text (Mahābhārata):* the epic makes Marutta Āvīkṣita the archetypal golden emperor. In the **[Aśvamedhika Parva](mahabharata/14-ashvamedhika-parva.md)** the Pāṇḍavas fund Yudhiṣṭhira's horse-sacrifice from the **mountain of gold Marutta left buried in the Himālaya** after his own rite; the **[Anuśāsana](mahabharata/13-anushasana-parva.md)** and the *ṣoḍaśa-rājakīya* ("sixteen kings" eulogy) list him among the sixteen greatest sovereigns. There **Saṃvarta** (brother of Bṛhaspati) officiates because **Bṛhaspati refuses**, being priest to Indra — a rivalry told in full under [the gurus Bṛhaspati & Śukra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/the-gurus-brihaspati-and-shukra.md). [scholarly — the Saṃvarta episode is most developed in the epic; the Mārkaṇḍeya's telling is briefer.]

## Nariṣyanta and Dama — the rākṣasa vow (Cantos CXXXII–CXXXVI)

This is the grim finale for which the whole cycle is remembered.

- **Nariṣyanta the patron** — Marutta's son **Nariṣyanta** succeeded him and, like his forebears, **enriched the brāhmaṇas permanently** at a great sacrifice (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXXII).
- **Dama and the Daśārṇa princess** — Nariṣyanta's son **Dama** (his mother **Indrasenā**, daughter of Babhru) was **chosen at her svayaṃvara by Sumanā, princess of Daśārṇa**. When rival suitor-kings, in violation of the marriage-law, **opposed the match, Dama defeated them**, and the couple returned to Nariṣyanta's capital to the old king's delight (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXXIII–CXXXIV). Note the deliberate echo of his great-grandfather Avīkṣit's svayaṃvara — but where Avīkṣit was captured and shamed, Dama prevails.
- **The murder in the forest** — one of those defeated suitors, **Vapuṣmat**, nursed the grudge. He **fell upon Nariṣyanta in the forest and murdered him** (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXXV). Dama's father is thus killed by an enemy his own marriage had made.
- **Dama's vow of vengeance** — Dama **bewailed his father and swore a terrible oath**: to avenge the murder and to perform his father's obsequies with the murderer himself (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXXV–CXXXVI).
- **The slaying and the blood-libation** — Dama **hunted down and killed Vapuṣmat**, and then **celebrated his father Nariṣyanta's śrāddha (funeral rites) using Vapuṣmat's own flesh and blood** — offering the ancestral cakes (*piṇḍa*) shaped with the enemy's flesh, and **feasting brāhmaṇas who were themselves sprung from families of rākṣasas** (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* CXXXVI). It is a *pitṛ-yajña* turned into an act of ritual cannibal-vengeance — the funeral rite ([śrāddha & pitṛ-rites](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md)) inverted into a rākṣasa-oblation.
  - *Reading — why the corpus keeps it:* the episode is deliberately transgressive. A *śrāddha* is meant to nourish and pacify the ancestral dead with pure vegetarian *piṇḍa*; Dama satisfies his father's *preta* with the murderer's blood instead. The Purāṇa does **not** frame this as adharma to be condemned but as the fierce fulfilment of a son's debt (*pitṛ-ṛṇa*) — the rākṣasa-descended officiants make the rite ritually possible. The line that began with a miracle of breath ends with a meal of blood. [The scene is a Purāṇic outlier, cited by scholars as evidence of older, harsher kṣatriya funerary lore preserved in the Mārkaṇḍeya's antique genealogical stratum.]

## Themes and pattern

- **A dynasty defined by prowess and its costs** — each generation is tested at the point of personal force: Karandhama *creates* an army, Avīkṣit is *unmade* by capture, Marutta *masters* even the Nāgas (only to be restrained by his father), and Dama *weaponises* the funeral rite itself.
- **The svayaṃvara as fate-engine** — two svayaṃvaras bracket the cycle. Avīkṣit's abduction of Vaiśāliṇī brings on his captivity and lifelong shame; Dama's winning of Sumanā brings on his father's murder. The Purāṇa uses the bride-contest twice as the trigger for catastrophe.
- **Honour, debt, and the two refusals** — Avīkṣit refuses a *crown* he feels unworthy of; Dama refuses to leave a *debt* to his father's spirit unpaid. Wounded honour and filial debt are the twin motors of the arc.
- **Preservation without resolution** — in the house manner, the tradition records the blood-śrāddha exactly, with attribution, rather than editing it out. It sits alongside the Purāṇa's gentler bird-narrated romances ([Kuvalayāśva & Madālasā](kuvalayashva-madalasa.md)) and its infernal visions ([Vipaścit in Hell](vipashchit-in-hell.md)) as part of the same book's moral range.

## Sources

**Primary (scriptural):**

- **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, Cantos ~CXIX–CXXXVII** (Pargiter's numbering) — the continuous genealogy of Balāsva/Karandhama, Avīkṣit, Marutta, Nariṣyanta and Dama, narrated by the wise birds to Jaimini. Key cantos: **CXXI** (Balāsva named Karandhama by his "fanciful victory"); **CXXII–CXXVII** (Avīkṣit's svayaṃvara capture of Vaiśāliṇī, his defeat and imprisonment, Karandhama's rescue, his refusal of consecration, and the birth of Marutta); **CXXVIII–CXXXI** (Marutta consecrated, cakravartin and great sacrificer, the Nāga trouble averted by the ṛṣis, Saṃvarta the priest); **CXXXII** (Nariṣyanta); **CXXXIII–CXXXVI** (Dama chosen by Sumanā of Daśārṇa, the defeat of the suitor-kings, Vapuṣmat's murder of Nariṣyanta, Dama's vow, and the slaying of Vapuṣmat with the offering of his flesh and blood at Nariṣyanta's śrāddha).
- **Mahābhārata, Aśvamedhika Parva** (the Pāṇḍavas recover Marutta's buried Himālayan gold) and **Anuśāsana Parva** / *ṣoḍaśa-rājakīya* (Marutta Āvīkṣita among the sixteen great kings; Saṃvarta officiates when Bṛhaspati refuses) — the epic's parallel Marutta tradition.
