# Pingala-Gita — The Courtesan of Mithilā's Song of Detachment

> *Up to [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · master [index](../README.md). The "Song of Piṅgalā" — a short but famous Mokṣa-dharma parable (≈ Mbh XII.168) in which a Mithilā courtesan, disappointed in a night's waiting, awakens to renunciation; promoted here from its one-line mention in the [lesser-Gītās catalog](./lesser-gitas-catalog.md), and a key cross-node to the [Bhāgavata's Uddhava-Gītā](./uddhava-gita.md) and the [Avadhūta-Gītā](./avadhuta-gita.md).*

The **Piṅgalā-Gītā** ("Song of Piṅgalā") is one of the smallest and most quoted of the [Mahābhārata's lesser Gītās](./lesser-gitas-catalog.md) — a single tight parable inside the [Mokṣa-dharma dialogues](./mokshadharma-dialogues.md) of the [Śānti Parva](./mahabharata/12-shanti-parva.md). A **veśyā (courtesan / dancing-girl) of Mithilā named Piṅgalā**, after a whole night spent restlessly waiting for a wealthy lover who never comes, abruptly perceives the emptiness of craving and, the instant she **abandons hope (āśā)**, falls into a deep peace and turns to renunciation. Its governing maxim — *āśā hi paramaṃ duḥkhaṃ, nairāśyaṃ paramaṃ sukham*, "hope is the supreme misery, hopelessness the supreme bliss" — is among the most repeated single lines of epic *vairāgya* literature, and the parable recurs almost verbatim centuries later in the [Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/bhagavata-purana-skandha-map.md) as one of the **Avadhūta's twenty-four gurus**. [folk] [widely quoted]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural time** — In the epic's frame the parable is recited by **Bhīṣma to Yudhiṣṭhira** from the bed of arrows, in the closing [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) of our 28th Mahāyuga ([7th, Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)), in the war's immediate aftermath on the eve of [Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) (3102 BCE). Piṅgalā herself is an undated exemplary figure quoted *within* that teaching as an *itihāsa* (old story). [scriptural]
- **Manuscript / academic time** — Text-critically the Piṅgalā parable belongs to the **Mokṣa-dharma's late didactic stratum** (the Sāṃkhya-coloured liberation block, broadly dated ~200 BCE–400 CE), among the youngest narrative layers of the epic. Keep the scriptural Dvāpara setting distinct from this manuscript dating. [scholarly]

## Placement & frame

- **Where it sits** — The parable falls in the **Mokṣa-dharma-parvan** of the [Śānti Parva](./mahabharata/12-shanti-parva.md), conventionally around **chapter 168** of the Ganguli/Kumbhakonam vulgate (the chapter is also the conventional *start* of the Mokṣa-dharma block). It immediately follows or sits near the **Manki-** and **Śampāka-** type renunciation songs and shares their single theme: **release through the death of craving**. (MBh XII.168) → [Mokṣa-dharma dialogues](./mokshadharma-dialogues.md).
  - *Variant (chapter number):* sources cite the Piṅgalā section variously around **XII.168** (and nearby chapters in the 168–174 cluster); exact numbering shifts between the **vulgate** and the **Critical Edition (BORI)**, which renumbers the Mokṣa-dharma. Treat the chapter as **≈168, edition-dependent.** [disputed]
- **Genre** — A *gāthā* (versified saying) attributed to Piṅgalā and quoted by Bhīṣma as a proof-text — the "Piṅgalā-Gītā" is really these few *ślokas* she is said to have sung, not a long discourse. Like the Bodhya- and Ajagara-Gītās of the same block, its teacher is an unlikely one (here a courtesan), underscoring the Mokṣa-dharma's recurring point that wisdom is found outside caste and status. (MBh XII.168) [scholarly]

## The parable

- **Piṅgalā the courtesan of Mithilā** — A *veśyā* (courtesan / *gaṇikā*) of **Mithilā** — the Videha capital of the Janaka kings, the same city that frames the [Vyādha-](./lesser-gitas-catalog.md) and Yājñavalkya-Gītās — has made an assignation with a wealthy man and waits at her door, dressed and adorned, for him to come and pay her. (MBh XII.168)
- **The night of futile waiting** — Hour after hour she paces between door and threshold, straining at every sound, hoping each passer-by is her awaited patron; the night wears on and **he never comes**. Her restlessness, expectation, and disappointment are drawn out as the very picture of a mind enslaved to *āśā* (hope/craving). (MBh XII.168)
- **The sudden turn (vairāgya)** — Exhausted and crushed, Piṅgalā at last grows **disgusted (nirviṇṇā)** — not merely with the lover but with the whole machinery of desire that had bound her. In that instant of total disappointment her perspective inverts: she sees that the lover she truly neglected is the **Self / the Lord within**, ever-present, whom she abandoned for fickle paying men. (MBh XII.168)
- **Peace through abandoning hope** — The moment she **gives up hope (āśā)** she is freed of the fever it caused; a profound calm settles on her, and she lies down and sleeps in peace — the parable's pivot: it is *hope itself*, not its frustration, that was the torment. She resolves on renunciation and a life turned inward. (MBh XII.168)

## The celebrated verses

- **"Hope is the supreme misery"** — Her signature line: ***āśā hi paramaṃ duḥkhaṃ, nairāśyaṃ paramaṃ sukham*** — "Hope is the supreme misery; hopelessness (freedom from expectation) is the supreme bliss." It is quoted across later *vairāgya* and Advaita literature as the compact formula of the whole parable. (MBh XII.168; echoed Bhāgavata 11.8.44) [widely quoted]
- **Cutting off expectation** — A paired sentiment runs that having **"cut off hope and expectation" (āśāṃ chittvā... nirāśā)** she could sleep happily — *suṣupāpa* in peace — whereas while hoping she had no rest. The contrast of the sleepless hopeful night versus the restful hopeless dawn is the parable's rhetorical engine. (MBh XII.168) [folk]
- **The neglected true Beloved** — Several retellings give her the realization that she had ignored **Janārdana / the inner Lord**, "so near and so dear," to chase men who give only sorrow — a devotional reading that the Bhāgavata version makes explicit (below). [regional] [folk]
  - *Variant (emphasis):* the Mokṣa-dharma version foregrounds **Sāṃkhya-style detachment** (craving as bondage, its cessation as freedom); the Bhāgavata version foregrounds **bhakti** (the Lord as the only worthy lover). The core verse on hope is shared; the theistic framing is the Purāṇa's overlay. [scholarly]

## Cross-textual node — Piṅgalā among the Avadhūta's twenty-four gurus

- **The Bhāgavata retelling (11.8)** — The same Piṅgalā appears in the **[Uddhava-Gītā](./uddhava-gita.md)** of the [Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/bhagavata-purana-skandha-map.md), inside the famous discourse where the young **avadhūta** brāhmaṇa tells **King Yadu** of the **twenty-four gurus** he learned from in nature and life. Piṅgalā the courtesan of Videha (Mithilā) is counted among them — the teacher of the lesson of **hope and its abandonment**. (Bhāgavata 11.8.22–44) → [Uddhava-Gītā](./uddhava-gita.md); [Avadhūta-Gītā](./avadhuta-gita.md) [the avadhūta archetype].
- **What she teaches the avadhūta** — In the Bhāgavata she explicitly concludes that her restlessness ceased only when **hope died**, and that one should give up all longing and rest in the Self / Hari, the lover ever within — the Purāṇa quoting essentially the Mahābhārata's *āśā hi paramaṃ duḥkham* maxim (Bhāgavata 11.8.44) almost word-for-word. This makes Piṅgalā one of the clearest cases of a **Mokṣa-dharma parable migrating intact into Purāṇic bhakti.** (Bhāgavata 11.8) [scholarly]
- **Sister parables in the same cluster** — The Avadhūta's twenty-four-guru sermon structurally mirrors the Mahābhārata's own **"many-gurus" teachings** — the **Bodhya-Gītā** (Yayāti) and the **Ajagara/python-vow** discourse of the same Mokṣa-dharma block — all teaching detachment by example from creatures and persons of low estate. The [Dattātreya](./dattatreya.md) tradition fuses these into the avadhūta's curriculum. See [lesser-Gītās catalog → Bodhya-gītā](./lesser-gitas-catalog.md). [scholarly]
- **Note — two distinct Piṅgalās** — This courtesan is **not** the same as the *nāḍī* **Piṅgalā** (the right-side solar channel paired with Iḍā in yoga physiology), nor the serpent-king Piṅgala; the name simply recurs. The Gītā's Piṅgalā is solely this Mithilā veśyā. [disputed — name collision]

## Reception & place among the Gītās

- **A "lesser Gītā" of the renunciation cluster** — Piṅgalā-Gītā is grouped with the **Manki-, Śampāka-, Hārīta-, and Ajagara-Gītās** as the Mokṣa-dharma's short songs on the futility of craving; popular "21 Gītās" lists name it explicitly. It is never one of the formal "core eighteen" but is among the most *quoted* for its single maxim. (See [lesser-Gītās catalog](./lesser-gitas-catalog.md).) [folk]
- **A favourite of Advaita & bhakti teachers alike** — Because the parable works equally as a **jñāna** lesson (craving is bondage) and a **bhakti** lesson (the Lord is the only true beloved), it is cited across both streams — by Sāṃkhya-leaning expositors of the Mokṣa-dharma and, via the Bhāgavata, by Vaiṣṇava preachers. The image of the **sleepless hopeful versus the restful hopeless** is its enduring contribution. [scholarly]
