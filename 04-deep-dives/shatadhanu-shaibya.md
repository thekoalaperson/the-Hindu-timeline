# Śatadhanu and Śaibyā — the Peril of Conversing with Heretics

> *Up to [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · master [index](../README.md). The closing narrative of [Viṣṇu Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/vishnu-purana.md) Aṃśa III (chapter 18), told by Parāśara to Maitreya as the practical coda to the [Māyāmoha / Buddha-avatāra](./buddha-avatara.md) sequence — set in an unspecified royal past ("there was formerly a king…"), on no fixed rung of the yuga ladder.*

The tale of King **Śatadhanu** and his queen **Śaibyā** is the Viṣṇu Purāṇa's starkest orthodoxy parable: a single act of **polite conversation with a pāṣaṇḍa (heretic)**, committed while under a vow of fasting, wipes out a lifetime of impeccable Vaiṣṇava devotion and hurls a righteous king through six animal wombs — dog, jackal, wolf, vulture, crow, peacock — while his wife, who merely **turned away and looked at the sun**, is reborn a jātismarā princess who tracks him down in every birth, names his fault to his face, and talks him back up the ladder to a human body, a second wedding, and heaven. Book III has just explained *why* heretics exist at all (Viṣṇu himself emitted the deluder Māyāmoha to strip the Daityas of Vedic merit); Śatadhanu's story then shows what their mere company does to the faithful, and the Aṃśa closes on the rule it dramatizes: shun the discourse of the unbeliever, above all at seasons of devotion. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural time** — The Purāṇa gives the couple **no dynasty, no yuga, and no genealogy**: the story opens simply "there was formerly a king named Śatadhanu," and is told inside the outer **Parāśara → Maitreya** frame of the whole Purāṇa. Internal furniture — an established **Kāśī** kingship, the city of **Vaidiśā (Vidiśā)**, and above all the presence of organized Veda-rejecting mendicants — presupposes the post-Māyāmoha world in which heresies circulate among men; the Purāṇic frame treats such pāṣaṇḍas as a standing feature of later, degenerating time (cf. the Kali-age heresy prophecies gathered under the [Buddha-avatāra](./buddha-avatara.md) file). The corpus therefore shelves the tale itself as an undated, recurring-type narrative. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **Manuscript / academic time** — The Viṣṇu Purāṇa is among the oldest Purāṇas in extant form; scholarly datings range **c. 400–300 BCE (V. Smith) to c. 275–325 CE (Hazra) and c. 450 CE (Doniger)** — see the [Viṣṇu Purāṇa profile](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/vishnu-purana.md) for the full debate. Historians read III.17–18, with its naked (Jain-coded) and red-robed (Buddhist-coded) deluders and its quarantine rules against pāṣaṇḍa contact, as brahmanical **boundary-maintenance polemic** from the early centuries CE, when Buddhist and Jain communities competed directly with Vaidika householder religion for patronage. [scholarly]

## The frame — Māyāmoha and the "naked" (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.17–18)

- **Why heretics exist** — Chapters III.17–18 open with the devas, beaten by Daityas whose Vedic observance makes them invulnerable, appealing to Viṣṇu; from his body he emits **Māyāmoha**, the "great delusion," who approaches the Daityas on the Narmadā as a **naked, shaven mendicant carrying peacock feathers** and seduces them into the **Ārhata (Jain)** doctrine, then, **in red garments**, into the **Bauddha** — until, stripped of the Vedas, they can be slain. Full treatment (with the Bhāgavata and other variants) at [Buddha-avatāra & Māyāmoha](./buddha-avatara.md). (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.17–18)
- **The definition of the "naked" (nagna)** — Parāśara then defines the target class: **"those who have seceded from their original belief are said to be naked, because they have thrown off the garment of the Vedas"** — the triple Veda being the covering of all beings; the man of the three varṇas who abandons its duties stands nagna whatever he wears. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18, Wilson tr.)
- **The contamination rules** — The chapter legislates the danger by degrees: a man who neglects his obligatory rites for a **twelvemonth** becomes a sinner; **conversation, exchange of civilities, or association** with such a one, or with an unbeliever, transmits **equality of guilt**; **speaking to them destroys the religious merit of a whole day**; and a **śrāddha looked upon by apostates pleases neither gods nor pitṛs**, however zealously performed — the ancestral stakes of pāṣaṇḍa contact (cf. [śrāddha and pitṛ rites](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md)). (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **The expiation of the gaze** — Even *seeing* the wrong person at the wrong time requires remedy: the virtuous who have looked upon such a man must **stop and gaze upon the sun** to cleanse the eye — the very reflex the story then puts into Śaibyā's hands (on Sūrya as purifier see [Sūrya and his family](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/surya-and-his-family.md)). (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **The connective question** — On this scaffolding Parāśara mounts the story: since even a glance calls for expiation, what does *speech* cost a man under a vow? "Hear what happened of old to Śatadhanu." (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)

## The lapse at the Bhāgīrathī

- **The exemplary couple** — King **Śatadhanu** ("hundred-bowed") and his wife **Śaibyā**, "a woman of great virtue," endowed with truth, humility, and discretion, daily worship **Janārdana (Viṣṇu)** with meditation, fire-oblations, prayers, gifts, and fasts — the Purāṇa's picture of the perfect Vaiṣṇava royal household. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
  - *Note (the name):* **Śaibyā** ("woman of the Śibi line," cf. the [Śibi](./shibi.md) dynasty of self-sacrifice fame) is borne by several distinct Purāṇic queens — most famously [Hariścandra's](./hariscandra.md) wife; the Viṣṇu Purāṇa's Śaibyā is a separate figure. [scholarly]
- **The Kārttika full-moon fast** — On the **full moon of Kārttika** — the high point of the Vaiṣṇava observance calendar (see [Kārttika māhātmyas](./masa-mahatmyas-kartika-magha-vaishakha.md) and [festivals & vratas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md)) — the couple, having fasted, bathe together in the **Bhāgīrathī** ([Gaṅgā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganga.md)). (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **The heretic on the bank** — As they come up from the water a **pāṣaṇḍa** approaches — not a stranger, but **"the friend of the Rājā's military preceptor"** (his teacher in the science of arms). Caught between the etiquette owed his guru's circle and the discipline of his vow, **the king chooses courtesy: out of respect for his preceptor he enters into conversation with the heretic**. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **Śaibyā's silence** — The queen, "reflecting that she was observing a fast," **turns away from the man and casts her eyes up to the sun** — performing on the spot the exact expiation the chapter has just prescribed for the sight of the fallen. One glance managed by the rulebook, one word breached against it: the whole story hangs on this asymmetry. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **The sentence** — The couple return home and worship Viṣṇu as ever; nothing else in their lives changes. But "in consequence of the fault committed by Śatadhanu, by speaking to an infidel when he was engaged in a solemn fast, **he was born again as a dog**" — the single conversation, not weighed against but *cancelling* the day's merit. Śaibyā, for the same encounter rightly handled, is reborn as **daughter of the Rājā of [Kāśī](../06-sacred-geography/kashi-varanasi.md)** (on the dynasty see the [Kāśī line](../03-lineages-vamsha/kashi-line-divodasa.md)), a **jātismarā** — carrying full knowledge of her former life — accomplished in every science and virtue. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)

## The wheel of births — dog, jackal, wolf, vulture, crow, peacock

- **1. The dog of Vaidiśā** — The princess, knowing by her unbroken memory that her husband wears a dog's body, **refuses every offer of marriage**; travelling to the city of **Vaidiśā (Vidiśā)** she recognizes her former lord in a dog, and — startling image — **places the bridal garland on his neck with the marriage rites and prayers**, and feeds him delicacies. When the dog fawns and wags "after the fashion of his species," she is stung with shame and names the fault aloud: *"Recall to memory, illustrious prince, the ill-timed politeness on account of which you have been born as a dog."* The king remembers, is crushed with humiliation, wanders broken-spirited from the city, and **falls dead in the desert**. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **2. The jackal of Kolāhala** — Reborn a **jackal**, he is found within the year by the princess, who has tracked him to **mount Kolāhala**: *"Dost thou not remember, O king, the circumstance of conversing with a heretic, which I called to thy recollection when thou wast a dog?"* Remembering, he refuses food and **dies**. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **3. The wolf** — Reborn a **wolf** in a lonely forest, he is again sought out; she wakes his memory — *"No wolf art thou, but the illustrious sovereign Śatadhanu"* — and on recollecting, he **abandons his life**. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **4. The vulture** — As a **vulture** he is found and admonished: *"Prince, recollect yourself: away with this uncouth form, to which the sin of conversing with a heretic has condemned you!"* (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **5. The crow** — As a **crow**, the barb turns on kingship itself: *"Thou art now thyself the eater of tributary grain, to whom, in a prior existence, all the kings of the earth paid tribute."* Stung by the recollection, he casts off the body. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **6. The peacock** — He becomes a **peacock**, and here the pattern softens: the princess **takes the bird to herself**, pets it, and feeds it constantly with food agreeable to its kind — the exile now literally kept at his wife's side, one birth from redemption. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **The pattern** — Each release follows the same liturgy: recognition by the faithful wife → naming of the original sin → the king's own remembrance (*smṛti*) → voluntary abandonment of the animal body. The engine of ascent is not expiation by rite but **memory administered by another** — Śaibyā functions as her husband's living jātismara, doing for him across six wombs what he cannot do for himself (on the karmic mechanics see [karma, saṃsāra, mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md)). (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)

## Aśvamedha, svayaṃvara, and the ascent

- **The horse-sacrifice ablution** — The **king of Kāśī**, her father, institutes an **aśvamedha**; at the concluding **avabhṛtha ablutions** the princess **bathes her peacock and bathes herself**, and reminds Śatadhanu of the whole chain of his animal births. Recollecting, he **resigns his life** — his last animal death sanctified inside the greatest of Vedic rites. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **Reborn a man** — He is born again **as the son of a person of distinction** (an eminent family of kings, in Wilson's rendering); with her husband once more marriageable, the princess now asks her father to convene a **svayaṃvara**, "and again invested him with the character of her husband" — **choosing her former lord from among the assembled suitors**. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **The restored reign** — Wedded again, Śatadhanu rules (receiving, on his father-in-law's death, the sovereignty), **offers many sacrifices, gives gifts, begets sons, and subdues his enemies in war**; the interrupted royal biography resumes exactly where a heretic's greeting had derailed it. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **Death, pyre, heaven** — He dies **in battle, as became his warrior birth**; his queen "again followed him in death, and, conformably to sacred precepts, **once more mounted cheerfully his funeral pile**" — the "once more" implying she had done the same at his first death (on the anugamana/sahagamana institution and its history see [satī and the immolation rites](../05-concepts-and-dharma/sati-jauhar-and-the-immolation-rites.md)). Together they ascend **beyond the sphere of Indra** to regions "where all desires are for ever gratified," in ever-during and unequalled happiness. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
  - *Note:* the reward is a supreme **svarga**, not explicit mokṣa — the tale is a householder's parable of merit protected and recovered, not a liberation narrative. [scholarly]

## The moral codified

- **Parāśara's closing rule** — The Purāṇa draws the lesson explicitly and closes Aṃśa III with it: such is the fruit of **conversing with a heretic**; therefore let a man **carefully avoid the discourse or contact of an unbeliever, especially at seasons of devotion** and when engaged in acts of worship or vows; and having looked upon a neglecter of rites, let him **gaze upon the sun**. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **A gendered mirror** — The story pointedly grades outcomes by conduct, not status: the king who *spoke* falls to a dog; the queen who *silently expiated* rises to a princess with unbroken memory — and then becomes the instrument of his rescue. Traditional readers take Śaibyā as a model of **pativratā** fidelity *and* of ritual exactness; the two virtues are made inseparable here. (Viṣṇu Purāṇa III.18)
- **Placement is the argument** — Structurally, Aṃśa III is the Purāṇa's book of **right practice** (Vedic śākhās, varṇāśrama duty, saṃskāras, śrāddha); ending it with Māyāmoha (III.17–18) and Śatadhanu means the whole book closes on the boundary between orthodoxy and the **nāstika** systems — on which frame see [āstika–nāstika and the heterodox darśanas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/nastika-darshanas-and-the-astika-nastika-frame.md). [scholarly]
- **The other face of the same Purāṇa** — The corpus keeps the tension visible: the same Viṣṇu Purāṇa that here punishes a courteous word to a heretic elsewhere teaches, through [Ṛbhu and Nidāgha](./ribhu-nidagha-vishnu-purana.md), the non-difference of self and other. The tradition holds both — universalism in metaphysics, strict boundary-keeping in ritual practice — without harmonizing them. [scholarly]
- **Kindred cautionary tales** — Within this corpus the story sits beside other Purāṇic parables where one small act outweighs a biography: [Ajāmila](./ajamila.md) (a lifetime of sin cancelled by a single utterance of "Nārāyaṇa" — the exact inverse of Śatadhanu's single fatal utterance), and [Ambarīṣa](./ambarisha-durvasa.md), whose vrata-observance withstands even a sage's assault. [scholarly]
