# Bhāgavata-Māhātmya — Bhakti and Her Sons; Gokarṇa and the Preta Dhundhukārī

> Cosmic address: a devotional frame-narrative set squarely in the **[Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)** of the present Vaivasvata Manvantara — the "glorification of the Bhāgavata" recited as the **prologue to every Bhāgavata-saptāha** (seven-day recitation). Carried in the **[Padma Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/padma-purana.md)**, Uttara-khaṇḍa chs. 193–198 (chapter numbers vary by edition; cited below as *BhMāh* 1–6). [late text]
> Parent: [Deep Dives](README.md)

The **Śrīmad-Bhāgavata-Māhātmya** is the charter myth of Bhāgavata devotionalism in the Kali age. Its two braided narratives — the allegory of **Bhakti**, a young mother wandering with her two prematurely aged sons **Jñāna** and **Vairāgya**, revived by the Bhāgavata's recitation at Haridvāra; and the **Ātmadeva cycle**, in which the ghost (**preta**) **Dhundhukārī** is liberated as his cow-eared brother **Gokarṇa** reads the [Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/bhagavata-purana-skandha-map.md) for seven days while the ghost sits inside a **seven-knotted bamboo**, one knot bursting per day — together establish the doctrine that in Kali Yuga *kathā-śravaṇa* (hearing the Bhāgavata) outstrips yoga, tapas, and even Gayā-śrāddha. **Disambiguation:** the Gokarṇa here is a brāhmaṇa's cow-born son on the Tuṅgabhadrā, **not** the Karnataka Ātma-liṅga kṣetra ([Gokarṇa Mahābaleśvara](../06-sacred-geography/gokarna-mahabaleshwar-atma-linga.md)), and not the philanthropist merchant Gokarṇa of the [Varāha Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/varaha-purana.md)'s Dharma-saṃhitā — three unrelated bearers of one name.

---

## I. The Frame — Naimiṣāraṇya and Nārada's Despair at Kali

- **Śaunaka's question at Naimiṣa** — In [Naimiṣāraṇya](../06-sacred-geography/naimisharanya-chakra-tirtha.md), **Śaunaka** and the sages ask **Sūta** how beings sunk in the Kali age, weak in discernment, may be purified; Sūta answers that the remedy is the **Śrīmad-Bhāgavata**, and to prove it narrates what befell [Nārada](narada.md). (*Padma Purāṇa*, Uttara-khaṇḍa, *BhMāh* 1)
- **The Four Kumāras meet a dejected Nārada** — The **[Four Kumāras](the-four-kumaras.md)** (Sanaka, Sanandana, Sanātana, Sanatkumāra), arriving at Viśālā (Badarī) for satsaṅga, find the normally blissful Nārada downcast; he reports having wandered the whole earth — Puṣkara, Prayāga, Kāśī, the Godāvarī, Haridvāra, Kurukṣetra and more — and found **Kali's dominion everywhere**: truth, tapas, purity and charity withered. (*BhMāh* 1)
- **The woman on the Yamunā bank** — On the bank of the **Yamunā** at [Vṛndāvana](../06-sacred-geography/mathura-vrindavan-braj.md), Nārada sees a marvel: a beautiful **young woman** sitting despondent beside **two old men lying unconscious**, breathing heavily, while celestial women attend and console them. (*BhMāh* 1)
- **She is Bhakti; her sons are Jñāna and Vairāgya** — The woman names herself **Bhakti** (Devotion); the two aged sons are **Jñāna** (Knowledge) and **Vairāgya** (Dispassion), grown decrepit before her eyes through the neglect of the Kali age, while she herself stays inexplicably young. (*BhMāh* 1)
- **Bhakti's autobiography — the south-to-north journey** — In the Māhātmya's most quoted verses Bhakti declares: *"I was born in Draviḍa, grew up in Karṇāṭaka, was honoured here and there in Mahārāṣṭra, and grew worn out (jīrṇa) in Gujarat"* — until, reaching **Vṛndāvana**, she was renewed to youth and beauty, though her sons remained aged. (*BhMāh* 1.48–50)
  - *Interpretation [scholarly]:* modern historians read this itinerary as a self-conscious allegory of the **historical bhakti movement** spreading from the Tamil south (the Āḻvārs) northward to Braj — the verse is the classic proof-text for the very idea of a pan-Indian "bhakti movement" (Hawley, *A Storm of Songs*, 2015).
- **Old means fail** — Nārada undertakes to revive the sons and recites for them the **Vedas, Vedānta and the Bhagavad-Gītā**; they stir drowsily but do not wake. An **ākāśavāṇī** (bodiless voice) announces that a specific *sat-karma* — a good work Nārada must discover — alone will rouse them. (*BhMāh* 1–2)

## II. The Prescription — the Saptāha at Haridvāra

- **The Kumāras name the remedy** — Nārada, searching, questions the Four Kumāras; they reveal the *sat-karma*: a **jñāna-yajña**, the recitation of the **Śrīmad-Bhāgavata**, which is the essence of Vedānta and the ripened fruit of the Veda-tree — to be heard over **seven days (saptāha)**. (*BhMāh* 2)
- **The first saptāha at Ānanda-ghāṭ, Haridvāra** — The Kumāras themselves conduct the recitation at the **Ānanda** tīrtha on the Gaṅgā at **[Haridvāra](../06-sacred-geography/haridwar-rishikesh-ganga-dwara.md)** ("Gaṅgā-dvāra"), with Nārada as chief listener and hosts of sages and devotees assembled. (*BhMāh* 2)
- **Bhakti revived, her sons made young** — As the kathā proceeds, **Bhakti appears in the assembly with Jñāna and Vairāgya restored to youth and vigour**, dancing and crying out "Where shall I dwell?"; the Kumāras install her **in the hearts of the Bhāgavatas** (the Lord's devotees), declaring that where Bhakti dwells the Lord himself resides — the text's core claim that in Kali, **bhakti sustains jñāna and vairāgya**, not the reverse. (*BhMāh* 2–3) — cf. the three paths in [the three mārgas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/the-three-margas.md).
- **The devas' bargain refused** — The gods arrive at the session bearing a pot of **amṛta**, offering to trade nectar for the nectar of the kathā; the offer is refused — the story-nectar (*kathā-rasa*) is not for sale to the faithless, being rarer than immortality itself. (*BhMāh* 3)
- **Nārada asks for a precedent** — Nārada asks the Kumāras for the rule (*vidhi*) of the saptāha and for proof of its power; as proof they narrate **the story of Gokarṇa**, "by hearing which even a preta was freed." (*BhMāh* 3–4)

## III. The Ātmadeva Cycle — Miser, Wife, and Two Sons

- **Ātmadeva and Dhundhulī** — In a town on the bank of the **Tuṅgabhadrā** lives the brāhmaṇa **Ātmadeva**, learned and wealthy, with his wife **Dhundhulī** — sharp-tongued, obstinate, and scheming. They are **childless**, and Ātmadeva's grief drives him to the forest resolved to die. (*BhMāh* 4)
- **The sannyāsī's fruit** — A renunciant reads Ātmadeva's fate: **no son is written for him**; he should renounce instead. Ātmadeva refuses; relenting, the sannyāsī gives him a single **fruit** — his wife is to eat it and will bear a son in a year, provided she keeps a year's discipline of truthfulness, purity and restraint. (*BhMāh* 4)
- **Dhundhulī's fraud** — Dhundhulī, dreading pregnancy, labour, and a spoiled figure, **does not eat the fruit**: she **feeds it to the household cow**, and conspires with her pregnant sister — the sister's newborn boy is secretly handed over and presented as Dhundhulī's own child. He is named **Dhundhukārī**. (*BhMāh* 4)
- **Gokarṇa born of the cow** — In time the **cow that ate the fruit gives birth to a human child**, perfect in form but with **the ears of a cow** — hence named **Gokarṇa** ("cow-ears"). Ātmadeva raises the two boys together; Gokarṇa grows learned and self-controlled, a born paṇḍita. (*BhMāh* 4)
- **Dhundhukārī's wickedness** — Dhundhukārī grows into a terror: violent, thieving, feeding on others' houses, consorting with harlots, tormenting his parents and squandering the family's wealth. (*BhMāh* 4)
- **Ātmadeva's renunciation — the Tenth Skandha** — Broken by grief, Ātmadeva is counselled by **Gokarṇa** in vairāgya — the body is not the self, the household a snare; the old man (the text makes him sixty) departs to the forest, where he **daily recites the Tenth Skandha of the Bhāgavata** (the Kṛṣṇa book) and attains the Lord. (*BhMāh* 4) — the first of the cycle's three graded liberations.
- **Dhundhulī's end** — Dhundhukārī beats his mother, demanding hidden money; Dhundhulī, tormented, **throws herself into a well** and dies. Gokarṇa leaves on pilgrimage. (*BhMāh* 4–5)

## IV. The Preta Dhundhukārī

- **Murdered by the harlots** — Dhundhukārī keeps **five prostitutes** in the house, stealing to satisfy their demands for ornaments and finery; fearing royal punishment for his thefts and coveting his goods, the women **strangle him with ropes** by night, and when he does not die quickly, **kill him with burning coals**, bury the body secretly, and tell the neighbours he has gone away. (*BhMāh* 5)
- **A preta of the whirlwind** — Dead by violence, laden with sin, and **denied funerary rites**, Dhundhukārī becomes a **preta** (restless ghost), a tormented wind-form (*vāyu-rūpa*), racked by hunger, thirst, heat and cold. (*BhMāh* 5) — on the preta state see [pitṛs and ancestors](../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md) and [śrāddha rites](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md).
- **Even Gayā-śrāddha fails** — Gokarṇa, hearing of the deaths on his travels, performs **śrāddha for his brother at [Gayā](../06-sacred-geography/gaya-pitru-tirtha.md) and at every tīrtha he visits** — yet the preta remains bound. (*BhMāh* 5)
- **The night apparition** — Returning to the old house, Gokarṇa sleeps in the courtyard; at night Dhundhukārī manifests in **rapidly shifting terrible shapes** — now a ram, now an elephant, now a buffalo, now Indra-like, now fire, now a man. Gokarṇa, fearless, demands who he is; sprinkled with water sanctified by prayer, the ghost gains speech and confesses his life and murder, begging rescue. (*BhMāh* 5)
- **Sūrya's counsel** — Amazed that Gayā-piṇḍa has not freed his brother, Gokarṇa seeks a higher answer and appeals to **Sūrya**, the witness of the worlds; the Sun-god answers briefly: **liberation will come through a seven-day hearing (saptāha-śravaṇa) of the Śrīmad-Bhāgavata**. (*BhMāh* 5)

## V. The Seven Knots of the Bamboo

- **The reading convened** — Gokarṇa convenes the recitation; people of the town and countryside throng to hear, and Gokarṇa takes the reader's seat with the Bhāgavata before him. (*BhMāh* 5)
- **The preta in the bamboo** — The bodiless Dhundhukārī, seeking a place to sit, spies a **bamboo cane of seven knots (sapta-granthi vaṃśa)** standing by the assembly and **enters its hollow**, settling there to listen. (*BhMāh* 5)
- **One knot bursts per day** — At the close of each day's reading, **one knot of the bamboo splits** with a crack; over seven days, skandha by skandha, all **seven knots burst** in sequence. (*BhMāh* 5)
  - *Interpretive gloss (devotional tradition):* the seven knots are read as the successive coverings/bonds of the subtle body (or the seven vices) severed day by day by the kathā; the pun on *vaṃśa* — "bamboo" and "lineage" — is savoured by expositors: the blocked family line itself is burst open. [oral tradition]
- **Dhundhukārī emerges divine** — At the end of the seventh day, as the Twelfth Skandha closes, Dhundhukārī bursts from the bamboo **transfigured**: dark-hued (*śyāma*), clad in yellow silk, wearing a **tulasī garland**, crown and earrings — the very form of Vaikuṇṭha's residents. He bows to Gokarṇa, proclaiming himself freed from the preta state by the Bhāgavata's grace. (*BhMāh* 5)
- **The vimāna and the pointed question** — A celestial **vimāna** with the Lord's attendants (*pārṣadas*) descends and bears Dhundhukārī up. Gokarṇa asks: many heard the same reading — **why is he alone taken?** The attendants answer: the difference lay not in the hearing but in the **manana** — Dhundhukārī, fasting, single-minded, a ghost with nothing left, heard with unwavering reflection, while the others heard with wandering minds; let them hear again with firm discipline and the fruit will be theirs. (*BhMāh* 5)
- **The second saptāha — the whole town to Goloka** — In the month of **Śrāvaṇa** Gokarṇa holds the saptāha again; this time the listeners hear as prescribed, and at its end **the Lord himself appears**, embraces Gokarṇa, and takes **the entire assembly — the whole town, of every station — in divine form to Goloka**, declaring that in Kali the kathā grants what sāṅkhya and yoga could not. (*BhMāh* 5)

## VI. The Rules of the Saptāha (BhMāh 6) — the Charter in Practice

- **The vidhi** — The closing chapter fixes the **saptāha-vidhi**: an astrologically chosen time (months such as Śrāvaṇa, Bhādrapada, Āśvina, Kārttika, Mārgaśīrṣa, Āṣāḍha commended), invitations sent to devotees far and wide, a decorated pavilion, a qualified **vaktā** (expounder) — detached, learned in Vedānta — honoured with worship, and disciplined listeners: continence, light diet, sleeping on the ground, no anger or greed, hearing from sunrise through the day for seven days, with worship of the book and a concluding rite. (*BhMāh* 6)
- **The living institution** — This chapter is the direct charter of the modern **Bhāgavata-saptāha**, one of the most widely performed ritual formats of Vaiṣṇava (and broadly Hindu) devotional life; the Māhātmya itself is customarily recited as its prologue, and is printed at the head of most modern Bhāgavata editions (e.g. Gita Press). — the saptāha economy of kathā-vācakas flourished especially from the 17th–18th c. onward. [scholarly]

## VII. Text, Variants, and the Two Clocks

- **Placement and parallel recension** — The Māhātmya stands in the **Uttara-khaṇḍa of the Padma Purāṇa** (chs. 193–198; numbering varies across the Bengal and printed Devanāgarī recensions). A **closely parallel Bhāgavata-Māhātmya circulates attributed to the [Skanda Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md)**; the two share the Bhakti allegory and saptāha charter with variant wording and are catalogued separately, never merged. [disputed]
  - *Variant (Skanda-attributed Māhātmya):* the Skanda version foregrounds rules of recitation and the greatness of the book itself; the Padma version carries the fuller Bhakti-and-sons narrative and the Gokarṇa–Dhundhukārī tale.
- **Scriptural clock** — Internally the narrative is **Kali-Yuga through and through**: Nārada's tour finds Kali enthroned; Bhakti's youth amid her sons' decay is explicitly Kali's paradox (echoing *Bhāgavata* 11.5.38–40 on Kali-age devotees arising in the Draviḍa country); the Ātmadeva cycle is told by the Kumāras as an exemplum within that same age. On the scriptural clock all of it falls after the yuga's onset (3102 BCE).
- **Academic clock [scholarly]** — The Māhātmya is a **late addition** to the Padma Purāṇa, absent from earlier strata; scholars date its composition to roughly the **16th–18th centuries CE** (John Stratton Hawley argues for the early 18th century in North India, in the Braj–Galta orbit), i.e. many centuries after the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* itself (academically c. 8th–10th c. CE). Its Bhakti-journey verse became the master narrative by which moderns imagined a single south-to-north "bhakti movement." [late text] [scholarly]
- **Theological function** — The text ranks **śravaṇa-bhakti above jñāna, yoga, and ritual expiation**: Vedas and Gītā fail to revive the sons where the Bhāgavata succeeds; Gayā-śrāddha fails to free the preta where the saptāha succeeds — the same Kali-age "easy means" logic as the nāma-doctrine of [Ajāmila](ajamila.md), with **kathā** in place of **nāma**. Its themes feed the saptāha cultures of the [Vallabha and Caitanya sampradāyas](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/nimbarka-vallabha-chaitanya.md) and of Bhāgavata performance generally — see the [Bhāgavata tales catalog](bhagavata-tales-catalog.md).
- **Name disambiguation (three Gokarṇas)** — (1) **Gokarṇa the cow-eared brāhmaṇa** of this Māhātmya (Tuṅgabhadrā country); (2) **Gokarṇa the kṣetra** in coastal Karnataka, site of the Rāvaṇa Ātma-liṅga legend ([Gokarṇa Mahābaleśvara](../06-sacred-geography/gokarna-mahabaleshwar-atma-linga.md)); (3) **Gokarṇa the merchant** of the [Varāha Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/varaha-purana.md)'s Dharma-saṃhitā. The traditions are unrelated; only the name is shared.
