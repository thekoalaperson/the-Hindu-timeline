# Sāvitrī & Satyavān — Winning a Husband Back from Death

> Cosmic address: an *upākhyāna* (interpolated tale) narrated within the present Kali Yuga's recension of the **Mahābhārata**, set in an earlier age among the Madra and Shalwa kingdoms. A deep-dive episode-file. Up: [04-deep-dives](./) · Parent corpus: [../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md](../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)

The **Sāvitrī-Upākhyāna** ("the Tale of Sāvitrī"), also called the **Pativratā-māhātmya Parva** ("the Glory of the Devoted Wife"), is one of the most celebrated sub-tales of the Mahābhārata. It is told by the sage **Mārkaṇḍeya** to **Yudhiṣṭhira** during the Pāṇḍavas' forest exile, in answer to Yudhiṣṭhira's question of whether any woman ever equalled the suffering of **Draupadī** — Mārkaṇḍeya answers with Sāvitrī, who out-argued **Yama** himself. It occupies sections **291–297** (in the Kisari Mohan Ganguli numbering, CCXCI–CCXCVII; the BORI Critical Edition places it in the Āraṇyaka/Vana Parva). It is the locus classicus of the *pativratā* ideal and the scriptural charter of the living **Vaṭ-Sāvitrī** vow. Related death-dialogue: [./nachiketa-katha-upanishad.md](./nachiketa-katha-upanishad.md).

---

## Setup — birth and the choice of a doomed husband

- **Aśvapati's eighteen-year penance** - Aśvapati, the righteous but childless king of **Madra**, propitiates the goddess **Sāvitrī** (a form of Sāvitrī/Gāyatrī, consort-aspect of Brahmā) with eighteen years of austerity and a hundred-thousand-oblation offering of the Sāvitrī mantra (MBh Vana Parva 291).
- **The goddess's boon of a daughter** - Pleased, the goddess Sāvitrī appears and grants him not the sons he sought but a single radiant daughter; the child is named **Sāvitrī** after the goddess who gave her (MBh Vana Parva 291).
  - *Variant (regional vrata-kathās):* she is born to queen **Mālatī** / Mālavī; folk retellings stress that Aśvapati had a hundred wives yet remained sonless until the vow. [folk tradition]
- **No suitor dares ask** - Sāvitrī grows so brilliant and beautiful that, goddess-like, no prince ventures to seek her hand; her father, anxious, bids her go forth and **choose a husband herself** (svayaṃ-vara by personal quest) (MBh Vana Parva 291).
- **The choice of Satyavān** - Touring hermitages, Sāvitrī chooses **Satyavān**, son of **Dyumatsena**, the blind, dethroned king of the **Shalwa(s)**, now living as a forest ascetic. Satyavān is righteous, generous, handsome; nicknamed **Citrāśva** for his love of modelling horses in clay (MBh Vana Parva 292).
- **Nārada's death-prophecy** - The sage **Nārada**, present at Aśvapati's court when Sāvitrī announces her choice, declares Satyavān flawless but fatally flawed in one thing: he is destined to **die exactly one year from this day** (MBh Vana Parva 292).
- **Sāvitrī refuses to recant** - Counselled to choose another, Sāvitrī answers that a *pativratā* chooses her husband **once**; the heart having been given, she will not give it twice, whatever his lifespan. Nārada blesses the union and departs (MBh Vana Parva 292–293).
- **The marriage in the forest** - Aśvapati gives Sāvitrī to Satyavān in the hermitage; she sheds her royal ornaments, dons bark-cloth, and serves her blind father-in-law Dyumatsena and his wife as a devoted ascetic-wife (MBh Vana Parva 293).

## The vow and the foreknown day

- **Counting toward the day** - Sāvitrī tells no one of Nārada's prophecy but silently counts the year; as the fatal day nears she fixes it precisely in her mind (MBh Vana Parva 293).
- **The Triratra (three-night) vow** - Three days before the appointed death, Sāvitrī undertakes the severe **Triratra-vrata** — standing fast (*sthāna*), eating nothing, day and night — as an act of accumulated tapas to fortify her husband (MBh Vana Parva 293).
  - *Note:* the in-text vow is a **three-night standing fast**; the later popular observance attaches the merit instead to the banyan and the full-moon/new-moon day (see Vaṭ-Sāvitrī below). [scholarly]
- **Permission to follow Satyavān** - On the fatal morning Satyavān sets out to gather fruit and firewood; breaking her custom, Sāvitrī begs and obtains Dyumatsena's leave to accompany him into the forest (MBh Vana Parva 294).

## The death and the pursuit of Yama

- **Satyavān is stricken** - Splitting wood, Satyavān is suddenly seized by a splitting headache and weakness; he lies down with his head in Sāvitrī's lap (MBh Vana Parva 294).
- **Yama comes in person** - Sāvitrī sees a dread, red-clad, dark figure with noose in hand — **Yama himself**, who has come personally (not a mere messenger) precisely *because* of Satyavān's great virtue. He draws out a thumb-sized **puruṣa** (soul) bound by his noose, and Satyavān's body goes lifeless (MBh Vana Parva 294–295).
- **Sāvitrī follows Death southward** - Yama turns and walks **south** (the direction of the dead) carrying Satyavān's soul; Sāvitrī, undeterred, follows on foot (MBh Vana Parva 295).

---

## The dialectical duel — the five boons

Yama repeatedly orders Sāvitrī to turn back; instead she walks beside him, reciting *dharma* aphorisms so apt that Yama, pleased, offers her boons — each time with the fatal **exception clause: "ask anything except Satyavān's life."** Her wisdom and persistence are the engine of the tale.

- **Sāvitrī's aphorisms on the road** - She tells Yama that **walking seven steps together makes one a friend** (*saptapadī* friendship), and discourses on how communion with the virtuous (*satām*) is never barren, on dharma, truth, self-restraint, and the duty of the righteous to protect even the suppliant (MBh Vana Parva 296).
- **First boon — sight for Dyumatsena** - Charmed, Yama grants a boon; Sāvitrī asks that her blind father-in-law **Dyumatsena regain his eyesight** and vigour. Granted (MBh Vana Parva 296).
- **Second boon — Dyumatsena's kingdom** - After more discourse, a second boon: Sāvitrī asks that Dyumatsena **regain his lost Shalwa kingdom**. Granted (MBh Vana Parva 296).
- **Third boon — a hundred sons for Aśvapati** - For her own father, sonless save herself, Sāvitrī asks **a hundred sons** to continue his line. Granted (MBh Vana Parva 296).
  - *Note on the line:* her brothers-to-be are sometimes called the founders of the **Mālavas** in later glosses, mirroring her Madra ancestry. [late text]
- **Fourth boon — the trap: a hundred sons by Satyavān** - Yama, again excepting Satyavān's life, grants a fourth boon. Sāvitrī asks for **a hundred sons begotten of herself and Satyavān**. Yama, moved, grants it — and is thereby caught: a chaste *pativratā* can bear sons by **no man but her husband**, so the granted boon is impossible unless Satyavān lives (MBh Vana Parva 297).
- **Fifth boon / the release** - Pressing the contradiction, Sāvitrī forces Yama to honour his own word. Yama relents, **looses the noose**, and restores Satyavān to life, decreeing him a lifespan of **four hundred years** in which to beget the hundred sons (MBh Vana Parva 297).
  - *Variant counting (some retellings):* the episode is often summarized as **four boons** by folding the "sons by Satyavān" and "life restored" into one; the text's structure is better read as a graded series of grants culminating in the implicit fifth (his life). Catalog both framings. [scholarly]
- **Yama's parting praise** - Yama blesses Sāvitrī as the supreme *pativratā*, praises her dharma-knowledge, and departs (MBh Vana Parva 297).

---

## Aftermath — every boon comes true

- **Satyavān revives** - In the forest, Satyavān wakes as if from deep sleep, remembering only the dark figure and his pain; Sāvitrī, choosing the moment, leads him home before telling all (MBh Vana Parva 297).
- **Dyumatsena's sight restored** - Even before Sāvitrī returns, the blind king's eyesight is suddenly restored, fulfilling the first boon (MBh Vana Parva 297).
- **The kingdom recovered** - Messengers arrive announcing that Dyumatsena's **usurper has died** (slain by his own minister), and the people summon the now-sighted king back to his throne — the second boon fulfilled (MBh Vana Parva 297).
- **The two hundred sons** - In time Sāvitrī bears Satyavān **a hundred sons**, and her father Aśvapati likewise gains **a hundred sons** (the Mālavas) — third and fourth boons fulfilled (MBh Vana Parva 297).
- **Mārkaṇḍeya's moral** - Mārkaṇḍeya closes by assuring Yudhiṣṭhira that, as Sāvitrī raised her whole family — husband, father-in-law, father, and herself — from misfortune, so Draupadī will deliver the Pāṇḍavas; the tale is offered as proof of the saving power of the devoted, dharma-wise wife (MBh Vana Parva 297).

## The living tradition — Vaṭ-Sāvitrī / Vaṭ Pūrṇimā vrata

The episode is not merely literary; it underwrites a major women's vow still observed across India for the long life of one's husband.

- **The banyan (vaṭa) connection** - Folk tradition holds that Satyavān died and was revived beneath a **banyan (vaṭa) tree**, or that Sāvitrī sat by one during her vigil; the banyan, long-lived and ever-spreading, becomes the emblem of marital longevity. Women circumambulate the tree winding raw thread around its trunk (MBh tradition; regional vrata-kathā). [folk tradition]
- **Vaṭ Sāvitrī Amāvāsyā (Western/Northern India)** - In Maharashtra, Gujarat, and much of the north, married women observe the vow on the **Jyeṣṭha new-moon (amāvāsyā)**, fasting and worshipping the banyan for their husbands' welfare. [folk tradition]
- **Vaṭ Pūrṇimā (Maharashtra/Goa/South Gujarat)** - The same vow is kept by many on the **Jyeṣṭha full-moon (pūrṇimā)** instead, hence the name Vaṭ Pūrṇimā. [folk tradition]
- **Sāvitrī Brata / Savitri Amavasya (Odisha, Bihar, Jharkhand)** - Eastern observance on the Jyeṣṭha amāvāsyā, with the Sāvitrī-kathā recited; regarded as a foundational *strī*-vrata. [folk tradition]
- **"Satī-Sāvitrī" as ideal** - In popular and devotional usage Sāvitrī becomes the byword for the ideal wife (often paired with Satī, Sītā, and Damayantī); note this is a *cultural ideal* layered onto the epic narrative. [scholarly]
