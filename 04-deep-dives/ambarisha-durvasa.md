# Ambarīṣa & Durvāsā — the Sudarśana and the Ekādaśī Vow

> Cosmic address: a *Manvantara-frame tale* of the present **[7th — Vaivasvata — Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)**, set among the Ikṣvāku kings of the **[Sūryavaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)** and narrated by Śuka to Parīkṣit in the 9th Canto of the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* (SB 9.4–9.5). It is the tradition's definitive statement that the **bhakta outranks the curse**, and that even Viṣṇu declares Himself bound by His devotees.
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · See also: [Gajendra Mokṣa](gajendra-moksha.md) · [Ajāmila](ajamila.md) · [Viṣṇu / Daśāvatāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) · [karma / saṃsāra / mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md) · [Dattātreya](dattatreya.md) · [Śiva avatāras & forms](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-avatars-and-forms.md)

The episode of **King Ambarīṣa and the sage Durvāsā** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4–9.5*) is the great Vaiṣṇava set-piece on the **supremacy of devotion over both penance and curse**. A perfect king-devotee keeps a year-long Ekādaśī vow; a notoriously irascible brāhmaṇa-sage, contrived into wrath, hurls a fire-demon at him; the **Sudarśana-cakra** (Viṣṇu's discus) annihilates the demon and then hunts the sage across the three worlds. Brahmā and Śiva confess their helplessness; Viṣṇu Himself refuses to intervene, uttering the most-quoted self-description in the bhakti corpus — *"I am subject to My devotees"* (SB 9.4.63). Durvāsā is saved only when the **wronged king prays for his persecutor's life**.

---

## Why it sits in the Sūryavaṃśa / Manvantara frame

- **Narrative placement (SB 9.4)** - Śuka recounts it while cataloging the Solar-dynasty kings descended from Vaivasvata Manu. It is inset into the dynastic survey as the supreme illustration of *bhāgavata-dharma* lived by a reigning monarch (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.1–15*).
- **Which Ambarīṣa** - This Ambarīṣa is the son of **Nābhāga** (himself son of Nabhaga, grandson of Vaivasvata Manu), an Ikṣvāku-line emperor of the seven continents (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.13*).
  - *Variant / disambiguation (Purāṇic genealogies):* A **different** Ambarīṣa is recorded as a son of **Māndhātṛ** (with Purukutsa and Mucukunda), further down the same Solar line. Some retellings conflate the two; the Bhāgavata's devotee-of-the-Sudarśana is specifically Nābhāga's son. [scholarly] — see [Sūryavaṃśa lineage](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **A devotee-protection cycle** - It belongs with the Bhāgavata's other "Viṣṇu rescues the surrendered" tales — [Gajendra Mokṣa](gajendra-moksha.md) (the helpless elephant) and [Ajāmila](ajamila.md) (the fallen brāhmaṇa) — but inverts the danger: here the *threat itself is a brāhmaṇa-sage*, and the lesson is that the Lord's protective grace overrides even legitimate brahminical power.

---

## Ambarīṣa the king-devotee (SB 9.4.15–25)

- **The ideal sovereign** - Ambarīṣa ruled the entire earth and possessed inexhaustible wealth, yet regarded it all as a "dream" (*svapnaupamya*), worthless beside devotion to Viṣṇu (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.15–16*).
- **Engaging every sense in service** - The famous catalog of his *bhakti*: he fixed his **mind** on Kṛṣṇa's feet, his **words** on describing the Lord, his **hands** in cleaning Hari's temple, his **ears** on hearing of Acyuta, his **eyes** on the deity, his **body** on serving devotees, his **nose** on the tulasī offered to Him, his **tongue** on prasāda, his **legs** on pilgrimage, his **head** in obeisance, his **desires** on the Lord alone — the locus classicus of total sensory consecration (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.18–20*).
- **The Lord gifts the Sudarśana** - Pleased by this flawless devotion, Viṣṇu personally awarded Ambarīṣa His own **Sudarśana-cakra** as a perpetual guardian over the king, his realm, and his line — the detail on which the whole episode turns (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.21–22*).
- **The Dvādaśī-Ekādaśī vrata** - To worship Kṛṣṇa, Ambarīṣa and his queen undertook a **one-year vow of Ekādaśī and Dvādaśī** — fasting on the eleventh lunar day and breaking it (the *pāraṇa*) at the prescribed moment of the twelfth (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.23–25*).
- **The climactic Kārtika vrata** - The vow culminated in the month of **Kārtika** with a three-night fast, bathing in the Yamunā, worship at Madhuvana, and lavish gifts of cows and food to brāhmaṇas (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.25–28*).

---

## The Dvādaśī-pāraṇa dilemma (SB 9.4.28–40)

- **Durvāsā arrives as guest** - As the king prepared to break his fast at the correct Dvādaśī moment, the wandering sage **Durvāsā** (an *aṃśa*/portion of Śiva, legendarily short-tempered) arrived with his disciples. Ambarīṣa received him with full honor and invited him to dine (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.28–30*).
- **The fatal delay** - Durvāsā accepted, then went to the Yamunā to perform his noon ablutions and rites — and, absorbed in meditation, **lingered past the closing of the Dvādaśī window** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.31–32*).
  - *Variant (folk / commentarial):* Several retellings add that **Indra**, jealous of the king's spiritual stature, instigated the delay — prompting or exploiting Durvāsā's tardiness to wreck the vow. The core Bhāgavata text simply attributes it to Durvāsā's prolonged rites; the Indra-instigation is a popular embellishment. [folk tradition]
- **Two transgressions, one window** - Ambarīṣa faced a precise dharma-conflict: to break the fast *before the guest ate* violates the duty of **atithi** (hospitality); to *let the Dvādaśī pass unbroken* violates the **vrata** (one must complete the *pāraṇa* within the twelfth day) (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.33–36*).
- **The brāhmaṇas' ruling** - Consulting learned brāhmaṇas, Ambarīṣa adopted the canonical middle path: **drinking only water** (*āpaḥ / ācamana*), which the śāstra reckons as *both* fasting (no food taken) *and* breaking the fast (the rite is technically performed) — thus honoring the vow without formally dining ahead of his guest (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.37–40*).

## Durvāsā's wrath and the Kṛtyā demon (SB 9.4.45–52)

- **The sage returns enraged** - Durvāsā, returning to find the king had "eaten" (sipped water) before serving him, blazed with anger, trembling, and denounced Ambarīṣa as an arrogant upstart who insulted a guest (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.45–47*).
- **The fire-demon Kṛtyā** - Durvāsā tore a lock from his matted hair (*jaṭā*) and, charging it with a dreadful mantra, created a **blazing female fire-demon — a Kṛtyā** — like the flame of universal dissolution, who rushed at Ambarīṣa sword in hand (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.46–48*).
  - *Note on "Kṛtyā":* a *kṛtyā* is a conjured destructive entity / artificial demoness raised by abhicāra (black-magic) ritual; the Bhāgavata describes it as a roaring spirit of fire. [scripture]
- **The king stands unmoved** - Ambarīṣa did **not flee or retaliate**; serene and unafraid, he simply stood firm in remembrance of the Lord (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.49*).
- **The Sudarśana acts** - The **Sudarśana-cakra**, the Lord's own discus set to guard His devotee, instantly **incinerated the Kṛtyā** — then, blazing, turned upon its sender, **Durvāsā himself** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.50–52*).

## The pursuit across the three worlds (SB 9.4.53–67)

- **Durvāsā flees** - Terrified, the sage ran in every direction with the discus at his heels, "like a snake pursued by fire." He fled across the earth, into the sky, into the cardinal directions and the seas (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.53–55*).
- **Refuge denied — Brahmā** - Reaching **Brahmaloka**, he begged Brahmā for shelter. Brahmā confessed he was powerless: at the end of his own life the entire cosmos he governs is annihilated by the Lord's will; he cannot withstand Viṣṇu's *cakra* (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.56–58*).
- **Refuge denied — Śiva** - Durvāsā fled to **Kailāsa** and **Śiva**, who likewise declined: he and the lesser gods cannot oppose the Sudarśana, the weapon of the Supreme; he too is ultimately dependent on Viṣṇu (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.59–61*). [Note the irony — Durvāsā is himself traditionally a portion of Śiva, yet Śiva cannot save him.]
- **Refuge sought — Viṣṇu at Vaikuṇṭha** - At last Durvāsā cast himself before **Viṣṇu in Vaikuṇṭha**, clasping His feet and pleading for protection from the burning wheel (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.62*).

### Viṣṇu's declaration — the heart of the episode (SB 9.4.63–68)

- **"I am subject to My devotees"** - Viṣṇu replies that He is **not free / not independent** with respect to His pure devotees; their hearts have captured Him, and He has given His heart to them — so He cannot act against them. The famous verse:
  > *ahaṃ bhakta-parādhīno hy asvatantra iva dvija | sādhubhir grasta-hṛdayo bhaktair bhakta-jana-priyaḥ*
  > "I am completely under the control of My devotees, O brāhmaṇa; I am, as it were, not independent. Because My devotees are wholly devoted to Me, My heart is captured by them; I am dear to My devotees, and they are dear to Me." (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.63*)
- **The devotee values nothing above the Lord** - Viṣṇu continues that His devotees, having Him, desire nothing else — not the four goals, not the eight siddhis, not even liberation (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.64–67*).
- **"Go to him whom you wronged"** - The Lord states plainly that **He cannot recall the discus**; the only refuge for the offender of a great soul is the **forgiveness of the offended saint himself**. He instructs Durvāsā to return to earth and beg the pardon of Ambarīṣa — "approach him; that will bring you peace" (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.4.68*).

## The pardon and the lesson (SB 9.5)

- **A full year of pursuit** - The chase had consumed an entire year. Throughout it, **Ambarīṣa never broke his fast** — he refused all food and took only water, waiting in patient remembrance of the Lord for the sage's return, blaming himself for the brāhmaṇa's plight (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.5 / 9.4*).
- **Durvāsā falls at the king's feet** - Returning, the proud sage **clasped the feet of Ambarīṣa** — an extraordinary reversal of brāhmaṇa-over-kṣatriya rank. Embarrassed, the king tried to withdraw his feet (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.5.1–2*).
- **The Sudarśana-stuti** - Ambarīṣa, ever the devotee, **prayed to the Sudarśana-cakra itself** — a celebrated hymn praising the discus as fire, sun, moon, and the upholder of dharma — beseeching it to be **merciful and spare the brāhmaṇa** for the welfare of all beings (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.5.3–11*).
- **The discus relents** - Pacified by the prayer of the very devotee it was protecting, the **Sudarśana withdrew**, its fire cooled; Durvāsā was freed and his burning ceased (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.5.12–13*).
- **The feast and reconciliation** - Ambarīṣa then **fed and honored Durvāsā** with great reverence, and only after the sage had eaten did the king himself break his year-long fast. Durvāsā, humbled, praised the king's matchless devotion and forbearance (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.5.14–20*).
- **Durvāsā's confession** - The sage declared that nothing is impossible for those who have taken shelter of Hari, and that **a Vaiṣṇava's tolerance and the Lord's protection of His own are without limit** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.5.16–20*).
- **Ambarīṣa's end** - Having demonstrated *bhāgavata-dharma* perfectly, Ambarīṣa in old age divided his kingdom among his sons, retired to the forest, fixed his mind on Vāsudeva, and attained the Lord's abode (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.5.25–28*).

## Themes and significance

- **Bhakta over tapas, bhakta over curse** - The episode is cited across Vaiṣṇava schools as proof that **devotion outranks austerity**: Durvāsā's enormous penance and brahminical power are utterly overmatched by a king's pure *bhakti*. The curse-wielder becomes the supplicant. (Contrast the protective theme of [Gajendra Mokṣa](gajendra-moksha.md) and [Ajāmila](ajamila.md).)
- **The Lord's voluntary dependence** - SB 9.4.63 is the scriptural anchor of the doctrine of *bhakta-vātsalya* — that the omnipotent Lord makes Himself "controllable" by love, refusing even to override His own weapon against His devotee's wish.
- **The discus heeds the devotee, not the giver** - Strikingly, the Sudarśana stops not at Viṣṇu's command but at **Ambarīṣa's prayer** — the bhakta's word moves the very instrument of God.
- **Ekādaśī as a living vrata** - The story is recited as the charter-myth of strict **Ekādaśī observance** and of the precise timing of *pāraṇa* on Dvādaśī. [scripture]

## Durvāsā the wrathful sage — the curse-cycle figure in his own right

Though the Ambarīṣa episode is the great *humbling* of Durvāsā, the sage is across the corpus one of mythology's most consequential **curse-engines**: a single flash of his temper repeatedly sets a major narrative in motion. He is an **aṃśa (portion) of Śiva**, born of the Saptarṣi **Atri** and the supremely chaste **Anasūyā** — brother to **Soma/Candra** (the Brahmā-portion) and **Dattātreya** (the Viṣṇu-portion) — making him the irascible, Rudra-natured limb of the Trimūrti-triad born at Atri's hermitage (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 4.1; Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa*). See [Dattātreya & the Anasūyā cycle](dattatreya.md) and [Śiva avatāras & forms](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-avatars-and-forms.md).

- **Birth and nature** - Durvāsā is the **Śiva-aṃśa** son of Atri and Anasūyā; tradition glosses his very name (*dur-vāsas*, "ill-clad" / "hard to dwell with") as marking his ferocious, ungovernable temper, the Rudra-portion expressing itself as wrath. He wanders as a homeless ascetic, testing hosts and dispensing both boons and curses with terrifying suddenness (*Bhāgavata 4.1; Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, Anasūyā cycle*). [scripture] — see [Saptarṣi](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/saptarishi.md) on Atri.
- **A curse-engine across the corpus** - Unlike most sages, whose curses are episodic, Durvāsā's wrath is structurally load-bearing in *several* of the tradition's central stories — the slight, the curse, and the narrative consequence form a recurring template. The four below are the most consequential beyond the Ambarīṣa set-piece.

### Durvāsā's curse on Śakuntalā (Kālidāsa)

- **The slight and the curse** [Kālidāsa's invention] - Lost in daydreams of her absent husband **Duṣyanta**, **Śakuntalā** fails to rise and greet the visiting Durvāsā; enraged at the breach of *atithi*-honor, he curses that **the one she is thinking of will utterly forget her** (*Abhijñānaśākuntalam*, Act 4).
- **The mitigated curse and the ring** - Her friends plead, and Durvāsā softens the curse: the king's memory will return **the instant he sees the token (the signet ring)** he gave her — the device that drives the drama's entire second half, the lost ring, the fisherman, and the heavenly reunion (*Abhijñānaśākuntalam*, Act 4).
  - *Source-critical note:* the Durvāsā-curse is **absent from the Mahābhārata's Śakuntalā** (Ādi Parva), where Duṣyanta's "forgetting" is a deliberate public denial; the curse is **Kālidāsa's dramatic invention** to exonerate the king. [scholarly] — see [Śakuntalā & Bharata](shakuntala-bharata.md).

### Durvāsā's boon and the birth of Karṇa (Mahābhārata)

- **The boon to Kuntī** - As a young princess of Kuntibhoja, **Pṛthā/Kuntī** flawlessly serves the irascible Durvāsā for a full year; *pleased rather than angered*, he grants her a **mantra (the Durvāsā-boon / *atharvaśiras*)** by which she may summon any deity and beget a child sharing that god's nature (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva 1.104; Vana Parva ~305).
- **The unintended son** - Testing the mantra out of girlish curiosity, Kuntī invokes **Sūrya**, who — bound by the boon's own power — begets **Karṇa** in her and restores her virginity; the secret pre-marital birth seeds the entire tragedy of Karṇa's identity and the Kuru war (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva ~305–306).
  - *Note on inversion:* here Durvāsā's intervention is a **boon**, not a curse — yet, characteristically, it sets a catastrophe in motion. [scripture] — see [Karṇa](karna.md) and [Kuntī](characters/kunti.md).

### Durvāsā and Kṛṣṇa's mortal heel (Mausala-frame tradition)

- **The kheer test** - In a widely told tradition, Durvāsā visits Dvārakā and demands **kheer (pāyasa)**, then orders Kṛṣṇa to smear the boiling pudding over his own body; Kṛṣṇa obeys without flinching, but leaves the **soles of his feet** unanointed (out of reverence, not daring to touch the sage's offering to his feet, or simply overlooking them).
- **The curse / boon of invulnerability with a flaw** - Pleased, Durvāsā grants Kṛṣṇa that **his body will be invulnerable wherever the kheer touched** — leaving only the **unanointed sole/heel** as his single fatal point, foreshadowing his death by the hunter **Jara's** arrow (*Mausala Parva* tradition; later Purāṇic and folk glosses).
  - *Variant / classification:* this "Achilles-heel" episode is **not in the core Critical-Edition Mausala Parva**, where Kṛṣṇa's death follows from **Gāndhārī's curse** and the **sages' iron-club (*musala*) curse at Pindāraka**; the Durvāsā-kheer explanation is a **popular / later devotional accretion** harmonizing why the raised foot was his weak spot. [folk tradition] — see [Mausala Parva](mahabharata/16-mausala-parva.md).

### Durvāsā's garland and the Samudra-manthana (Bhāgavata / Viṣṇu Purāṇa)

- **The slighted garland** - Durvāsā gifts **Indra** a divine, Śrī-bearing garland; Indra carelessly drapes it on his elephant **Airāvata**, who tramples it. Insulted, the sage curses the **Devas to lose all *śrī*** (fortune, vigor, splendor) — the catastrophe that drives the gods, weakened and defeated by the asuras, to seek Viṣṇu's counsel and undertake the **churning of the ocean** for *amṛta* (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.5; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.9*).
  - *Variant (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.9):* the garland is the **Santānaka** flower-garland received from a Vidyādharī; the same trampling and curse follow — this is the *locus classicus* for the **Durvāsā frame** of the churning.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, Amṛta-manthana):* the **Durvāsā curse is absent**; the Devas simply resolve to churn for *amṛta* to escape death. [scholarly] — see [Samudra-manthana](samudra-manthana.md).

## Durvāsā: theme and pattern

- **The Rudra-portion as narrative catalyst** - Durvāsā's wrath is the mythology's recurring **plot ignition**: his curse or boon repeatedly converts a small social breach (an ungreeted guest, a year's service, a trampled garland) into a world-scale consequence (a forgotten queen, the birth of Karṇa, the fall of the gods, the death of Kṛṣṇa's clan). He is the Śiva-aṃśa precisely as *disruptive force*.
- **Boon and curse as one instrument** - Strikingly, the *same* irascible sage who curses Śakuntalā and the Devas also *blesses* Kuntī and (in the heel-tradition) Kṛṣṇa — and even those blessings carry a sting. His grace and his wrath are a single, dangerous power.
- **The one humbling** - Against this pattern, the **Ambarīṣa episode stands as the lone tale where Durvāsā is decisively overmatched** — not by a greater curse, but by a king's pure *bhakti* and the Sudarśana, ending with the curse-wielder clasping his victim's feet.
