# Aśokasundarī, Nahuṣa and the demon Huṇḍa

> Where this sits: a **Padma-Purāṇa-unique Śaiva romance-and-vendetta cycle** that recasts the Lunar-dynasty king **[Nahuṣa](nahusha.md)** — elsewhere the epic parable of pride (his *mortal → Indra → python* arc) — as the destined bridegroom of **Aśokasundarī**, the Kalpavṛkṣa-born daughter of **Śiva and Pārvatī**, and the fated slayer of the *daitya* **Huṇḍa**.
>
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Śaiva frame: [Sati, Dakṣa & Pārvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md) · [Gaṇeśa & Kārttikeya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md) · Lineage: [Sūrya- & Candra-vaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) · Text-map: [Padma Purāṇa khaṇḍa-map](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/padma-purana-khanda-map.md). **Two clocks:** as *itihāsa* the cycle belongs to the reign of Nahuṣa, fifth king of the Candravaṃśa, deep in the 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga (traditionally the **Tretā Yuga** of the present Mahāyuga); on the academic clock the narrative stratum is a **[scholarly] c. 8th–12th c. CE** Śaiva-Purāṇic composition (the Bhūmi-khaṇḍa is among the later-dated layers of the Padma).

## Textual home & the disambiguation you must keep straight

- **Source and location** — the whole cycle is told in the **Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa** (the story-rich second khaṇḍa of the Western/Devanāgarī six-khaṇḍa recension), across roughly **chapters 102–121**. It is unattested in the Mahābhārata, the Bhāgavata, or the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, all of which know a completely different Nahuṣa. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 102–121*)
- **Name-collision, resolved** — in the corpus's canonical Nahuṣa file the name **Aśokasundarī** surfaces only as a **variant one-liner**: the Bhāgavata/Viṣṇu-Purāṇa tradition calls Nahuṣa's queen **Virajā**, and merely *notes* that "the Padma Purāṇa makes his wife Aśokasundarī, a daughter created by Pārvatī." This file is the standalone home of that Padma cycle; the epic-Purāṇic Nahuṣa (Śacī, Agastya's curse, the *ajagara*) stays in **[nahusha.md](nahusha.md)**. The two Nahuṣas are the *same king*, differently framed — do not merge the wives or the plots. [scholarly]
- **What the recasting does** — the epic Nahuṣa is a Vaiṣṇava/kṣatriya-dharma cautionary tale; the Padma pulls the same king into a **Śaiva household drama**, giving Śiva–Pārvatī a *third* child alongside [Gaṇeśa and Kārttikeya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md) and making a Lunar-dynasty monarch her son-in-law. The Bhūmi-khaṇḍa's own catalog flags the cycle as **[regional]/[late text]** and keeps it apart from its king-cycles of Vena/Pṛthu, Nahuṣa and Yayāti. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa*)

## The birth of Aśokasundarī — the Kalpavṛkṣa daughter

- **Pārvatī's loneliness** — dwelling on Kailāsa, Pārvatī longs for a companion of her own to dispel her solitude while Śiva is absorbed in meditation. Śiva directs her to the **Kalpavṛkṣa** (Kalpataru), the wish-fulfilling tree — one of the treasures churned up at the [Samudra-manthana](samudra-manthana.md) and set in the divine gardens. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 102–103*)
- **The wish granted** — Pārvatī voices her desire before the tree and it produces a maiden of surpassing beauty, born of the goddess's wish rather than of the womb. Because she is born to remove Pārvatī's **śoka** (grief/sorrow), she is named **Aśoka-sundarī**, "the beautiful one who ends grief." (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 103*)
- **The prophecy of her marriage** — Pārvatī blesses her daughter with the boon that she will wed a king equal to Indra in glory: **Nahuṣa** of the Candravaṃśa, who will rule the three worlds. This prophecy is the mainspring of everything that follows — it is what makes the girl a prize, and what makes Nahuṣa a marked man. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 103–104*)
  - *Note (Śaiva genealogy):* by this account Aśokasundarī is reckoned a daughter of Śiva and Pārvatī and thus sister to Gaṇeśa and Skanda — a filiation found in this Śaiva stratum and in later regional/folk devotion, not in the Vaiṣṇava Purāṇas. [regional]

## Huṇḍa's abduction and the curse

- **The demon who covets her** — **Huṇḍa**, a mighty *daitya/dānava* and lord of the great fortress-city **Mahodaya**, learns of Aśokasundarī's beauty and of the prophecy that she will wed Nahuṣa. Determined to have her himself — and to defy the prophecy — he schemes to seize her. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 104–106*)
- **The abduction by illusion** — finding her sporting with her companions in a celestial garden, Huṇḍa carries Aśokasundarī off to Mahodaya by force and guile. When she refuses him, he resorts to **māyā**: conjuring an illusory hermitage and a false ascetic-woman to disarm and deceive her. Aśokasundarī sees through the deception. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 106–108*)
  - *Variant (mechanism of the trick):* the tellings vary in the exact contrivance — a fabricated āśrama, a disguised confidante, or a shape-shift — but agree that Huṇḍa's illusion fails and that the goddess-born maiden cannot be forced or fooled into consent. [late text]
- **Aśokasundarī's curse** — enraged and inviolate, she **curses Huṇḍa**: the very Nahuṣa she is destined to marry will be his death. The curse converts the prophecy into a doom, and it is the engine of Huṇḍa's subsequent, self-defeating attempt to kill the child before he can grow. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 108*)
- **Her return** — freed or escaped, Aśokasundarī withdraws to await her destined husband, in some tellings performing austerity, in others returning to the protection of Kailāsa until Nahuṣa comes of age. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 108–109*)

## The infant Nahuṣa — Vasiṣṭha's ward

- **Birth in the Lunar line** — Nahuṣa is born to King **Āyu** (son of [Purūravas](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md), grandson of Budha and [Ilā/Sudyumna](../03-lineages-vamsha/ila-sudyumna.md)) and his queen. The genealogical seat is the same as in the epics — **Soma → Budha → Purūravas → Āyu → Nahuṣa → [Yayāti](yayati.md)** — but the Padma threads the Śaiva prophecy through the cradle. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 109–110*)
- **Huṇḍa strikes at the child** — learning that his destined slayer has been born, Huṇḍa resolves to destroy Nahuṣa in infancy. He **abducts the newborn** from the palace (by a servant or by sorcery) and hands him to his kitchen with orders that the child be killed and cooked. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 110–112*)
- **The cook's compassion** — the cook (a maid, in some tellings) cannot bring himself to murder the infant. Instead he **secretly carries the baby to the hermitage of the sage [Vasiṣṭha](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md)** and lays him there, thwarting Huṇḍa's design without his knowledge. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 112–113*)
  - *Note (structural echo):* the "tyrant kills the prophesied slayer as an infant, a compassionate servant saves him, the child is fostered by a holy protector, the grown hero returns to fulfil the doom" is a widespread type-scene; the Padma fits Nahuṣa to it exactly, with Vasiṣṭha in the fosterer's role. [scholarly]
- **Raised by Vasiṣṭha and Arundhatī** — Vasiṣṭha and his wife **Arundhatī** raise Nahuṣa in the āśrama, and the sage educates him in the Vedas, dharma, and the science of arms, so that the boy grows to heroic maturity ignorant, at first, of his royal birth and the crime done to him. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 113–115*)

## The storming of Mahodaya — Huṇḍa slain

- **Nahuṣa learns his identity** — grown and accomplished, Nahuṣa is told (by Vasiṣṭha, or by the gods) of his lineage, of Huṇḍa's crime, and of Aśokasundarī who waits for him. He is furnished with **celestial weapons** for the coming battle. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 115–117*)
- **The assault on Mahodaya** — Nahuṣa marches on Huṇḍa's stronghold **Mahodaya**, breaks the *daitya* host, and in single combat **kills Huṇḍa** — fulfilling both Pārvatī's marriage-prophecy and Aśokasundarī's death-curse in one stroke. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 117–119*)
- **The wedding** — with Huṇḍa dead, Nahuṣa **weds Aśokasundarī** under the blessing of Śiva and Pārvatī, uniting the Candravaṃśa with the Śaiva household. In this cycle she — not the epics' Virajā — is the mother of his heir **[Yayāti](yayati.md)** (and, in some tellings, of a daughter and further sons). (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 119–120*)

## The sequel — Vihuṇḍa and the Kamodā episode

- **Vihuṇḍa's revenge** — the cycle appends a coda in which **Vihuṇḍa**, Huṇḍa's kinsman (brother), takes up the vendetta to avenge his brother's death; Nahuṣa **slays Vihuṇḍa** as well, closing the demon line. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa 120–121*) [late text]
- **The Kamodā episode** — the Bhūmi-khaṇḍa rounds the narration with an episode centred on **Kamodā**, a celestial maiden/apsarā drawn into Nahuṣa's later story; the corpus records the sub-episode as part of this cycle at the khaṇḍa level without the finer detail being stable across editions. (*Padma Purāṇa, Bhūmi-khaṇḍa*) [late text] [regional]
  - *Caution:* the Vihuṇḍa and Kamodā material is the least-standardized part of the cycle and is reported here at the **khaṇḍa/chapter-range level only**; specifics differ by recension, and no fixed verse-attribution should be read into them. [disputed]

## How the Padma cycle sits against the epic Nahuṣa

- **Same king, incompatible frames** — the epic Nahuṣa *rises* to Indra's throne and *falls* by pride, cursed by [Agastya](agastya.md) into a python and freed ages later by Yudhiṣṭhira (see [nahusha.md](nahusha.md)); the Padma Nahuṣa is a Śaiva son-in-law who *earns* his bride by heroism. The corpus **catalogs both and merges neither** — the Bhāgavata's queen is Virajā, the Padma's is Aśokasundarī. (*cf. Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.18; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.10*) [scholarly]
- **A Śaiva annexation of a Lunar king** — the cycle is best read as the Bhūmi-khaṇḍa's characteristic move: pulling famous figures into a Śaiva devotional orbit (here by manufacturing a Śiva–Pārvatī daughter and marrying her to a canonical king), consonant with the khaṇḍa's late compositional stratum and its taste for boon-and-curse romance. [scholarly]
- **Living devotion** — Aśokasundarī survives outside the text as an object of regional and popular devotion (and modern media retellings) as "the forgotten daughter of Śiva and Pārvatī," a status that rests almost entirely on this Padma cycle rather than the pan-Indian epic tradition. [folk]
