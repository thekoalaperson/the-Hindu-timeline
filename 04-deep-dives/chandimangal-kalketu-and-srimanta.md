# Caṇḍīmaṅgal — Kālketu-Phullarā and the Dhanapati–Khullanā–Śrīmanta Cycle

> Cosmic address: a **regional-Kali** node — the great Bengali *maṅgal-kāvya* of the goddess **Caṇḍī**, composed on the **historical clock** (16th c. CE, [scholarly]) but framed on the **cyclic clock** as heaven-cursed celestials sent to earth to establish the goddess's worship among mortals.
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Genre home: [Regional & Oral Epics](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md) · Sister cycles: [Manasā & the Behulā–Lakhindar legend](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/manasa.md) · [Dharma Ṭhākur & the Lausen cycle](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dharma-thakur.md) · The goddess herself: [Devī-Māhātmya & the Great Goddess](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)

The **Caṇḍīmaṅgal** (Caṇḍī-maṅgal-kāvya) is medieval Bengal's poem of how the goddess **Caṇḍī** — at once the Purāṇic Caṇḍikā and a forest-and-fortune goddess of hunters and traders — **forces open her own worship on earth** through two mortal arcs: the **Ākheṭika (hunter) khaṇḍa** of **Kālketu and Phullarā**, and the **Bāṇik (merchant) khaṇḍa** of **Dhanapati, his wife Khullanā and their son Śrīmanta**. Its masterwork is the *Caṇḍīmaṅgal* of **Mukundarāma Cakravartī "Kavikaṅkaṇa"** (c. 1590s [scholarly]), prized as much for its unheroic realism — famine, market-fraud, revenue oppression, co-wife jealousy — as for its theology of the goddess who will be worshipped.

---

## Two Clocks — Text, Setting, Frame

- **Historical clock (composition)** — the Caṇḍīmaṅgal tradition runs from roughly the **15th–18th centuries CE**: **Māṇik Datta** is remembered as the earliest poet of the cycle (Malda region; date uncertain) [disputed] [scholarly]; **Dvija Mādhava's** recension is internally dated to **Śaka 1501 = 1579 CE**; **Mukundarāma Kavikaṅkaṇa's** canonical version is placed c. **1590s** (late 16th c.), with later poets carrying the tradition into the 18th century. (Caṇḍīmaṅgal recensions) [scholarly] [regional]
- **Scriptural/cyclic clock (the story's own frame)** — the poem opens in **heaven**: celestial beings (**Nīlāmbara**; the dancer **Ratnamālā**) are **cursed to mortal birth** so that Caṇḍī's *pūjā* can be planted among humans, and both arcs end with the protagonists **returning to heaven** once the worship is established. The mortal action sits in an undated "present" of Bengali village, forest and sea-trade life — effectively the poet's own Kali-yuga world. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [regional]
- **The poet's own story** — Mukundarāma's famous autobiographical prologue tells how he **fled his village Dāmunyā** (Burdwan region) under the revenue oppression of the local official **Māmud Sarif**, and how, resting on the road, **the goddess appeared to him in a dream** and commissioned the poem; he found refuge with the chief **Bāṅkurā Rāy of Brahmanbhūm/Āraṛā** (Medinipur) and became tutor to his son **Raghunāth Rāy**. The prologue is a prime document of Mughal-era Bengali social history. (Mukundarāma, ātma-kāhinī prologue) [scholarly] [regional]
- **Who this Caṇḍī is** — the poem's goddess fuses the **Purāṇic Caṇḍī/Caṇḍikā-Ambikā** of the [Devī-Māhātmya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md), the **wife of Śiva** (Umā/Abhayā — see [Sati, Dakṣa & Pārvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md)), and — in the scholarly reading — an older **non-Brahmanical forest goddess of hunters** and **fortune-goddess of traders**, Sanskritized into the Devī. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The maṅgal logic** — like the [Manasāmaṅgal](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/manasa.md), the [Dharma-maṅgal](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dharma-thakur.md) and the [Śītalā-maṅgal](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shitala.md), the Caṇḍīmaṅgal is a **deity-glorifying narrative** whose plot *is* the propagation of the cult: the goddess rewards devotion, breaks resisters, and ends with her *pūjā* instituted on earth. A **Deva-khaṇḍa** (gods' section) retelling Śiva–Pārvatī myths (see [Śiva's myth-cycle](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md)) precedes the two mortal khaṇḍas in Mukundarāma's design. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [regional] [scholarly]

## Ākheṭika Khaṇḍa — Kālketu the Hunter and Phullarā

- **The curse from heaven** — Caṇḍī, resolved to win worship among mortals, contrives that **Nīlāmbara**, a celestial of Śiva's/Indra's court, incur a **curse to be born on earth**; he is born as **Kālketu**, son of the hunter **Dharmaketu** and his wife **Nidayā**, and his heavenly wife is born as **Phullarā**, whom he marries. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal, Ākheṭika khaṇḍa) [folk] [regional]
- **The hunter's life and Phullarā's poverty** — Kālketu grows into a giant of prodigious strength and appetite who lives by the bow; Phullarā **hawks the meat in the market**. Her **"Phullarār bāromāsī"** — the twelve-month recital of the hunter-wife's hunger, rags and toil through the seasons — is one of the most celebrated realistic passages in Bengali literature. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [regional]
- **The animals' petition** — the beasts of the forest, decimated by Kālketu's hunting, **appeal to Caṇḍī for protection**; the goddess hides the game, emptying the forest — the device that brings hunter and goddess face to face. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk]
- **The golden iguana (svarṇa-godhikā)** — finding the forest empty, Kālketu catches only a **golden monitor-lizard** — the goddess herself in disguise — ties it with his **bowstring** and carries it home. Left in the hut while he seeks Phullarā, the iguana becomes a **beautiful woman**. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk]
- **Phullarā's alarm and the theophany** — Phullarā, returning to find a bejewelled beauty in her hovel, fears a **co-wife** and pleads with her to leave (her lament and worldly-wise counsel is another famous set piece); when Kālketu too is bewildered, the woman reveals herself as **Caṇḍī in her four-armed, lion-borne form**, and hunter and wife fall at her feet. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [regional]
- **The ring and the treasure** — the goddess gives Kālketu a **priceless ring** and directs him to **hoards of treasure**, commanding him to give up slaughter, **clear the forest of Gujarāṭ**, found a city there, and **establish her worship**. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk]
- **Murāri Śīl, the cheating banker** — sent to turn the ring into cash, Kālketu meets the goldsmith-moneylender **Murāri Śīl**, who pretends the ring is near-worthless and tries to **fleece the simple hunter** — a sharply drawn satire of the bazaar; the goddess's favour defeats the fraud and Kālketu gets his capital. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [regional]
- **The founding of Gujarāṭ city** — Kālketu **fells the forest** (the goddess's power clearing what no axe could) and founds the city of **Gujarāṭ**, proclaiming **light rents and tax remissions** to draw settlers — a passage prized by historians as a mirror of frontier land-clearing and *ābādi* settlement in medieval Bengal. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [regional] [scholarly]
- **The storm over Kālinga** — to people the new city, Caṇḍī **devastates the kingdom of Kālinga with rainstorm and flood** — the poem's virtuoso storm-description — and the ruined peasants migrate to Kālketu's Gujarāṭ. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [regional]
- **Bhāṇḍu Datta, the crooked kāyastha** — among the settlers comes **Bhāṇḍu (Bhāṅḍu/Bhāṛu) Datta**, a glib kāyastha who talks his way into office under Kālketu, then **extorts and oppresses the market-folk**; exposed and disgraced, he turns traitor. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [regional]
- **The Kālinga invasion and Kālketu's capture** — Bhāṇḍu Datta **instigates the king of Kālinga** to attack the upstart city; Kālketu fights but is overwhelmed — in the poem's famously unheroic realism he is found **hiding (in the common telling, in a granary/paddy-store)**, captured and imprisoned by the Kālinga king. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [regional]
- **The dream-command and restoration** — **Caṇḍī appears to the Kālinga king in a dream**, revealing Kālketu as her chosen devotee; the terrified king **releases him with honour** and restores his city. Kālketu rules Gujarāṭ, institutes **Caṇḍī-pūjā** there, and at the end he and Phullarā **shed their mortal bodies and return to heaven** as Nīlāmbara and his wife. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk]
- *Parallel:* the hunter transformed by the goddess/god's grace echoes the Śaiva [hunter of the Śivarātri legend](shivaratri-hunter-legend.md) and the [Vyādha of the Vyādha-Gītā](vyadha-gita.md) — Bengal's maṅgal poets make the despised **vyādha** the founding king of the goddess's cult. [scholarly]

## Bāṇik Khaṇḍa — Dhanapati, Khullanā, and Śrīmanta's Voyage

- **Ratnamālā cursed** — in the frame, the heavenly dancer **Ratnamālā** suffers a curse for a lapse in Indra's court and is born as **Khullanā**, so that through her the goddess's worship may spread among the **merchant (bāṇik) community**. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal, Bāṇik khaṇḍa) [folk]
- **Dhanapati the Śaiva merchant** — **Dhanapati Sadāgar** of **Ujānī**, a wealthy ship-owning merchant and staunch **devotee of Śiva who scorns Caṇḍī**, already married to **Lahanā**, weds the young **Khullanā** as his second wife. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [regional]
- **The co-wife's persecution** — while Dhanapati is away on business, **Lahanā** — goaded by the scheming maidservant **Durbalā** — strips Khullanā of her comforts and sends her to **herd goats in the wilds** in rags. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [regional]
- **Khullanā wins the goddess** — in her herding exile Khullanā is taught **Caṇḍī's vrata/pūjā** and worships her; the goddess **protects her flock, ends her afflictions and restores her state**, and the returning Dhanapati finds the truth. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk]
- **The chastity ordeal** — Khullanā's long exposure outside the household makes her the target of slander, and she must pass a **public chastity ordeal** before the merchant community, which she survives **by Caṇḍī's grace** (the form of the test varies across recensions). (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [disputed]
- **The kicked ghaṭ** — summoned to sail for **Siṁhala (Ceylon)**, Dhanapati finds Khullanā worshipping the goddess's **ghaṭ (sacred pot)** and, in Śaiva contempt, **kicks it over / spurns the pūjā** — the offence for which Caṇḍī will break him at sea. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk]
- **The Kamale-Kāminī vision** — on the voyage, at the whirlpool-waters of **Kālīdaha**, Dhanapati beholds the **Kamale-Kāminī**: a woman of ravishing beauty seated on a **lotus in mid-ocean**, who **swallows a tusker elephant and disgorges it again** — the goddess's māyā shown to the man who denies her. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [regional]
- **Imprisonment in Ceylon** — at the Siṁhala court Dhanapati reports the marvel; the king sails out to see it, but Caṇḍī **withholds the vision**, and the merchant, branded a liar, is **thrown into prison** and his goods seized. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk]
- **Śrīmanta's birth and taunts** — back in Ujānī Khullanā bears **Śrīmanta**, conceived before the voyage; growing up fatherless, taunted at school over his paternity, he learns of Dhanapati's fate and — over his mother's terror — **fits out ships to seek his father**. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [regional]
- **The son sees the same vision** — at Kālīdaha Śrīmanta too beholds the **Kamale-Kāminī**; at the Siṁhala court he too tells of it, the vision is again withheld, and the young merchant is condemned and **led to the execution-ground (maśān)**. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk]
- **Caṇḍī's rescue at the execution-ground** — as the blade is readied Śrīmanta cries out to the goddess; **Caṇḍī appears** (in Mukundarāma's telling first in disguise as an old brahmin woman, then in her **terrible form**), her **host of spirits routs the king's men**, and the humbled **Siṁhala king surrenders and worships her**. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [regional]
- **Vindication, marriage, release** — the goddess now **grants the king the Kamale-Kāminī vision**, proving father and son truthful; the king **frees Dhanapati from prison**, restores the wealth, and gives Śrīmanta his daughter **Suśīlā** in marriage. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk]
- **The Śaiva reconciled to the Śākta** — Dhanapati, stubborn to the last, is reconciled when the goddess shows herself **one with / inseparable from Śiva** — the poem's charter for the harmony of Śiva-worship and Caṇḍī-worship in Bengal; the family returns to Ujānī, **Caṇḍī-pūjā is established among the merchants**, and Khullanā and Śrīmanta in time resume their heavenly forms. (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [folk] [scholarly]
- *Parallel:* the sea-merchant broken and restored by a slighted deity is Bengal-eastern India's great narrative mould — compare **Cānd Sadāgar** in the [Manasāmaṅgal](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/manasa.md) (and its epic hinge in [Jaratkāru & Manasā](jaratkaru-and-manasa.md)), the merchant Sādhu of the [Satyanārāyaṇa vrata-kathā](satyanarayana-vrata-katha.md), and the goddess-compelled reversals of the Odia [Lakṣmī Purāṇa](lakshmi-purana-shriya-chandaluni.md). [scholarly]

## Recensions and Poets

- **Māṇik Datta** — remembered in the tradition as the **earliest Caṇḍīmaṅgal poet** (Malda region); his date (possibly 15th c. or earlier) and the integrity of the text transmitted under his name are uncertain. (Caṇḍīmaṅgal tradition) [disputed] [scholarly]
- **Dvija Mādhava** — his recension (also known as **Sāradāmaṅgal / Sāradācarita**) carries an internal date of **Śaka 1501 = 1579 CE**, making it the key **pre-Mukundarāma** landmark of the cycle. (Dvija Mādhava, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [scholarly] [regional]
- **Mukundarāma Cakravartī "Kavikaṅkaṇa"** — the **canonical poet** of the genre (c. 1590s [scholarly]); his version, in **Deva-khaṇḍa + Ākheṭika khaṇḍa + Bāṇik khaṇḍa**, is celebrated for its social realism and earned him the honorific *Kavikaṅkaṇa* ("bracelet of poets"). (Mukundarāma, Caṇḍīmaṅgal) [regional]
- **Later poets and the courtly afterlife** — further recensions continued into the 18th century, and the genre's courtly transformation is **Bhāratcandra Rāy's Annadāmaṅgal (1752)** for Raja Krishnachandra of Nadia, where the goddess-as-Annadā inherits the maṅgal frame. (Annadāmaṅgal) [scholarly] [late text]
- **Performance** — like all maṅgal-kāvyas the Caṇḍīmaṅgal lived in **sung recitation** (professional singers performing the cycle over successive nights at pūjās and vratas), not only as a written text. [oral tradition] [regional]

## Why the Caṇḍīmaṅgal Matters

- **The goddess of the margins** — Caṇḍī chooses a **hunter** and a **merchant's persecuted co-wife** as the founders of her cult: the maṅgal genre's persistent theology that the great goddess enters history from below, among forest-folk and bazaar-folk, before the brahmanical establishment accepts her. [scholarly]
- **A mirror of Mughal-era Bengal** — famine-diet, meat-hawking, revenue farming, land-clearing with tax incentives, market fraud (Murāri Śīl), corrupt clerks (Bhāṇḍu Datta), co-wife households and the Indian-Ocean trade to Ceylon: scholars read Mukundarāma as a near-documentary witness to 16th-century Bengali society. [scholarly]
- **The Kamale-Kāminī** — the lotus-woman swallowing and disgorging the elephant became one of Bengal's iconic goddess-images, invoked in later literature and art as the emblem of the Devī's māyā over the sea-trader's world. [regional]
- **Śiva's household and the goddess's ambition** — framed by the Deva-khaṇḍa's Hara-Gaurī domesticity, both arcs turn resisting **Śaiva men** (the poem's Dhanapati; the Śiva-given order of the hunter's world) into Caṇḍī-worshippers — the literary face of the Śākta ascendancy that would culminate in Bengal's Durgā-pūjā culture. [scholarly] [disputed]
