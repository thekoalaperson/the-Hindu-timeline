# Famous tales of the Kathāsaritsāgara — Upakośā, Devasmitā, Lohajaṅgha, Śaktivega's City of Gold

> Where this sits: a **tale-by-tale reader** for the most anthologized *stand-alone* stories emboxed in Somadeva's **Kathāsaritsāgara** ("Ocean of the Streams of Story," c. 1070 CE **[scholarly]**) — the clever-wife tricks (Upakośā, Devasmitā), the revenge-farce (Lohajaṅgha), and the two Vidyādhara marvels (Śaktideva's golden city; Somaprabhā's automata). The **frame-legend, the 18-lambhaka map, and the Vetāla/Siṃhāsana cycles** live in the canonical home, [Bṛhatkathā, Kathāsaritsāgara & the Vikramāditya cycle](../90-literature-corpus/brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md); this file is the story-close companion to it.
>
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Text-home: [Kathāsaritsāgara](../90-literature-corpus/brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md) · Sister-cycle: [Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati](vetala-panchavimshati-vikram-and-betal.md) · Beings: [Vidyādharas, Siddhas & spirits](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md). **Two clocks:** on the cyclic clock every one of these tales sits inside ordinary human **Kali-Yuga** — the Nanda-era court of Pāṭaliputra (Upakośā), merchant Tāmraliptī (Devasmitā), Mathurā (Lohajaṅgha), and the Vatsa court of [Udayana](udayana-vasavadatta.md); the Vidyādhara marvels are timeless fairy matter reported into that frame. On the academic clock the *text* that fixes them is Somadeva's Kashmiri Sanskrit of **[scholarly] c. 1063–1081 CE**, itself condensing the lost Paiśācī *Bṛhatkathā*.

## Orientation: what this file catalogs, and how the tales are numbered

- **Scope** — the Kathāsaritsāgara hangs *hundreds* of emboxed tales on the spine of Naravāhanadatta's rise; this file gives close treatment to **five celebrated set-pieces** that circulate on their own in anthologies and world-literature courses. It does **not** re-tell the Guṇāḍhya frame-legend, the burning of the *Bṛhatkathā*, or the Vetāla riddles — those are the canonical home's job. (Kathāsaritsāgara, *passim*)
- **Numbering convention** — references here use **lambhaka (book) . taraṅga (wave/chapter)** in the continuous Brockhaus/Tawney–Penzer chapter-count: Book I *Kathāpīṭha* = taraṅgas 1–8, Book II *Kathāmukha* = taraṅgas 9–14, and so on. Editions differ by a chapter in the later books, catalogued as variants where it matters. **[scholarly]**
- **A structural echo worth naming** — two of these five are the same folk-type: a **chaste, clever wife turns four would-be seducers into branded fools** (Upakośā, I.4; Devasmitā, II.13). Somadeva sets them deliberately as paired exempla of *strī-buddhi* (women's wit); this file keeps them separate rather than merging the doublet. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.4; II.13)

## Upakośā and her four suitors — the chest of blackened dignitaries (I.4)

- **The setting** — **Upakośā** is the virtuous wife of **Vararuci (Kātyāyana)** — in the frame-legend the first mortal birth of Śiva's cursed *gaṇa* Puṣpadanta, and here a rising scholar at the [Nanda](../90-literature-corpus/brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md) court of Pāṭaliputra. While Vararuci is away (studying grammar / on the king's business), Upakośā keeps a vow of fasting and is left to manage the house. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.4)
- **The four solicitors** — seeing her bathe in the Ganges, or dealing with her over money, **four dignitaries** press her for a tryst: **the king's family priest (purohita)**, **the commander of the guard**, **the prince's tutor**, and the **banker Hiraṇyagupta**, who holds Vararuci's deposited wealth and demands her body as the price of returning it. She feigns consent to all four, appointing them the **same night at staggered hours**. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.4)
- **The trap** — as each arrives she has him strip for a "bath," has her maids smear him head to foot with a paste of **lamp-black (soot), oil and perfume**, and shuts him — naked and blackened — in a great dark chest or bathing-room as the next man knocks; by dawn all four are boxed in the dark, each ignorant of the others. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.4)
- **The public unmasking** — needing to recover the deposit the banker denies holding, Upakośā carries the chest to the **king's court**, declares that Vararuci's household wealth was "deposited with the banker and with these household gods," and opens it: the four grandees tumble out, soot-black and shamed, before the laughing king. Hiraṇyagupta is forced to restore the deposit; Upakośā's fidelity and wit are celebrated, and Vararuci returns to a vindicated wife. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.4)
  - *Note (why it is famous):* the Wikipedia editors and Penzer alike single this out as "one of the best-told stories in the whole work" — a tight farce of the *clever-chaste-wife* type that the Devasmitā tale (below) reworks with a branding twist. **[scholarly]**

## Lohajaṅgha as Viṣṇu — the revenge on the bawd of Mathurā (II.12)

- **The lover cast out** — **Lohajaṅgha** ("Iron-legs," so named for his tirelessness afoot) is a poor but proud Brāhmaṇa of **Mathurā**, the beloved paramour of the courtesan **Rūpiṇikā**. Her mother, the **procuress (kuṭṭinī)**, despises a penniless lover; she has Lohajaṅgha beaten and thrown out to make room for a paying merchant. (Kathāsaritsāgara II.12)
- **The dead elephant and the jewel island** — destitute and in famine, Lohajaṅgha sets out on pilgrimage; caught in the rains he crawls into the **hollow carcass of a dead elephant**. Floodwaters float the swollen carcass to the sea, where a **giant bird** seizes it, flies it to a far island, and tears it open to feed — Lohajaṅgha springs out sword in hand, scares the bird off, and finds the island strewn with **rubies**. A friendly **rākṣasa** he meets there, hearing his story, carries him by air back to Mathurā overnight and agrees to help his revenge. (Kathāsaritsāgara II.12)
- **The Viṣṇu masquerade** — knowing the greedy bawd is a fervent **Viṣṇu-devotee**, Lohajaṅgha disguises himself as four-armed **Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa** — conch, discus, mace and lotus — mounted on the rākṣasa serving as his **Garuḍa**, and descends to her by night. He announces that Hari, pleased with her devotion, will bear her bodily to heaven as a new Lakṣmī. (Kathāsaritsāgara II.12)
- **The disgrace** — the rapturous bawd is carried aloft and left stranded, at daybreak, atop a temple spire (in tellings, with her **nose and ears cut off** by the "discus"), a public spectacle for the townsfolk. Lohajaṅgha then reveals himself, reclaims **Rūpiṇikā**, and lives with her in wealth and comfort — the humiliated mother permanently disarmed. (Kathāsaritsāgara II.12)
  - *Variant (mechanics):* tellings differ on whether the aerial ride is worked by the rākṣasa, by a captured real bird, or by a fabricated mechanical Garuḍa, and on the exact mutilation — but agree on the impersonation of Viṣṇu and the bawd's exposure. **[late text]**

## Devasmitā and the red lotus of fidelity (II.13)

- **The talisman lotuses** — **Devasmitā**, wife of the merchant's son **Guhasena** of **Tāmraliptī**, worships [Śiva](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) with her husband before his trading voyage; the god (in a dream) gives each a **red lotus that will wilt only if its holder is unfaithful** — a mutual chastity-token. Guhasena sails to the far port of **Kaṭāha** carrying his ever-fresh lotus. (Kathāsaritsāgara II.13)
- **The four merchants' plot** — four young merchants of Kaṭāha notice Guhasena's unfading flower, ply him with wine until he blurts the secret, and resolve to make it fade by seducing Devasmitā back home. They hire a hypocrite go-between, the wandering nun **Yogakaraṇḍikā**, who worms into the household. (Kathāsaritsāgara II.13)
- **The weeping-bitch trick** — the nun feeds Devasmitā's pet **bitch a morsel laced with pepper/sneezing-powder** so its eyes stream, then claims the weeping dog is her own daughter reborn as a bitch — punished for having *refused* a lover in a former life; Devasmitā should not make the same "cruel" mistake. Devasmitā sees the trap at once and plays along to spring it. (Kathāsaritsāgara II.13)
- **The branding** — she has each of the four merchants admitted in turn, drugs his wine with **datura**, has her maids **brand his forehead with the mark of a dog's foot**, strips him, and casts him out into filth. Then, disguising **herself as a merchant**, she sails to Kaṭāha, denounces the four before its king as her runaway **slaves** — proving it by the dog's-foot brands hidden under their turbans — and, honor and husband recovered, returns triumphant. (Kathāsaritsāgara II.13)
  - *Note (the migratory motif):* the **weeping-bitch** ruse is one of the most-traveled tale-motifs in world literature — it recurs in the *[Śukasaptati](../90-literature-corpus/shuka-saptati.md)*, passes through the Arabic/Persian *Book of Sindibād* and the *Seven Sages of Rome*, and reaches medieval Europe in Petrus Alfonsi's *Disciplina Clericalis*. **[scholarly]**

## Śaktideva → Śaktivega and the Golden City of Kanakapurī (V)

- **The disambiguation you must keep straight** — the hero of the Golden-City quest is **Śaktideva**, a Brāhmaṇa of Vardhamāna; he acquires the name **"Śaktivega"** only *at the end*, when the Vidyādhara king **Śaśikhaṇḍa** of Kanakapurī honors him and renames him. Do not treat "Śaktideva" and "Śaktivega" as two people — they are one man before and after his elevation. (Kathāsaritsāgara V) **[scholarly]**
- **The impossible bride-price** — Princess **Kanakarekhā** of Vardhamāna vows to wed only the man who has *seen* **Kanakapurī, the Golden City** — a place no one on earth can find. Śaktideva sets out to win her by finding it. (Kathāsaritsāgara V)
- **The fairy-tale quest** — his search runs through the classic marvels of the *lambhaka* called *Caturdārikā* ("the Four Princesses"): he is **swallowed by a giant fish** and cut out alive, ferried by ascetics and a boat-of-birds, wrecked and rescued, until at last he reaches the sky-city whose **walls, palaces and thrones are all of gold** — the northern citadel of the Vidyādharas. (Kathāsaritsāgara V, *Caturdārikā*)
- **The reward** — in Kanakapurī he weds the **four Vidyādhara princesses** who had been exiled there under a curse, wins celestial *vidyā*, is crowned among the sky-wizards and **renamed Śaktivega**, and takes his place in the roster of [Vidyādhara emperors](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md) whose destined overlord is Naravāhanadatta. (Kathāsaritsāgara V)

## Kaliṅgasenā, Somaprabhā and the automata of Maya (VI)

- **The two friends** — **Kaliṅgasenā**, daughter of the Buddhist king **Kaliṅgadatta** of Takṣaśilā, befriends **Somaprabhā**, a maiden of magical arts — daughter of the *asura* architect-engineer **[Maya Dānava](../08-beings-and-bestiary/maya-danava.md)** and wife of a Vidyādhara. Their friendship, and Somaprabhā's counsel, frame the opening of the *Madanamañcukā* book. (Kathāsaritsāgara VI)
- **The automata of Maya** — to entertain Kaliṅgasenā, Somaprabhā displays the **mechanical figures (yantra-puttalikā)** built by her father Maya: wooden dolls worked by hidden machinery — **one fetches a garland, one brings water, one dances, one recites, one guards the door** — together with flying wooden contrivances. It is a **locus classicus of Indian automata**, cited in histories of robotics beside the relic-guarding *bhūta-vāhana-yantra* of Buddhist legend. **[scholarly]** (Kathāsaritsāgara VI)
- **The flight to Udayana** — resolved to marry **[Udayana of Vatsa](udayana-vasavadatta.md)**, Kaliṅgasenā travels through the air to Kauśāmbī; Udayana is smitten, but his minister **Yaugandharāyaṇa**, fearing rupture with the existing queens Vāsavadattā and Padmāvatī, blocks the match. Somaprabhā, bound by her own circumstances, cannot simply carry her friend away. (Kathāsaritsāgara VI)
- **The Vidyādhara's deception and the birth of Madanamañcukā** — the Vidyādhara **Madanavega**, himself in love with Kaliṅgasenā, assumes **Udayana's exact form** and weds her by *gāndharva* marriage; she bears a daughter, **Madanamañcukā**, who grows to become the **chief queen of Naravāhanadatta** — so this romance-of-error feeds directly into the main dynastic spine of the ocean of story. (Kathāsaritsāgara VI, *Madanamañcukā*)
  - *Variant:* the tale is sometimes read as a cautionary counter to Kaliṅgasenā's headstrong pursuit; Somaprabhā's earlier warnings against a hasty match are set against the trickery that ensues. **[scholarly]**

## The cosmic frame these human tales hang inside

- **Śiva's tale on Kailāsa** — every one of the five is, within the fiction, part of the **single great narration Śiva gives Pārvatī** and which descends through Puṣpadanta → Guṇāḍhya's *Bṛhatkathā* → Somadeva; the human cleverness of Upakośā or Devasmitā is thus framed as *divine* storytelling. The descent-legend is told in full at the [text-home](../90-literature-corpus/brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md). (Kathāsaritsāgara I, *Kathāpīṭha*)
