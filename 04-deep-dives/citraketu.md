# Citraketu — The Grieving King, the Deathless Self, and the Curse to Become Vṛtra

> Cosmic address: a self-contained instructional history (*upākhyāna*) of the **Ṣaṣṭha Skandha** (Sixth Canto) of the [Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/smriti-puranas.md), narrated by Śuka to Parīkṣit in the human world of the present [Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md). It is the Purāṇa's great teaching on **grief** and the **deathless self (ātman)**, and the narrative hinge by which the canto pivots into the [Indra–Vṛtra–Dadhīci cycle](dadhichi.md): Citraketu's curse makes him reborn as **Vṛtra**.
> Parent: [Deep Dives](README.md)

The **Citraketu-upākhyāna** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.14–17) is a first-tier standalone story: a sonless emperor finally fathered one heir, only to lose him to poison; the sages **Nārada** and **Aṅgiras** briefly recall the dead boy's soul to teach the king that no person is permanently "his son"; Citraketu, awakened, rises to become a *vidyādhara* lord; yet a single careless mockery of **Śiva** seated with **Pārvatī** earns the Goddess's curse — to be reborn a *daitya*. His serene, devotional acceptance of that curse is the episode's moral climax, and his next birth is the demon **Vṛtrāsura**, ennobled into a Viṣṇu-devotee. The tale thus doubles as a meditation on grief and as the **backstory of Vṛtra**.

---

## Where it sits in the timeline

- **Frame** — A *kali-scriptural* episode of the Vaivasvata Manvantara's antique past, narrated within the Ṣaṣṭha Skandha after the Ajāmila and Dakṣa/Marut material and before the Vṛtrāsura war proper (6.9–13). On the **two clocks**: scripturally it belongs to the deep past of the present Manvantara; it carries no fixed dynastic/"historical-clock" date. [scholarly]
- **Function in the canto** — It is deliberately placed so that the reader meets **Vṛtra** first as the saintly soul Citraketu *before* the war narrative; the *Bhāgavata* uses it to explain why its Vṛtrāsura dies a liberated devotee rather than a mere drought-demon. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.14–17 → 6.9–13)
- **A grief-text** — Within Hindu pastoral/funerary use it stands beside the [Kṛṣṇa–Arjuna](../04-deep-dives/) and *Mokṣadharma* consolations as a classic Purāṇic answer to bereavement. [scholarly]

## I. The sonless emperor and the longed-for heir

- **Citraketu, lord of Śūrasena** — A mighty, virtuous emperor of **Śūrasena**, Citraketu rules the earth and lacks nothing — except a son. Of his many wives (**ten million** in the hyperbolic idiom), **none bears a child**, and the king grieves his barrenness despite all his fortune. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.14.10–17)
- **Aṅgiras's visit and the sacrifice** — The sage **[Aṅgiras](angiras.md)**, wandering, comes to the court; learning the king's sorrow, he performs (or grants) a sacrifice of the remnant offering (*caru*) to the **Tvaṣṭṛ** deities and gives the consecrated food to the king's eldest queen, **Kṛtadyuti**. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.14.17–28)
- **The double-edged boon** — Aṅgiras forewarns Citraketu that the son to be born will bring him **both joy and grief** (*harṣa-śoka*). The king, desperate, accepts even on those terms. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.14.27)
- **Birth of the prince** — Kṛtadyuti conceives and bears a son. The king's delight is boundless; he showers gifts, and his love for the child becomes total and possessive, while the co-wives, sonless, are consumed by **jealousy**. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.14.28–43)

## II. The poisoning and the king's collapse into grief

- **The co-wives' poison** — Burning with envy at Kṛtadyuti's status as queen-mother, the rival wives **administer poison** to the infant prince, killing him. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.14.43–45)
- **The household's lament** — The nurse's scream brings Kṛtadyuti, who falls senseless; the palace erupts in wailing. Citraketu, told of his only son's death, is **shattered** — he collapses, his learning and royal composure useless against the blow. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.14.45–60; 6.15.1)
- **Grief as the human universal** — The episode dwells on the king's helpless mourning precisely to set up the teaching: even an emperor, even a wise man, is undone by the death of a child. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.15.1–9)

## III. Nārada and Aṅgiras console — the dead boy speaks

- **The sages arrive to teach** — **[Nārada](narada.md)** and Aṅgiras come to the grieving king. They do not merely comfort; they undertake to **show** him the truth of the self. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.15.1–10)
- **The lesson of transient relations** — The sages teach that beings come together and part **like sticks and straws carried by a river**, meeting by the force of time and karma, then separating; the "son," "father," "wife" are temporary configurations, not the abiding reality. The relationships of *saṃsāra* are real only while the bodies last. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.15.3–9, 21–26)
- **Nārada revives the soul temporarily** — By his yogic power, Nārada **summons the departed jīva back into the dead body** so that it can address the mourners. The boy's soul speaks. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.15.10; 6.16.1–3)
- **"Whose son am I?" — the soul's discourse (BhP 6.16)** — The revived soul asks the weeping parents: *in which of my countless births* were they his mother and father? The eternal *ātman*, driven by karma through endless bodies, has had **innumerable parents, kin, and homes** across many species and lives; the present "parents" have no exclusive or lasting claim on it. There is no permanent kinship between the deathless self and any one body. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.16.2–9)
  - *Key teaching:* the soul is **unborn and undying** (*aja*, *amṛta*); it neither truly belongs to the parents nor they to it. Grief springs from the **illusion of ownership** (*mamatā*) over what was never one's own. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.16.5–9)
- **The soul departs again** — Its lesson delivered, the jīva **leaves the body once more**, and the parents' delusion and grief are broken; they cease to mourn, recognising the corpse as no longer "their son." (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.16.10–15)

## IV. Initiation, the mantra, and Citraketu's rise

- **Nārada's grace and the Saṅkarṣaṇa mantra** — His sorrow dispelled, Citraketu is purified. Nārada, pleased, teaches him a **mantra/hymn glorifying Saṅkarṣaṇa (Ananta/Viṣṇu)**, the highest knowledge, and departs with Aṅgiras for **Brahmaloka**. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.16.18–53)
- **Seven days of mantra-discipline** — Citraketu drinks only water, chants the mantra with concentration for **seven days**, and his consciousness is wholly purified. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.16.27–28)
- **Vision of Saṅkarṣaṇa; becoming a vidyādhara lord** — He attains the lordship (*adhipatya*) of the **Vidyādharas** and, sustained by the mantra, after some days beholds **Ananta Śeṣa / Saṅkarṣaṇa** directly. The Lord praises and blesses him with transcendental knowledge and yogic mastery. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.16.28–65)
- **A million years aloft** — Empowered, Citraketu wanders the heavens as **chief of the Vidyādharas**, his aerial craft (*vimāna*) ranging the worlds, singing the Lord's glories — flourishing for "a million years" in the celestial idiom. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.17.1–3)

## V. The fatal mockery and Pārvatī's curse (BhP 6.17)

- **The encounter on Kailāsa** — Travelling through the sky, Citraketu comes upon **Śiva** seated in an assembly of sages and *siddhas*, **embracing Pārvatī, who sits on his lap** in the midst of the gathering. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.17.4–6)
- **The careless jest** — Citraketu, in the hearing of all, **laughs and mocks** the Lord of ascetics for sitting like an ordinary lustful man with his wife on his lap before an assembly of saints — a remark made (the text stresses) **without malice**, the slip of a confident, recently-exalted devotee. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.17.7–10)
- **Śiva's silence** — **Śiva says nothing**, smiling; he is beyond such censure and unmoved. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.17.10)
- **Pārvatī's curse** — The **Goddess Pārvatī**, offended on her Lord's behalf and judging the upstart fit for correction, **curses Citraketu to take a demon's (āsurī) birth**, that he may learn humility — saying that since he presumes to instruct his betters, let him fall from the path of the godly. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.17.11–15)
  - *Note:* the curse is framed as **corrective, not vindictive** — and Śiva afterwards praises Citraketu's response as the mark of a true devotee, normalising the apparent harshness. [scholarly]

## VI. The unshaken devotee — acceptance and rebirth as Vṛtra

- **Citraketu's serene acceptance** — Far from raging or pleading, Citraketu **descends from his vimāna, bows to Pārvatī with folded hands, and calmly accepts the curse** — declaring that pleasure and pain, heaven and hell, friend and foe, curse and blessing are all the **Lord's dispensation**, arising from one's own past karma; he asks no reprieve and even begs the Goddess's pardon for any offence. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.17.16–25)
- **Equanimity as the lesson** — His unmoved, *sama-darśana* (equal-vision) response — treating the curse as neither good nor evil but as God's will — is the episode's spiritual climax: the proof that he has truly absorbed the teaching on the deathless self. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.17.21–25)
- **Śiva and Pārvatī marvel** — **Śiva extols** Citraketu's equanimity to Pārvatī as the hallmark of the Lord's exalted devotees, who are undisturbed by fortune or curse; the Goddess is reconciled. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.17.26–34)
- **Rebirth as Vṛtrāsura** — In consequence of the curse, Citraketu takes birth in the demon race — by the sacrifice of **Tvaṣṭṛ** (whose deities his own son-boon had once invoked) he is reborn as **Vṛtrāsura**, the asura who wars with Indra; yet, retaining his devotion, he becomes the **saintly demon** who dies meditating on Viṣṇu and attains liberation. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.17.38–40; → 6.9–13)
  - *The narrative bridge:* this is why the *Bhāgavata*'s **Vṛtra** is no ordinary drought-serpent but a Kṛṣṇa-conscious soul — and why the war that requires **[Dadhīci's](dadhichi.md)** bone-vajra is also a tale of two devotees (Dadhīci and Vṛtra) giving up their bodies in surrender. See [Dadhīci](dadhichi.md) and the Indra–Vṛtra cycle. (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 6.9–13)

## VII. Variants, parallels & significance

- **Devī-Bhāgavata parallel** — The **Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa** retells the Citraketu story within its own Śākta frame; the curse-and-rebirth-as-Vṛtra structure is preserved, but the episode is read to magnify the **Goddess's** authority and the inexorability of her word. [late text] [regional]
- **The double-edged boon motif** — Aṅgiras's warning that the son will bring "joy and grief" is a recurring Purāṇic device (cf. boons that carry a hidden cost); here it frames the whole tale as a lesson that attachment to any granted joy ripens into sorrow. [scholarly]
- **On Pārvatī's curse** — Commentators note the *theological* point: a perfected devotee like Citraketu is **beyond curse's real harm**, so the curse functions as a divinely-sanctioned vehicle to deliver him into the higher destiny of dying as Vṛtra in Viṣṇu-remembrance. The "punishment" is in truth a grace. [scholarly]
  - *Variant emphasis:* a more straightforward reading takes the curse at face value — pride before the Goddess incurs a real fall — and reads Citraketu's calm as the *means* by which he redeems it. The two readings coexist in commentary.
- **Why he did not resent Pārvatī** — His refusal to counter-curse or appease (he could have, as a *vidyādhara* adept) is held up as the definitive contrast to lesser sages whose curses fly in anger; Citraketu embodies *titikṣā* (forbearance) and *prasāda* (serene acceptance). [scholarly]
- **Place among the Bhāgavata's great teachings** — Alongside Ajāmila (the Name) and Gajendra (surrender), Citraketu is one of the canto-defining instructional histories — specifically the Purāṇa's set-piece on **grief, the immortal self, and equanimity under apparent injustice**. See the [Bhāgavata Tales Catalog](bhagavata-tales-catalog.md). [scholarly]
