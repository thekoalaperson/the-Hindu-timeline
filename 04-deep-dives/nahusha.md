# Nahuṣa — the Mortal Who Became Indra

> Where this sits: a Chandravamsha (Lunar dynasty) episode of the deep past, narrated as ancestral cautionary history in the great epics and Purāṇas. Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Lineage context: [../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) · Cosmic slot: long before the [Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/), in the 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga; his serpent-redemption falls within the Pāṇḍava exile late in the [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md).

Nahuṣa is the supreme parable of the corruption of power: a virtuous mortal king of such accumulated tapas that the gods, regent-less after Indra fled the sin of slaying Vṛtra, install him on the throne of heaven itself — only for the splendour of Svarga to inflame his pride and lust until he yokes the Saptarṣis to his palanquin, covets Indra's queen Śacī, and kicks the sage Agastya, who curses him to crash to earth as a python (ajagara). For thousands of years he writhes as a serpent until his own great-great-grandson Yudhiṣṭhira frees him by answering his riddles on dharma. His arc — *mortal → Indra → snake → liberated soul* — is told repeatedly and with variation across the Mahābhārata (Vana, Udyoga, Anuśāsana parvas) and the Purāṇas. Principal sources: **Mahābhārata, Vana Parva 176–181** (the Ajagara episode), **Udyoga Parva 11–17** (the rise and fall, narrated by Śalya), **Anuśāsana Parva 99–100**; **Bhāgavata Purāṇa 6.13 & 9.18**, **Vishnu Purāṇa 4.10**, **Devī Bhāgavata**, and the **Padma** and **Vāyu** Purāṇas.

---

## I. Lineage & placement — son of Āyu, father of Yayāti

- **Genealogical seat** - Nahuṣa is the fifth king of the Chandravamsha: **Soma → Budha → Purūravas → Āyu → Nahuṣa → [Yayāti](yayati.md)**. He is grandson of [Purūravas](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) and great-grandson of [Ilā/Ilā-Sudyumna](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md). (Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva; Vishnu Purāṇa 4.10; Bhāgavata 9.18)
  - *Variant (mother):* the Vishnu Purāṇa names his mother as **Prabhā**, daughter of the asura **Svarbhānu** (Rāhu); other genealogies leave her unnamed. (Vishnu Purāṇa 4.10)
- **His six sons** - By his queen **Virajā / Aśokasundarī** (name varies), Nahuṣa fathers six (some lists seven) sons, of whom **[Yayāti](yayati.md)** succeeds him; the others are commonly given as Yati, Saṃyāti, Āyati, Viyati, and Kṛti. (Bhāgavata 9.18; Vishnu Purāṇa 4.10)
  - *Variant ([late text]):* the Padma Purāṇa makes his wife **Aśokasundarī**, a daughter created by Pārvatī, weaving Nahuṣa into a Śaiva frame absent from the epic. [late text]
- **A "second Indra"** - His name is glossed in the epics as one who "binds (nahyati) all creatures" by his power; the Mahābhārata repeatedly calls him an emperor (samrāṭ) whose hundred horse-sacrifices rivalled Indra's own merit — the very surplus of merit that qualifies him for heaven and dooms him by pride. (Udyoga Parva 11–13)

## II. The vacancy in heaven — why Indra fled

- **The sin of Vṛtra-slaying** - After Indra kills **Vṛtra** (a brāhmaṇa-born adversary, son of Tvaṣṭṛ in many tellings), he is overtaken by **brahmahatyā** (the sin of brahmin-murder) and hides himself, shrinking into a lotus-stalk in Lake Mānasa, leaving Svarga without a king. (Udyoga Parva 9–11; Bhāgavata 6.13)
  - *Variant (motive of flight, Bhāgavata):* the Bhāgavata 6.13 frames Indra's withdrawal as guilt and dread of brahmahatyā after Vṛtra (a devotee-asura) is slain, the sin pursuing him as a hag-like figure; the throne stands empty for want of an untainted ruler. (Bhāgavata 6.13)
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Śalya's telling):* in Udyoga Parva the gods and ṛṣis, terrified of a kingless heaven invaded by chaos, deliberately seek a mortal of sufficient tapas to hold the office until Indra is purified. (Udyoga Parva 11)
- **The gods elect Nahuṣa** - Devas and ṛṣis settle on the earthly emperor Nahuṣa for his unmatched austerity and justice; he protests his unworthiness but is persuaded, and his merit is augmented so he can wield the office. (Udyoga Parva 11–12)
  - *Variant (the fatal boon):* in several tellings Nahuṣa accepts only on the condition — or is granted the boon — that **whoever comes within his sight loses half their strength / lustre to him**, so that even gods and sages are drained in his presence; this very boon makes his later seizure of Bhīma possible. (Vana Parva 178–179; cf. Udyoga Parva 17)

## III. The corruption of the throne — pride and the lust for Śacī

- **Splendour breeds arrogance** - Seated on Indra's throne, served by apsarās and the wealth of the three worlds, Nahuṣa loses his humility, gives himself to sensual indulgence, and begins to demand Indra's every privilege. (Udyoga Parva 12–13)
- **He covets Śacī (Indrāṇī)** - Beholding Indra's queen **Śacī / Indrāṇī**, Nahuṣa is seized by lust and commands the gods to bring her to his bed as the consort owed to whoever holds Indra's seat. (Udyoga Parva 13–14)
- **Śacī's stratagem** - Terrified, Śacī takes refuge with **Bṛhaspati** (preceptor of the gods); she stalls Nahuṣa, finally promising to come to him only if he arrives in a wholly unprecedented conveyance — **a palanquin borne by the seven ṛṣis (Saptarṣi) themselves.** (Udyoga Parva 14–17)
  - *Variant (counsel of Bṛhaspati/devas):* in the Udyoga account the gods and Bṛhaspati engineer the delay specifically to provoke Nahuṣa into an offence against the brāhmaṇas grave enough to topple him; Śacī's condition is a deliberate trap, not mere coyness. (Udyoga Parva 16–17)
  - *Variant (Devī Bhāgavata):* the Goddess-centred retelling foregrounds Śacī's appeal to the Devī and the use of Indra's hidden whereabouts (revealed by the goddess Upaśruti) as the engine of the plot. [late text]

## IV. "Sarpa, sarpa!" — the kick and Agastya's curse

- **The ṛṣis yoked to the palanquin** - Maddened by desire, Nahuṣa harnesses the **Saptarṣis** as palanquin-bearers and is carried toward Śacī's mansion — the supreme inversion of dharma, a king of heaven treating the holiest sages as draught-beasts. (Udyoga Parva 17; Vana Parva 178)
- **The double-meaning command** - Impatient at their slow gait, Nahuṣa urges them on crying **"sarpa, sarpa!"** — from the root *√sṛp* (to creep/glide), meaning "move on, move on," but also the noun *sarpa*, "serpent." (Udyoga Parva 17; Vana Parva 178)
- **The kick on Agastya's head** - Among the bearers is the diminutive sage **Agastya**; in his impatience Nahuṣa **strikes/kicks Agastya on the head** (or touches him with his foot), the crowning sacrilege. (Udyoga Parva 17; Vana Parva 178)
  - *Variant (sequence):* some tellings put the kick first and the cry "sarpa" after; others have Agastya seize on the word "sarpa" Nahuṣa was shouting and turn it back on him — "since you said *sarpa*, become a *sarpa*." The pun is the fixed core; the order of kick-and-word varies by text.
- **The curse to become a python** - Agastya, blazing, curses: **"Fall from heaven, wretch, and become a python (ajagara) upon the earth!"** Nahuṣa instantly tumbles from Svarga and crawls the forests as a giant serpent. (Udyoga Parva 17; Vana Parva 178; Bhāgavata 6.13)
  - *Variant (the release-clause):* in the Vana Parva version Agastya (or, in some retellings, a compassionate qualification within the curse) fixes the **term of release**: Nahuṣa will be freed when **Yudhiṣṭhira** answers his questions on dharma — binding the curse to the future epic. (Vana Parva 178–181)
  - *Variant (mercy of Agastya):* a softer strand has Agastya, his anger spent, grant that the curse shall not be perpetual but end at a virtuous descendant's hand — explaining why so terrible an offence does not earn eternal damnation. [folk]
- **Indra restored** - With the usurper fallen, Indra is purified of brahmahatyā (by the Aśvamedha / distribution of his sin) and returns to his throne. (Udyoga Parva 18; Bhāgavata 6.13)

## V. The Ajagara episode — Bhīma seized, Yudhiṣṭhira's riddles (Vana Parva 176–181)

- **Bhīma in the serpent's coils** - Ages later, during the Pāṇḍavas' forest exile, **[Bhīma](mahabharata/03-vana-parva.md)** hunting in the Himālaya is seized by an enormous python in a mountain cave; the boon-strengthened serpent drains his might and Bhīma — strongest of men — lies helpless. (Vana Parva 176–177)
- **The serpent names itself** - The python reveals he is **Nahuṣa**, the Pāṇḍavas' own ancestor, cursed by Agastya; hunger and the curse compel him to devour whatever falls into his grasp. (Vana Parva 178)
- **Yudhiṣṭhira arrives** - Tracking Bhīma, **[Yudhiṣṭhira](mahabharata/03-vana-parva.md)** finds his brother bound and addresses the serpent; Nahuṣa sets the terms: **answer my questions and I release Bhīma; fail, and I eat him.** (Vana Parva 179–180)
- **The dharma-catechism** - Nahuṣa and Yudhiṣṭhira debate, among other things:
  - **What defines a brāhmaṇa?** — Yudhiṣṭhira answers that brāhmaṇa-hood rests on **conduct and qualities (truth, charity, forgiveness, virtue), not birth alone**, a celebrated passage on the varṇa question. (Vana Parva 180)
  - **What is the supreme virtue?** — the relative weight of **truth, charity, kind speech, and non-injury (ahiṃsā)**. (Vana Parva 180)
  - **The soul's transmigration** — how the embodied self attains heaven, perceives sense-objects, and the distinction between **manas (mind) and buddhi (intellect)**, the nature of the universal Self and the path to mokṣa. (Vana Parva 180–181)
- **Liberation** - Satisfied by Yudhiṣṭhira's wisdom, Nahuṣa **releases Bhīma**, sheds the serpent body, reassumes his celestial form, and ascends to heaven (svarga) — the curse fulfilled and dissolved. (Vana Parva 181)
  - *Variant (section/title):* this block is the **Ajagara (Ajagara-parva) sub-section, Vana Parva ~176–181**; Yudhiṣṭhira's reply to the serpent's questions falls at sections ~180–181 in the standard (Sacred-Texts / Wisdomlib) numbering. [scholarly]

## VI. Retellings & cross-text variants

- **Three Mahābhārata loci** - The Nahuṣa material recurs **at least three times** in the epic, each from a different narrator and emphasis:
  - **Vana Parva 176–181** — the Ajagara/serpent-and-Bhīma frame (action + dharma-dialogue).
  - **Udyoga Parva 11–17** — **Śalya** narrates the full rise-and-fall (election, Śacī, the palanquin, the curse) to hearten Yudhiṣṭhira, alongside the Indra-Vṛtra background. (Udyoga Parva, Indra-vijaya section)
  - **Anuśāsana Parva 99–100** — Nahuṣa appears in didactic/genealogical context among exemplary kings and the consequences of pride. (Anuśāsana Parva)
- **Purāṇic notices** - The Bhāgavata tells it twice — briefly at **6.13** (as the sequel to Indra's brahmahatyā after Vṛtra) and within the Chandravamsha at **9.18**; the **Vishnu Purāṇa 4.10** gives the lineage and the bare fall; the **Padma** and **Vāyu** Purāṇas and the **Devī Bhāgavata** add devotional and Śaiva/Śākta colour. (Bhāgavata 6.13, 9.18; Vishnu Purāṇa 4.10)
  - *Variant (Manu's lawbook frame):* later tradition reads Nahuṣa as the archetype of the king who **comes into conflict with brāhmaṇas** and is destroyed by it — a charter-myth for brahmanical inviolability. [scholarly]
- **Ramāyaṇa / genealogical mentions** - Nahuṣa surfaces in dynastic lists and as an exemplar of a great king fallen by pride, invoked in passing rather than narrated at length. [scholarly]
- **The name applied elsewhere** - "Nahuṣa" is also an ancient Vedic/Ṛgvedic epithet (sometimes a people or a generic "man/mortal"); the epic king is the figure who gathers all the later narrative. [scholarly]

## VII. Why this episode matters — pride, power, and the dharma-test

- **The power-corruption parable** - Nahuṣa is the canonical illustration that **even the highest merit cannot survive unchecked power**: tapas wins him heaven, pride loses it. Pairs naturally with his son [Yayāti](yayati.md)'s kāma-vairāgya parable as the two great Chandravamsha cautionary tales.
- **The varṇa-by-conduct teaching** - Yudhiṣṭhira's reply — that brāhmaṇa-hood is defined by character, not birth — is one of the Mahābhārata's most-cited passages in debates on caste. See [../05-concepts-and-dharma/](../05-concepts-and-dharma/) (varṇāśrama, dharma).
- **Brahmanical inviolability** - The yoking and kicking of the Saptarṣis, punished by instant ruin, is a foundational statement of the danger of offending brāhmaṇas; it rhymes with [Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) and the Agastya curse-cycle.
- **Genealogical hinge** - As father of [Yayāti](yayati.md), Nahuṣa stands one generation above the great Chandravamsha bifurcation into the Yādava and Paurava/Kaurava lines — see the full tables in [../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **Narrative function in the exile** - The Ajagara episode lets the epic deliver a dharma-discourse mid-exile while reminding the Pāṇḍavas of their own fallible ancestry; it sits within the [Vana Parva](mahabharata/03-vana-parva.md).
