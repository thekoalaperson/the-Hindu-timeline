# Kuvalayāśva (Ṛtadhvaja) and Madālasā — the nāga-world romance

> *Cosmic address: an embedded romance in the **bird-dialogues (dharma-pakṣin)** of the [Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/markandeya-purana.md), Cantos XX–XXVII (Pargiter), narrated by the four wise birds to **Jaimini**; set in legendary royal antiquity with no yuga-address, spanning the earth's surface, **Pātāla**, and the nāga-world of Aśvatara. The heroic prelude to the already-covered **Madālasā Upadeśa** lullaby.*
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Up to [README](../README.md)

The tale of **Ṛtadhvaja** — better known by his horse-derived name **Kuvalayāśva** — and his gandharva-born queen **Madālasā** is the **longest sustained narrative** of the Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, a serial romance-and-adventure that runs across eight cantos before it hands off to the Purāṇa's famous jñāna-material. A prince rides a heaven-sent horse into a chasm in the earth, slays a shape-shifting Dānava in the netherworld, wins a captive princess, loses her to a false report of his own death, and recovers her — restored to life from the very body of a nāga king — from the depths of the serpent-world. Only after all this does Madālasā begin to sing her infant sons awake to the non-dual Self. This file tells the **adventure**; the lullaby and the education of Alarka are treated as the Madālasā Upadeśa. The romance is also a rare Purāṇic showpiece of the **nāgas as benefactors** rather than adversaries, and of **music (Sarasvatī's gift) and Śaiva grace** as the machinery by which the dead are recovered.

---

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — The Purāṇa gives the story **no yuga-marker**. It is told inside the frame of the four learned birds answering the sage **Jaimini's** questions, set in an undefined legendary antiquity of a righteous royal house (King Śatrujit, whose sacrifices gratified Indra). Later dynastic lists graft its issue **Alarka** onto the **Kāśī line** (see [Kāśī: the Divodāsa line](../03-lineages-vamsha/kashi-line-divodasa.md)); the Purāṇa itself keeps the tale free-floating in heroic time. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa*, Cantos XX–XXVII)
- **Academic / historical frame** — The Mārkaṇḍeya is among the **oldest Mahāpurāṇas**; its narrative core (bird-dialogues, the Madālasā cycle, Hariścandra, the manvantaras) is dated **[scholarly] c. 3rd–5th century CE**, older than the interpolated Devī-Māhātmya (cantos 81–93, c. 6th c.). F. E. Pargiter's 1904 translation (Asiatic Society of Bengal) is the standard English text and supplies the canto numbering used here. [scholarly]

## Dramatis personae

- **Ṛtadhvaja / Kuvalayāśva** — son of King **Śatrujit**; a hero reckoned superior even to divine beings. Renamed **Kuvalayāśva**, "he of the horse Kuvalaya," after the miraculous steed. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XX)
- **Madālasā** — daughter of the **gandharva king Viśvāvasu**; abducted into Pātāla by the Dānava, rescued and wed by Kuvalayāśva. A **brahmavādinī / jīvanmuktā** who later becomes the lullaby-singing queen. Her companion in captivity is **Kuṇḍalā**. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXI)
- **Gālava** — the brahman sage whose sacrifice the Dānava disrupts; the plot's spark. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XX)
- **Pātālaketu** — a **Dānava (daitya)** of the netherworld who harasses Gālava in the shapes of lion, elephant, and forest beasts, and finally a boar; the abductor of Madālasā. His **younger brother is Tālaketu**, the villain of the second act. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XX–XXII)
- **Aśvatara** — a **nāga king**, whose two sons (in disguise) befriend Ṛtadhvaja; the benefactor who ultimately restores Madālasā from death. His **brother is Kambala**; both are nāga master-musicians. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XX, XXIII–XXIV)
- **Kuvalaya** — the sky-born, tireless horse that carries the prince across the earth and down into Pātāla. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XX)

## The horse and the hermitage (Canto XX)

- **Gālava's complaint** — The sage **Gālava**, unable to complete his rites, comes to **King Śatrujit**: a vile **Dānava** repeatedly wrecks his hermitage, "assuming the several forms of a lion, an elephant, and forest-roving beasts." He asks for the king's peerless son to guard the sacrifice. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XX)
- **The gift of Kuvalaya** — A **horse named Kuvalaya descends from the sky** in answer to the sage's distress; a divine voice declares it can range the whole earth untiring and will let **Ṛtadhvaja** conquer the demon. From this steed the prince takes the name **Kuvalayāśva**. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XX)
  - *Nāga sub-plot (within the text):* the horse and the whole rescue are quietly the doing of the **nāga king Aśvatara**, whose **two sons — in human/disguised form — had befriended Ṛtadhvaja**. The friendship planted here is what makes Aśvatara, cantos later, move heaven and the netherworld to give the prince his wife back. The Purāṇa thus frames the nāgas as **grateful friends of a mortal hero**, not the treacherous serpents of the Mahābhārata sarpa-satra. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XX, XXIII)

## Into Pātāla: the rescue of Madālasā (Canto XXI)

- **The chase into the chasm** — When the Dānava **Pātālaketu** attacks Gālava's rite as a **boar**, Kuvalayāśva pursues it on the horse Kuvalaya, loosing arrows across thousands of yojanas until "the quick-paced boar fell into an open chasm in the earth." Horse and rider **plunge in after it, descending into Pātāla**. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXI)
- **Madālasā in Purandarapura** — In the subterranean city **Purandarapura** the prince finds **Madālasā** — "her face like the clear moon… large-hipped and full-breasted" — the **daughter of the gandharva king Viśvāvasu**, carried off by Pātālaketu. Her companion **Kuṇḍalā** explains the captivity and reports the prophecy of the divine cow **Surabhi**: whoever pierces the Dānava with arrows shall shortly be Madālasā's husband. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXI)
- **Marriage and the demon's end** — Kuvalayāśva **weds Madālasā** on the spot. When the Dānava host attacks their departure, he wields the divine weapon of **Tvaṣṭṛ**, and the demons "together with Pātālaketu" are turned to **heaps of bones**. He bears his bride up to his father's city, where **Śatrujit** blesses the marriage. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXI)

## The false ascetic and Madālasā's death (Canto XXII)

- **Tālaketu's revenge** — **Tālaketu**, the **younger brother of the slain Pātālaketu**, disguises himself as a **sage dwelling on the bank of the Yamunā**. He inveigles Kuvalayāśva into guarding his hermitage while he supposedly performs water-rites to **Varuṇa**, and asks the prince to leave behind his **neck-ornament (jewel)**. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXII)
- **The false death-report** — Once he holds the ornament, Tālaketu **plunges into the river**, travels to the prince's city, and delivers a fabricated tale: that Kuvalayāśva, guarding ascetics near the hermitage and fighting a wicked Daitya, "was pierced in the breast with a spear," and that śūdra ascetics gave his body to the fire. He shows the neck-jewel as proof. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXII)
- **Madālasā yields up her life** — Hearing the report and seeing the ornament, **Madālasā at once gives up her dear life** in grief. The perfect wife's death on a lie is the hinge of the whole romance — a death that only a nāga's power can undo. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXII)

## Aśvatara's grief and the double boon (Canto XXIII)

- **Kuvalayāśva's return and vow** — The prince returns to find Madālasā dead of the false report. He grieves, yet — refusing to abandon a king's duty — resolves that he will **renounce women** rather than his throne, and takes up his obligations again. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXIII)
- **Sarasvatī's gift of all sound** — The **nāga king Aśvatara**, moved by the couple's ruin, propitiates the goddess **Sarasvatī** by austerity. She grants him and his brother **Kambala** perfect mastery of **all sound** — the musical notes, the scales (grāmas), and the poetic arts — making the two nāgas paradigmatic celestial musicians (cf. [Sarasvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/saraswati.md)). (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXIII)
- **Śiva's boon: Madālasā reborn in the nāga** — Armed with this gift, Aśvatara and Kambala **hymn Śiva with song** and beg that **Madālasā be restored as Aśvatara's own daughter**. Śiva consents, instructing Aśvatara to **perform the śrāddha (funeral rite)** for Madālasā and to **consume the ritual offering**, whereupon she shall arise, reborn, out of the nāga's own body. Music (Sarasvatī) and Śaiva grace are thus the twinned engines of the resurrection. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXIII)

## The recovery from the nāga's breast (Canto XXIV)

- **The invitation to Pātāla** — Aśvatara's **sons** — the same friends of Ṛtadhvaja's youth — invite Kuvalayāśva down into the nāga-realm. There Aśvatara **presses gifts and treasures on the prince**, who refuses all material wealth; only then do the sons reveal that his lost wife, "the daughter of the gandharva king," is there to be regained. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXIV)
- **"It is illusion — touch her not!"** — Aśvatara produces Madālasā and warns the prince: **"It is illusion, my son! touch her not!"** — testing whether grief will make him grasp at a phantom. Kuvalayāśva, overcome, moves toward her and the nāga restrains him; the prince **faints**. Revived, he hears from Aśvatara **the whole story of her recovery from death** — that this Madālasā, reborn from the nāga's body by Śiva's boon, is no illusion but his very wife restored. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXIV)
  - *On "from his own breast":* the Purāṇa's mechanism is the **śrāddha-and-consumption rite** of Canto XXIII — Aśvatara ritually takes Madālasā's death into himself and she is **reborn out of the nāga's own body**, so the tradition speaks of her being restored "from his breast." The motif fuses funerary rite, serpent-regeneration, and the yogic power of a nāga king. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXIII–XXIV)
- **The return to earth** — Reunited, Kuvalayāśva "took again his loved one" and, **mounted on the horse Kuvalaya, departed in great splendour to his own city** — closing the netherworld arc and returning the couple to the surface world. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXIV)

## The sequel: the lullaby and Alarka (Cantos XXV–XXVII onward)

- **Handoff to the Upadeśa** — Restored to earth, Madālasā bears sons and rocks each crying infant to sleep not with comfort but with **non-dual Vedānta** — *"śuddho'si buddho'si nirañjano'si…"* Her first three sons — **Vikrānta, Subāhu, Śatrumardana** — renounce the world at once; warned by Kuvalayāśva that an all-renouncing line will end the dynasty, she rears the **fourth son, Alarka**, for **rāja-dharma**, leaving him a ring with counsel to open when worldly life fails. This is the **Madālasā Upadeśa**, treated in the [Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa profile](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/markandeya-purana.md) and folded into the Purāṇa's yoga-treatise. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XXV–XXVII, and chs. ~39–43)
- **Alarka and Dattātreya** — When Alarka's rule is later threatened (by his ascetic brother **Subāhu**, to provoke his awakening), he opens the ring, seeks the avadhūta **Dattātreya**, and receives the realization of the Self — the Purāṇa's principal jñāna-course. See [Dattātreya](dattatreya.md). (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* ~chs. 39–43)

## Themes and kinship

- **The benevolent nāga** — Unusually for the corpus, the serpents are the **rescuers**: Aśvatara's gratitude, austerity, and Śaiva-won power drive the resurrection. It sits beside other tales of nāga alliance and marriage — [Mātali and Sumukha the nāga bridegroom](matali-sumukha-naga-bridegroom.md), the brahmin and the nāga [Padmanābha](unchavritti-brahmin-naga-padmanabha.md), and the wider [nāgas and serpents](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md) file; on Pātāla as a loka see [devas, asuras and the lokas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md).
- **Descent-and-recovery pattern** — A hero enters the netherworld, wins a bride, loses her to deception, and recovers her from death: the romance-of-Pātāla shape, here doubled (rescue **from** Pātāla in Canto XXI, resurrection **within** the nāga-world in Canto XXIV).
- **The jīvanmuktā queen** — Madālasā is the Purāṇa's woman-sage, kin to Queen **Cūḍālā**, who likewise enlightens her family from within royal life ([Cūḍālā and Śikhidhvaja](chudala-shikhidhvaja.md)); both dramatize liberation-while-living amid worldly duty (cf. [puruṣārthas, karma-saṃsāra-mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md)). Her adventure-origin, though, is unique: a princess twice pulled back from the borders of death before she becomes a teacher.
- **Music as cosmic power** — Sarasvatī's gift of "all sound" to Aśvatara and Kambala makes song the literal means of moving Śiva to overturn death, a rare Purāṇic charter for the nāgas as archetypal musicians.
