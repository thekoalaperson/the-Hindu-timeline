# Atri and Anasūyā

> *Where this sits: a deep-dive into the saptarṣi household at the root of the timeline. Atri is a mind-born son of Brahmā from the first creation and one of the Seven Sages of the current ([7th, Vaivasvata](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)) Manvantara. Anasūyā is the exemplary pativratā, and the couple's three sons Soma, Dattātreya and Durvāsā are portions of the three great gods. ⬆ Parent index: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Master index: [README](../README.md)*

**Atri** (*Atri*, "devourer", from √*ad* "to eat"; one of the original Saptarṣi) and his wife **Anasūyā** (*An-asūyā*, "she who is free of envy/ill-will") are among the most generative couples of the Purāṇic genealogies. Their sons are the **Moon-god Soma**, and through him the whole **[Chandravaṃśa (Lunar dynasty)](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)**; the yogin **[Dattātreya](dattatreya.md)**; and the wrathful sage **[Durvāsā](ambarisha-durvasa.md)**. Anasūyā is held up across the Purāṇas as the model chaste wife (*pativratā*), and her counsel to Sītā in the *Rāmāyaṇa* is the tradition's best-known sermon on *pātivratya-dharma*.

---

## Atri — Prajāpati and Saptarṣi

- **Mind-born son of Brahmā** — Atri is one of the **mānasaputras** (mind-born sons) and Prajāpatis whom Brahmā emits at the dawn of creation. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa names nine such sons, the "nine Brahmās": Bhṛgu, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu, Aṅgiras, Marīci, Dakṣa, Atri and Vasiṣṭha (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.7.4–6). The Mahābhārata names six great ṛṣis as Brahmā's sons, Marīci, Aṅgiras, Atri, Pulastya, Pulaha and Kratu, and says Atri had many sons, all Veda-knowing, perfected and calm (Mahābhārata CE 1.60.4–6). The Mārkaṇḍeya calls him Brahmā's "second son" (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 17.1).
  - *Variant (born from Brahmā's eyes):* The Bhāgavata gives each sage a part of Brahmā's body. Atri comes from the **eyes** (*akṣṇo 'triḥ*), Aṅgiras from the mouth, Marīci from the mind (Bhāgavata 3.12.24). The image comes back in the next generation: the Bhāgavata has Soma born from **Atri's** eyes (Bhāgavata 9.14.3).
- **Saptarṣi of the current Manvantara** — Atri is one of the **Saptarṣi** (Seven Sages) of the present **[Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)**, with Vasiṣṭha, Kaśyapa, Jamadagni, Gautama, Viśvāmitra and Bharadvāja (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.1.32; Bhāgavata 8.13.5). See [Saptarṣi](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/saptarishi.md).
  - *Variant (rotating office):* The Purāṇas give a different set of seven for each Manvantara, so the office is not fixed. In the first (Svāyambhuva) age the Bhāgavata makes Marīci the head of the seven (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.1, the roster for each Manvantara in turn; Bhāgavata 4.1.8).
- **Vedic ṛṣi** — Tradition ascribes the **fifth maṇḍala** of the **Ṛgveda** (the "Ātreya maṇḍala") to Atri and his family, the Ātreyas, who are also one of the foundational gotras (Ṛgveda, Maṇḍala V; gotra tradition). Two Atri stories are already in the hymns themselves:
  - **Atri and the eclipsed Sun** — When the asura **Svarbhānu** pierces the Sun with darkness, Atri finds the hidden Sun "with the fourth *brahman*" and sets the Sun's eye back in the sky. The hymn's close says the Atris found it, and no one else could (Ṛgveda 5.40.5–9).
  - **Atri rescued by the Aśvins** — The Aśvins cool the fire around Atri in the *ṛbīsa*, a burning pit or chasm, and bring him out with his whole company. Atri's cry from the pit, "like a woman in need", brings them at a falcon's speed (Ṛgveda 1.116.8, 1.117.3, 5.78.4).
- **Genealogical root via Soma** — Through his son **Soma/Candra**, Atri is the ultimate ancestor of the whole Lunar dynasty: Soma → Budha → Purūravas → the Aila kings → Yadu, Pūru, and the houses of the Yādavas, Kauravas and Pāṇḍavas. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa's line is short: Brahmā's son is Atri, Atri's is Soma, and Brahmā anoints Soma lord of plants, brahmins and stars (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.6.5–7; Bhāgavata 9.14.2–3). See [Soma, Tārā & Budha](soma-tara-budha.md) and the [Lunar lineage](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **Atri at Vainya's horse-sacrifice** — In the Mahābhārata, Mārkaṇḍeya tells how Atri's wife urges him to seek wealth from King **Vainya** (Pṛthu), who is performing an aśvamedha. At the sacrifice Atri praises the king as the first of rulers and the equal of Indra. **Gautama** objects, the dispute goes before **Sanatkumāra**, and he rules for Atri. Pleased, Vainya loads Atri with gifts (Mahābhārata CE 3.183; Ganguli Vana 184).

## Anasūyā — the first-tier pativratā

- **Daughter of Kardama and Devahūti** — In the Bhāgavata, Anasūyā is one of the nine daughters of the Prajāpati **Kardama** and **Devahūti**, and so a sister of **Kapila**. Kardama gives Kalā to Marīci and **Anasūyā to Atri** (Bhāgavata 3.24.22–23; recalled at 4.1.12–15).
  - *Variant (daughter of Dakṣa):* The Viṣṇu Purāṇa makes her a daughter of **Dakṣa** and Prasūti. Of Dakṣa's eleven younger daughters, Khyāti, Satī, Sambhūti, Smṛti, Prīti, Kṣamā, Sannati, **Anasūyā**, Ūrjā, Svāhā and Svadhā, are given "in order" to Bhṛgu, Bhava, Marīci, Aṅgiras, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu, **Atri**, Vasiṣṭha, Agni and the Pitṛs, so she falls to Atri (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.7.24–27). A few verses earlier the same Purāṇa lists Anasūyā among the wives whom Brahmā himself creates for his nine mind-born sons (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.7.7–8).
- **The supreme chaste wife** — Anasūyā's *pātivratya* works as a cosmic force, and her name, "free of envy", makes her the type of the un-jealous, wholly devoted wife (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 117–118; Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 16.46–90). She does not appear in the familiar *pañcakanyā* verse (Ahalyā, Draupadī, Sītā or Kuntī, Tārā, Mandodarī). No canonical "pañca-satī" list naming her was found in the texts consulted. [unverified]
- **Tapas-power over nature** — Three texts each tell a different miracle. They are kept separate here:
  - *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa:* When Atri presents his wife to Rāma, he lists her deeds. In a ten-year drought that scorched the world she produced roots and fruit and **made the Jāhnavī (Gaṅgā) flow**. She practised ten thousand years of severe tapas, cleared away obstacles by her vows, and, in haste for the gods' work, **turned ten nights into one** (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 117.8–12; CE 2.109.8–12). Vālmīki does not explain the ten nights.
    - *Translator's note (vulgate, valmikiramayan.net):* The note explains the ten nights with a Purāṇic tale. **Māṇḍavya** curses a hermitess and friend of Anasūyā, named here as **Śāṇḍilī**, to be widowed one morning within ten days. She answers that there will be no more dawn, and the alarmed gods go to Anasūyā, who turns ten nights into one and averts the husband's death. The name Śāṇḍilī comes from this commentarial note, not from Vālmīki's verse. [commentarial]
  - *Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa (the Kauśika story):* A leprous, harsh brahmin **Kauśika** of Pratiṣṭhāna is carried at night on his devoted wife's shoulders. His foot jostles the sage **Māṇḍavya**, who is impaled on a stake as a supposed thief, and Māṇḍavya curses Kauśika to die at sunrise. The wife, who is not named, answers that the sun shall not rise, and endless night stops all sacrifice. On Brahmā's advice the gods go to **Anasūyā**. She persuades the wife and **calls up the Sun after ten nights**. When the husband falls dead, she revives him, young and free of disease, by an act of truth sworn on her own fidelity (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 16.14–85). The boon the gods then offer her leads to the birth of the three sons (below).
  - *Śiva Purāṇa (Atrīśvara):* During a **hundred-year drought** in the Kāmada forest near Citrakūṭa, Atri sinks into trance and his disciples abandon him. Anasūyā alone serves him and worships Śiva in a clay liṅga (Śiva Purāṇa, Koṭirudra-saṃhitā 3.9–37). When Atri wakes and asks for water, **Gaṅgā** appears to Anasūyā, has her dig a pit, and fills it. Anasūyā asks her to stay, and Gaṅgā agrees in return for **one year of Anasūyā's merit**. Śiva then appears from the liṅga and stays as **Atrīśvara**, and the rains return (Koṭirudra-saṃhitā 4.1–61). [sthala-purāṇa]

## The Trimūrti chastity-test (Mārkaṇḍeya & Bhāgavata)

The Bhāgavata and the Mārkaṇḍeya tell **how the three sons were born**: a boon earned by Atri's tapas, or by Anasūyā's service to the gods. Neither contains the famous **test of Anasūyā's chastity** (the demand that she serve food naked). That story is attested in the Marathi *Gurucaritra* and in popular retellings, below. [late text]

- **The Bhāgavata version: Atri's tapas** — Ordered by Brahmā to beget offspring, Atri goes with Anasūyā to a valley of the **Ṛkṣa** mountain on the river **Nirvindhyā**. There he stands on one leg for **a hundred years**, living on air, and asks for a son like the Lord of the universe. A fire rises from his head. Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva come together on swan, Garuḍa and bull. Atri asks which of them he has called. They answer that the one he meditated on is all three of them, and that sons bearing their portions will be born to him (Bhāgavata 4.1.15–33). Anasūyā is present but not tested.
- **The Mārkaṇḍeya version: Anasūyā's boon** — After Anasūyā restores the sunrise and revives Kauśika, the gods offer her a boon. She asks that **Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Maheśvara become her sons**, and that she and her husband attain yoga. The gods grant it (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa 16.86–90).
- **The provocation** — In the Marathi **Gurucaritra**, **Indra and the gods** fear that Anasūyā's merit will cost them heaven, and they ask the Trimūrti to stop her (Gurucaritra, adhyāy 4). [late text]
  - *Variant (popular):* In the version most often told today, **Nārada** praises Anasūyā to **Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī and Pārvatī** as the most chaste woman alive. In one telling he says her chastity is such that she could bake beans made of iron (Kannan 2016, p. 19, cited in Wikipedia, *Anasuya*). Stung, the goddesses send their husbands to break her vow. This version was not found in any primary text consulted, and Wikipedia's *Anasuya* article states that it is not in the primary Purāṇas. [folk] [unverified]
- **The impossible demand** — The Trimūrti come as mendicants while Atri is away at his rites. She washes their feet and sets out the meal, and before eating they ask her to serve them **naked (*nagna*)** or they will go elsewhere: a guest must not be turned away, but a chaste wife cannot comply (Gurucaritra, adhyāy 4.26–37). [late text]
- **The infants miracle** — Meditating on her husband, Anasūyā agrees, and when she serves them the three become **infants**. She suckles them, cradles them and sings them lullabies. When Atri returns he recognises the gods, who offer a boon. Atri tells Anasūyā to choose; she asks him to make the request, and the boon asked is that the three stay in their house as sons (Gurucaritra, adhyāy 4.38–62). [late text]
  - *Variant (popular):* She sprinkles water and declares, "If my chastity is true, let these become my children," and the change is instant. [folk]
- **The goddesses' plea** — In the popular version, the three goddesses find their husbands missing, come to Anasūyā and beg for their return. She restores the gods, who promise to be **born as her sons**. The Gurucaritra chapter has no such scene. [folk]
- **The three deity-portions born** —
  - **Soma / Candra** — the **Brahmā-portion** (Bhāgavata 4.1.33; Mārkaṇḍeya 17.11). His birth is told differently in each text. In the Mārkaṇḍeya, Atri's desire for Anasūyā produces a white, moon-formed emission that the wind carries off and the ten directions receive, and Soma is born to her as Atri's mind-born son (Mārkaṇḍeya 17.1–5). In the Bhāgavata's dynastic book he is born from Atri's **eyes** (Bhāgavata 9.14.3). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa's dynastic book mentions no mother (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.6.6). He becomes the [progenitor of the Lunar dynasty](soma-tara-budha.md).
  - **Dattātreya** — the **Viṣṇu-portion**, the yogin and teacher (Bhāgavata 4.1.33). In the Mārkaṇḍeya, Viṣṇu produces Datta **from his own body**, and Anasūyā nurses him. He is "Viṣṇu himself descended", Atri's second son (Mārkaṇḍeya 17.6–7, 17.13). See [Dattātreya](dattatreya.md).
  - **Durvāsā** — the **Śiva-portion**, the irascible ascetic (Bhāgavata 4.1.33). In the Mārkaṇḍeya he is a Rudra-portion full of *tamas* who burns up whoever slights him. He leaves the womb after **seven days**, enraged at a Haihaya king who had wronged Atri (Mārkaṇḍeya 17.8–10, 17.14). See [Ambarīṣa–Durvāsā](ambarisha-durvasa.md).
  - *Variant (single child):* In the Gurucaritra the three **merge into one, Dattātreya**, while Candra and Durvāsā depart (Gurucaritra, adhyāy 4). The three-headed, six-armed Datta icon of the Trimūrti in one is the common later form. A few older temples, such as Mahur, show him with one head, and the Agni Purāṇa prescribes one head and two hands (Rigopoulos 1998, pp. 223–228, cited in Wikipedia, *Dattatreya*). [late text]
- **Apportionment of the births** — The Bhāgavata and the Mārkaṇḍeya agree on the mapping **Brahmā↔Soma / Viṣṇu↔Datta / Śiva↔Durvāsā** (Bhāgavata 4.1.15, 4.1.33; Mārkaṇḍeya 17.11). The Viṣṇu Purāṇa names the three sons, Soma, Durvāsas and the yogin Dattātreya, without assigning portions (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.10.8). No text consulted swaps the assignments.

## The Rāmāyaṇa episode — Anasūyā counsels Sītā

- **Rāma's visit to Atri's hermitage** — The ascetics of Citrakūṭa leave because Khara's rākṣasas are harassing them (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 116; CE 2.108). Rāma, haunted by memories of Bharata's visit and the trampled ground, moves on with Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa to the **āśrama of Atri**. Atri receives him like a son, calls his aged wife, and introduces her by her deeds (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 117.1–13; CE 2.109.1–13).
- **Anasūyā's discourse on pātivratya** — Anasūyā praises Sītā for leaving kin and comfort to follow Rāma. For a noble woman, she teaches, the husband is the supreme deity, whether he lives in the city or the forest, and even if he is ill-behaved, lustful or poor. Women who follow their own desires win infamy, and women like Sītā reach heaven (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 117.20–28; CE 2.109.21–28). Sītā replies that she learned this from her mother at the wedding fire and from Kausalyā at parting. She names **Sāvitrī** and **Rohiṇī** as models (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 118.1–12; CE 2.110.1–12).
- **The divine gifts** — Pleased, Anasūyā offers a boon. When Sītā asks for nothing, Anasūyā gives her a **divine garland, garment, ornaments, *aṅgarāga* (cosmetic) and costly body-cream**, which "will stay unsullied for ever" (*asaṃkliṣṭaṃ nityam*) (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 118.18–19; CE 2.110.17–18). At Anasūyā's request, Sītā then tells the story of her birth from the furrow and of the bow-svayaṃvara (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 118.23–54; CE 2.110.22–52). Vālmīki's own table of contents in the Bāla Kāṇḍa remembers the episode by the gift: "the meeting with Anasūyā and the giving of the *aṅgarāga*" (CE 1.3.11).
  - **What the gifts become** — Vālmīki does not say. At nightfall Anasūyā has Sītā put on the gifts before her eyes, and Sītā shows them to Rāma (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 119.11–14; CE 2.111.11–14). Much later, during the abduction, Sītā drops her golden silk upper cloth and "auspicious ornaments" among five vānaras on a mountain-top (Vālmīki, Araṇya 54.1–3; CE 3.52.1–2). Sugrīva later shows the bundle to Rāma, who recognises it himself, presses it to his heart and grieves (Vālmīki, Kiṣkindhā 6.9–21; CE 4.6.7–19). Readers often take these to be Anasūyā's gifts, but the text never makes the connection. [interpretation] See [Araṇya Kāṇḍa](ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md).
    - *Variant (vulgate only):* **Lakṣmaṇa's anklet verse** follows in the vulgate: he does not know the armlets or the earrings, only the anklets, from bowing every day at her feet (Vālmīki, Kiṣkindhā 6.22–23). The verse is **absent from the Critical Edition**, where Rāma turns straight to Sugrīva (CE 4.6.20), and the vulgate translator's note flags it as missing from older manuscripts. [interpolation]
- **Departure toward Daṇḍaka** — Anasūyā sends Sītā back to Rāma at nightfall. Next morning the ascetics warn the brothers that the forest is overrun by rākṣasas and show them the path the sages use to gather fruit. Rāma enters the forest "as the sun enters a bank of cloud" (Vālmīki, Ayodhyā 119.10–22; CE 2.111.10–20). The vulgate adds two verses on man-eating demons and blood-drinking beasts (Ayodhyā 119.19–20) that the Critical Edition lacks. The next kāṇḍa opens with the entry into the **Daṇḍaka** forest (Vālmīki, Araṇya 1.1; CE 3.1.1).
  - *Variant (placement):* The vulgate and the Critical Edition both close the **Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa** with this episode (Ayodhyā 119; CE 2.111.20 → 3.1.1). But Vālmīki's Bāla Kāṇḍa table of contents lists the Anasūyā meeting **after** the entry into Daṇḍaka and the meeting with Sutīkṣṇa (CE 1.3.10–11). Tulsīdās moves the whole visit into his **Araṇya Kāṇḍa**. There Atri sings the hymn *namāmi bhakta-vatsalaṃ*, Anasūyā dresses Sītā in ever-new garments and ornaments, and she teaches **four grades of pativratā**, from the wife who cannot even dream of another man to the lowest, held back only by fear and lack of opportunity. A wife who deceives her husband, she adds, goes to the Raurava hell (Rāmcaritmānas, Araṇya 2–5). [late text] See [Rāmcaritmānas](ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md).

## Tradition-by-tradition notes (variant catalog)

- **Padma Purāṇa** — Sometimes credited with an elaborated Anasūyā chastity-test, a river tale, and her cult at Atri's hermitage sites. None of this was located in an accessible text, and the Purāṇic Encyclopaedia's Anasūyā entry cites only the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, the Bhāgavata and Vālmīki. [unverified]
- **Datta Sampradāya** — The tradition treats Anasūyā as the divine mother of the unified Dattātreya and honours her in her own right. Her *pātivratya* is read as the spiritual engine of the avatāra (Gurucaritra, adhyāy 4; Datta Sampradāya hagiography).
- **Regional cult sites** — Hermitages and shrines of Atri and Anasūyā are placed in various regions, each with its own māhātmya tying the couple to local geography. The Citrakūṭa region on the Mandākinī matches Vālmīki's next stop after Citrakūṭa and the Śiva Purāṇa's Kāmada forest "near Citrakūṭa" with its Atrīśvara liṅga (Śiva Purāṇa, Koṭirudra-saṃhitā 3–4). Other sites are in the Sahyādri/Western Ghats. [regional]
