# Puñjikasthalā and Brahmā's curse — why Rāvaṇa cannot force Sītā

> Where this sits: the **war-book's own answer** to the question the Rāmāyaṇa's audiences have always asked first — Sītā is a year in Rāvaṇa's keeping and is never touched, and the epic says why. In open war-council, under his general **Mahāpārśva's** urging to take her by force, Rāvaṇa confesses that he once violated the apsaras **Puñjikasthalā** on her way to Brahmā's world, and that **Brahmā** cursed him: force another unwilling woman and his head will burst into **a hundred pieces** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Yuddha Kāṇḍa **13.11–15**).
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Kāṇḍa frame: [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md) · Cosmic address: Śveta-Vārāha Kalpa › 7th ([Vaivasvata](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)) Manvantara › 28th Mahāyuga › [Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) — the offence long before the epic, the confession in the course of the Laṅkā war.

This is the canonical home of the **Yuddha 13 curse**. It is short, undisputed, and load-bearing: it stands in the sixth of the seven books, in the base text of every recension, in Rāvaṇa's own mouth, before his own court, at the moment he has every reason to lie and none to invent a restraint on himself. The corpus's other, better-known "he cannot force her" curse — **Nalakūbara's**, over the apsaras **Rambhā** — is a different curse, from a different giver, in a different book, with a different number (*seven* pieces, *saptadhā*, [Uttara](ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md) 26). The two are catalogued side by side below and must never be merged; retellings fuse them constantly, and the fusion is how the war-book's answer got lost.

There is also a **name-collision** to keep straight before anything else: this Puñjikasthalā is *not* the apsaras Puñjikasthalā who is cursed to be born as **Añjanā**, mother of Hanumān ([Kiṣkindhā](ramayana/4-kishkindha-kanda.md) 66). Two apsarases, one name, no connection.

## The scene — Yuddha Kāṇḍa 13

- **The council and Mahāpārśva's advice** — With the war going badly and Kumbhakarṇa and the champions already spent, Rāvaṇa's general **Mahāpārśva** puts the crude proposal to him plainly: enjoy Sītā by force, live "in the mode of a cock" among the women of his house, and let the rākṣasas deal with whatever follows. It is counsel from a subordinate who assumes his king has never been restrained by anything. (*Yuddha* 13)
- **Rāvaṇa's refusal, and the reason** — Rāvaṇa answers that there is "a secret" that forbids it, and tells it: long ago, going to **Brahmā's abode**, he saw the apsaras **Puñjikasthalā** flashing like a flame and hiding herself in the sky; he stripped her and took her by force. She went to Brahmā "like a crushed lotus," and told what had been done. (*Yuddha* 13.11–12)
- **Brahmā's sentence** — The enraged Brahmā pronounced the terms in a single verse: *adya-prabhṛti yām anyām balān nārīṃ gamiṣyasi | tadā te śatadhā mūrdhā phaliṣyati na saṃśayaḥ* — "from this day, should you go to any other woman by force, then your head shall split **into a hundred pieces**, beyond doubt." (*Yuddha* 13.13–14)
- **The application to Sītā** — Rāvaṇa states the consequence himself: it is **fear of that curse** — *tasmād bhayāt* — that keeps him from making Sītā mount his bed against her will. The most-asked question about the epic is answered in one line by the man who would know. (*Yuddha* 13.15)
- **And then the boast** — He does not linger on the confession. In the same breath he compares his own force to the ocean and the wind, declares Rāma cannot stand before him, and vows to burn him down with arrows. The curse is admitted as a technical constraint, not as remorse, and the sarga closes on his self-praise — the characterization the scene exists to deliver. (*Yuddha* 13)

## Two curses, one condition — Puñjikasthalā and Rambhā kept apart

- **The distinctions that matter** — Same prohibition, everything else different. **Brahmā's curse** is over **Puñjikasthalā**, its term is **a hundred** pieces (*śatadhā*), it stands at **Yuddha 13**, and Rāvaṇa cites it himself. **Nalakūbara's curse** is over **Rambhā** — betrothed to Nalakūbara and therefore Rāvaṇa's own nephew's woman — its term is **seven** pieces (*saptadhā*), it stands at **Uttara 26**, and it is narrated about him, not by him. (*Yuddha* 13; *Uttara* 26) — see [Rāvaṇa §V](characters/ravana.md) and [Tapas, boons & curses](../05-concepts-and-dharma/tapas-boons-and-curses.md)
- **Vālmīki never juxtaposes them** — Neither passage refers to the other; the epic sets no order between the two offences and never remarks that Rāvaṇa is twice bound by the same clause. The doubling is a fact of the received text, not a contradiction to resolve, and both stay on the ledger. [scholarly]
- **The war-book is the primary locus** — Of the two, only the Yuddha 13 curse sits in the core five kāṇḍas, and only it is spoken in the narrative present, in the character's own voice, as an operative reason. A treatment of Sītā's inviolability that cites Uttara 26 alone has cited the secondary witness and dropped the primary one. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (retellings, near-universal):* popular retellings, television versions and most summaries carry **one** curse, usually Nalakūbara's, sometimes with the hundred-piece number attached to it. The merge is so standard that the Yuddha 13 confession is widely unknown; it is nevertheless plain vulgate text. [folk / late retelling]
  - *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas and the shadow-Sītā strand):* the Adhyātma-derived tradition answers the same question a different way — the real Sītā enters the fire before the abduction and only a **Chāyā/Māyā-Sītā** is carried to Laṅkā, so the question of force never arises. That is a theological solution belonging to the devotional recensions, not to Vālmīki, who has the actual Sītā in the Aśoka-vana throughout. (*Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*; *Rāmcaritmānas*; *Kūrma Purāṇa*) — see [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](ramayana/recensions/adhyatma-ramayana.md), [Rāmcaritmānas](ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) [late text]

## How the curse shapes the plot

- **It converts abduction into a siege of consent** — Because he cannot take her, Rāvaṇa must try to **obtain** her: hence the courtship, the threats, the display of wealth, and above all the **deadline**. He gives Sītā **twelve months** at [Araṇya](ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md) 56 and the clock is still running when Hanumān arrives — "two months remain" at [Sundara](ramayana/5-sundara-kanda.md) 22. The whole Aśoka-vana apparatus of persuasion, rākṣasī guards and terror exists because the direct route is closed. (*Araṇya* 56; *Sundara* 20–22; *Yuddha* 13) [scholarly]
- **It explains the guard, not the mercy** — Nothing in the sarga makes Rāvaṇa scrupulous. He threatens Sītā with being carved for his breakfast when the term expires (*Sundara* 22), and Vālmīki's point is precisely that the restraint is **external** — a boundary set by a curse he cannot argue with, holding a man who would otherwise not stop. It is not respect, and the epic never calls it that. [scholarly]
- **It is the hinge of the agni-parīkṣā argument** — Rāma's public demand for the fire-ordeal at [Yuddha](ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md) 116–118 is made in a text that has already told the audience, in the antagonist's own words, why the suspicion is groundless. The Yuddha 13 confession is the epic's own answer to its most contested scene, planted a hundred sargas ahead of it. [scholarly]
- **It is one of four sentences on Rāvaṇa, and the only one about his hands** — The doom-roster runs: **Anaraṇya's** dying curse that an Ikṣvāku will kill him ([Uttara](ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md) 19, canonical home at [Anaraṇya](../03-lineages-vamsha/anaranya-ravana-curse.md)); **Vedavatī's** vow to be reborn as his death (*Uttara* 17); the **abducted women's collective curse** that a woman will be the cause of his destruction (*Uttara* 24); **Nandī's** curse of monkeys (*Uttara* 16). Those four kill him. Brahmā's over Puñjikasthalā is the only one that **binds him while he lives** — a restraint rather than a sentence. (*Yuddha* 13; *Uttara* 16, 17, 19, 24) — see [Tapas, boons & curses](../05-concepts-and-dharma/tapas-boons-and-curses.md)

## Puñjikasthalā herself — and the other Puñjikasthalā

- **What Vālmīki gives her** — Almost nothing: a name, a station (an apsaras of Brahmā's world), a direction of travel, a light like flame, an attempt to hide in the sky, and a walk to the Creator's court afterwards to say what happened. She has no lineage, no father, no later appearance, and the epic never returns to her. She exists in the text solely as the occasion of the curse — which is itself the point worth recording, since retellings that need a character invent one. (*Yuddha* 13.11–12) — see [Apsarās](../08-beings-and-bestiary/apsaras.md)
- **The complaint is hers** — The curse is not pronounced on Brahmā's initiative. Puñjikasthalā carries the offence to him herself and Brahmā acts on her report. In a corpus of curses laid by fathers, husbands and sages on other people's behalf, this one is delivered because the woman assaulted went and said so. [scholarly]
- **Not Añjanā** — The apsaras **Puñjikasthalā** who is cursed to be born as the vānara princess **Añjanā**, daughter of Kuñjara, wife of Kesarī and mother of **Hanumān** ([Kiṣkindhā](ramayana/4-kishkindha-kanda.md) 66) is a **separate figure**. Vālmīki names no curser and states no terms for her; the Bṛhaspati / Indra / Durvāsā chain that supplies one is folk tradition. Two apsarases share a name and nothing else, and merging them produces the nonsense that Hanumān's mother was Rāvaṇa's victim. (*Kiṣkindhā* 66; *Yuddha* 13) — see [Vānaras & Ṛkṣas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vanaras-and-rikshas.md), [Hanumān](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/hanuman.md) [disputed]
- **Nor the Ṛgvedic Puñjikasthalā** — The name appears in the standard ten-apsaras roster and in Vedic-adjacent lists; those attestations are name-bearers, not this episode. [scholarly]

## Two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic** — The assault on Puñjikasthalā belongs to Rāvaṇa's long pre-epic career of conquest and outrage, within the **Tretā Yuga** of the current 28th Mahāyuga; the confession falls late in the Laṅkā war, in the same yuga, roughly a year after the abduction. Kali Yuga, the frame from which this corpus reads, began **3102 BCE**.
- **Academic / text-critical** — Yuddha 13 sits in the **core five kāṇḍas** (2–6), the stratum scholarship treats as the older Rāmāyaṇa, while the Rambhā/Nalakūbara curse belongs to the **Uttara Kāṇḍa**, widely held to be a later appended book. On the historical-critical clock the war-book curse is therefore the earlier attestation of the motif and the Uttara version a redoubling of it in the supplementary layer — a text-critical judgement about strata, not a statement about which offence Rāvaṇa committed first, which no text says. [scholarly]
