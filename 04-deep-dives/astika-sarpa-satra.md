# Āstīka and the Snake-Sacrifice (Sarpa-satra of Janamejaya)

> Cosmic address: a *Vaivasvata-Manvantara* tale of the present [Mahāyuga's](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md) Dvāpara→Kali cusp, set one generation *after* the Kurukṣetra war, in the reign of **Janamejaya** (great-grandson of [Arjuna](characters/arjuna.md)). It is the **outermost narrative frame** of the *Mahābhārata*: the rite at which Vaiśampāyana first recites the whole epic.
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · See also: [Ādi Parva overview](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md) (the Āstīka sub-parva, Ādi 13–58) · [Abhimanyu](characters/abhimanyu.md) (Parikṣit's father, the broken Kuru line) · [Vyāsa](vyasa.md) (composer of the epic recited at this very rite) · [Sāmudra Manthana](samudra-manthana.md) (the nectar-feud that births Garuḍa vs. the nāgas) · [Uttaṅka](uttanka.md) (the parallel goad that drives Janamejaya to the sacrifice) · [Jaratkāru & Manasā](jaratkaru-and-manasa.md) (the marriage-cycle that begets Āstīka, in full) · [Janamejaya & the Sarpa-satra](../03-lineages-vamsha/janamejaya-sarpa-satra.md) (the dynastic/succession angle in the Lunar-line section)

The **Āstīka Parva** (Ādi 13–58, Critical Ed.) is the snake-sacrifice frame that holds the entire *Mahābhārata*. King **Parikṣit**, sole continuator of the Kuru line, is cursed and slain by the serpent-king **Takṣaka**; his son **Janamejaya** vows a *sarpa-satra* ("snake-sacrifice") to immolate every nāga on earth. The rite is halted only by the boy-sage **Āstīka** — half-brahmin, half-nāga, son of the ascetic **Jaratkāru** and the serpent-goddess **Manasā** (Jaratkāru) — who is born for exactly this purpose. Because the recitation of the *Mahābhārata* is staged *at* this sacrifice, the epic's own existence is wound into the saving of the snakes. The matter is doubled in the text: the same events are summarized in **Sauti's prologue (Ādi 13–53)** and the **Āstīka sub-parva proper (Ādi 13–58)**; they are merged here as one arc. [scripture]

## Where it sits in the timeline (the "two clocks")

- **Scriptural clock** - Set in the early **[Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)** of the present [Mahāyuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md), one generation after the Dvāpara→Kali cusp battle of Kurukṣetra; the [Pāṇḍava](characters/yudhishthira.md) line has narrowed to one heir. [scripture]
- **Historical clock** - The Naimiṣa-forest recitation frame and the Janamejaya court are the **transmission setting** by which the epic claims its own provenance; text-critically the Āstīka Parva is an early framing layer welded to older nāga-and-Garuḍa myth (the *Āstīka* myth has Vedic roots in the *sarpasattra* of the Brāhmaṇas). [scholarly]
- **Triple frame** - The narration nests three deep: **Ugraśravas Sauti** tells it to the ṛṣis at **Naimiṣāraṇya**; he reports the *Mahābhārata* that **Vaiśampāyana** recited to **Janamejaya** at the snake-sacrifice; which **Vyāsa** had composed (Vyāsa, Ādi 1, 13). See [Vyāsa](vyasa.md).

## Backstory I: the nāga curse (why the snakes are doomed)

- **Kadrū and Vinatā's wager** - The co-wives of sage **Kaśyapa** — **Kadrū** (mother of the nāgas) and **Vinatā** (mother of **Garuḍa** and **Aruṇa**) — bet on the colour of the celestial horse **Uccaiḥśravas**; Kadrū wins by cheating, enslaving Vinatā (Vyāsa, Ādi 14–20). See [Sāmudra Manthana](samudra-manthana.md) (the churning that produced the horse).
- **Kadrū's curse on her sons** - When some nāgas refuse to help her cheat (by hanging on the horse's tail to darken it), Kadrū curses her own serpent-sons to be **burned in Janamejaya's sacrifice** — and **Brahmā ratifies it**, because the nāgas had grown too numerous and venomous, a population-cull built into cosmic order (Vyāsa, Ādi 20).
- **Garuḍa and the amṛta** - Garuḍa frees his mother by fetching the gods' **amṛta** as ransom for the nāgas; [Indra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md) snatches it back, and the snakes, licking the *kuśa*-grass it rested on, get only **split tongues** — the etiology of the forked serpent tongue (Vyāsa, Ādi 23–34). Garuḍa becomes the hereditary nāga-eater, foreshadowing the holocaust to come.

## Backstory II: Jaratkāru and the birth of Āstīka (the appointed saviour)

- **Jaratkāru's ancestors hang in the pit** - The wandering celibate ascetic **Jaratkāru** finds his **forefathers (pitṛs)** dangling head-down over a chasm, clinging to a single root being gnawed by a rat (Time); they tell him their line will end unless he, their last descendant, **marries and begets a son** (Vyāsa, Ādi 13, 41–44).
- **The marriage by name** - Jaratkāru vows to wed only a bride who shares his own name and is given to him without his having to support her. The nāga-king **Vāsuki** offers his sister — also named **Jaratkāru** (the serpent-goddess later called **Manasā**) — precisely so her son can stop the sacrifice that the snake clan dreads (Vyāsa, Ādi 13, 45–47).
  - *Variant (the bride's name / identity):* the epic calls her **Jaratkāru** (Vāsuki's sister); the later **Manasā-maṅgal** Bengali tradition identifies her as the goddess **Manasā** (Padmāvatī), daughter/mind-born of [Śiva](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md), and makes her the protagonist. [regional]
- **The ascetic's brittle vow** - Jaratkāru warns he will abandon his wife the instant she displeases him. When she wakes him (so he won't miss sunset prayers) he takes offence and leaves — but she is already pregnant, the unborn child glowing like fire (Vyāsa, Ādi 47).
- **Āstīka is born** - The boy is named **Āstīka** ("He Is / There Exists" — from Jaratkāru's parting words *asti*, "[the child] exists"); reared by Vāsuki in nāga-realm and schooled by **Cyavana**, he embodies both the brahmin and serpent lines, the one being who can plead for the snakes from inside dharma (Vyāsa, Ādi 13, 48). [scripture]

## The trigger: Parikṣit's death by Takṣaka

- **Parikṣit, the lone Kuru heir** - **Parikṣit**, son of [Abhimanyu](characters/abhimanyu.md) and Uttarā, is the sole surviving Kuru after Kurukṣetra (revived in the womb by [Kṛṣṇa](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) from Aśvatthāman's *brahmāstra*); he rules well for sixty years (Vyāsa, Ādi 36–40; cf. *Bhāgavata* 1.16–19).
- **The dead-snake insult** - Hunting, the thirsty king finds the meditating sage **Śamīka** silent, and in pique loops a **dead snake** over the sage's shoulders (Vyāsa, Ādi 36–40).
- **Śṛṅgin's curse** - Śamīka's hot-tempered son **Śṛṅgin** curses the king to die within **seven days**, bitten by **Takṣaka**, lord of serpents; the grieving Śamīka sends warning, and Parikṣit builds a single guarded **pillar-palace** (Vyāsa, Ādi 37–43).
- **Takṣaka's stratagem** - On the seventh day Takṣaka **bribes/turns back** the brahmin healer **Kāśyapa** (who could neutralize the venom), enters the fortress **hidden in a fruit as a worm**, and bites Parikṣit, who is consumed by fire on the spot (Vyāsa, Ādi 40–43).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata framing):* the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* foregrounds Parikṣit's *response* to the curse — he fasts to death on the Gaṅgā bank hearing **Śuka** recite the Bhāgavata over seven days, so the venom is almost incidental; the Mahābhārata foregrounds the bite and revenge. [late text]

## The Sarpa-satra: Janamejaya's revenge

- **The vow** - Told by his ministers how his father died, the boy-king **Janamejaya** vows a **sarpa-satra** — a sacrificial fire that will magically *summon every serpent on earth into the flames* until the species is annihilated, with Takṣaka the chief target (Vyāsa, Ādi 13, 49–52).
- **The session at Takṣaśilā** - The rite is mounted as a long multi-priest *sattra* at **Takṣaśilā** (Taxila), on a specially raised altar with a host of *ṛtvij* priests, the **hotṛ Caṇḍabhārgava** officiating (Vyāsa, Ādi 49–52).
- **The burning of the nāgas** - Brahmin priests chant; nāgas of every name **fall helplessly into the fire** by thousands — a roll-call of serpent clans is recited as they perish. The cull Brahmā had sanctioned begins to come true (Vyāsa, Ādi 52–53). [scripture]
- **The omen of a halt** - Even as the burning peaks, the priests foresee that a **brāhmaṇa boy** will come and stop the rite — announced while Takṣaka still eludes the flames (Vyāsa, Ādi 52–53).
- **Takṣaka flees to Indra** - Terrified, **Takṣaka** takes refuge with **Indra** himself, coiling round the god's throne; the priests intensify the mantra to drag down *Indra and Takṣaka together*, and Indra abandons him as he begins to slide toward the flames (Vyāsa, Ādi 53, 56).
- **Vāsuki's appeal** - Watching his clan burn, **Vāsuki** turns to his sister's son — the now-grown boy **Āstīka** — the saviour bred for this hour, and sends him to the sacrificial ground (Vyāsa, Ādi 53–54).

## Āstīka halts the rite

- **The boy's eulogy** - The child **Āstīka** enters Janamejaya's enclosure and praises the king and the sacrifice in faultless hymns; delighted, Janamejaya offers him **any boon**, over the priests' objection that Takṣaka is just then falling (Vyāsa, Ādi 53–56).
- **The boon: "Let the sacrifice stop"** - Āstīka asks that the **snake-sacrifice cease**. Bound by his royal word, Janamejaya reluctantly agrees; **Takṣaka, suspended mid-air over the fire, is saved**, and the surviving nāgas with him (Vyāsa, Ādi 56). [scripture]
- **Āstīka's blessing on the snakes** - The grateful serpents grant Āstīka a boon; he decrees that **whoever recalls Āstīka's name (and the verse of Jaratkāru's line) shall fear no snakes** — the charm still invoked against snakebite (Vyāsa, Ādi 58). [folk]
  - *Variant (Nāga-pañcamī):* popular tradition ties the halting of the sacrifice and Āstīka's protection-charm to the festival **Nāga Pañcamī**, when serpents are worshipped and Āstīka's name recited for safety. [folk][regional]

## The epic is recited

- **The occasion of the Mahābhārata** - With the snakes spared, Janamejaya — still wishing to honour his ancestors — has **Vyāsa's pupil Vaiśampāyana** narrate at this very sacrificial session, the entire history of the Kurus: the **Mahābhārata itself** (Vyāsa, Ādi 54–58). See [Ādi Parva](mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md).
- **The frame closes outward** - Among the listeners is **Ugraśravas Sauti**, who later re-tells the whole epic (with this Āstīka frame embedded) to the ṛṣis at **Naimiṣāraṇya** — the outermost layer the reader actually "hears" (Vyāsa, Ādi 1, 4–5). See [Vyāsa](vyasa.md).
- **Āśvamedhika doublet** - The companion goad to Janamejaya's rite — the sage **Uttaṅka's** grudge against Takṣaka — is told just before the Āstīka material in the **Pauṣya sub-parva**; cross-link [Uttaṅka](uttanka.md) for that parallel trigger. [scholarly]

## Why it matters

- **Self-authenticating frame** - The *Mahābhārata* narrates *the occasion of its own first telling*; the saving of the nāgas is structurally inseparable from the epic's transmission. The story is therefore the literal "outermost ring" of the work. [scholarly]
- **Dharma over vengeance** - Janamejaya's *sarpa-satra* is a righteous-seeming revenge that nonetheless becomes *adharma* (genocide of a species, dragging down even Indra); Āstīka — kin to both victim and judge — embodies the resolution, redeeming a vow without breaking the king's word. [scholarly]
- **The Manasā connection** - In eastern India the same Jaratkāru is the goddess **Manasā**, and the Āstīka frame is the Sanskrit seed of the vast **Manasā-maṅgal** snake-goddess literature (Bengal, Assam), where serpent worship and protection-charms eclipse the Kuru frame entirely. [regional]
