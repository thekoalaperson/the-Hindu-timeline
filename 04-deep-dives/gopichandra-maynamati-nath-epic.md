# Gopīcandra and Queen Maynāmatī — the Nāth Renouncer-King Epic

> Cosmic address: a **regional-Kali** node on the **historical clock** — the great Bengali **Nāth** oral epic of the yoginī-queen **Maynāmatī**, her son **King Gopīcandra**, and his forced renunciation to cheat **death (Yama)**. Framed on the **cyclic clock** by the Nāth ideal of *kāya-siddhi* / deathlessness (*jīvanmukti*), the story dramatizes a mortal king's escape from the death that even kings suffer in Kali-yuga.

The **Gopīcandra** epic — sung as the **Gopīcandrer Gān** and, in its North-Bengal form, the **Māṇikcandra Rājār Gān** — is the most widely diffused of all **Nāth** oral epics. It tells how the immortal yoginī **Maynāmatī** (Maināvatī / Mayanāmatī), a disciple of **Gorakṣanāth**, foresees that her son, King **Gopīcandra** of Rangpur, must **die young unless he abandons his throne and two young wives to become a *jogī***; how he resists, is at last initiated by the outcaste siddha **Hāḍipā**, and after long ordeals — his own, his wives' **Aduna-Paduna**'s, and his mother's — returns from the yogic wilderness. Coupled with the twin **Bhartṛhari** ballad, it was sung by mendicant **Jogī**-bards in an unbroken belt **from Bengal across the Gangetic plain to Rajasthan and Punjab**.

---

## Two Clocks — Text, Setting, Frame

- **Historical clock (setting)** — the legend places Māṇikcandra and Gopīcandra as kings of **northern Bengal / the Rangpur–Kāmarūpa borderland**, and tradition (and much scholarship) locates them roughly in the **Pāla-period Bengal of c. 900–1100 CE** [scholarly] — the same milieu in which the Nāth/Siddha movement flourished; several scholars have tried, inconclusively, to tie Māṇikcandra to a historical local ruler [disputed]. (Gopīcandrer Gān; Grierson's introduction) [scholarly]
- **Historical clock (transmission)** — the surviving epic is **oral ballad**, not a dated literary text: **G. A. Grierson** recovered and published a **Rangpur singer's text** as *Māṇik Chandra Rājār Gān* in the **Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal, 1878**; **Dineśacandra Sen (D. C. Sen)** collected and edited the fuller **Gopīcandrer Gān** for the **University of Calcutta (2 vols, c. 1922–24)**. The living recensions are **18th–19th c.** in their recorded form, over an older core. (Grierson 1878; D. C. Sen, *Gopīcandrer Gān*) [scholarly] [oral tradition]
- **Scriptural/cyclic clock (the story's own frame)** — the epic's engine is the Nāth doctrine of **conquest of death**: Maynāmatī's yoga makes her *amara* (deathless), able to defy **Yama's messengers**; the prophecy is that Gopīcandra's allotted span is short, but that *sannyāsa* under a true guru can win him **hundreds of years / immortality**. The renunciation is thus not moral but **soteriological-magical** — an escape from the mortality that binds even a crowned king. (Nāth hagiography; Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
- **Where it sits in the corpus** — outside the Sanskrit canon: a **[folk] [oral tradition] [regional]** epic of the vernacular Nāth world, kin to the Marathi **Navnāth Bhaktisār** cycle and the Punjabi **Pūraṇ Bhagat** and **Bhartṛhari** ballads, not to Purāṇic yuga-chronology. The two clocks must not be collapsed. (Nāth ballad tradition) [regional]

## The Cast

- **Maynāmatī (Maināvatī / Mayanāmatī)** — queen of **Māṇikcandra**, mother of **Gopīcandra**; a **yoginī initiated by Gorakṣanāth** who has mastered the yoga of deathlessness and possesses foreknowledge; the true spiritual power behind the whole action. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
- **Māṇikcandra** — king of **Rangpur** (northern Bengal); Maynāmatī's husband, whose **death** despite his queen's warnings opens the epic and sets its theme. His story is the *Māṇikcandra Rājār Gān* proper. (Māṇik Chandra Rājār Gān, Grierson 1878) [folk]
- **Gopīcandra (Gopīcand / Govindacandra)** — their son; the young king who must renounce to live. In the North-Indian ballad he is the **sister's son (nephew) of Bhartṛhari** of Ujjain, linking the two Nāth epics. (Gopīcandrer Gān; Bhartṛhari-Gopīcand ballads) [folk] [regional]
- **Aduna and Paduna (Aduṇā-Paduṇā)** — Gopīcandra's **two young queens**, whose grief, resistance and testing form the epic's great emotional register — the human claim of marriage against the yoga of renunciation. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
- **Hāḍipā (Hāḍi-siddha / Hāḍiphā)** — the **outcaste (Hāḍi / Ḍom sweeper-caste) siddha** who is secretly a great Nāth master and becomes Gopīcandra's **guru**; identified in the Nāth lineage with **Jālandhara/Jālandharipā**. His low caste is the epic's deliberate scandal: the crowned king must take initiation from a scavenger. (Gopīcandrer Gān; Nāth hagiography) [folk]
- **Gorakṣanāth (Gorakhnāth)** — the great Nāth organizer, **Maynāmatī's own guru**, who stands behind the lineage that saves Gopīcandra; distinct here from the yogic/Gugga-cult Gorakhnāth references elsewhere in the corpus. (Nāth hagiography) [folk]
- **Yama and his messengers (jam / jamadūt)** — **Death** personified, come first for Māṇikcandra and destined for Gopīcandra; the adversary the yoga is meant to defeat. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]

## Maynāmatī and the Death-Prophecy

- **The death of Māṇikcandra** — King Māṇikcandra of Rangpur dies, taken by **Yama's messengers**; the *Māṇikcandra Rājār Gān* dwells on Maynāmatī's widowhood and her power — being deathless herself, she cannot be so easily taken. His death establishes the epic's premise: even a king cannot escape death **without yoga**. (Māṇik Chandra Rājār Gān, Grierson 1878) [folk]
- **Maynāmatī's deathless yoga** — trained by **Gorakṣanāth**, Maynāmatī has attained *kāya-siddhi*: she can restore the dead, walk through fire, defy Yama's noose. This yogic mastery — not queenly rank — is her authority over her son. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
- **The prophecy** — by her yogic sight Maynāmatī reads that **Gopīcandra's lifespan is nearly spent**: unless he renounces the throne, leaves **Aduna and Paduna**, and takes initiation as a *jogī*, he will die within days. Renunciation under a true guru will win him vastly extended life. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
- **The mother's terrible demand** — Maynāmatī must therefore **command her son to become a beggar-yogī** at the height of his youth and kingship — a demand that reads to the court as cruelty or madness, and drives the epic's central conflict between a mother's saving foreknowledge and a son's (and his wives') attachment. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]

## Gopīcandra's Resistance and Renunciation

- **The king's refusal** — Gopīcandra, young, married and enthroned, **rejects the demand**; he does not believe the prophecy and cannot abandon Aduna and Paduna. His resistance — argument, anger, disbelief — occupies much of the ballad and is its psychological core. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
- **The proof by ordeal (Maynāmatī's trials)** — to convince her son that yogic power over death is real, Maynāmatī **submits to ordeals**: in the ballad tradition she passes unharmed through **fire / a blazing pyre**, is unhurt by being **cast into water** or crushed, demonstrating the deathlessness that Gopīcandra too may attain. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (regional recensions):* the specific ordeals (fire-pit, boiling oil, burial, trampling by elephant) **vary by singer and region** and are never to be merged into a single canonical list. (Nāth ballad tradition) [regional] [disputed]
- **Sent to the outcaste guru** — Maynāmatī directs Gopīcandra to take initiation from **Hāḍipā**, the sweeper-siddha found tending buffalo / doing menial filth-work — the epic's pointed lesson that the true guru wears the lowest caste, and the king's pride must break to receive him. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
- **The ordeals of Hāḍipā** — at his wives' and courtiers' urging, the still-resisting Gopīcandra first **abuses and tests his destined guru**: in the common telling Hāḍipā is **buried alive in a dry well / pit of filth** (in some versions for twelve years), from which he emerges unharmed by his yoga — a demonstration that finally compels the king. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
  - *Variant (Nāth lineage):* because Hāḍipā is identified with **Jālandhara/Jālandharipā**, the buried-siddha ordeal parallels the Nāth motif of Jālandhara cast into a well by **Gopīcand** and rescued by **Kāṇipā** in other branches of the cycle. (Nāth hagiography) [folk] [regional]
- **Initiation** — at last convinced, Gopīcandra **renounces the throne**, has his ears split or takes the *jogī*'s patchwork and begging-bowl, and is **initiated by Hāḍipā** into the Nāth path, becoming the guru's disciple and servant. (Gopīcandrer Gān; Nāth hagiography) [folk]

## The Ordeals of Aduna-Paduna

- **The queens' grief** — when Gopīcandra dons the *jogī*'s garb, **Aduna and Paduna** break into the epic's most famous lament: the two young queens, newly wed, mourn the living husband who abandons them for the road. Their *bilāp* (lamentation) is the emotional summit of the sung performance. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The claim of marriage against yoga** — the wives beg, reason and reproach Maynāmatī and Gopīcandra, embodying **saṃsāra's legitimate pull** — conjugal love and duty set against the cold logic of deathless yoga; the epic does not dismiss their claim but stages it at full weight. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
- **The wives' testing and endurance** — through Gopīcandra's long absence (twelve years in the common reckoning) Aduna and Paduna **endure trials of fidelity, poverty and doubt**, kept and tested by Maynāmatī's power; their steadfastness is what makes the final reunion possible. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
  - *Variant (recensions):* the number and nature of the wives' ordeals, and even whether Aduna-Paduna are wives, co-queens or sisters, **differ across Bengal, Bihar and North-Indian singers**. (Nāth ballad tradition) [regional] [disputed]

## Wandering, Kadalī, and the Return

- **The mendicant years** — as Hāḍipā's disciple, the former king wanders as a **begging *jogī***, undergoing the humiliations of the road — the reversal of a monarch into a mendicant that the *Jogī*-bards, themselves mendicants, sang with relish. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
- **The women's kingdom of Kadalī** — in a widespread episode Gopīcandra passes through the **enchantress-realm of Kadalī (Kajali-vana)**, where sorceress-queens seek to bind wandering yogīs — the same **Kadalī** that ensnares **Matsyendranāth** in the Nāth corpus; Gopīcandra must resist or be rescued by his guru's power. (Gopīcandrer Gān; Nāth hagiography) [folk] [regional]
- **The return after twelve years** — his span secured by yoga, Gopīcandra **returns to Rangpur** and to **Aduna and Paduna**, reunited by Maynāmatī's and Hāḍipā's grace; the epic closes on the vindication of the prophecy — the king has cheated death by becoming, for a season, a beggar. (Gopīcandrer Gān) [folk]
  - *Variant (renunciant ending):* in more austere Nāth tellings Gopīcandra **does not resume the throne** but remains a perfected *jogī*, the reunion being spiritual rather than a return to householder kingship. (Nāth hagiography) [folk] [disputed]

## Transmission — Bengal to Punjab

- **Māṇikcandra Rājār Gān (Rangpur)** — the North-Bengal recension centered on the father's death and Maynāmatī, **published by G. A. Grierson from a Rangpur singer in 1878**; the foundational scholarly text of the cycle. (Grierson, *JASB* 1878) [scholarly]
- **Gopīcandrer Gān (D. C. Sen)** — **Dineśacandra Sen's** University of Calcutta collection (**c. 1922–24**) gathered the fuller Bengali ballads of Gopīcandra, Maynāmatī and the wives, and is the standard edition of the epic. (D. C. Sen, *Gopīcandrer Gān*) [scholarly]
- **Related Bengali texts** — the tradition also survives in named works such as the **Govindacandrer Gīt** of **Durlabh Mallik** and the **Mayanāmatīr Gān**, transmitting the same matter in various Bengali forms. (Bengali Nāth-ballad corpus) [regional]
- **The North-Indian Bhartṛhari-Gopīcand ballad** — carried west by mendicant **Jogī**-singers, the epic fuses with the **Bhartṛhari** (king of Ujjain) legend: Gopīcand becomes **Bhartṛhari's nephew**, and the paired renunciations are sung across **Bihar, Rajasthan and Punjab** as the great Nāth ballad of world-abandonment — studied in the folklore of **Ann Grodzins Gold** (*A Carnival of Parting*). (Bhartṛhari-Gopīcand ballads) [folk] [regional]
- **Performance** — like all Nāth epics it lives in **sung recitation** by *jogī* mendicants (with the *sāraṅgī* / one-string *kiṅgrī*), performed for alms and merit, not primarily as a written text. (Nāth ballad tradition) [oral tradition] [regional]

## Why the Epic Matters

- **Renunciation as the conquest of death** — unlike the moral-renunciation of the Purāṇic *rāja-ṛṣi*, Gopīcandra's *sannyāsa* is a **technology against mortality**: the Nāth promise that the perfected body (*kāya-siddhi*) can defeat Yama himself, dramatized on a royal stage. [scholarly]
- **The low-caste guru** — the crowned king initiated by the **sweeper Hāḍipā** is the epic's charter for the Nāth order's **caste-transgressive** authority: spiritual power outranks birth. [scholarly]
- **The woman as guru** — **Maynāmatī**, a female yoginī who commands and outranks her king-son and instructs him toward the guru, is one of vernacular India's most powerful images of **female spiritual mastery**. [scholarly] [regional]
- **The pathos of parting** — the laments of **Aduna-Paduna** made the epic a vehicle for the tension between **worldly love and yogic detachment**, giving the mendicant bards their most moving material across a thousand miles of northern India. [scholarly]
