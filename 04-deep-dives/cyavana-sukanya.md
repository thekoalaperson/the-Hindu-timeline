# Cyavana, Sukanyā & the Aśvins

> Cosmic address: a *Manvantara-frame tale* of the present **[7th — Vaivasvata — Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)**, set in the reign of **Śaryāti**, a son of [Vaivasvata Manu](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md). Its oldest stratum is Vedic (*Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5), retold at length in the *Mahābhārata* (Vana Parva 122–125; the Mada-distribution closes it in Anuśāsana Parva) and re-versioned in the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* (Canto 9).

The tale of **Cyavana** (Chyavana), the Bhārgava sage so old he is mistaken for an anthill, his marriage to the princess **Sukanyā**, and the **Aśvins** (the divine twin physicians) who restore his youth, is one of the oldest continuous narratives in the tradition — already fully formed in the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa*. It is a triple charter-myth: (1) the **wifely fidelity** of Sukanyā, who refuses the youthful gods for her decrepit husband; (2) the **rejuvenation** lore that gives Āyurveda its famous tonic *cyavanaprāśa*; and (3) the **admission of the Aśvins to the soma-cult** — Cyavana forces Indra to grant the once-excluded twin-gods their share of the sacrifice, in the process generating the giant Asura **Mada** ("Intoxication"), whom he then carves into the four great human vices.

---

## Where it sits in the timeline

- **Frame** - A *Vaivasvata-Manvantara* episode of the present [Mahāyuga's](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md) deep dynastic past, in the reign of King **Śaryāti** (Śaryāta), one of the nine/ten sons of [Vaivasvata Manu](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) — thus very early in the Solar/Manu genealogy, long before the Ikṣvāku kings (*Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5.1; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 9.3).
- **Narrative occasion (Mahābhārata)** - Recited as an *upākhyāna* (inset tale) in the **Vana Parva (Tīrtha-yātrā section)** while the Pāṇḍavas are on pilgrimage; the sage **Lomaśa** narrates it to Yudhiṣṭhira at the hermitage / tīrtha associated with Cyavana (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 122–125, Critical Edition ~3.122–125).
- **Antiquity** - The germ is **Ṛgvedic** (the Aśvins repeatedly hymned as rejuvenators who "made Cyavāna young again," e.g. RV 1.116, 1.117, 5.74, 7.68), expanded into full narrative in the **Brāhmaṇa** layer, and only later moralized in the **Purāṇas**. [scholarly]

## Cyavana's birth and identity

- **Born of Bhṛgu and Pulomā** - Cyavana is a son of the great Bhārgava sage **Bhṛgu** and his wife **Pulomā**. The Rākṣasa **Puloman**, to whom Pulomā had earlier been betrothed, carried off the pregnant woman; in fury the unborn child **slipped/fell from the womb** prematurely — hence the name *Cyavana* (from √*cyu*, "to fall, slip"). The Rākṣasa was burnt to ashes by the infant's radiance (*Mahābhārata*, Ādi Parva 5–6).
  - *Etymological variant:* the name is glossed both as "the fallen/slipped one" (premature birth) and as one who causes others to "fall" or wither — fitting his role in withering Indra's arm. [scholarly]
- **A Bhārgava ascetic** - He belongs to the line of [Bhṛgu among the Saptarṣi/Prajāpati sages](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/saptarishi.md); his descendants include the line that produces Ṛcīka, Jamadagni and Paraśurāma. He is reckoned among the long-lived ṛṣis of immense *tapas*.

## The anthill and the piercing of the eyes

- **Cyavana in deep tapas** - Cyavana sat motionless in austerity for so long that an **anthill (valmīka)** grew over his entire body; creepers and ants covered him until **only his two eyes shone out**, like a pair of glowing points (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 122; *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5.2).
- **Mahābhārata version — Sukanyā pierces the eyes** - King **Śaryāti** came to the lake near the hermitage with his retinue and his daughter **Sukanyā**. Sporting in the forest, the curious girl saw the two glow-points in the anthill and, taking them for fireflies or gems, **pierced them with a thorn / sharp stick**, blinding the sage. Enraged, Cyavana's *tapas* struck the king's army with **constipation/suppression of urine and stool (mūtra-purīṣa-rodha)** — the whole host fell ill (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 122–123).
  - *Variant (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa IV.1.5.1–3):* the agent of insult is **not Sukanyā** but **Śaryāta's (boorish) sons / boys**, who pelt the senile, anthill-buried sage with **clods of earth**. Cyavana retaliates by sowing **discord and internecine strife among Śaryāta's people/sons** (father and sons quarrel, the clan splits). Only on investigation does Śaryāta discover the cause and offer Sukanyā in atonement. [Vedic]
- **The king discovers the cause** - Learning that his folk had wronged the sage, the terrified king sought out Cyavana, made obeisance, and begged to know how to appease him (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 123; *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5.3).

## The marriage of Cyavana and Sukanyā

- **Sukanyā given to the sage** - To appease Cyavana, Śaryāti **bestowed his young daughter Sukanyā** in marriage on the aged, blind ascetic. Sukanyā accepted without complaint and devotedly served her decrepit husband, the model of *pativratā* fidelity (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 123; *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5.7).
- **The disproportion** - The texts stress the grotesque mismatch — a radiant young princess wedded to a wrinkled, sightless, ant-eaten old man — precisely to set up the test of her constancy (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 124).

## The Aśvins and the restoration of youth

- **The Aśvins approach Sukanyā** - The **[Aśvins](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md)** — Nāsatya and Dasra, the beautiful twin horse-headed physicians of the gods — came upon Sukanyā bathing and, struck by her beauty, **urged her to abandon her old husband and choose one of them**, two perfect youths, instead (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 124; *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5.8–9).
- **Sukanyā refuses** - She rebuffed them, declaring she would never desert her lawful husband. Impressed (and, in some tellings, taunted by her), they offered to **make Cyavana young** — on a condition (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 124).
- **The lake and the three-way contest** - On the Aśvins' instruction, **Cyavana entered a magical lake/pond (the "lake of beauty")** together with the two Aśvins; **all three emerged identical** — three flawless, godlike youths of the same form, each then demanding that Sukanyā **choose** which was her husband (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 124; *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5.10–13).
- **Sukanyā chooses rightly** - By her devotion (and, in some versions, a sign or her inner perception), Sukanyā **correctly identified the true Cyavana** and chose him, now restored to youth, beauty and sight. The Aśvins blessed the couple (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 124).
  - *Āyurvedic tradition:* the rejuvenation is elaborated as a **threefold treatment** — immersion in an herb-charged pond, a body-paste/kāyakalpa, and a medicinal electuary — and the tonic the Aśvins prepared is the namesake **cyavanaprāśa**, the classic Āyurvedic *rasāyana* (rejuvenative jam). [late text / folk]
  - *Variant (what Sukanyā promises in return):* in the *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* (IV.1.5.10ff) the Aśvins restore Cyavana **in exchange for being told the secret of how to obtain a share of the soma** — i.e. the bargain that drives the next act is struck *here*, with Sukanyā/Cyavana agreeing to win the twins their soma-portion. [Vedic]

## The soma-sacrifice and the war over the Aśvins' share

- **Cyavana's pledge** - Grateful for his renewed youth, Cyavana vowed to **secure for the Aśvins a rightful share of the soma sacrifice (soma-bhāga)** — from which the twins had hitherto been excluded by the orthodox gods (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 124).
- **Śaryāti's soma-yajña** - Cyavana officiated as **priest at a soma sacrifice performed by King Śaryāti**, and at the offering he **invited the Aśvins to receive a soma-cup** alongside the other gods (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 125; *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5.13–18).
- **Indra's objection** - **Indra** protested furiously: the Aśvins are mere **wandering physicians / servants among mortals (bhiṣaj)**, ritually impure for moving among men, and therefore **unworthy of soma**. He forbade the offering (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 125; *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5.4–6).
  - *Vedic rationale (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa):* the gods' aversion is explicitly that the Aśvins "wandered among men, performing cures," and so were ritually unfit — Cyavana's intervention is what **purifies and admits them** to the company of soma-drinkers. [Vedic]
- **Cyavana defies Indra** - When the sage poured the cup regardless, Indra raised his **vajra (thunderbolt)** to strike him. Cyavana, by his *tapas*, **paralyzed/withered Indra's arm**, freezing it in mid-air (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 125).

## Mada, the giant Asura of Intoxication

- **Creation of Mada** - To overwhelm Indra, Cyavana offered into the fire and, from his ascetic energy, **brought forth a colossal Asura named Mada ("Intoxication / Drunkenness")** — a monster of terrifying size with **gigantic jaws** and **four enormous fangs/tusks**, eyes like sun and moon, a mouth wide as the cavern of death, who advanced to **devour Indra and the worlds** (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 125).
  - *Iconography:* Mada is described with one jaw on the earth and one in heaven, tongue lapping the directions — the very image of all-swallowing dissolution; into his gigantic maw the gods and Indra are about to vanish. [scripture]
- **Indra submits** - Faced with annihilation, **Indra relented** and conceded the Aśvins their **rightful share of the soma**, ending their exclusion forever — the etiological point of the whole tale (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 125; *Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa* IV.1.5.18).
- **The distribution of Mada into the four vices** - Having won his point, Cyavana **dissolved Mada and parceled him out among four "ready-made" human indulgences** — so that "Intoxication" should persist in the world distributed among:
  1. **drink / intoxicants (pāna, pānaka)**,
  2. **women (striyaḥ)**,
  3. **dice / gambling (akṣa / dyūta)**, and
  4. **hunting (mṛgayā)**
  — the classic catalog of the **four kingly vices born of desire** (*kāmaja* vices). The Mahābhārata returns to this distribution and lists these four as the legacy of Cyavana's Mada (*Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva 125; restated in Anuśāsana Parva ~13.156 / the *vyasana* discourses).
  - *Variant ordering (sources):* the four are variously listed — most commonly **dice, hunting, drinking, women** — and these same four recur in the *Mahābhārata*'s catalog of the **ten/eighteen vices of kings (rāja-vyasanas)** born of *kāma*; some retellings give the count as "four fangs of Mada = four vices," mapping fang-to-vice. [scholarly]

## Variant accounts across the texts

- **Ṛgveda (earliest stratum)** - Only the **rejuvenation** survives, as praise of the Aśvins' miracle-working: they "made Cyavāna young again," restoring the aged sage like a refurbished chariot (RV 1.116.10, 1.117.13, 5.74, 7.68, 7.71, 10.39). No Sukanyā, no Mada, no soma-war yet. [Vedic]
- **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (IV.1.5.1–18)** - The fullest *Vedic* narrative: insult by **Śaryāta's people/boys** (not Sukanyā); marriage; Aśvins' seduction-attempt and the **lake** of identical youths; the deal struck for the **soma-secret**; the **admission of the Aśvins to soma** at Kurukṣetra — but **no Mada-monster** (the conflict is resolved through ritual knowledge, not a created Asura). The Mada-demon is the **epic/Purāṇic amplification**. [Vedic]
- **Mahābhārata, Vana Parva 122–125** - The "classic" full version: **Sukanyā** pierces the eyes; the army's affliction; marriage; the Aśvins' contest in the **lake**; the **soma-quarrel with Indra**; the paralysis of Indra's arm; the **creation of Mada** and his **fourfold distribution**. Narrated by Lomaśa on the pilgrimage.
- **Mahābhārata, Anuśāsana Parva (~13.52–56 and the vyasana sections)** - Continues / re-uses Cyavana: his dealings with **King Kuśika and his queen** (the Kuśika episode), where Cyavana tests them with extravagant demands, rewards their forbearance, and foretells the eventual birth of **Viśvāmitra** in their line — see [Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md). The Mada-as-four-vices teaching is also recalled here. [late text]
- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Canto 9 (9.3)** - In the Solar-dynasty survey, **Śaryāti** brings his daughter **Sukanyā**; she pierces the anthill-sage's eyes; the marriage and the Aśvins' youth-restoration in the lake are retold with a **bhakti/devotional gloss** emphasizing Sukanyā's chastity; the soma-share is granted, and the line continues to **Ānarta / Revata / Raivata** and ultimately **Revatī** (Balarāma's wife). The grotesque **Mada-monster is muted or absent** in the Bhāgavata's telling, which foregrounds wifely virtue over the soma-war. [Purāṇic]
  - *Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa:* a parallel retelling, again centering **Sukanyā's pātivratya** as the moral, with the Aśvins' lake-test as the set-piece. [late text]
- **Padma / other Purāṇas & Āyurvedic texts** - Later strata foreground the **cyavanaprāśa** rejuvenation recipe and the herb-lake, attaching the *rasāyana* lore to the episode; geography fixes "Cyavana's tīrtha/āśrama" at various pilgrimage sites. [folk / late text]
- **Other Cyavana episodes (not part of this cycle, but the same sage):**
  - *The fish-and-fishermen tale:* Cyavana lets himself be **caught in a fisherman's net along with fish** and, refusing to be separated from the creatures, forces a king (Nahuṣa, in some versions) to value him equal to all his subjects — a lesson on the worth of life (told of Cyavana in Anuśāsana Parva / Purāṇic anthologies). [variant — separate episode]

## Themes and significance

- **Pātivratā charter** - Sukanyā stands beside [Sāvitrī](savitri-satyavan.md), [Damayantī](nala-damayanti.md) and Śakuntalā ([Śakuntalā & Bharata](shakuntala-bharata.md)) as an exemplar of the faithful wife: offered the choice of two perfect young gods, she keeps her wrinkled husband — and is rewarded with his restored youth. The lake-of-three-identical-youths is the dramatic crux of her fidelity-test.
- **Tapas overpowers Indra** - Like [Ambarīṣa](ambarisha-durvasa.md) (bhakti over curse) and the [Viśvāmitra](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) cycle (tapas vs. status), this is a **brāhmaṇa/ascetic vs. Indra** confrontation: the sage's austerity literally paralyzes the king of the gods and forces a permanent change in ritual order. Compare the soma/amṛta politics of the [Samudra Manthana](samudra-manthana.md).
- **The Aśvins admitted to soma** - The episode's deepest (Vedic) purpose is etiological: it explains **why the Aśvins — outsiders, healers who mingle with mortals — receive soma** in the developed cult. Cyavana is their patron and sponsor.
- **Mada and the four vices** - The dissolution of "Intoxication" into **drink, women, dice and hunting** is a moralized origin-myth for the **rāja-vyasanas** (vices that ruin kings) — the same four (especially **dice** and **hunting**) that drive the tragedies of [Naḷa](nala-damayanti.md) and Yudhiṣṭhira. Intoxication, the tale says, was never destroyed — only hidden inside human pleasures.
- **cyavanaprāśa** - The story is the folk-etiology of Āyurveda's most famous rejuvenative tonic, named for the sage the Aśvins made young. [folk]
