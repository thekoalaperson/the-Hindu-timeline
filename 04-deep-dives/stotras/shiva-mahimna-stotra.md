# Shiva Mahimna Stotra

> *Up one level: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · The **Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra** ("Hymn on the Greatness of Śiva"), the celebrated Śaiva praise-poem framed by its own author-legend — the [gandharva](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/gandharvas-and-kinnaras.md) Puṣpadanta, stripped of his powers for trampling Śiva's flowers, sings the god's immeasurable greatness to win back grace; sibling to the [Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra](../shiva-tandava-stotra.md) and rooted in the [Śiva myth-cycle](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md).*

The **Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra** (शिवमहिम्नः स्तोत्रम्, "Hymn to the Greatness — *mahiman* — of Śiva"), opening *mahimnaḥ pāraṃ te paramavidūṣo yady asadṛśī...* ("If, not knowing the limit of Thy greatness, my praise be unworthy of Thee..."), is among the most widely recited of all Śaiva hymns. Its **~43 verses** (Puṣpadanta recension) circle one confessional idea: that **Śiva's greatness lies utterly beyond the reach of speech, mind, and even the Vedas**, so that the only honest praise is praise that confesses its own inadequacy. Uniquely among stotras, the hymn embeds its own **frame-legend and author's name** in its closing verses — the celestial musician **Puṣpadanta**, who composed it in atonement. [scholarly]

---

## The frame-story: Puṣpadanta and the trampled garden

- **Puṣpadanta the gandharva** — A celestial musician of Śiva's retinue (in some tellings *king of the gandharvas*) and his devotee, granted by the god the **boon of invisibility / unseen movement** wherever he wished. (Puṣpadanta frame, stotra & Purāṇic legend) [folk]
  - *Cross-reference:* on the class of celestial musicians to which he belongs, see [gandharvas and kinnaras](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/gandharvas-and-kinnaras.md).
- **The nightly flower-gathering** — Moving invisibly, Puṣpadanta took to plucking choice blossoms from a splendid royal garden each night to offer Śiva, slipping past the guards undetected thanks to his boon. (legend) [folk]
  - *Variant (garden's owner):* the garden is most often that of **King Citraratha** (himself a gandharva-king and devotee); other retellings make it a generic royal pleasure-garden whose master worshipped Śiva. [regional]
- **The fatal misstep** — Among the offerings the king habitually laid out were **bilva (bel) leaves and flowers already consecrated to Śiva**. Not seeing them, Puṣpadanta **trod upon this sacred Śiva-offering** — and the sacrilege instantly **stripped him of his invisibility**. (legend) [folk]
  - *Variant (what was trampled):* some versions say he trod on Śiva's own **nirmālya** (spent worship-offerings), others on the bilva-grove or the dedicated flowers; the constant is that the trespass is against *Śiva's sacred flowers*, which is why the power vanishes. [regional]
- **Caught and bereft** — Made suddenly visible, Puṣpadanta is exposed; in many tellings the king's guards seize him, and in all he is left robbed of the gift that defined his very name. (legend) [folk]
- **The hymn as atonement** — Realizing the offense was against **Śiva** himself, Puṣpadanta composed the **Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra** to win back the god's favour; the hymn pleasing Śiva, **his powers were restored**. The stotra thus works at once as confession, praise, and the very instrument of his expiation. (Puṣpadanta frame) [folk]
- **Self-naming in the text** — The hymn names its own author in its concluding verses — the colophon/signature ślokas attribute it to **Puṣpadanta** ("flower-toothed," also given as *Kusumadanta*) — a rare case of a stotra embedding its composer's name *and* frame-legend within the poem itself. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra*, signature/colophon verses) [scholarly]

## Content and structure

- **The opening confession of inadequacy** — The first verse sets the governing logic: if even **Brahmā's wearied speech, exhausting the Vedas, cannot reach the end of Śiva's greatness**, the poet's praise is necessarily defective — yet praise offered with devotion is never wholly vain, for it purifies the speaker's own mind. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* 1) [scholarly]
- **The limits of language and the Vedas** — A recurring move: the **Vedas, Brahmā, and the greatest sages fall silent** before Śiva's nature; speech "returns without reaching" Him, echoing the Upaniṣadic *yato vāco nivartante* ("whence words turn back"). The hymn makes its own inexpressibility its central rhetorical and theological device. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* 2–7) [scholarly]
- **Devotion over capacity** — A consoling corollary much loved in the tradition: a child's babble or a poet's stammer, offered sincerely, reaches the god as surely as Bṛhaspati's eloquence — what counts is *bhāva* (feeling), not skill. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* 2–3) [scholarly]
- **Cosmic functions and paradox** — Śiva is praised as creator, sustainer, and destroyer; the verses delight in **paradox** — the naked ascetic who is lord of all wealth, the cremation-ground dweller who is supremely auspicious, the formless one worshipped in form. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* 8–14)
- **The one reality behind the systems (Hari–Hara samanvaya)** — An inclusivist verse harmonizes the rival paths — **Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Pāśupata, Vaiṣṇava, the Vaidika** — and the names **Hari and Hara** as facets pointing to one supreme reality; the famous *trayī-sāṃkhya-yoga* verse (~v. 7) is widely quoted as a charter of sectarian reconciliation. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* ~7) [scholarly]
- **The myths of Śiva woven in as proofs** — The hymn alludes to the great Śaiva narratives to demonstrate His power: the **drinking of the hālāhala poison** at the churning, the burning of the three **Tripura** cities, the chastening of **Kāma**, the bearing of the **Gaṅgā** in His matted hair, the cosmic **tāṇḍava** dance, and the **humbling of Rāvaṇa** beneath Kailāsa. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra*, narrative verses ~14–30)
  - *Cross-reference:* the Rāvaṇa-under-Kailāsa episode is treated at length in [Rāvaṇa and Kailāsa (Rāvaṇānugraha)](../ravana-kailasa-ravananugraha.md); the cosmic dance, in the [Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra](../shiva-tandava-stotra.md).
- **The Tripura-chariot image** — A celebrated passage pictures the gods' war on Tripura with the **earth as chariot, sun and moon as wheels, Brahmā as charioteer, Meru as bow** — and Śiva needing none of it, destroying the cities by a mere **smile**, to show His power is independent of all instruments. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra*, the Tripura verse) [scholarly]
- **The closing dedication and phala-śruti** — The final verses give the author's signature (Puṣpadanta), declare the hymn's power to purify and to please Śiva, and promise the fruit of recitation. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* 38–43)

## Themes: the inexpressibility of Śiva's greatness

- **Apophasis as the highest praise** — The hymn's signature theme is **theological apophasis**: the *mahiman* of Śiva is *aparimeya* (immeasurable), so the only truthful praise is one that confesses its insufficiency. Far from defeating the hymn, this becomes its supreme tribute — the devotee offers the *effort*, not a claim to have captured the deity. [scholarly]
- **The Vedas as the measure that fails** — Where most stotras pile up epithets, the Mahimna repeatedly stages the **failure of even scripture** to circumscribe Śiva: Brahmā tires, the Vedas are exhausted, the word turns back. This makes it as much a meditation on the limits of language as a hymn of praise. [scholarly]
- **Inclusivism** — By declaring rival names and systems to be facets of one reality, the stotra is cited as a model of reconciliation and is recited by Śaivas and non-Śaivas alike; it shares this irenic register with the broader [Śiva myth-cycle](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) and stands beside the doctrinal [Śiva-Gītā](../shiva-gita.md) as a popular-devotional counterpart to that text's metaphysics. [scholarly]

## Recitation tradition

- **A daily and festival hymn** — The Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra is among the **most popular of all Śaiva stotras**, recited daily by devotees, during **Śiva-pūjā**, and especially on **Mahā-śivarātri** and through the month of **Śrāvaṇa**. (living tradition)
- **The gandharva's hymn as forgiveness-prayer** — Because a gandharva composed it to *regain divine grace*, tradition holds its recitation peculiarly efficacious for **removing offenses (aparādha)** and faults committed in worship; many liturgies append it as a *kṣamā* (forgiveness) prayer at the close of a pūjā. (devotional tradition) [folk]
- **Recensions and length** — The familiar Puṣpadanta text runs to **~43 verses** (some printings count 43–45 with the concluding phala-śruti / dedicatory ślokas). (textual tradition) [disputed]
  - *Variant (Jain Mahimna):* a **Jain Mahimna-stotra** modeled on the same opening and *śikhariṇī* meter, redirecting the praise to a Tīrthaṅkara, is preserved in Jain liturgy — a measure of the original's prestige and cross-community reach. [regional] [disputed]
- **Place among the great stotras** — It is grouped with the foremost recited praise-poems of Śaivism alongside the [Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra](../shiva-tandava-stotra.md), the Rudra-praśna, and the Liṅgāṣṭakam. (liturgical tradition)

## Cosmic placement — the "two clocks"

- **In-narrative time** — The frame-legend is a **trans-yuga / heavenly episode**: a gandharva of Śiva's retinue offending and atoning belongs to the timeless celestial register of the Purāṇic world rather than to a fixed point in a single Mahāyuga; it is best read as **cross-yuga**. [scholarly]
- **Text-critical time** — As a polished Sanskrit *stotra* in elaborate meter (chiefly *śikhariṇī*), the hymn as transmitted is a work of the **classical/medieval devotional period**, not of Vedic antiquity; its attribution to a primordial gandharva is a traditional frame, not a manuscript date. Keep the scriptural attribution distinct from the literary dating. [scholarly]
