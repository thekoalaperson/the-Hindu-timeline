# Shiva Tandava Stotra

> *Up one level: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · The thunder-hymn of [Rāvaṇa](../characters/ravana.md) to Śiva's cosmic dance, companion-piece to the apophatic [Śiva Mahimna Stotra](shiva-mahimna-stotra.md).*

The **Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra** ("Hymn to Śiva's [Cosmic] Dance"), opening *jaṭā-ṭavī-galaj-jala-pravāha-pāvita-sthale...* ("On the ground hallowed by the torrent of water streaming from the forest of His matted locks..."), is a virtuoso Sanskrit praise-poem traditionally ascribed to the rākṣasa king **Rāvaṇa**. Across its **sixteen verses** (some recensions add a seventeenth phala-śruti) it pours out a torrent of compound epithets describing **Śiva mid-tāṇḍava** — the dance that creates, sustains, and dissolves the cosmos. Its fame rests as much on its **dense, drumming meter** as on its content, and it is inseparable from the frame-legend of **Rāvaṇānugraha**, "the grace [shown] to Rāvaṇa," in which the demon composes the hymn while pinned beneath Mount Kailāsa. [scholarly]

---

## The frame-story: Rāvaṇa beneath Kailāsa (Rāvaṇānugraha)

- **The lifting of Kailāsa** — Returning by his aerial car *Puṣpaka*, **Rāvaṇa** finds his path blocked by Mount **Kailāsa**, abode of Śiva and Pārvatī; in arrogance he reaches under the mountain and begins to **uproot and shake it** to clear his way. (*Rāmāyaṇa*, Uttarakāṇḍa; Rāvaṇānugraha tradition)
- **The pressing-down** — **Śiva**, unwilling to be disturbed, presses the mountain back down with the **toe of His foot**, trapping Rāvaṇa's arms and crushing him beneath its weight. (*Rāmāyaṇa*, Uttarakāṇḍa) — see the longer treatment in [Rāvaṇa](../characters/ravana.md).
- **The cry and the name** — Pinned and in agony for (in the epic) a thousand years, Rāvaṇa **roars so terribly** that the worlds tremble; from this cry Śiva gives him the very name **"Rāvaṇa"** (*the one who makes [the worlds] roar/weep*). (*Rāmāyaṇa*, Uttarakāṇḍa) [scholarly]
- **The hymn as release** — To win release and Śiva's favor, Rāvaṇa **sings in praise of the dancing god**; pleased, Śiva frees him, restores his strength, and grants him a boon (in many tellings the invincible sword *Candrahāsa*). The stotra is identified by tradition as **this very song of praise**. (Rāvaṇānugraha legend) [folk]
  - *Variant (the vīṇā of his own body):* a widely-loved telling — strong in **Tamil Śaiva** memory — holds that beneath the mountain Rāvaṇa, lacking an instrument, **tore off one of his heads and strung a vīṇā from it, using his own sinews/nerves as strings**, and played it to accompany the hymn until Śiva relented. (Tamil Śaiva / Tevāram-associated tradition) [regional] [folk]
  - *Variant (Tevāram allusions):* the **Nāyaṉār** hymns of the *Tevāram* repeatedly invoke Śiva as the lord **who crushed the ten-headed demon beneath the mountain and then graced him**, making Rāvaṇānugraha a stock image of grace-after-pride in Tamil Śaivism. (*Tevāram*, Campantar / Appar / Cuntarar) [regional]

## Content of the hymn

- **The streaming locks and the Gaṅgā** — The first verses fix the eye on Śiva's **jaṭā** (matted hair) as a forest from which the **Gaṅgā** pours, His neck garlanded with serpents, the **ḍamaru** (hand-drum) beating *ḍamad-ḍamad-ḍamad-ḍamat* to set the dance's rhythm. (*Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra* 1–2)
- **The blazing throat and the serpents** — Repeated images of the **dark-blue throat** (from the hālāhala poison), the **moon-crest**, and the hooded **snakes** flickering about His matted hair, their breath fanning a forest-fire of glory. (*Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra* 2–4)
- **Pārvatī and the play of the world** — Verses dwell on Śiva delighting **Pārvatī (Gaurī)**, the dance as the very sport (*līlā*) by which the universe is moved; the poet begs that his **mind delight at every moment** in this dancing lord. (*Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra* 5–7)
- **The equal eye — Kāmadeva and the cremation-fire** — A famous refrain prays for the day when, **equal-eyed toward a strand of grass and a beautiful woman, toward friend and foe, prince and pauper, a snake and a garland**, the devotee will dwell by the Gaṅgā in a cave with eyes closed on Śiva. (*Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra* 8–14)
- **Destruction of the cities and of Kāma** — Allusions to the burning of the **Tripura** fortresses and the incineration of **Kāmadeva (Smara)** by the third eye, recalling Śiva's myths as proofs of the dancer's power. (*Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra* 11–13)
- **The phala-śruti** — The closing verse(s) promise that one who recites this hymn at dusk, purified, gains Śiva's grace, prosperity, and devotion — and, in the added verse, names **Rāvaṇa** as the reciter who thereby won the chariot, fortune, and the god's favor. (*Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra* 15–17) [scholarly]

## The meter and the sound-texture

- **Pañcacāmara meter** — The hymn is composed almost entirely in **pañcacāmara** (also *nārācā*), a meter of **sixteen syllables per quarter-line** built on a relentless **iambic (laghu-guru, ⏑—) pulse** repeated eight times — a galloping, drum-like cadence that mimics the beat of the *ḍamaru* and the stamp of the dance. (Sanskrit prosody; *Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra*) [scholarly]
- **Alliteration and chiming compounds** — Each verse is one or two **enormous compound words**, chosen so that the same consonant-clusters recur and echo (*galaj-jala*, *ḍhalad-ḍhalad*, *dhagad-dhagad-dhagaj-jvalal*), making the poem famous as a feat of **sound-painting (śabda-alaṃkāra)** as much as of meaning. (*Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra*) [scholarly]
- **Onomatopoeia of the dance** — Syllabic clusters imitate the **drum (ḍamad-ḍamad)**, the **fire (dhagad-dhagad)**, and the **swaying serpents**, so that the hymn *sounds like* the tāṇḍava it describes — a quality that has made it a favorite for chanting and, in modern times, musical setting. (living recitation tradition) [folk]
- **Contrast with the Mahimna** — Where the [Śiva Mahimna Stotra](shiva-mahimna-stotra.md) works by **apophasis** (praising Śiva by confessing speech's failure, in the gentler *śikhariṇī* meter), the Tāṇḍava-stotra works by **sensory overload** — heaping concrete image on image in a hammering meter. The two are often paired as the contemplative and the ecstatic poles of Śaiva hymnody. [scholarly]

## The Rāvaṇānugraha iconographic tradition

- **A standard sculptural theme** — **Rāvaṇānugraha-mūrti** — Śiva and Pārvatī seated atop a tiered **Mount Kailāsa**, with the many-armed, many-headed **Rāvaṇa** shown beneath, straining to lift it — is one of the canonical forms of Śiva in temple art, treated in the **Śilpa/Āgama** image-prescriptions. (Śaiva iconography) [scholarly]
- **Famous examples** — The motif appears as a celebrated relief at the **Kailāsanātha (Kailāsa) cave-temple, Ellora**, and at **Elephanta**, among many others — Śiva calm above, Pārvatī clutching Him in alarm, attendants scattering, and the demon's massed arms below. (art-historical record) [scholarly]
- **Theological reading** — The image fixes the legend's lesson: **brute power (Rāvaṇa) humbled, then graced** — *anugraha*, grace, given precisely to the one who attacked, once he turns to song and devotion. The crushing toe and the granted boon are read as the two faces of the same dancing lord. [scholarly]

## Cosmic placement — the "two clocks"

- **In-narrative time** — As an episode of **Rāvaṇa's** life, the frame-legend sits in the **Tretā-yuga** of the *Rāmāyaṇa* world, before his war with Rāma; the Uttarakāṇḍa places it among his earlier conquests and clashes with the gods. [scholarly]
- **Text-critical time** — The stotra as a finished Sanskrit poem in elaborate pañcacāmara belongs to the **classical/medieval stotra-literature**; its ascription to Rāvaṇa is a traditional devotional frame, not a manuscript date, and the polished prosody argues against Tretā-yuga authorship. Keep the scriptural attribution distinct from the literary dating. [scholarly] [disputed]

## Sources

**Primary texts**
- *Śiva-tāṇḍava-stotra* (Shiva Tandava Stotram), attributed to **Rāvaṇa** — 16-verse (with phala-śruti, 17) Śaiva hymn in pañcacāmara meter; opening *jaṭāṭavī-galajjala...*, closing verse naming Rāvaṇa as reciter.
- *Rāmāyaṇa*, **Uttarakāṇḍa** — the lifting of Kailāsa, Śiva's pressing of the mountain by His toe, Rāvaṇa's roar and naming, the boon (Rāvaṇānugraha episode).
- *Tevāram* (Tamil Śaiva canon of Campantar, Appar, Cuntarar) — recurrent invocations of Śiva who crushed and then graced the ten-headed demon beneath the mountain.

**Iconographic / art-historical**
- Rāvaṇānugraha-mūrti reliefs at the **Kailāsa temple, Ellora** and **Elephanta**, and the Śaiva Āgama/Śilpa prescriptions for the form.

**Note on attribution**
- The Rāvaṇa authorship and the vīṇā-from-his-own-head detail are **traditional/regional** elaborations (strong in Tamil Śaivism); they are recorded here as catalogued variants, not as text-critical claims about the poem's date.
