# Kanakadhara Stotram

> *Up one level: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · The **Kanakadhārā-stotra** ("Hymn of the Golden Stream"), Ādi Śaṅkara's 18–21-verse praise of **Śrī / Lakṣmī** sung — in its beloved frame-story — as a boy to win a rain of golden āmalaka fruit for a destitute woman who had nothing to give but a single gooseberry; companion among the Śaṅkara hymns to the [Saundarya-laharī](soundarya-lahari.md) and the [Bhaja Govindam](bhaja-govindam.md), and a Lakṣmī-stotra kindred to the Vedic [Śrī Sūkta](sri-suktam.md).*

The **Kanakadhārā-stotra** (कनकधारास्तोत्र, "Hymn of the Stream of Gold") is a short, lyrically intense Sanskrit hymn to the goddess **Lakṣmī / Śrī** — consort of [Viṣṇu](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishnu-forms-and-vyuhas.md) and giver of wealth, fortune, and grace — attributed to **Ādi Śaṅkarācārya** (see [Ādi Śaṅkara](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/adi-shankara.md) and the [Ācāryas & Sampradāyas](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/README.md) overview). Its name comes from its founding legend: the verses are said to have caused a literal **kanaka-dhārā, a "stream / shower of gold,"** to fall on a poor household. Unlike the *vairāgya* (renunciation) of the [Bhaja Govindam](bhaja-govindam.md) or the tantric architecture of the [Saundarya-laharī](soundarya-lahari.md), the Kanakadhārā is a hymn of **karuṇā (compassion) and prosperity-grace**: dense, rhyming praise of Lakṣmī's sidelong glance, beseeching her mercy on the helpless. It is among the most-recited Śaṅkara hymns in domestic worship, especially for relief from poverty. [scholarly]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / hagiographic time** — In its own legend the hymn is composed *in situ* by the **boy Śaṅkara** during his student years (often placed at **Kāladī / a Kerala village**, or en route during early *brahmacarya*), before his great renunciation and *dig-vijaya*; the frame belongs to the saint's traditional biography (the *Śaṅkara-vijaya* literature) and carries no yuga-claim beyond "within our present age." [hagiographic]
- **Manuscript / academic time** — Text-critically the hymn is a **post-Vedic, Kali-yuga historical-period stotra**: if by Śaṅkara himself it dates to the traditional **8th c. CE** (some scholars place Śaṅkara 7th–early 8th c.). As with most *stotra* literature ascribed to him, authorship is **traditional rather than text-critically secured**, but the poem sits squarely in **recorded history**, not mythic time — keep the saintly ascription distinct from the manuscript record. [scholarly] [disputed]

## The frame-story — the single āmalaka and the rain of gold

- **The boy on bhikṣā rounds** — As a young **brahmacārin (Vedic student)**, Śaṅkara goes house to house for **bhikṣā (alms-food)**, a daily duty of the student-ascetic. (frame of the *Kanakadhārā-stotra*) [folk]
- **The destitute woman's single gooseberry** — He comes to the hut of a **desperately poor Brahmin woman** who has nothing whatever to offer; ashamed to send a guest away empty, she searches her bare house and gives him the one thing she has — **a single dried āmalaka (āmalakī / gooseberry, the Indian myrobalan)** she had been keeping. (traditional frame) [folk]
- **The boy moved to compassion** — Touched by her self-giving in utter poverty, Śaṅkara cannot bear to leave her destitute; he turns and **spontaneously praises Lakṣmī in verse**, begging the goddess to look with mercy on this house. (traditional) [folk]
- **The stream of gold** — Pleased by the hymn, **Lakṣmī** appears (or makes her grace fall) and **showers golden āmalaka fruit** — *kanaka* (gold) *dhārā* (stream/torrent) — into the woman's home, ending her poverty. From this rain of golden gooseberries the hymn takes its name. (traditional) [folk]
  - *Variant (Lakṣmī's initial reluctance, Śaṅkara-vijaya tradition):* in a much-loved expansion, Lakṣmī at first **demurs**, saying the woman has accumulated no merit (*puṇya*) in this life to warrant such reward; Śaṅkara replies that the woman's act of giving her last possession is itself supreme merit — moving the goddess to relent and pour the gold. [folk]
  - *Variant (placement & locale):* the episode is set variously at **Kāladī (Śaṅkara's Kerala birthplace)** or a nearby village during his boyhood; the *Śaṅkara-vijaya* biographies (Mādhava-Vidyāraṇya's *Śaṅkara-dig-vijaya* and others) differ in detail and are **devotional, not documentary**. [hagiographic]
  - *Variant (the fruit):* some retellings make the gift a fresh āmalaka, others a withered one kept for hunger; the point in every version is that it is **her sole possession**. [folk]

## Structure and verse-count

- **A compact hymn of ~18–21 verses** — The Kanakadhārā is short; printed editions give it as **18, 20, or 21 ślokas** (the **21-verse** count is the most widely circulated). (*Kanakadhārā-stotra*) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (verse total):* manuscripts and printed editions range roughly **18 to 21 verses**; the closing *phala-śruti* (fruit-of-recitation) stanza or two are counted in some editions and not others, accounting for much of the spread. Treat exact totals as edition-dependent. [disputed]
- **Ornate, rhyming praise-metre** — The verses are in rich, alliterative Sanskrit (chiefly **vasanta-tilakā** metre), each a self-contained jewel of *stuti* heavy with internal rhyme and imagery — a poetic showcase befitting its legend of a precocious boy-poet. [scholarly]
- **The recurring motif of the glance** — Several verses pivot on Lakṣmī's **kaṭākṣa (sidelong / merciful glance)** falling on Viṣṇu's chest and, by extension, on the devotee — the half-turned, grace-bestowing look being the hymn's central image. (*Kanakadhārā-stotra*)

## Śrī / Lakṣmī theology in the hymn

- **Lakṣmī as Viṣṇu's inseparable Śakti** — Throughout, the goddess is praised as the one who dwells on **Viṣṇu's / Hari's chest** (the *vakṣaḥ-sthala*) and whose glance Viṣṇu himself craves; she is his consort and his power, not a subordinate but the active grace by which he blesses. (*Kanakadhārā-stotra*) [scholarly]
- **The goddess of fortune and abundance** — Lakṣmī is invoked under her wealth-and-auspiciousness aspect — giver of *dhana* (wealth), *aiśvarya* (sovereignty), food, and worldly well-being — the same prosperity-deity addressed by the Vedic [Śrī Sūkta](sri-suktam.md), here approached through a personal, narrative appeal rather than Vedic mantra. [scholarly]
- **Identity of her many names** — The verses name and weave together Lakṣmī's forms and epithets — **Śrī, Kamalā, Indirā, Padmā, Hari-priyā, Kṣīra-sāgara-kanyā** (daughter of the milk-ocean, from the [Samudra-manthana](../samudra-manthana.md) churning) — treating them as one supreme Goddess of grace. (*Kanakadhārā-stotra*)
- **Grace beyond desert (compassion over merit)** — Read against its frame-story, the hymn teaches that the goddess's mercy can **override the strict ledger of karma/merit**: Lakṣmī gives not because the recipient has earned it but because she is moved by compassion and by sincere praise — a *bhakti* theology of grace. [scholarly]

## Devotional use and reception

- **The hymn against poverty** — Kanakadhārā is recited above all by those seeking **relief from want and material distress**; its *phala-śruti* and popular reputation promise wealth, prosperity, and the removal of poverty to the devout reciter, making it a fixture of household Lakṣmī worship. [folk]
- **Friday and festival recitation** — It is commonly chanted on **Fridays (sacred to Lakṣmī)**, during **Dīpāvalī / Lakṣmī-pūjā**, and on **Akṣaya-tṛtīyā** and **Varalakṣmī-vrata**, often alongside the [Śrī Sūkta](sri-suktam.md) and other Lakṣmī hymns. [regional]
- **A staple of the Śaṅkara stotra-corpus** — Within the large body of **stotras attributed to Śaṅkara** — alongside the [Bhaja Govindam](bhaja-govindam.md), the Śākta [Saundarya-laharī](soundarya-lahari.md), the *Nirvāṇa-ṣaṭka*, and the (attributed) [Śiva Mahimna](shiva-mahimna-stotra.md) — the Kanakadhārā is the foremost **Lakṣmī / prosperity hymn**, and the one whose origin-legend is most often retold to children. [scholarly]
- **Musical and recorded settings** — It has been widely set to music and recorded; renditions by **M. S. Subbulakshmi** and others have fixed its melody in popular devotion across South and pan-Indian Hindu communities. [regional]
