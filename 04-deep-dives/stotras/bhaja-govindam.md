# Bhaja Govindam (Mohamudgara)

> *Up one level: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Ādi Śaṅkara's short, fierce *vairāgya* (renunciation) hymn — "Worship Govinda, O fool; grammar will not save you at death" — also titled **Moha-mudgara** ("Hammer that Smashes Delusion"); a street-song distillation of his Advaita, sibling to the [Saundarya-laharī](soundarya-lahari.md) among hymns ascribed to him, catalogued with the [Lesser Gītās](../lesser-gitas-catalog.md), and rooted in the system of the [Brahma-sūtras](../../90-literature-corpus/darshanas/brahma-sutras.md).*

The **Bhaja Govindam** (भज गोविन्दम्; also **Bhajagovindam**, **Mohamudgara**, **Dvādaśa-mañjarikā Stotra**) is a brief, melodic Sanskrit devotional poem attributed to **Ādi Śaṅkarācārya**, the great systematizer of **Advaita Vedānta** (see [Ādi Śaṅkara](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/adi-shankara.md) and the broader [Ācāryas & Sampradāyas](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/README.md)). In a handful of singable, hard-hitting verses it compresses the austere non-dualism of Śaṅkara's commentaries on the [Brahma-sūtras](../../90-literature-corpus/darshanas/brahma-sutras.md) and the [Bhagavad Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md) into a popular **call to renunciation, devotion to Govinda (Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa), and the remembrance of death** — warning that grammar, wealth, lust, and worldly ties are worthless at the hour one dies. Its refrain *bhaja govindaṃ bhaja govindaṃ govindaṃ bhaja mūḍha-mate* ("Worship Govinda, worship Govinda, worship Govinda, O deluded mind!") is among the most-recited verses in all Hindu devotional life. [scholarly]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / hagiographic time** — In its own legend the hymn is composed *in situ* on the streets of **Kāśī (Vārāṇasī)** by Śaṅkara during his earthly mission; the frame belongs to the saint's traditional biography (the *Śaṅkara-vijaya* literature) and carries no yuga-claim beyond "within our present age." [hagiographic]
- **Manuscript / academic time** — Text-critically the poem is a **post-Vedic, Kali-yuga historical-period stotra**: if by Śaṅkara himself it dates to the traditional **8th c. CE** (some scholars place Śaṅkara 7th–early 8th c.); many academics regard at least the supplementary verses, and possibly the whole, as a later *stotra* in his name. Either way it sits squarely in **recorded history**, not mythic time — keep the saintly ascription distinct from the manuscript record. [scholarly] [disputed]

## The frame-story — Śaṅkara and the old grammarian

- **The aged scholar reciting grammar** — Walking the lanes of **Kāśī** with his disciples, **Ādi Śaṅkara** comes upon an **old paṇḍita (a Brahmin grammarian)** laboriously rote-memorizing the rules of Pāṇinian grammar — drilling the aphorism **"ḍukṛñ-karaṇe"** (the grammatical formula for the verbal root *kṛ*, "to do/make") — even as death visibly nears him. (traditional frame of the *Mohamudgara*) [folk]
- **The rebuke set to song** — Moved to pity, Śaṅkara breaks into spontaneous verse: *bhaja govindaṃ … nahi nahi rakṣati ḍukṛñ-karaṇe* — "Worship Govinda, O deluded one; when the appointed hour of death arrives, your **rules of grammar will not, will not, save you.**" Dry erudition cannot rescue the soul at the end; only devotion to God can. (Bhaja Govindam, opening verse) [folk]
- **Why "Mohamudgara"** — From this scene the poem takes its alternate title **Moha-mudgara**, "the **hammer (*mudgara*) that shatters delusion (*moha*)**" — the wake-up blow struck against attachment and intellectual vanity. (traditional)
  - *Variant (the grammarian's identity, Śaṅkara-vijaya tradition):* tradition does not name the old man; he stands as an everyman type of the scholar who mistakes learning for liberation. Some retellings make him the occasion for an impromptu street-teaching to the gathering crowd. [folk]
  - *Variant (placement in the hagiographies):* the **Mādhava-Vidyāraṇya *Śaṅkara-dig-vijaya*** and other *Śaṅkara-vijaya* texts set the episode during Śaṅkara's residence at Kāśī; details and wording differ across the competing biographies, which are devotional, not documentary. [hagiographic]

## Structure — the Dvādaśa-mañjarikā and the disciples' verses

- **The core Dvādaśa-mañjarikā ("twelve-blossom bouquet")** — The hymn's nucleus is a cluster styled a **mañjarī ("blossom-cluster, bouquet")**; the central body is the **Dvādaśa-mañjarikā Stotra**, the **"twelve-blossom"** hymn — twelve verses (beyond the refrain-verse) ascribed to Śaṅkara himself. (Bhaja Govindam / Dvādaśa-mañjarikā) [scholarly]
- **The Caturdaśa-mañjarikā — the disciples' added verses** — Tradition holds that, inspired by the master, **fourteen of Śaṅkara's disciples each contributed one verse**, expanding the set into the **Caturdaśa-mañjarikā ("fourteen-blossom") Stotra**. With the refrain-verse and a few closing / *phala-śruti* stanzas, the composite poem runs to roughly **31 verses**, though counts vary by edition. (traditional ascription) [folk] [scholarly]
  - *Variant (named disciples, Cinmayānanda edition):* the popular tradition (followed by Swāmī Cinmayānanda's widely-used edition) assigns the fourteen verses to disciples including **Padmapāda, Toṭaka(cārya), Hastāmalaka, and Sureśvara (the converted Maṇḍana Miśra)**, among others; the exact roster and verse-attributions differ across sources and are **devotional, not text-critical.** [folk] [disputed]
  - *Variant (verse total):* manuscripts and printed editions give the whole as **anywhere from ~12 to ~31 (or 33) verses**, depending on whether only the Dvādaśa-mañjarikā or the full Caturdaśa-mañjarikā plus closing verses is counted. Treat exact totals as edition-dependent. [disputed]

## Content and themes

- **Worship Govinda, not grammar** — The governing thesis: at death, ritual erudition, syntax, and book-learning are powerless; **devotion (bhakti) to Govinda** is the one refuge. Knowledge that does not turn the heart to God is vanity. (verse 1) [scholarly]
- **The futility of wealth and lust** — Verses warn against craving riches (*mā kuru dhana-jana-yauvana-garvam*, "take no pride in wealth, retinue, or youth") and against infatuation with the body and sensual beauty — all of which **time sweeps away in a moment**. (Dvādaśa-mañjarikā)
- **The body's decay and the wheel of rebirth** — Repeated meditations on **old age, sickness, grey hair, the toothless mouth**, and the relentless round of *punar-api jananaṃ punar-api maraṇam* — "**again birth, again death, again lying in the mother's womb**" — the *saṃsāra* one must yearn to escape. (Dvādaśa-mañjarikā, a much-loved verse)
- **The impermanence of family and relationship** — Stanzas observe that **wife, children, and kin cling only while one prospers**; love rooted in self-interest evaporates with one's fortunes, so one must not over-attach to household ties. (Dvādaśa-mañjarikā)
- **Vairāgya (dispassion) and satsaṅga** — The path it urges: **the company of the holy (*satsaṅga*)**, which leads to non-attachment (*nissaṅgatva*), thence to freedom from delusion (*nirmohatva*), thence to the **immovable Reality** and liberation-in-life (*jīvan-mukti*). (Dvādaśa-mañjarikā) [scholarly]
- **Breath, sense-control, and the inner witness** — The disciples' verses fold in distinctly **Advaita / Yoga** counsel: *prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra*, discrimination (*viveka*), and meditation on the **Self that is the witness** — the practical bridge from devotion to non-dual realization. (Caturdaśa-mañjarikā)
- **The Name as the boat across saṃsāra** — The closing thrust returns to grace: take refuge in **Murāri (Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa)**, for the divine **Name** ferries the devotee across the ocean of worldly existence when nothing else can. (concluding verses) [scholarly]
- **Bhakti *and* jñāna — not opposed** — Though cast as a *bhakti* hymn, in Advaita circles the poem is read as teaching that **bhakti is the preparatory discipline** that purifies the mind for *jñāna*; Śaṅkara the supreme non-dualist here speaks the language of loving devotion deliberately, for the ordinary seeker who cannot begin with the [Brahma-sūtras](../../90-literature-corpus/darshanas/brahma-sutras.md). [scholarly]

## Recitation tradition and reception

- **A staple of daily and congregational devotion** — Bhaja Govindam is **memorized, sung, and chanted across India** — in homes, temples, and *satsaṅg* gatherings — prized for its simple, urgent message and its singable metre; it is among the first Sanskrit hymns many devotees learn. [folk]
- **Swāmī Cinmayānanda's exposition** — In the 20th century **Swāmī Cinmayānanda** (founder of the Chinmaya Mission) delivered and published a celebrated discourse-commentary on Bhaja Govindam that made it a centerpiece of modern Vedānta teaching and popularized the disciple-verse tradition. [scholarly]
- **Musical settings** — The hymn has been set to music repeatedly; the rendition by **M. S. Subbulakshmi** (paired with discourses of the Kāñcī tradition) is among the best-known recordings, fixing the melody in the popular ear. [regional]
- **One of the most-quoted Advaita devotional poems** — Within the large corpus of **stotras attributed to Śaṅkara** (alongside the *Nirvāṇa-ṣaṭka, Kanakadhārā-stotra,* and the Śākta [Saundarya-laharī](soundarya-lahari.md)), Bhaja Govindam stands out as the one most often cited to convey Advaita *vairāgya* in a single breath; in the wider devotional anthologies it is grouped with the [Lesser Gītās](../lesser-gitas-catalog.md) and the [Bhagavad Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md) whose teaching it so often paraphrases. [scholarly]

## Sources

- ***Bhaja Govindam / Mohamudgara***, attributed to **Ādi Śaṅkarācārya** — the primary text (refrain-verse + Dvādaśa-mañjarikā + Caturdaśa-mañjarikā + closing *phala-śruti* verses). Sanskrit text and translations widely available (e.g. sanskritdocuments.org; Chinmaya Mission editions).
- **Swāmī Cinmayānanda**, *Bhaja Govindam* (Central Chinmaya Mission Trust) — the standard modern commentary; source for the frame-story of the Kāśī grammarian, the *Mohamudgara* title, and the disciple-verse (Caturdaśa-mañjarikā) tradition. [scholarly]
- Traditional ***Śaṅkara-vijaya*** hagiographies (Mādhava-Vidyāraṇya's *Śaṅkara-dig-vijaya* et al.) — for the Kāśī episode and Śaṅkara's biography. [hagiographic]
- *Note on authorship / verse-count:* the ascription to Śaṅkara, the assignment of fourteen verses to named disciples, and the total verse-count (~12 / ~31 / ~33) are **devotional traditions**; academic opinion on Śaṅkara's date (traditionally 8th c. CE) and on whether all verses are his is divided. Borderline points flagged [disputed] above. [scholarly]
