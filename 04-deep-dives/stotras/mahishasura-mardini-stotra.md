# Mahishasura Mardini Stotra

> *Up one level: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · The **Mahiṣāsura-mardinī stotra** ("Hymn to the Slayer of the Buffalo-Demon"), the hammering **Ayi giri-nandini** hymn traditionally ascribed to [Ādi Śaṅkara](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/adi-shankara.md) — the lyric, sung counterpart to the battle of the [Devī Māhātmya](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md), praising [Durgā](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-forms-and-navadurga.md) as she destroys Mahiṣa; a staple of Navarātri sibling to the [Soundarya Laharī](soundarya-lahari.md) and the [Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra](../shiva-tandava-stotra.md).*

The **Mahiṣāsura-mardinī stotra** (महिषासुरमर्दिनी स्तोत्रम्), known in living practice by its opening words **"Ayi giri-nandini nandita-medini"** (अयि गिरिनन्दिनि नन्दितमेदिनि), is among the most popular and frequently recited of all Devī hymns. It is a *stuti* of roughly **21–23 verses** addressed to the Goddess as **Mahiṣāsura-mardinī** — "she who crushed the buffalo-demon Mahiṣa" — and functions as the lyric, musical retelling of the central combat narrated in prose-verse by the **Devī Māhātmya** (Durgā Saptaśatī). Where the Māhātmya narrates, this hymn **exults**: it is built almost entirely of vocatives, an unbroken cascade of epithets hurled at the victorious Goddess in a galloping, internally-rhymed meter. It is a fixture of **Navarātri / Durgā Pūjā** recitation and one of the most-set Devī texts in Indian devotional music. [scholarly]

---

## Title, refrain, and meter

- **The signature refrain** — Almost every verse closes on the burden **"jaya jaya he mahiṣāsura-mardini ramya-kapardini śaila-sutē"** ("Victory, victory to you, O Slayer of Mahiṣa, of lovely matted locks, O daughter of the mountain"), making the hymn a litany of triumph rather than petition. (*Mahiṣāsura-mardinī stotra*) [scholarly]
- **"Ayi giri-nandini"** — The opening epithet hails the Goddess as **daughter of the mountain (Himavat)** — i.e. Pārvatī/Durgā — "who gladdens the earth," immediately fusing the gentle mountain-bride with the war-goddess of the Māhātmya. (*Mahiṣāsura-mardinī stotra* 1) [scholarly]
- **The driving meter** — The hymn is prized for its relentless, drum-like **internal rhyme and alliteration** (each line packed with rhyming compound-epithets), which makes it as much a feat of Sanskrit sound-craft as of devotion and explains its enduring popularity as a **sung** piece. [scholarly]
- **Verse count** — Printed recensions run to **~21, 22, or 23 stanzas**; the figure varies by edition and by which closing *phala-śruti* verses are counted. [disputed]

## What the hymn praises — the structure

- **The cosmic queen (opening verses)** — The first stanzas hail her as **giri-nandinī** (mountain-daughter), **nandita-medinī** (gladdener of earth), **bhagavatī**, queen over the worlds, and consort-power of the gods — establishing her supremacy before the battle. (*Mahiṣāsura-mardinī stotra* 1–3) [scholarly]
- **The slayer of demons** — She is praised explicitly as crusher not only of **Mahiṣa** but of the broader demon-host — including epithets pointing to **Śumbha and Niśumbha** and the asura armies — the same foes felled across the Māhātmya's three *caritas*. (*Mahiṣāsura-mardinī stotra*) [scholarly]
- **The battle-imagery** — Verses paint the **clash of weapons, the trembling of the asuras, the flashing sword and trident, the lion mount**, and the Goddess's beauty-in-fury — *vīra-rasa* (the heroic mood) shot through with *śṛṅgāra* (her loveliness even in combat). [scholarly]
- **The closing surrender** — Later stanzas turn from narration to **plea**: the devotee, helpless and sinful, asks when the Mother's grace-glance will fall on him — the hymn's one shift from triumph to supplication, and its devotional pay-off. (*Mahiṣāsura-mardinī stotra*, closing verses) [scholarly]

## Relation to the Devī Māhātmya

- **Lyric counterpart, not the source** — The **myth** of Mahiṣa's slaying belongs to the **Devī Māhātmya** (Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, the second *carita*), where the Goddess **Durgā/Caṇḍikā**, formed from the pooled radiance (*tejas*) of the gods, beheads the shape-shifting buffalo-asura. This stotra **versifies and sings** that episode but adds no new narrative. (*Devī Māhātmya*; *Mahiṣāsura-mardinī stotra*) [scholarly]
  - *Cross-reference:* the full narrative — Mahiṣa's boon, his shape-shifting, the gods' tejas, and the beheading — is treated in **[Devī Māhātmya / Durgā Saptaśatī](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md)**.
- **Two clocks** — In **scriptural time** the Mahiṣa-slaying is a *yuga*-spanning, cosmically-recurring deed of the eternal Goddess (the Māhātmya places her victories across cosmic ages); the **stotra itself** is a far later **medieval devotional composition** built on that myth, not a record of it — its date belongs to **textual history**, the deed it sings to sacred time. [scholarly]

## Authorship, date, and attribution

- **Traditional ascription to Ādi Śaṅkara** — Like the [Soundarya Laharī](soundarya-lahari.md), [Kanakadhārā](kanakadhara-stotram.md), and a large body of stotras, the hymn is **conventionally attributed to Ādi Śaṅkara** in printed collections and devotional usage. (devotional tradition) [disputed]
  - *Variant (Rāmakṛṣṇa Kavi / other authors):* the polished, ornate *citra-kāvya* style leads several scholars to doubt the Śaṅkara attribution and to assign it to a **later medieval poet**; specific alternative authors are sometimes proposed but none is text-critically settled. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Stotra-corpus pattern** — Attribution of a sound-virtuoso Devī hymn to Śaṅkara follows the same hagiographic pattern that gathers the great Śākta and Śaiva stotras under his name; the ascription is best read as **traditional rather than historical**. [scholarly]

## Living use

- **Navarātri and Durgā Pūjā** — The hymn is a **standard Navarātri recitation**, sung especially on the days devoted to Durgā's martial forms and at Mahiṣāsura-mardinī shrines; its triumphal refrain suits the festival's celebration of the Goddess's victory. (devotional tradition)
- **A musical favourite** — Its rhythmic density has made it one of the **most-recorded Devī stotras** in classical and devotional music; the galloping refrain carries readily into melody, sustaining its popularity far beyond temple liturgy. [scholarly]
- **Daily and votive japa** — Recited as **stuti for strength, protection, and removal of obstacles**, invoking the Goddess in her demon-slaying aspect — the protective, victorious mode of the one Devī. (devotional tradition)

## Sources

**Primary text**
- *Mahiṣāsura-mardinī stotra* ("Hymn to the Slayer of Mahiṣa"), opening **"Ayi giri-nandini nandita-medini..."** and refrained on **"jaya jaya he mahiṣāsura-mardini ramya-kapardini śaila-sutē"** — a ~21–23-verse Devī *stuti* in a dense internally-rhymed meter, traditionally attributed to **Ādi Śaṅkara**.

**Thematic / mythic source**
- *Devī Māhātmya* (Durgā Saptaśatī), embedded in the **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa** — the second *carita*, in which **Durgā/Caṇḍikā**, formed of the gods' pooled *tejas*, slays the shape-shifting buffalo-asura **Mahiṣa**; the narrative the stotra lyricizes. [scholarly]

**Attribution / tradition**
- The **Śaṅkara ascription** — received in printed stotra-collections and devotional use; doubted by scholars on stylistic grounds (the ornate *citra-kāvya* register), who place the hymn in a **later medieval** stratum. [scholarly] [disputed]
- Living recitation — **Navarātri / Durgā Pūjā** use, votive *japa* for protection and victory, and its prominence as one of the most-set Devī hymns in Indian devotional and classical music. (devotional tradition)
