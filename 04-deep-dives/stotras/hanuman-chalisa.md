# Hanuman Chalisa

> *Up one level: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Goswami Tulsīdās's forty-verse Awadhī hymn to Hanumān — the most-recited Hanumān text in living Hindu practice — composed as a devotional companion to his [Rāmcaritmānas](../ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) and catalogued among the song-discourses of the [Lesser Gītās catalog](../lesser-gitas-catalog.md).*

The **Hanumān Cālīsā** ("Forty [Verses] to Hanumān," from *cālīs*, "forty"), opening with the maṅgalācaraṇa *śrī guru caraṇa saroja raja...* ("With the dust of my Guru's lotus-feet..."), is a short devotional hymn in the **Awadhī** dialect of Hindi, traditionally ascribed to the poet-saint **Goswami Tulsīdās** (c. 1532–1623 CE). It is, by recitation-count, almost certainly the **single most-recited Hindu devotional text in the world**, chanted daily, on Tuesdays and Saturdays, and in moments of fear across North India and the global Hindu diaspora. Its structure is fixed: **two opening dohās**, **forty caupāīs** (the "forty" that names the poem), and **one closing dohā**, totalling forty-three verses. [scholarly]

---

## Authorship and date

- **Tulsīdās the composer** — Tradition attributes the hymn to **Goswami Tulsīdās**, the sixteenth–seventeenth-century Rāmabhakti poet of Vārāṇasī / Ayodhyā, author of the **Rāmcaritmānas**; the Cālīsā is read as a devotional offshoot of his larger Rāma corpus, written in the same vernacular register accessible to ordinary devotees rather than in Sanskrit. (Tulsīdās tradition) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (legend of composition):* a popular hagiographical tale holds that Tulsīdās composed the Cālīsā while **imprisoned by the Mughal emperor Akbar / Jahāngīr**, and that on its recitation an army of monkeys besieged the court, winning his release; this frame-legend is devotional folklore, not securely documented. [folk] [disputed]
- **Companion to the Rāmcaritmānas** — The Cālīsā functions as a stand-alone **stuti** (praise-hymn) extracted from and harmonized with the world of the *Mānas*; many of its references (Hanumān as Rāma's messenger, the burning of Laṅkā, the carrying of the mountain) presuppose the Rāmāyaṇa narrative Tulsīdās had already retold. (See [Rāmcaritmānas](../ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md).) [scholarly]
- **Self-naming colophon** — The closing verses embed the author's signature: the line *tulasīdāsa sadā hari cerā* ("Tulsīdās, ever the servant of Hari") names the poet within the text, in the manner of the bhakti *chāp* (poetic signature). (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, cl. cpr.) [scholarly]

## Structure and verse-sequence

The forty caupāīs move through a deliberate sequence — invocation, Hanumān's form, his powers, his Rāmāyaṇa deeds, and protective benediction.

- **Opening dohās (maṅgalācaraṇa)** — Two dohās frame the hymn: the first praises the **Guru's lotus-feet** as the means of purifying the mind; the second confesses the poet's lack of wit (*buddhi-hīna*) and prays Hanumān for **strength, intelligence, and wisdom** (*bala buddhi bidyā dehu mohiṃ*). (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, dohā 1–2) [scholarly]
- **Praise of Hanumān's form and titles (cpr. 1–3)** — Hanumān hailed as **Jaya Hanumān, jñāna-guṇa-sāgara** ("ocean of knowledge and virtue"), **lord of the monkeys**, illuminator of the three worlds; son of **Vāyu** (Pavanasuta), of immense valour, companion of **Kesarī** and **Añjanī**. (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, cpr. 1–3) [scholarly]
- **The radiant body (cpr. 4–7)** — Description of his **golden hue**, fine garments and curling locks, the **vajra and dhvajā (mace and banner)** in his hands, the sacred thread of *muñja* grass across his shoulder; ear-rings flashing as he serves Rāma. (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, cpr. 4–7) [scholarly]
- **The learned and devoted servant (cpr. 8–10)** — Hanumān as **Śaṅkara's amśa / suvana** (in this tradition a portion or son of Śiva), wise, valorous, **dear to Rāma**, treasury of countless virtues, ever delighting in Rāma's service. (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, cpr. 8–10) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Śiva-relation):* the line *śaṅkara suvana kesarī nandana* is read variously as making Hanumān an **incarnation / portion of Śiva** (the dominant Śaiva-inflected reading) or merely as praise-epithet; the Rudrāvatāra identification is itself a later devotional layer. [regional] [disputed]
- **The Rāmāyaṇa feats (cpr. 11–18)** — The hymn rehearses Hanumān's epic deeds, presupposing the **Sundara-kāṇḍa**: he **bears Rāma's message to Sītā** and shows her the signet-ring; he **burns Laṅkā** (*laṅkā jāri*); he **carries the whole mountain** of healing herbs (the Sañjīvanī episode) to revive Lakṣmaṇa, for which Rāma embraces him; praise of his service to Sugrīva and Vibhīṣaṇa. (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, cpr. 11–18; cf. [Sundara-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/5-sundara-kanda.md)) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (swallowing the sun):* cpr. 18, *juga sahasra jojana para bhānū... līlyo tāhi madhura phala jānū*, recalls the child Hanumān **leaping at the sun mistaking it for a fruit**; some modern readers compute the stated distance (*yuga-sahasra-yojana*) as a numerically exact figure for the earth–sun distance — a popular but apologetic [folk] reading, not the verse's devotional intent. [folk] [disputed]
- **Cosmic reach and grace (cpr. 19–26)** — Hanumān as guardian of the four ages (*cāroṃ juga paratāpa tumhārā*), praised by Rāma as equal to a brother (*tuma mama priya bharatahi sama bhāī*), invoked by sages, Brahmā and the gods; whoever takes his refuge crosses every difficulty. (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, cpr. 19–26) [scholarly]
- **The protective benedictions (cpr. 27–35)** — The hymn turns frankly apotropaic: by Hanumān's grace **all ailments and pains are removed** (*nāse roga hare saba pīrā*), **ghosts, demons and evil spirits flee** (*bhūta piśāca nikaṭa nahiṃ āvai... mahābīra jaba nāma sunāvai*), troubles and terrors vanish for the one who recites him constantly. This protective core is the chief reason for the hymn's daily use. (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, cpr. 27–35) [scholarly]
- **Refuge and devotion (cpr. 36–40)** — Closing caupāīs make Hanumān the **gatekeeper of Rāma's grace** (*rāma duāre tuma rakhavāre*), the giver of *sukha* and the destroyer of distress; the poet begs to dwell ever in Hanumān's heart along with Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa and Sītā. (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, cpr. 36–40) [scholarly]
- **Closing dohā** — A single concluding dohā, *pavana-tanaya saṅkaṭa-haraṇa...* ("O son of the wind, remover of distress..."), prays Hanumān to dwell in the devotee's heart with Rāma. (*Hanumān Cālīsā*, closing dohā) [scholarly]

## The recitation tradition

- **Daily and weekly recitation** — The Cālīsā is recited **daily** by countless devotees, and especially on **Tuesdays (Maṅgalavāra)** and **Saturdays (Śanivāra)**, the two days associated with Hanumān worship and with mitigating the affliction of **Maṅgala (Mars)** and **Śani (Saturn)** in popular astrology. (living North-Indian practice) [regional]
- **Protective / apotropaic use** — Because of its benedictory verses, the hymn is invoked as a **shield against fear, illness, nightmares and malign spirits**; it is recited at night, before travel, in illness, and at the threshold of danger, often a fixed number of times (e.g. 7, 11, 21, or 108 repetitions in vows). (living practice) [folk]
- **Temple and public chanting** — Mass collective recitation (*Sundar-kāṇḍ pāṭh* and *Hanumān Cālīsā* sessions) is a fixture of North-Indian temple life and of Hanumān shrines; the hymn is among the first Hindu texts a child memorizes. (living practice) [regional]
- **Catalogued among song-hymns** — Within this knowledge base it sits alongside the other independent devotional hymns and song-discourses treated in the [Lesser Gītās catalog](../lesser-gitas-catalog.md). [scholarly]

## Two clocks: scriptural time vs. composition date

- **Scriptural / narrative time** — The *content* of the Cālīsā belongs to the **Tretā-yuga** world of the Rāmāyaṇa — Hanumān's service to Rāma, the crossing to Laṅkā, the Sundara-kāṇḍa feats — which the hymn treats as eternally real and ever-present. [scholarly]
- **Historical / composition time** — The *text* is a **late-medieval Awadhī devotional poem**, composed by Tulsīdās in roughly the **late sixteenth century CE** as a vernacular bhakti hymn; its forty-caupāī form, signature, and Akbar-era frame-legend are all features of this historical period, not of epic antiquity. Keep the eternal narrative subject distinct from the datable literary work. [scholarly]

## Sources

**Primary text**
- *Hanumān Cālīsā*, attributed to **Goswami Tulsīdās** — forty-caupāī Awadhī hymn with two opening dohās and one closing dohā; the author's *chāp* (signature, *tulasīdāsa sadā hari cerā*) appears in the closing verses. Composed as a devotional companion to Tulsīdās's **Rāmcaritmānas**.
- **Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa**, *Sundara-kāṇḍa* and *Yuddha-kāṇḍa* — narrative substratum for the messenger, burning-of-Laṅkā, and Sañjīvanī-mountain feats the Cālīsā rehearses (see [Sundara-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/5-sundara-kanda.md)).

**Tradition / context**
- Tulsīdās corpus and Rāmabhakti devotional milieu (see [Rāmcaritmānas](../ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md)).
- Living North-Indian recitation practice: Tuesday/Saturday observance, apotropaic and vow-based repetition (folk/regional, oral tradition).
