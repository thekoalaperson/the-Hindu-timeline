# Mukunda Mala Stotra

> *Up one level: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · The **Mukunda-mālā** ("Garland for Mukunda"), the Sanskrit devotional garland of the Chera king-saint **[Kulaśekhara Āḻvār](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/alvars-and-nayanars.md)** — a first-tier Vaiṣṇava bhakti hymn of total surrender and renunciation by an Āḻvār who laid down a throne for the Name; sibling to the [Viṣṇu Sahasranāma](vishnu-sahasranama.md) and the surrender-hymn [Gajendra Mokṣa](../gajendra-moksha.md), and rooted in the Tamil [Āḻvār / Divya Desam](../../06-sacred-geography/108-divya-desams.md) world.*

The **Mukunda-mālā Stotra** (मुकुन्दमाला, "the Garland [offered to] Mukunda") is a short Sanskrit hymn of about **thirty to fifty-three verses** (recensions vary) addressed to **Mukunda** — Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa as "the giver of liberation (*mukti-da*)." It is ascribed to **Kulaśekhara**, the Chera king counted among the twelve **Āḻvārs**, the Tamil Vaiṣṇava poet-saints whose hymns make up the *Nālāyira Divya Prabandham*. Where most Āḻvār output is in Tamil, the Mukunda-mālā is Kulaśekhara's celebrated work **in Sanskrit**, a *prapatti* (surrender) and *vairāgya* (renunciation) garland prized across Śrī-Vaiṣṇava and broader Vaiṣṇava devotion. Its most-quoted prayer asks only for unbroken remembrance of Hari at the hour of death. [scholarly]

---

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / devotional time** — As bhakti utterance the hymn stands outside narrative chronology: it addresses the eternal Viṣṇu-Nārāyaṇa and invokes episodes from across the yugas — the *avatāras*, the rescue of **[Gajendra](../gajendra-moksha.md)** the elephant-king, the protection of **Draupadī** and **Prahlāda** — as timeless proofs of the Lord's saving grace. Its events are thus **cross-yuga**, gathered into a single plea for refuge. [scriptural]
- **Manuscript / historical time** — Kulaśekhara the Āḻvār is traditionally dated to the **Kali Yuga's historical layer, c. 7th–9th century CE** [disputed], identified by tradition with a **Chera ruler** of the Tamil country [traditional]. The Sanskrit Mukunda-mālā's exact authorship is debated: some scholars distinguish the **Āḻvār Kulaśekhara** from a later **Kulaśekhara of the Sanskrit text**, and a longer South-Indian recension differs from the shorter North-Indian one. Keep the timeless devotional address distinct from the historical poet-king. [scholarly] [disputed]

---

## The poet — Kulaśekhara Āḻvār, the king who renounced

- **The Chera king-saint** — **Kulaśekhara** is remembered as a **Chera (Kerala) monarch** who abandoned the cares of kingship for absorption in Viṣṇu, the paradigmatic *rāja-ṛṣi* (royal sage) of the Āḻvār canon; his Tamil work is the **Perumāḷ Tirumoḻi**, the Sanskrit garland the **Mukunda-mālā**. ([Āḻvārs and Nāyaṉārs](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/alvars-and-nayanars.md)) [traditional]
- **The frame of renunciation** — The hymn's animating posture is a **king laying down his crown**: the verses repeatedly disclaim wealth, kingdom, and worldly desire (*na dhanaṃ na janaṃ na sundarīm*) in favour of devotion alone — the literary frame of royal *vairāgya* that gives the garland its emotional charge. [scholarly]
- **Dāsya and śaraṇāgati** — Kulaśekhara's devotion is marked by **dāsya** (the servant's mood) and **prapatti / śaraṇāgati** (self-surrender), the keynotes the Śrī-Vaiṣṇava tradition would later systematize; the Mukunda-mālā is read as an early lyric monument of that surrender-theology. [scholarly]

## Structure and famous verses

- **A garland of surrender-prayers** — The hymn is a string (*mālā*) of independent stanzas, each a "flower" of prayer to Mukunda; there is no narrative thread, only an accumulating plea for refuge, remembrance, and the company of the devout. (Mukunda-mālā) [scholarly]
- **The "only this" prayer** — Its single most-quoted verse asks for nothing but devotion: *na dhanaṃ na janaṃ na sundarīṃ kavitāṃ vā jagad-īśa kāmaye / mama janmani janmanīśvare bhavatād bhaktir ahaitukī tvayi* — "I do not crave wealth, followers, a beautiful woman, or poetic fame, O Lord of the world; let me have, birth after birth, **motiveless devotion (bhaktir ahaitukī)** to You." (Mukunda-mālā) [scholarly]
- **The death-bed prayer** — A second celebrated stanza begs that the Name be on the lips at death, when the throat is choked: *kṛṣṇa tvadīya-padapaṅkaja-pañjarāntam … prāṇa-prayāṇa-samaye …* — that the soul, caged at the lotus-feet, may remember Hari in the final hour, the moment the hymn most fears to face unprotected. (Mukunda-mālā) [scholarly]
- **The roll-call of the rescued** — Several verses invoke past saving-acts as warrant for the poet's own hope: the Lord who **freed Gajendra**, **clothed Draupadī**, **saved Prahlāda**, and **lifted Ahalyā** is asked to be the singer's refuge too — a cross-yuga catalogue of grace. (Mukunda-mālā) [scriptural]
- **Jihvē kīrtaya — command to the tongue** — A famous opening-cluster addresses the poet's own faculties — *jihvē kīrtaya keśavaṃ* ("O tongue, sing of Keśava!"), and the mind, hands, ears, eyes each commanded to their part of worship — turning the whole body into an instrument of *kīrtana*. (Mukunda-mālā) [scholarly]
- **Preference for present devotion over future liberation** — A recurring theme prizes **living bhakti now** over even *mukti*: the poet would rather sing Mukunda's names than possess kingship or even a disembodied liberation that lacked the joy of devotional service — anticipating later Vaiṣṇava valuations of *bhakti* above *mokṣa*. [scholarly]

## Recensions and the authorship question

- **Short and long recensions** — The hymn circulates in a **shorter (~30–34 verse)** and a **longer (~40–53 verse)** form; the additional stanzas in the longer South-Indian recension are not uniform across manuscripts. Verse counts cited in editions therefore differ. (manuscript tradition) [scholarly] [disputed]
  - *Variant (the two Kulaśekharas):* some scholars hold the Sanskrit Mukunda-mālā was composed by a **later Kulaśekhara** distinct from the 7th–9th-c. Tamil Āḻvār, on linguistic and stylistic grounds; the **devotional tradition identifies them as one**. Do not merge the claims. [disputed] [scholarly]
  - *Variant (regional attributions):* a **Kerala tradition** firmly claims Kulaśekhara as a Chera king of its own soil, while **Tamil Śrī-Vaiṣṇava** memory absorbs him fully into the Āḻvār line; both honor the same saint through different regional lenses. [regional]

## Place in the corpus and living use

- **Sanskrit voice of a Tamil saint** — The Mukunda-mālā is unusual as an **Āḻvār's Sanskrit hymn**, a bridge between the Tamil **[Divya Prabandham / Divya Desam](../../06-sacred-geography/108-divya-desams.md)** devotion and the pan-Indian Sanskrit *stotra* tradition; it is recited in Śrī-Vaiṣṇava households and *maṭhas*. [folk]
- **Companion to the surrender-hymns** — Thematically it sits beside **[Gajendra Mokṣa](../gajendra-moksha.md)** (the elephant-king's cry of total surrender, which the hymn explicitly invokes) and the name-litany **[Viṣṇu Sahasranāma](vishnu-sahasranama.md)** — three monuments of *śaraṇāgati* and *nāma-smaraṇa*. [scholarly]
- **Kindred Āḻvār works** — It belongs with the hymns of the other Āḻvārs — **[Āṇḍāḷ](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/andal.md)**'s *Tiruppāvai* and **[Nammāḻvār](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/nammalvar.md)**'s *Tiruvāymoḻi* — the surviving corpus of the twelve poet-saints who sang Viṣṇu's abodes. [scholarly]
- **A Gauḍīya favourite too** — Outside the South, the Mukunda-mālā's *ahaitukī bhakti* verse is much-quoted in **Gauḍīya (Caitanya) Vaiṣṇavism** as a textbook statement of motiveless, selfless devotion, showing the hymn's reach beyond its Tamil home. [regional]
