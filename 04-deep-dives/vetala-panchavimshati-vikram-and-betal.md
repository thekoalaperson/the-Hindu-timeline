# Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati — Vikram and the Vetāla (the frame & the 25 riddle-tales)

> Deep-dive: the **"Twenty-five Tales of the Vetāla"** — the corpse-spirit's dilemma-tales told to a silent king in the cremation ground. *Two clocks:* the story runs on the **Kali-Yuga legend clock** of the [Vikramāditya cycle](../90-literature-corpus/brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md) (Vikrama era, epoch 57 BCE — see [calendar](../00-time-and-cosmology/hindu-calendar-and-panchanga.md)); every *extant text* is Kali-Yuga documented history, 11th c. CE onward **[scholarly]**. The vetāla as *being-class*: [Vidyādharas, Siddhas & spirits](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md); the legend's civic home: [Ujjain / Avantikā §IX](../06-sacred-geography/ujjain-avantika.md). ## The text and its clock

- **What it is** — a frame-plus-catalog: a king carries a vetāla-haunted corpse through a śmaśāna by night while the spirit tells tale after tale, each ending in a **dilemma-question**. Oldest surviving home: **Somadeva's Kathāsaritsāgara** (Kashmir, c. 1070 CE **[scholarly]**), Lambhaka XII (Śaśāṅkavatī), **Taraṅgas 75–99** (Tawney/Penzer vols. VI–VII); Kṣemendra's **Bṛhatkathāmañjarī** (c. 1037 CE **[scholarly]**) carries a parallel set; both descend from Guṇāḍhya's lost **Bṛhatkathā** — see the [Bṛhatkathā node](../90-literature-corpus/brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md). **[late text]**
- **Counting to twenty-five** — in Somadeva the vetāla tells **24 tales**; the conclusion is reckoned the 25th, giving *pañca-viṃśati*. Recensions vary: **Jambhaladatta** swaps in tales absent from Somadeva (the four merchant princes and the courtesan; Mūladeva's bride for Śaśideva; the ogre who ravaged Arimaulimani's kingdom). (Penzer vol. VII, tale-tables) **[scholarly]**
- **Which king?** — in Somadeva and Kṣemendra: **Trivikramasena, son of Vikramasena**, ruling **Pratiṣṭhāna on the [Godāvarī](../06-sacred-geography/godavari-gautami-dakshina-ganga.md)** (= Paithan **[scholarly]**). (Kathāsaritsāgara XII.75)
  - *Variant (Śivadāsa; Hindi Baitāl Pacīsī):* the hero is **Vikramāditya (Bikram)** of **Ujjayinī**, fusing the cycle to the [Ujjain](../06-sacred-geography/ujjain-avantika.md) throne-legend. **[late text]** [folk]
  - *Variant (Jambhaladatta, ed. Emeneau):* the king is **Vikramakeśarin**. **[scholarly]**

## The frame: Kṣāntiśīla's treachery (KSS XII, Taraṅga 75)

- **The gem-bearing fruits** — the mendicant **Kṣāntiśīla** ("Patience") daily presents the king **one fruit**, year after year; one, given to a pet monkey, splits open on a **priceless gem** — every discarded fruit in the treasury holds another. Obligated, the king agrees to meet him on a **dark-fortnight night** in the **great cremation ground**. (Kathāsaritsāgara XII.75)
- **Fetch the corpse** — from his magic circle the monk sends the king south, alone, to a **śiṃśapā (sissoo) tree** from which a **corpse hangs**, to be cut down and carried back **in silence**. The corpse is possessed by a **vetāla** — it shrieks when cut down, laughs when rubbed, and **flies back to its tree** whenever the king speaks. (Kathāsaritsāgara XII.75)
- **The rules of the game** — walking, the vetāla tells a tale ending in a riddle: **if the king knows the answer yet stays silent, his head bursts; if he answers, his silence breaks and the corpse returns to the tree; only honest ignorance lets him walk on.** Twenty-four times the king answers and shoulders the corpse again. (Kathāsaritsāgara XII.75–98)

## The twenty-four riddles, in Somadeva's order

*(Vetāla tale N = Taraṅga 74+N; titles after Tawney/Penzer and Ryder. Other recensions reorder and substitute freely.)*

- **1. The prince who won a wife by sign-language** — Prince **Vajramukuṭa** wins **Padmāvatī**, whose riddling gestures his shrewd **minister's son** decodes; their stratagems leave her parents dead of grief. *Whose the guilt?* — **King Karṇotpala's**, whose spies failed to know his own city. (KSS XII, Vetāla 1)
- **2. The three suitors of Mandāravatī** — three Brahmins love **Mandāravatī**, who dies of fever; one **sleeps on her ashes**, one carries her **bones to the Gaṅgā**, one wins a **spell of revival** and restores her. *Whose wife is she?* — the spell-giver acted as **father**, the bone-carrier as **son**; the constant **ash-sleeper** is her **husband**. (KSS XII, Vetāla 2)
- **3. The parrot and the śārikā** — a betrothed prince's **parrot** and princess's **myna** quarrel: *are men or women more wicked?* — the myna tells of a husband who murdered his wife for jewels; the parrot, of an adulteress who ruined her blameless husband. The king rules, with the text's own misogynist edge, **women**. (KSS XII, Vetāla 3)
- **4. Vīravara the loyal** — the retainer **Vīravara** gives his salary to charity and stands night-guard for King **Śūdraka**; when only the offering of Vīravara's **son** to the goddess can save the king, son, daughter, wife and Vīravara all die — and the king, about to offer his own head, moves the goddess to **restore all**. *Noblest?* — **Śūdraka**: servants owe masters their lives; a king who would die for a servant is beyond price. (KSS XII, Vetāla 4; parallel in Hitopadeśa III, cf. [fable corpus](../90-literature-corpus/panchatantra-hitopadesha-and-fable-literature.md) **[scholarly]**)
- **5. Somaprabhā and the three accomplished suitors** — **Somaprabhā** is promised at once to a man of all-seeing **knowledge**, a builder of a **flying chariot**, and a **warrior**; a rākṣasa abducts her — the knower finds her, the chariot carries them, the warrior kills the demon. *Whose is she?* — the **warrior's**; the others were his **instruments**. (KSS XII, Vetāla 5)
- **6. The transposed heads** — the washerman **Dhavala**, granted his beloved **Madanasundarī**, beheads himself in offering at a shrine of **Gaurī**; her brother follows; the Goddess grants the wife their lives — in haste she **sets each head on the wrong body**. *Which is her husband?* — he who **bears the husband's head**: the head rules identity. (KSS XII, Vetāla 6)
- **7. The king and his dependent** — a poor retainer of King **Pracaṇḍasiṃha** **leaps into the sea** after a divine banner-maiden beneath the waves; the king leaps after him to repay his service and wins the maiden **for his servant**. *More credit for the plunge?* — the **dependent**, who leapt without knowledge or hope; the king knew what waited. (KSS XII, Vetāla 7)
- **8. The three fastidious Brahmins** — brothers, connoisseurs of **food, women and beds**, tested by a king: one detects **corpse-smoke** in rice grown by a cremation ground, one smells **goat** beneath a beauty's perfume, one is **welted by a hair under seven mattresses**. *Most refined?* — the **bed-expert**: his mark was proof no foreknowledge could fake. (KSS XII, Vetāla 8)
  - *Variant (Śivadāsa line):* the tale drifts to near the collection's end (Śivadāsa's 23rd). **[scholarly]**
- **9. Anaṅgaratī and the four varṇa suitors** — princess **Anaṅgaratī** may choose a **śūdra** master-weaver, a **vaiśya** knowing beast-speech, a **kṣatriya** swordsman, or a **brāhmaṇa** who raises the dead. *To whom?* — the **kṣatriya swordsman**, her equal in birth and valor; wonder-arts do not outweigh varṇa. (KSS XII, Vetāla 9)
- **10. Madanasenā's rash promise** — accosted before her wedding, **Madanasenā** vows to visit her importuner first; her **husband** releases her for truth's sake, a **robber** lets her pass with her jewels, the shamed **lover** sends her back inviolate. *Most magnanimous?* — the **robber**, who renounced with nothing binding him. (KSS XII, Vetāla 10; Penzer traces the motif to the *Franklin's Tale* **[scholarly]**)
- **11. The three sensitive wives** — of King **Dharmadhvaja's** queens, one is **bruised by a falling lotus-petal**, one **blistered by moonbeams**, one **wounded by the mere sound of a pestle**. *Most delicate?* — the **pestle-hearer**: nothing ever touched her. (KSS XII, Vetāla 11)
- **12. King Yaśaḥketu and his celestial wife** — **Yaśaḥketu of Aṅga** leaves his realm to minister **Dīrghadarśin** and wins a divine maiden beyond the sea; brought home, she stays — and the old minister's **heart breaks**. *Why?* — not envy: foreboding that a king who neglected duty for mortal women would be **wholly lost** to a celestial one. (KSS XII, Vetāla 12)
- **13. Harisvāmin, who lost his wife and then his life** — the [Vārāṇasī](../06-sacred-geography/kashi-varanasi.md) Brahmin **Harisvāmin** loses his wife to an abductor, wanders begging, and dies when **poison drips from a snake in a hawk's claws** into alms a charitable woman gave him. *Who bears the Brahmin-murder?* — not snake, hawk, nor hostess: the sin falls on **whoever rashly imputes the guilt**. (KSS XII, Vetāla 13)
- **14. The merchant's daughter and the thief** — in [Ayodhyā](../06-sacred-geography/ayodhya.md), a girl who spurned every suitor loves a **robber led to impalement**; ransom is refused; dying, the thief **weeps, then laughs**; she mounts his pyre, and Śiva restores him. *Why tears, why laughter?* — he **wept** that he could never repay such devotion, **laughed** that she rejected kings for a thief. (KSS XII, Vetāla 14)
- **15. The magic pill** — the arch-rogue **Mūladeva** gives lovelorn **Manaḥsvāmin** a **gulikā** that turns him into a girl, deposited in trust as a "daughter-in-law" so he can reach the princess he desires; later Mūladeva claims the vanished "bride" for his companion **Śaśin**, and the king weds his daughter to Śaśin — the two men dispute her. *Whose wife?* — **Śaśin's**: given openly by her father in lawful rite; the secret union was **theft**. (KSS XII, Vetāla 15)
- **16. Jīmūtavāhana and Śaṅkhacūḍa** — the bodhisattva-like Vidyādhara prince **Jīmūtavāhana** takes the place of the [nāga](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md) youth **Śaṅkhacūḍa** on the rock where [Garuḍa](../08-beings-and-bestiary/garuda.md) daily devours a serpent; mangled, he is healed by **Gaurī**, and Garuḍa forswears serpents and revives past victims. *More self-sacrificing?* — **Śaṅkhacūḍa**: already spared, he **ran after Garuḍa** to offer his body back. (KSS XII, Vetāla 16; staged in Harṣa's *Nāgānanda*; not the Śaṅkhacūḍa of [Śaṅkhacūḍa–Tulasī](shankhachuda-tulasi.md))
- **17. Unmādinī** — the merchant's daughter **Unmādinī**, whose beauty maddens all beholders, is rejected by the king on his Brahmins' false report and married to his **commander**; seeing her at last, the king **refuses to take another man's wife though the general himself offers her**, wastes away and dies; the general enters the fire after him. *Noblest?* — the **king**: a monarch above restraint who chose death over adharma is the rarer hero. (KSS XII, Vetāla 17)
- **18. The Brahmin youth who lost the magic power** — (here the vetāla first multiplies into a **tree-full of identical corpses**, relenting at the king's resolve). In **Ujjayinī** a Brahmin youth, given a siddhi-rite, must enter its visionary pyre unflinching; he **hesitates** even after the fire proves cool, and the spell is lost to pupil and teacher alike. *Why?* — magic answers not hardship but the **unhesitating heart**; the teacher lost his for choosing the unworthy. (KSS XII, Vetāla 18)
- **19. The thief's son** — a still-living **impaled thief** buys marriage to a fleeing widow's daughter **Dhanavatī**, any son to count as his; the boy, born later of a Brahmin union, is left by divine command at a **childless king's gate** and reared as prince; offering **piṇḍa at [Gayā](../06-sacred-geography/gaya-pitru-tirtha.md)**, he sees **three hands rise from the well** — thief's, begetter's, foster-father's. *Whose the cake?* — the **thief's**: purchase and rite made him the lawful father. (KSS XII, Vetāla 19)
- **20. The Brahmin boy's laughter** — to ransom King **Candrāvaloka** of Citrakūṭa from the brahmarākṣasa **Jvālāmukha**, a seven-year-old Brahmin boy volunteers as the human sacrifice, his parents paid to hold his limbs; as the sword falls he **laughs**, and all — even the demon — stop and bow. *Why laugh at death?* — every appointed refuge stood **inverted** (parents restraining him for money, the king killing to save himself, a god-like being waiting to eat him): he laughed at their delusion over the perishable body. (KSS XII, Vetāla 20)
- **21. Anaṅgamañjarī, her husband and her lover** — **Anaṅgamañjarī**, wife of **Maṇivarman**, dies of sheer longing at her tryst with the Brahmin **Kamalākara**, who dies of grief upon her; the returning husband dies at the sight; the goddess **Caṇḍī** revives all three, their passion erased. *Most infatuated?* — **Maṇivarman**: where anger was warranted, he died of grief. (KSS XII, Vetāla 21)
- **22. The four brothers and the lion** — four learned sons of **Viṣṇusvāmin**, testing their sciences on bones, give a skeleton **flesh, hide, limbs — and life**: it is a **lion**, and it kills all four. *Who is guilty?* — the **life-giver**: the rest worked in ignorance; he saw the lion's form and animated it to flaunt his skill. (KSS XII, Vetāla 22)
- **23. The hermit who wept and danced** — an aged ascetic, master of **parakāya-praveśa** (entering another's body), quits his withered frame for the fresh corpse of a Brahmin boy on the pyre — first **weeping aloud, then dancing**. *Why?* — grief at leaving the body his parents reared and his tapas won; joy at the youth that could carry his powers further. (KSS XII, Vetāla 23)
- **24. The Great Puzzle** — a slain fugitive king's widow and daughter, judged by their **footprints**, are wed by a hunter-chief and his son: the small-footed **mother to the son**, the large-footed **daughter to the father**, inverting the generations. *What kin are the children of the two pairs?* — the king **cannot answer** and, for the first time, walks on in honest silence. (KSS XII, Vetāla 24)

## The pay-off: the mendicant beheaded (KSS XII, Taraṅga 99)

- **The vetāla's warning** — pleased by the king's perseverance, the vetāla yields the corpse and reveals that **Kṣāntiśīla means to offer the king himself** as the rite's human sacrifice, to win sovereignty over the sky-beings; he will bid the king **prostrate flat before the corpse** — the killing stroke. Say instead: *"Show me how — I do not know this attitude of reverence."* (Kathāsaritsāgara XII.99)
- **The biter bitten** — the monk worships the vetāla-corpse in his blood-smeared maṇḍala and orders the prostration; the king asks to be shown, and as Kṣāntiśīla lies down **beheads him at a stroke**, offering head and heart to the spirit; the gods cry "Well done!" (Kathāsaritsāgara XII.99)
- **The boons** — the vetāla promises the **riddle-sessions will circulate the world with honor**, their telling releasing from sin, and that **wherever they are told the dark spirit-hosts have no power**; then it quits the corpse. [Śiva](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) appears, granting the sword **Aparājita ("Invincible")**, earth-rule, future **emperorship over the Vidyādharas** (Somadeva's frame), and final union with himself. (Kathāsaritsāgara XII.99) **[late text]**

## Recensions, transmission, afterlife

- **Śivadāsa recension** (c. 11th–14th c. CE **[scholarly]**) — independent Sanskrit version in **prose mixed with verse**; hero Vikramāditya; basis of most vernacular lines.
- **Jambhaladatta recension** (c. 11th–14th c. CE **[scholarly]**) — a second Sanskrit line (ed./tr. Emeneau, 1934) with a partly different tale-roster (above); a **Newari** version descends from it. **[regional]**
- **Hindi Baitāl Pacīsī** — Śivadāsa's line rendered into **Braj Bhasha by Ṣūrat Kabīshwar (1719–49)**, then into **Hindustani in 1805 by Lallū Lāl** and colleagues under John Gilchrist — a **foundational text of literary Hindi**; hence the popular "**Vikram aur Betāl**." **[scholarly]** [regional]
- **Wider spread** — a Tamil *Vedāla-kadai* and other regional versions cross South Asia; the Mongolian *Siddhi-Kür* is the best-known Central Asian descendant. [regional]
- **Modern echoes** — the transposed-heads riddle (Vetāla 6) seeds Thomas Mann's *The Transposed Heads* (1940, via Heinrich Zimmer) and Girish Karnad's *Hayavadana* (1971); Ramanand Sagar's serial ***Vikram aur Betaal* (1985)** fixed the upside-down-hanging Betāl of popular culture. **[scholarly]** [folk]
