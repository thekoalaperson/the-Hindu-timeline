# Icakki Amman Katai (Nili Katai) — the vengeance of Palaiyanur Nili

> Cosmic address: **Kali Yuga — documented history**, at the folk edge of the record — a Tamil **bow-song (vil pāṭṭu / villuppāṭṭu)** revenge-goddess epic of the Kanyakumari–Tirunelveli country, wholly outside the Sanskrit canon [folk] [regional] [oral tradition]; its retribution runs on the **karma-and-rebirth clock** (a murder of one birth avenged in the next), not on purāṇic yuga time.
> Up: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Parent corpus layer: [Regional & oral epics](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md) · Sister bow-song: [Muttuppaṭṭaṉ Katai](muttuppattan-katai.md) · The Śaiva locale it touches: [Pañca-Sabhai — Tiruvalangadu](../06-sacred-geography/pancha-sabhai-nataraja-dance-halls.md).

The **Icakkiyammaṉ Katai** — better known across the Tamil country simply as the **Nīli Katai** ("the story of Nīli") — is the most celebrated revenge-goddess tale of the south: a wife **murdered for her gold** who is reborn a **pēy / yakṣi (Tamil *icakki* < Skt. *yakṣī*)**, hunts her killer across two births, takes the guise of his wife at the village of **Palaiyanur**, and destroys him after the **seventy Veḷāḷa elders of the village stand surety with their lives** — whereupon the seventy, to keep their word, **immolate themselves**. It survives in two intertwined lives: as the **charter-myth of the Icakki Ammaṉ cult** sung over the bow in the far south, and as a proverbial byword in general Tamil for treachery, false tears and a debt of honour paid in fire. This is the canonical deep treatment; the surrounding oral-epic stratum is mapped in [Regional & oral epics](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md).

## The goddess, the genre, and the name

- **Icakki = the deified Nīli** — in the Kanyakumari and southern Tirunelveli belt the murdered-and-reborn Nīli is worshipped as **Icakki Ammaṉ / Icakkiyammaṉ**, a fierce village goddess whose very name is the Tamilised **yakṣī** (Skt. *yakṣiṇī*), the tree-and-blood spirit-woman of pan-Indian demonology. Her temples (the *icakki kōyil*) dot the far-southern countryside; her scripture is the sung katai. (Perundevi Srinivasan, *Of Death and Birth: Icakkiyamman, a Tamil Goddess, in Ritual and Story*) [scholarly] [regional]
- **A bow-song (vil pāṭṭu)** — like its sister tale the [Muttuppaṭṭaṉ Katai](muttuppattan-katai.md), the Icakki/Nīli story is sung in the *vil pāṭṭu* idiom of the Tirunelveli–Kanyakumari–Travancore border: a great bell-strung **bow** rested on a pot and struck by the lead singer, backed by a troupe who answer his lines, performed at the deity's **koṭai** (offering-festival) to call the goddess down into **possession**. The song is not entertainment about the goddess — it is the liturgy that *produces* her. (Stuart Blackburn, *Singing of Birth and Death*) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **A "birth" and a "death" story fused** — in Blackburn's sorting of the repertoire, Icakki is unusual: she is at once a **death story** (a wronged human killed into divinity) and a **birth story** (a goddess divine and fierce from the start). The katai narrates *how a murdered woman becomes a yakṣi-goddess* — the death-and-deification engine of the southern folk pantheon in its darkest, female, avenging register. This **Nīli / Icakki** is unrelated to **Nīlakeśī** the Jain heroine or **Nīlā/Nappiṉṉai** of the Kṛṣṇa cycle; the shared syllable is coincidence. (Blackburn; Srinivasan) [scholarly]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural/cyclic clock — the karma of two births** — the katai carries **no purāṇic yuga-chronology**; its cosmic machinery is instead **rebirth and retribution (karma, saṃsāra)**: a crime committed in one birth ripens into vengeance in the next, the killer and the killed reborn together to close the account. The engine is the transmigratory frame mapped in [Puruṣārthas & karma-saṃsāra-mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md), applied to a village vendetta rather than to liberation. [folk]
- **Academic/historical clock [scholarly]** — the ballad is an **undatable oral composition** whose story-world evokes the late-medieval / early-modern far-southern countryside; some tellings self-date the first birth to the **"9th century"** [folk], but that is legendary interior time, not history. What is firmly datable is the **record**: Eveline Meyer's *Aṅkāḷaparamēcuvari* (1986), David Shulman's *Tamil Temple Myths* (1980), Blackburn's *Singing of Birth and Death* (1988), and Perundevi Srinivasan's *Of Death and Birth* monograph on Icakkiyammaṉ. Scholars trace the fierce demoness-goddess and pēy motifs to early Tamil literature, as far back as the post-Sangam / **Cilappatikāram** stratum, though a direct narration of *this* plot in that text is not attested. [scholarly] [disputed]

## The story — I. The first birth: the murder for gold

- **A greedy husband** — in a former birth a man weds a woman for the **gold and jewels** she carries, then **murders her for the treasure** and abandons the body. In the widely-sung Tamil telling he is a **merchant** who, married already at home, travels north (to **Kāśī / Vārāṇasī**), marries a Brahmin's daughter **Navaňjani (Navanjani)**, and on the road home **sends her brother off for water and kills her** in the interval for her ornaments. (Nīli Katai, vil pāṭṭu) [folk] [oral tradition]
  - *Variant (southern / folklore recension):* the wife is **Alli**, daughter of a temple dancer; the husband **Nambi**, a covetous **Śiva-temple priest**, who murders her for her jewels; her brother is **Ambi**. Name-sets differ across singers and regions; the deed — **uxoricide for gold** — is invariant. Catalog, do not merge. [folk] [regional]
- **The brother's despair** — the murdered woman's **brother**, finding her corpse, **takes his own life** in grief (hanging himself) — a second death bound into the same karmic knot, so that brother and sister will return together. (Nīli Katai) [folk] [oral tradition]

## II. Rebirth as the pēy / yakṣi

- **Reborn as uncanny children** — the murdered wife and her dead brother are **reborn as a sister and brother of monstrous, spirit-nature** — **Nīli and Nīlaṉ** — abnormal infants of supernatural power who **slaughter cattle in the night, leaving them blood-marked**. Terrified, their family (in some tellings a great king / the Cōḻa ruler) **abandons them**, tying them to a **karuvēl (acacia) tree** in the wilds. (Nīli Katai) [folk]
  - *Variant (birth-place):* the abandonment forest is named **Pañcavaṉkāḍu** ("five-forest") near **Nāgercoil** in the far south — the Icakki country. [regional]
- **The brother's second death** — village woodcutters who come to **fell the tree** kill **Nīlaṉ**, leaving Nīli alone and now doubly bereaved — the loss that fixes her wholly on vengeance. (Nīli Katai) [folk]
- **A yakṣi, not a widow** — from this point Nīli is a **pēy / icakki**: a shape-shifting blood-drinking spirit-woman who can wear any face. The tradition's dread of her is the dread of the **yakṣī** class generally — beautiful, tree-haunting, lethal to men (see [Yakṣas & Guhyakas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/yakshas-and-guhyakas.md)). [folk] [regional]

## III. Palaiyanur: the false wife and the phantom child

- **The killer reborn as a merchant** — the murderer of the first birth is himself reborn — in the Tamil telling as the merchant **Darśana Ceṭṭi (Darsana Chetty)**; in the southern recension as **Āṉantaṉ (Anandan)**, a trader of **Kāverippūmpaṭṭiṉam** — and he travels **guarded by a protective talisman / magic sword (or wand)** that keeps the pēy at bay. (Nīli Katai) [folk] [oral tradition]
- **The village of Palaiyanur** — Nīli tracks him to the village of **Palaiyanur** (Paḻaiyanūr, in the old Toṇḍai country, the twin-settlement of the great Śaiva shrine of **Tiruvalangadu** — the *Ratna Sabhā* of Śiva's *Ūrdhva Tāṇḍava*, sung in the Tēvāram and home of [Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/karaikkal-ammaiyar.md)). It is here that the seventy-Veḷāḷa memory attaches to the sacred landscape. (Nīli Katai; Tiruvalangadu tradition) [regional] [disputed]
- **The weeping woman with a child** — Nīli **takes the exact guise of the man's wife**, appears before the villagers **weeping, carrying an infant (a phantom child)**, and publicly claims the merchant as her **husband who has deserted her and their baby**. Her tears and grief are so convincing that the village turns against the man. (Nīli Katai) [folk]
- **The proverb of false tears** — this scene gives Tamil the byword **"நீலிக் கண்ணீர் / Nīli-k kaṇṇīr" ("Nīli's tears")** — deceiving weeping, the exact sense of English "crocodile tears" — and **"Paḻaiyanūr Nīli"** as a name for a treacherous, vengeful woman. (Tamil proverb tradition) [folk] [regional]

## IV. The seventy stand surety

- **The village council of seventy** — Palaiyanur's affair is judged by its **assembly of seventy Veḷāḷa (Vellala) elders** — the landholding-cultivator caste whose elders hold the moral authority of the village. Moved by Nīli's grief and persuaded of her tale, they **rule that the man must go with the "wife" who claims him**. (Nīli Katai) [folk] [regional]
- **Disarming the victim** — the elders **talk the merchant out of his protective talisman / sword**, assuring him it is needless; his one defence against the pēy is set aside on the strength of the assembly's word. (Nīli Katai) [folk]
- **The oath of surety** — knowing the man's terror, the seventy **pledge their own lives as surety (pori)**: they swear that **if any harm comes to him, all seventy will burn themselves** — the vow that makes the katai a parable of the **absolute Tamil ethic of the given word (*vākku / āṇai*)**. He is handed to Nīli for the night. (Nīli Katai) [folk] [oral tradition]

## V. The killing and the immolation of the seventy

- **The vengeance consummated** — that night Nīli **reveals her true pēy form and kills the merchant** — draining his blood / devouring him — the debt of the first-birth murder paid in full, killer and killed reunited across two births only for the account to close in death. (Nīli Katai) [folk]
- **The seventy keep their word** — at daybreak the truth is out; and the **seventy Veḷāḷa elders, true to their oath, build a great fire and immolate themselves** — the self-sacrifice that made "the seventy of Palaiyanur" proverbial in Tamil for a promise honoured unto death. (Nīli Katai; Aṅkāḷaparamēcuvari tradition) [folk] [regional]
  - *Variant (the escaped elder):* in one Tamil telling **sixty-nine burn** and **one flees**; Nīli hunts him down as the **seventy-first** victim (coming to him disguised as his own daughter), and with the phantom child she herself destroys, the toll reaches **seventy-two** — the source of the tradition's "**seventy-two killed**" tally. Other tellings keep it a clean **seventy who burn**. Catalog, do not merge. [folk] [disputed]
- **From vengeance to worship** — the pēy who has drunk her killer's blood is now **placated and installed as a goddess**: the very ferocity that destroyed the merchant and the seventy is enshrined and fed with offering, so that she guards rather than ravages. This is the deification that makes **Nīli into Icakki Ammaṉ**. (Nīli Katai; Srinivasan) [folk] [regional]

## Recensions, regional lives, and kindred cults

- **The Aṅkāḷammaṉ frame** — in the northern-Tamil (Toṇḍai/Kāñci) country the Nīli story is a **central myth of the fierce goddess Aṅkāḷaparamēcuvari (Aṅkāḷammaṉ)**: Eveline Meyer's monograph shows the pēy-Nīli of Palaiyanur absorbed into that goddess's cult and drama, sung and enacted at her festivals. (Meyer, *Aṅkāḷaparamēcuvari*, 1986) [scholarly] [regional]
- **The Icakki Ammaṉ frame** — in the far south (Kanyakumari, southern Tirunelveli) the same murdered-wife-turned-yakṣi is the temple goddess **Icakkiyammaṉ**, her katai the bow-song charter of her worship, studied in full by Perundevi Srinivasan. Here she stands beside the belt's other bow-song deities — [Sudalaimāḍaṉ](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sudalaimadan.md), the [kāval cluster](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/karuppasamy-madurai-veeran-and-tamil-kaval-deities.md), and the deified brahmin-cobbler of the [Muttuppaṭṭaṉ Katai](muttuppattan-katai.md). (Srinivasan; Blackburn) [scholarly] [regional]
- **The Kerala cousin — Kalliyaṅkāṭṭu Nīli** — the Malayalam tradition carries a closely-related **Yakṣi Nīli of Kalliyaṅkāḍu / Pañcavaṉkāḍu**, the fierce spirit finally subdued by the tantric Nampūtiri sorcerer **Kaṭamaṟṟattu Kattanār** — the same wronged-woman-into-yakṣi engine given a Kerala exorcism-ending. A distinct regional recension, not to be merged with the Tamil temple-founding one. (Kalliyaṅkāṭṭu Nīli tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The naming variants at a glance** — first-birth wife **Navaňjani** ~ **Alli**; killer reborn **Darśana Ceṭṭi** ~ **Āṉantaṉ of Kāverippūmpaṭṭiṉam**; the surety-council the **seventy Veḷāḷar** ~ the seventy **ūrāṇmai** (village headmen) — all attested tellings of one plot. [folk] [disputed]

## Why the katai matters

- **A charter of the given word** — the seventy who burn rather than break an oath they could not keep make the katai the great Tamil parable of **surety and honour**: the assembly's word is worth their lives, and the tale is invoked wherever fidelity-unto-death is praised or false grief unmasked. [scholarly] [regional]
- **The avenging woman deified** — like Kaṇṇaki in the [Cilappatikāram](silappatikaram-kannagi-pattini.md), Nīli is a **wronged woman whose rage becomes godhood** — but where Kaṇṇaki burns a city for a judicial murder and is enshrined as the chaste **Pattiṉi**, Nīli murders across rebirths and is enshrined as a blood-taking **yakṣi**: the two are the classical-epic and village-demonic poles of the same deep Tamil pattern, *woman-wronged → woman-divinised*. [scholarly]
- **Karma as vendetta** — the katai turns the doctrine of rebirth into a **revenge mechanism**: the first-birth murder is not merely "repaid" by cosmic law but hunted down and consummated by the victim herself across the gap of death — folk theology reading [karma and saṃsāra](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md) as personal, remembered, and lethal. Icakki/Nīli belongs to the non-brahmanical **grāmadevatā** layer — the yakṣī who must be fed with blood offering to be kept from killing. [scholarly] [folk]
