# Samudra Manthana — The Churning of the Ocean

> Cosmic address: in the *Bhagavata Purana* frame (Canto 8, from 8.5) this episode unfolds in the prior **[Chakshusha Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-06-chakshusha.md)** (6th) — the reign of **Chakshusha Manu**, with Indra named **Mantradruma** and Vishnu appearing as the avatara **Ajita** to bear Mandara as Kurma (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.5.7–10; cf. [manvantara-06-chakshusha](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-06-chakshusha.md)); some accounts instead frame it in primordial time (the *Ramayana* sets it in the Krita age) or the present **[Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)** (7th). [disputed: manvantara placement is text-dependent.] Narrated across the Itihasa and Puranas. It is the origin-event of **amrita** (immortality nectar), of **Lakshmi**'s emergence, of **Kurma** (the [Dashavatara](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) tortoise), and of the eternal **[deva–asura](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md)** rivalry.
> Parent: [Deep Dives](README.md)

The **Samudra Manthana** ("churning of the ocean") — also *Kshira-Sagara Manthana* (churning of the milk-ocean), *Amrita Manthana* — is one of the most widely depicted episodes in Hindu tradition. The Devas and Asuras jointly churn the cosmic ocean to extract **amrita**, using **Mount Mandara** as the churning-rod and the serpent **Vasuki** as the rope, with **Vishnu** as **Kurma** (tortoise) supporting the mountain. Fourteen (in the most common count) **ratnas** ("jewels/treasures") emerge, culminating in the nectar — which the Devas secure through Vishnu's **Mohini** ruse. The episode appears in the *Mahabharata* (Adi Parva 1.15–17 in the critical edition) and the *Ramayana* (Bala Kanda 1.44 critical edition / 1.45 Southern text) — the two epic versions, generally regarded as the oldest extant [scholarly] — and in many Puranas (Bhagavata, Vishnu, Padma, Matsya, Agni, Brahmanda, Vayu, Kurma, Linga, Skanda, Brahma-Vaivarta, Shiva — though per Wilson the *Shiva*, *Linga* and *Kurma* only allude to it cursorily, and several Puranas omit it [scholarly]), with significant divergence in cause, sequence, and list of treasures.

## I. The Cause — Why the Ocean Was Churned

- **Durvasa's curse on Indra** - The irascible sage Durvasa gifts Indra a divine garland (the abode of Sri/Lakshmi); Indra hangs it on the brow of his elephant **Airavata**, who, drawn by its scent, takes it in his trunk and flings it to the ground. Insulted, Durvasa curses Indra that his sovereignty over the three worlds will be overthrown, and the three worlds become *nihshrika* — bereft of *sri* (fortune, vigor, splendor). (*Vishnu Purana* 1.9.2–26) The *Bhagavata Purana* only alludes to the curse in passing — "the demigods had been cursed by Durvasa Muni, the three worlds were poverty-stricken" — without the garland story. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.5.15–16)
  - *Variant (Vishnu Purana 1.9.2–5):* the garland is of **santanaka** flowers, obtained by Durvasa from the hand of a **Vidyadhari**; the elephant casts it down and the curse follows — this is the locus classicus for the Durvasa frame. (Wilson's note: per the *Vayu Purana* the garland had been given to the nymph by Devi.)
  - *Note:* the Durvasa frame is absent from both epics, the *Matsya Purana* and the *Harivamsha*; Wilson judged it a later embellishment, with the *Agni* and *Bhagavata* only alluding to it. (Wilson, *Vishnu Purana*, note to 1.9) [scholarly]
- **Lakshmi withdraws into the ocean** - Stripped of fortune, the three worlds wither: plants die, sacrifices cease (*Vishnu Purana* 1.9.26–31). Lakshmi (Sri) is later re-born from the **Kshira-Sagara** (ocean of milk) (*Vishnu Purana* 1.9.100; *Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.8). Neither text narrates her actually departing into the ocean; the "withdrawal" is the bridge that later retellings draw between the curse and her rebirth. [popular tradition]
- **Asuras ascend; Devas defeated** - The Asuras, with serpent-weapons, strike down the weakened Devas, many of whom cannot be revived; the Asura king at the time of the churning is **Bali**, son of Virocana, with commanders such as Shambara and Arishtanemi. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.5.15–16, 8.6.28–31)
  - *Variant (Vishnu Purana 1.9.32–34, 1.9.107):* the Daityas and Danavas overcome the gods; at the churning their leader is named as **Viprachitti**, not Bali.
- **Appeal to Vishnu / Brahma** - The Devas go to Brahma on Mount Sumeru; Brahma leads them to Vishnu at Shvetadvipa in the Kshira-Sagara and praises him; Vishnu counsels a **temporary truce** with the Asuras to churn the ocean for amrita — "the demons will be engaged in labor, but you will gain the nectar" — warning of the *kalakuta* poison to come. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.5.17–8.6.25; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.34–82)
  - *Variant (Mahabharata, Adi Parva 1.15.5–13):* the framing is set on Mount Meru in primordial time: the gods, gathered there and eager for amrita, take counsel, and **Narayana tells Brahma** that the gods and Asuras should churn the ocean — the Durvasa curse is **absent**; the motive is simply the desire for amrita.
  - *Variant (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda 1.44.14–16 CE / 1.45.15–17 Southern):* in the **Krita age** the sons of Diti and the sons of Aditi together wonder how to become free of old age, death and illness, and resolve **themselves** to churn the milk-ocean for its essence — no curse, no divine counsel.
  - *Variant (Skanda Purana, Mahesvara-khanda, Kedara-khanda 9.3–63):* the fall begins not with Durvasa but with **Indra slighting his preceptor Brihaspati**, who vanishes (9.3–10). **Bali** comes up from Patala, conquers Amaravati by Shukra's grace, and carries off Indra's treasures — Airavata, Uchchaihshravas and many jewels — but **the best of them fall into the ocean** on the way (9.16–21); Shukra tells the puzzled Bali that heaven's pleasures belong only to the performer of a hundred horse-sacrifices (9.22–23). Brahma leads the gods to Vishnu, who tells Indra to ally with the Daityas (9.25–35); Indra lives with Bali in **Sutala**, then proposes that they churn the ocean together to **recover the fallen jewels** (9.55–59), and an ethereal voice prescribes Mandara and Vasuki (9.61–63). The translator notes that this background differs from the epic and other Puranic accounts. [late text]

## II. The Apparatus — Mountain, Serpent, Tortoise

- **Mount Mandara as churning-rod** - The Devas and Asuras uproot **Mandara** (a spur of/near Mount Meru, the world-axis) to serve as the *manthana-danda* (churning staff), carry it toward the ocean, tire, and drop it, crushing many of their own number; Vishnu then arrives on **Garuda**, revives the crushed, lifts the mountain with one hand onto Garuda's back, and Garuda carries it to the ocean and is sent away. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.6.33–39; so too *Skanda Purana*, Kedara-khanda 9.77–84, where Garuda sets it on the northern shore of the milk-ocean)
  - *Variant (Mahabharata, Adi Parva 1.16.4–7):* the gods **cannot** uproot Mandara; at the bidding of Vishnu and Brahma the serpent **Ananta** tears it up, with its forests and their creatures. The Ocean consents to the churning on condition of a share of the nectar (1.16.8–9).
- **Vasuki as the churning-rope** - The naga king **Vasuki** is summoned, promised a share of the nectar, and coiled around Mandara as the churning-cord. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.7.1; *Mahabharata*, Adi Parva 1.16.12; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.84)
  - **Side-allocation dispute** - Vishnu (as Ajita), followed by the Devas, first grasps Vasuki's head; the Asuras object that the tail is the inauspicious end; smiling, Vishnu gives up the head, so the **Asuras hold the head, Devas the tail** — exposing the Asuras to Vasuki's fiery breath. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.7.2–4)
  - *Variant (Mahabharata, Adi Parva 1.16.13–14; Vishnu Purana 1.9.85):* no dispute — the Asuras simply take the head end and the gods the tail (in the *Vishnu Purana* Krishna stations them so); in the *Mahabharata* Ananta, on the gods' side, repeatedly lifts and lowers the serpent's head.
- **The mountain sinks — Kurma avatara** - Lacking a base, Mandara plunges into the ocean. **Vishnu incarnates as Kurma (the tortoise)** and dives beneath, bearing the mountain on his back/shell as the pivot. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.7.6–10; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.88; *Agni*, *Kurma*, *Padma* Puranas) — see [Dashavatara](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md).
  - *Variant (Mahabharata, Adi Parva 1.16.10–11):* the gods and Asuras ask the **tortoise-king Akupara** (*kurmaraja*) to be the mountain's base; he agrees, and **Indra** presses the summit down with an instrument. The tortoise is **not identified with Vishnu** in this passage.
  - *Variant (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda 1.45.27–31 Southern):* Mandara sinks into Patala; at the gods' prayer Vishnu becomes a tortoise and lies beneath it, while also holding the summit with his hand. [interpolation: this passage, like the poison episode, is absent from the Baroda critical edition's 1.44, which goes straight from the churning to the emergents]
  - *Variant:* Vishnu is also said to enter the Asuras (as *rajas*), the Devas (as *sattva*) and Vasuki (as *tamas*) as strength, and to press down on the mountain's summit from above with a thousand-armed form while supporting it as Kurma below (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.7.11–13; similarly *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.89–91) — multiple simultaneous Vishnu-forms.
- **Vasuki's fiery breath** - The strain makes Vasuki exhale smoke and blazing fire **from his thousand mouths**, scorching the Asuras at his head-end (Pauloma, Kaleya, Bali, Ilvala); clouds and sea-spray relieve the Devas. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.7.14–15; *Mahabharata*, Adi Parva 1.16.15; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.86–87) In the *Bhagavata* the **halahala itself rises from the churned ocean**, not from Vasuki (8.7.18). [iconographic cross-reference: the Angkor Wat churning relief matches the text — the crested-helmet Asura line holds Vasuki's head-end, the crowned Deva line the tail.]
  - *Variant (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda 1.45.19 Southern):* after a thousand years Vasuki, unable to bear the friction, **bites the rocks of Mandara and vomits venom**, which becomes the *halahala*. [interpolation: absent from the Baroda critical edition]

## III. The Fourteen Ratnas — Treasures of the Churning

The most-cited enumeration is **14 ratnas**; scriptural lists actually range widely and differ by text. Order of emergence below follows the *Bhagavata Purana* (8.7.18–8.8.35), with variants noted. In the *Bhagavata* the poison comes first and the nectar last; other texts differ (see the note after the list).

- **1. Halahala / Kalakuta (the world-poison)** - A cataclysmic poison surfaces from the churned ocean, threatening all creation. The Devas and Vishnu go to **Shiva** on Kailasa; Shiva tells his consort Bhavani he will drink it for the good of all beings; **Bhavani, knowing his power, gives her permission**; Shiva takes the whole poison in his palm and drinks it, and it leaves a blue line on his neck — hence **Nilakantha** ("blue-throated"). (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.7.18–45) The widely-depicted image of Parvati **physically clasping his throat** to halt the poison is not in the Bhagavata passage; it is commonly attributed to the *Shiva Purana* (*Rudra Samhita, Parvati-khanda*), but that locus was not verified in the latest check. [popular tradition]
  - *Variant (Bhagavata Purana 8.7.46):* scorpions, cobras, poisonous plants and other venomous creatures drink the drops that fall from Shiva's hand while he drinks — explaining their venom.
  - *Variant (Vishnu Purana 1.9.97):* Shiva does **not** drink the poison — "poison was engendered from the sea, of which the snake gods (**Nagas**) took possession"; Mahadeva takes only the **moon**. (Wilson notes that the *Harivamsha* instead explains Shiva's dark neck by Vishnu throttling him in the Daksha-yajna fight: note to *Vishnu Purana* 1.8.)
  - *Variant (Mahabharata, Adi Parva — vulgate, Ganguli §18):* the poison Kalakuta appears **last**, after Airavata, from over-churning; **Shiva (Rudra)** swallows it at **Brahma's request** and holds it in his throat — emphasis on Brahma's request rather than Parvati. [interpolation: in the critical edition this occurs only in rejected passages (1.16 apparatus *0274; Appendix I, nos. 9–10), not in the constituted text, which has no poison at all]
  - *Variant (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda 1.45.20–26 Southern):* the gods seek refuge with Rudra; **Vishnu** appears and tells Shiva that, as foremost of the gods, the first product of the churning is his; Shiva swallows the halahala "as if it were ambrosia." [interpolation: absent from the Baroda critical edition; the commentator Kataka already called the passage an insertion from a Purana]
- **2. Kamadhenu / Surabhi** - The wish-fulfilling cow, source of milk, curds and ghee for oblations; taken by the rishis for ritual (ghee/yajna). (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.1–2; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.92, where she is the **first** emergent)
- **3. Uchchaihshravas** - The white celestial horse (often seven-headed in later art and in the 14-ratna verse); in the *Bhagavata* **Bali** desires it and Indra, as Vishnu had advised, does not object. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.3) In the *Mahabharata* the white horse goes, with Sri, Sura and the Moon, to the side of the gods. (*Mahabharata*, Adi Parva 1.15.1–2, 1.16.34–36; *Ramayana* 1.44.24 CE)
- **4. Airavata** - The four-tusked white elephant, followed by eight elephants of the quarters headed by Airavana and eight she-elephants headed by Abhramu. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.4–5) Indra takes him as his mount in the *Mahabharata* vulgate (Ganguli §18; in the critical edition only in rejected passage *0274).
- **5. Kaustubha** - The supreme jewel (with the Padmaraga gem in the *Bhagavata*); worn by **Vishnu** on his chest. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.6; *Mahabharata*, Adi Parva 1.16.35; *Ramayana* 1.44.24 CE)
- **6. Parijata / Kalpavriksha** - The celestial wish-granting tree (Parijata flower-tree, never-fading blossoms); placed in Indra's heaven. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.6; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.95; many lists give **Kalpavriksha/Kalpataru**)
- **7. Apsaras (Rambha et al.)** - Troupes of celestial nymphs/dancers; take residence in Svarga. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.7; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.96)
  - *Variant (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda 1.44.18–20 CE):* sixty crores of Apsaras rise "from the waters" (*apsu*, hence their name), with countless attendants; since neither Devas nor Asuras take them as wives, they are reckoned "common to all."
- **8. Lakshmi (Sri)** - The goddess of fortune re-emerges radiant from the ocean and, of her own choice, garlands **Vishnu** and takes her place on his chest — restoring sri to the cosmos; the Asuras, neglected by her, are dejected. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.8–29; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.100–107, where she rises **last**, after Dhanvantari) — see [Trimurti and Tridevi](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md).
- **9. Varuni / Sura (Madira)** - The lotus-eyed goddess of wine/liquor; **taken by the Asuras, headed by Bali, with Vishnu's permission**. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.30; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.94 records her emergence without a taker)
  - *Variant (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda 1.44.21–23 CE / 1.45.36–38 Southern):* Varuni, daughter of Varuna, rises seeking a husband; the sons of Diti **refuse** her and the sons of Aditi **accept** her — "hence the Daityas are *a-suras* and the sons of Aditi are *suras*." This is the source of the traditional sura/a-sura etymology. [scholarly: modern historical linguistics derives "asura" from Proto-Indo-Iranian *Hásura* ("lord"), cognate with Avestan *ahura* — unrelated to *sura* ("wine"); the sura/a-sura wordplay is an epic folk-etymology, not the historical derivation.]
  - *Variant (Mahabharata, Adi Parva 1.16.34–36):* Sura goes, with Sri, the Moon and the white horse, to the side of the gods.
- **10. Chandra (the Moon)** - The cool-rayed moon arises and **Mahadeva (Shiva)** takes it upon his head (*Chandrashekhara*). (*Vishnu Purana* 1.9.97; many Puranic lists) In the *Mahabharata* the moon is the **first** emergent (Adi Parva 1.16.33). The *Bhagavata*'s list (8.8) does **not** include the moon.
- **11–13. Additional emergents (vary by text):** various lists include —
  - **Panchajanya** - Vishnu's conch (the *shankha* of the popular 14-ratna verse). (later lists) The *Vishnu Purana* gives the conch a different origin: Krishna takes it from the bones of the sea-demon **Panchajana** (*Vishnu Purana* 5.21.27–30).
  - **Sharanga** - Vishnu's bow (the *haridhanuh* of the popular 14-ratna verse). (later lists)
  - **Jyeshtha / Alakshmi (Nirriti)** - Goddess of misfortune/poverty, who emerges **before** Lakshmi and is dispatched to inauspicious places. (*Padma*/*Linga* traditions; also in a rejected *Mahabharata* passage, critical apparatus 1.16 *0268, where Jyeshtha rises right after the poison) Wilson gives the *Padma Purana*'s Uttara-khanda list as: poison, Jyeshtha/Alakshmi ("the elder born to fortune"), the goddess of wine, Nidra, the Apsarases, Indra's elephant, Lakshmi, the Moon and the Tulasi — Jyeshtha ahead of Lakshmi. (Wilson, *Vishnu Purana*, note to 1.9; locus not verified in the *Padma* text itself)
  - **Varuna's umbrella** and **Indra's earrings (kundala)**, which Indra gives to Aditi — added by the *Matsya Purana*, which may also count a second, white horse of the Sun. **Nidra** (sloth/sleep) — *Padma Purana*, Uttara-khanda list. The **Tulasi** plant — one of the twelve products in the *Vayu Purana* and also in the *Padma* Uttara-khanda list; Wilson calls the Tulasi, like Vishnu's bow and conch, a sectarian addition. (Wilson, *Vishnu Purana*, note to 1.9; loci not verified in the Puranic texts themselves)
- **14. Dhanvantari with the Amrita** - The divine physician **Dhanvantari**, dark, young and yellow-robed, rises bearing the *kalasha* (pot) of **amrita** — the goal of the entire enterprise. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.31–34; *Mahabharata*, Adi Parva 1.16.37; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.98) The *Bhagavata* calls him a portion of a portion of Vishnu, versed in medicine — hence the patron of Ayurveda.
  - *Variant (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda 1.44.18 CE):* Dhanvantari is the **first** to rise, followed by the Apsaras; the amrita itself comes last (1.44.24).

> **Variant on the count and order:** The *Vishnu Purana* (1.9.92–100) lists eight in this order — **Surabhi, Varuni, the Parijata, the Apsarases, the Moon (taken by Mahadeva), the poison (taken by the Nagas), Dhanvantari with the amrita, and Sri** — with no Kaustubha, Airavata or Uchchaihshravas. The *Mahabharata*'s critical text (Adi Parva 1.16.33–37) gives **the Moon, Sri, Sura, the white horse, the Kaustubha, and Dhanvantari with amrita** (Uchchaihshravas is also named at 1.15.1–2); Airavata, the poison, Surabhi, the Parijata and the Apsaras appear only in manuscript additions. The *Ramayana*'s critical text (1.44.18–24) gives **Dhanvantari, the Apsaras, Varuni, Uchchaihshravas, the Kaustubha and the amrita** — no Lakshmi, no Moon, no poison. The "14 ratnas" as a fixed didactic list comes from a popular benedictory verse sung at weddings (*lakshmih kaustubha-parijataka-sura dhanvantari-candramah ... ratnaniha caturdasha*), whose source is unknown; it adds Vishnu's bow and conch, which no epic or Puranic account studied lists. (V. M. Bedekar, "The Legend of the Churning of the Ocean in the Epics and the Puranas: A Comparative Study," *Purana* IX.1, 1967) [scholarly]

## IV. The Theft of the Nectar — Mohini

- **Asuras seize the amrita** - The moment Dhanvantari appears with the pot, the **Asuras snatch it**, then quarrel over who should drink first. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.35–40; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.108) In the *Mahabharata*'s critical text the Danavas only raise a great clamour of "this is mine" (*mamedam iti*, Adi Parva 1.16.38); that they hold the pot is implied when they hand it to Mohini (1.16.40) and when Vishnu takes it from the Danava chiefs (1.17.2). The explicit seizure — the Daityas, having beaten the gods, take the divine amrita (*jagṛhur amṛtaṃ divyam*) — is in a rejected manuscript addition (critical apparatus 1.16 *0275).
- **Vishnu becomes Mohini** - Vishnu assumes the form of **Mohini**, an irresistibly beautiful enchantress. Bewitched, the Asuras hand her the pot and agree to let her distribute it fairly. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.8.41–8.9.18; *Mahabharata*, Adi Parva 1.16.39–40, "Narayana, resorting to the enchanting illusion" — *mayam asthito mohinim*; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.109, "assuming a female form")
  - *Variant (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda 1.45.42 Southern):* the Southern text compresses the entire ruse into **one verse** — Vishnu "swiftly impounded the amrita, assuming the illusory power of Mohini" (*mohinim mayam asthaya*). The verse is **absent from the Baroda critical edition** (1.44.25–27 passes straight to the war over the nectar), so within the *Ramayana* the Mohini form is a Southern-recension reading; the **form** is nonetheless epic, since the *Mahabharata*'s critical text has it (1.16.39). The elaborated episode — the two rows, the flirtatious delay, the renewed war — is Puranic. Full treatment at [Mohini](mohini-avatara.md).
- **The nectar served to the Devas** - Mohini seats Devas and Asuras in separate rows and serves the amrita **only to the Devas**, distracting/deceiving the Asuras. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.9.19–23)
- **Rahu's deception and beheading** - The Asura **Svarbhanu (Rahu)**, disguised as a Deva, sits in the Devas' assembly and drinks. **Surya (Sun)** and **Chandra (Moon)** expose him; Vishnu cuts off his head with his razor-edged discus (*Sudarshana*). The amrita has reached only his throat: the **head becomes immortal**, while the **body, untouched by the nectar, dies**. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.9.24–25; the beheading also *Mahabharata*, Adi Parva 1.17.4–7)
  - **Rahu and Ketu** - **Brahma makes Rahu's head a graha** (planet); from enmity Rahu chases the Sun and Moon at the full and new moons — the eclipses. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.9.26; *Mahabharata*, Adi Parva 1.17.8) The pairing of the head as **Rahu** with the body as **Ketu**, making the two shadow-planets (*Navagraha*), is a later Puranic and *jyotisha* development, not in these passages. [late text] The *Vishnu Purana*'s churning account (1.9) has no Rahu episode at all.
  - *Variant (Mahabharata, Adi Parva 1.17.7):* in the critical text the mountain-peak-like **head falls and shakes the earth**; the vulgate (Ganguli §19, from manuscript additions *0281–0282) has the head **rise into the sky roaring** while the headless trunk falls and rolls on the ground, shaking the earth with its mountains and islands.
- **Mohini and Shiva [later texts]** - In a sequel, Shiva asks to see Mohini, is enchanted and pursues her; his seed falls to earth and becomes mines of gold and silver; Vishnu then resumes his own form and praises Shiva's self-mastery. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.12.1–41) The birth of a son from their union — **Ayyappa/Hariharaputra (Shasta)** — is **not** in the Bhagavata; it belongs to South Indian/Shaiva tradition. [regional] [variant — separate episode appended to the Manthana cycle]

## V. Aftermath

- **Renewed deva–asura war** - Nectar-strengthened, the Devas (with Vishnu) defeat the Asuras in the **Amrita-manthana war**; Indra is restored to Svarga and Lakshmi's fortune returns. (*Bhagavata Purana* 8.10–8.11; *Mahabharata*, Adi Parva 1.17.9–30; *Vishnu Purana* 1.9.110–115)
  - *Variant (Mahabharata, Adi Parva 1.17):* **Nara and Narayana** fight on the gods' side, Narayana's Sudarshana discus destroys the Danavas by thousands, the defeated Asuras flee into the earth and the salt sea, and the gods return Mandara to its place and Indra with the immortals gives the store of amrita to the diadem-wearer (*kiritin*) to guard (1.17.30; Ganguli renders this as Narayana).
- **Spillage and the Kumbh Mela tradition** - In the popular (Puranic/folk) tradition, drops of amrita fall at four earthly sites during the contest over the *kumbha* (pot) — **Prayag, Haridwar, Ujjain, Nashik** — origin of the **Kumbh Mela**. (Puranic/folk tradition; see [Sacred Geography](../06-sacred-geography/README.md)) [the four-site etiology is largely medieval/folk, not in the early epic]
- **Cosmic significance** - Establishes amrita and the immortality of the Devas, the marriage of Vishnu–Lakshmi, Shiva as Nilakantha, the lineage of celestial treasures (Airavata, Uchchaihshravas, Kamadhenu, Kalpavriksha), and the Navagraha shadow-planets Rahu/Ketu.

## Sources

**Primary (scriptural):**
- *Bhagavata Purana*, Canto 8 (Skandha VIII), chs. 5–12 — fullest canonical narrative (Durvasa allusion 8.5.15–16, Garuda and Mandara 8.6, Kurma and the poison 8.7, ratnas 8.8, Mohini and Rahu 8.9, war 8.10–11, Shiva and Mohini 8.12). Verse numbers follow the BBT edition (vedabase.io).
- *Mahabharata*, Adi Parva, Astika Parva — critical edition (Pune) **1.15–17** (GRETIL e-text, with apparatus), = Ganguli trans. §§17–19 (vulgate) — the epic version (no Durvasa frame; tortoise-king Akupara; poison and Airavata only in manuscript additions).
- *Vishnu Purana*, Book 1, ch. 9 (Wilson trans., 1840; Sanskrit verse numbers per GRETIL) — Durvasa curse (1.9.2–26), the churning (1.9.82–111), ratna list (1.9.92–100), the Nagas taking the poison (1.9.97); Book 5, ch. 21 (Panchajanya).
- *Valmiki Ramayana*, **Bala Kanda** — Baroda critical edition **1.44.14–27** (GRETIL) and Southern text **1.45** (valmikiramayan.net, verse-by-verse) — Vishvamitra's churning-account; the halahala, Kurma and Mohini verses (Southern 1.45.19–31, 1.45.42) are absent from the critical text.
- *Skanda Purana*, Mahesvara-khanda, Kedara-khanda ch. 9 (Tagare's English trans., on wisdomlib.org) — the Brihaspati-insult frame and the churning to recover Indra's sunken treasures; Garuda carrying Mandara.
- *Padma, Matsya, Agni, Brahmanda, Vayu, Kurma, Linga, Brahma-Vaivarta, Shiva* Puranas — additional variants (the *Matsya*, *Vayu* and *Padma* emergents above are cited through Wilson's note to *Vishnu Purana* 1.9).

**Secondary (scholarly):**
- V. M. Bedekar (BORI, Poona), "The Legend of the Churning of the Ocean in the Epics and the Puranas: A Comparative Study," *Purana* IX.1 (Jan. 1967), pp. 7–61 — comparative tables of entities and sequence; the 14-ratna wedding verse (via wisdomlib.org).
