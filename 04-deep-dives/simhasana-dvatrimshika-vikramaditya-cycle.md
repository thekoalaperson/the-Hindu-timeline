# Siṃhāsana Dvātriṃśikā — the 32 tales of Vikramāditya's throne

> Deep-dive: the **"Thirty-two Tales of the Lion-Throne"** (also **Vikramacarita**, "the Adventures of Vikrama"; vernacular **Siṅhāsan Battīsī** / **Singhasan Battisi**) — the framed tale-cycle in which **King Bhoja of Dhārā** unearths **Vikramāditya's buried siṃhāsana**, borne on **32 apsara statuettes** who, one by one, tell a story of Vikrama's generosity and forbid Bhoja to sit until he can match it. *Two clocks:* the inner golden age runs on the **Kali-Yuga legend clock** of Vikramāditya (Vikrama Saṃvat, epoch **57 BCE** — see [calendar](../00-time-and-cosmology/hindu-calendar-and-panchanga.md)); the frame is set in the **documented 11th-c. reign of the Paramāra Bhoja**, and every *extant* Sanskrit recension is 13th–14th-c. Kali-Yuga history **[scholarly]**. Sibling cycle: [Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati](vetala-panchavimshati-vikram-and-betal.md); canonical parent stream and summary: [Bṛhatkathā / Kathāsaritsāgara & the Vikramāditya cycle](../90-literature-corpus/brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md); civic home of the legend: [Ujjain / Avantikā §IX](../06-sacred-geography/ujjain-avantika.md). Up: [deep-dives index](README.md).

## The text, its recensions, and its clock

- **What it is** — a *frame-plus-catalog*: a reigning king (Bhoja) is repeatedly checked, on the very step of a throne, by talking statuettes who each narrate a self-contained *dāna*- or *sāhasa*-tale of the throne's dead owner. Unlike the [Vetāla](vetala-panchavimshati-vikram-and-betal.md) riddles, these tales end not in a dilemma-question but in a **challenge to worthiness**: *"Ascend only if you possess this virtue."* (Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā, frame + I–XXXII) **[late text]**
- **Edgerton's four recensions** — the Sanskrit survives in **four widely divergent recensions** critically edited and translated by **Franklin Edgerton** as *Vikrama's Adventures, or the Thirty-Two Tales of the Throne* (Harvard Oriental Series vols. **26–27**, 1926): the **Southern (SR)**, **Metrical (MR)**, **Brief (BR)**, and **Jainistic (JR)** recensions. Edgerton reconstructed a lost archetype he dated **before c. 1305 CE**; the recensions share the frame and a core of tales but **reorder, substitute, and re-number the thirty-two freely**. **[scholarly]**
- **Authorship & dating (two clocks)** — traditionally the composition is placed in Bhoja's own court or ascribed to court poets, but critically the cycle is a **13th–14th-c. CE** anthology that grew around the by-then-legendary Bhoja **[scholarly]**. The *frame's* king, **Bhoja Paramāra of Dhārā (Mālwā)**, is fully historical (**r. c. 1010–1055 CE**), the polymath-patron of the *Sarasvatīkaṇṭhābharaṇa*, *Samarāṅgaṇa-sūtradhāra*, etc. The *throne's* king, **Vikramāditya**, sits on the legend clock two centuries deep in the Kali Yuga, his era counted from **57 BCE**. The text yokes the two: the historical Bhoja is judged, and found wanting, against the legendary Vikrama. **[disputed] [scholarly]**
- **The vernacular Siṅhāsan Battīsī** — the cycle passed into nearly every North Indian language; the **Braj / Hindi Siṅhāsan Battīsī** (and its Persian and Urdu cousins) is the popular vehicle, in which the statuettes are **named** and the tales are retold with folk embellishment. In these versions the throne-king is squarely **Vikramāditya of Ujjayinī**. **[folk] [regional]**

## The frame — the speaking mound and the buried throne

- **The field that made men just** — near **Ujjayinī (Dhārā's domain)** a Brahmin's field holds a raised mound; whoever stands upon it — in the common telling a **cowherd boy** watching the crop — begins to **speak and judge with royal impartiality and largesse**, settling disputes like a *cakravartin*, and reverts to an ordinary man the moment he steps down. The marvel is reported to King **Bhoja**. (Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā, frame) **[late text]**
- **Bhoja excavates the siṃhāsana** — Bhoja has the mound dug up and finds, buried beneath it, a resplendent **lion-throne (siṃhāsana)** of gold and gems, supported by **thirty-two female statuettes** (*śālabhañjikā / puttalikā / puttalī*). It is the lost throne of **Vikramāditya**, whose sheer residual virtue had been radiating up through the soil. Bhoja has it installed and, on an auspicious day, mounts the steps to be enthroned. (Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā, frame)
  - *Variant (whence the throne):* most recensions make the throne a **gift of Indra** to Vikramāditya (below); some make it a work of **Viśvakarman**. **[late text]**

## The thirty-two statuettes — the challenge structure

- **The apsaras under a curse** — the thirty-two statuettes are not mere carvings: they are **apsaras (celestial nymphs)** of Indra's court, **petrified by a curse** and bound to the throne, animate only to guard it. Each time Bhoja lifts his foot to the seat, **one statuette speaks**, halts him, and tells a tale proving that Vikrama possessed a magnanimity Bhoja lacks, closing: *"If you have done the like, sit here."* Thirty-two attempts, thirty-two refusals. (Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā I–XXXII) **[late text]**
  - *Variant (the curse's author):* recensions and folk lines variously blame **Pārvatī**, **Indra**, or an offended **ṛṣi**; the constant is that release comes only when they have **recited Vikrama's virtues in full to a king humble enough to concede them**. [folk]
- **Named in the vernacular** — the Sanskrit recensions usually leave the thirty-two **anonymous**; the Hindi **Siṅhāsan Battīsī** supplies proper names for the *puttalīs* (e.g. **Ratnamañjarī, Citralekhā, Candrakalā, Prabhāvatī, Trilocanā, Kaumudī** and others) — but the **name-lists differ across editions**, so no single canonical roster of thirty-two names holds. **[folk] [regional]**

## How Vikramāditya won the throne — Indra's dance-contest

- **The dance of Rambhā and Urvaśī** — in the common back-story, **Indra** cannot decide which of two apsaras, **Rambhā** and **Urvaśī**, dances more perfectly, and summons the mortal connoisseur **Vikramāditya** to Amarāvatī to **judge**. Vikrama's verdict is flawless and impartial. Delighted, Indra rewards him with the **jewelled siṃhāsana borne by the thirty-two statuettes**, the very throne Bhoja later exhumes — closing the loop between the [apsaras](../08-beings-and-bestiary/apsaras.md) who staff it and the god who gave it. (Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā, frame; SR) **[late text]**

## Representative tales of the throne (a selection of the 32)

*(A sampling of recurrent tale-types; recensions substitute and re-order, so numbering is nominal. Edgerton's translation is the anchor.)*

- **The head offered to the goddess** — a Brahmin (or a devotee) is bound by a vow to sacrifice a perfect man's head to **Devī (Kālī / Kamalā)**; Vikrama, hearing that no one else will serve, **offers his own head** at the goddess's altar. The Goddess, overcome by such fearless generosity, **restores him to life** and grants boons. The tale of self-decapitation for another's vow is among the most repeated proofs of his *sāhasa*. (Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā, tale of the head-gift) **[late text]**
- **The philosopher's stone given away** — a mendicant or a grateful being gives Vikrama the **pāras-maṇi (touchstone that turns iron to gold)** or a wish-granting jewel; Vikrama, having no use for hoarded wealth, **bestows it on a poor Brahmin** without a second thought — the pattern of *he acquires only to give*. **[late text]**
- **The descent to Pātāla / crossing the ocean** — Vikrama undertakes a perilous journey to the **netherworld** or across the sea to win treasure, a celestial maiden, or a boon for a suppliant, and **distributes the entire gain** among the needy on his return. **[late text]**
- **Aid to the frustrated sacrificer** — a Brahmin cannot complete a rite for want of wealth or of a rare object; Vikrama supplies it — sometimes at the cost of his own comfort or safety — so the sacrifice may succeed. (A generic *dāna*-tale kindred to the [generosity-cycle](dana-upakhyanas-shveta-and-the-mongoose.md).) **[late text]**
- **The Vetāla in service** — several tales presuppose Vikrama's mastery of the **cremation-ground vetāla**, the spirit he carried on his shoulder in the [Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati](vetala-panchavimshati-vikram-and-betal.md); here the corpse-spirit or a subdued [siddha-being](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md) fetches a marvel for him, which he again gives away. The two cycles cross-reference the same fearless king. **[late text]**
- **The unbudging generosity** — a recurring closing beat: however wondrous the gift Vikrama gains, he keeps **nothing**; each statuette's verdict is the same — *"You, O Bhoja, would have kept it; Vikrama did not."* This is the moral spine of all thirty-two.

## The Bhartṛhari fruit-tale (attached to the cycle)

- **The immortality-fruit and the chain of love** — appended to the Ujjain legend-complex (and told in Nāth and folk tradition rather than in every Sanskrit recension) is the tale of Vikrama's brother, the poet-king **Bhartṛhari**: given an **amṛta-phala (fruit conferring agelessness/immortality)** by a Brahmin ascetic, he gives it to his beloved queen **Piṅgalā (Anaṅgasenā)**; she gives it to the **stable-master / kotwāl** she secretly loves; he gives it to a **courtesan**; she, judging the **king** the worthiest to live long, returns it to **Bhartṛhari** — who traces its full circuit, sees the vanity of desire, **renounces the throne of Ujjayinī, hands it to Vikramāditya**, and becomes a yogin (in later tradition a disciple of Gorakhnāth). (Ujjain legend-complex; Bhartṛhari tradition) **[folk]** — see [Ujjain / Avantikā §IX](../06-sacred-geography/ujjain-avantika.md) and the [Nāth epic milieu](gopichandra-maynamati-nath-epic.md)
  - *Variant (their kinship):* Bhartṛhari is called Vikrama's **elder brother** or **stepbrother**; the constant is that his renunciation **puts Vikrama on the throne**. **[folk] [disputed]**

## The Śālivāhana / Śātavāhana rivalry and the two eras

- **Vikrama vs. Śālivāhana** — the pan-Indian legend pairs Vikramāditya of Ujjayinī with his southern rival **Śālivāhana (Sātavāhana)** of **[Pratiṣṭhāna on the Godāvarī](../06-sacred-geography/godavari-gautami-dakshina-ganga.md)** (Paithan). In the folk tale Śālivāhana is a **child of miraculous birth** — fathered by the serpent **Śeṣa** (a nāga) on a potter's daughter — who **animates an army of clay soldiers and horses** (fashioned by his grandfather the potter) and with them **repels Vikrama's invasion**. **[folk] [regional]**
- **The two calendar eras (two clocks)** — the rivalry is the folk etiology of India's two great reckonings: **Vikrama Saṃvat (57 BCE)**, counted from Vikramāditya's victory over the **Śakas** (his title *Śakāri*, "foe of the Śakas"), and the **Śālivāhana Śaka era (78 CE)**, counted from Śālivāhana's triumph. Historically the "Śaka" era belongs to the **Western Kṣatrapa / Kuṣāṇa horizon** and the Vikrama era was first called **Kṛta / Mālava**, its "Vikrama" name attached only from c. the 9th c. CE — the legend retro-fitting the calendars, not the reverse. **[scholarly] [disputed]** — see [calendar & pañcāṅga](../00-time-and-cosmology/hindu-calendar-and-panchanga.md)

## The release of the statues and Bhoja's verdict

- **Thirty-two refusals, one lesson** — after the **thirty-second** tale Bhoja, unable to claim a single deed to rival Vikrama's, **abandons the attempt** to sit on the throne; his very willingness to concede is the humility the cycle exists to teach. (Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā XXXII, conclusion) **[late text]**
- **The apsaras freed** — their appointed task complete, the **curse on the thirty-two statuettes lifts**; the apsaras resume their celestial forms and **return to Indra's heaven**, blessing Bhoja before they go. (Conclusion) **[late text]**
  - *Variant (the endings):* recensions differ on the coda — in some the freed nymphs grant Bhoja a **boon** or **permit** him to reign in Vikrama's spirit; in others Vikrama himself, or a divine voice, sanctions Bhoja; in some the **throne ascends to heaven** or is enshrined. Edgerton notes the ending is among the least stable parts of the cycle. **[scholarly]**

## Recensions, transmission, afterlife

- **Sanskrit lines** — Southern, Metrical, Brief, and Jainistic recensions (Edgerton), the last reframing the tales through **Jain** ethical emphasis. **[scholarly]**
- **Vernacular spread** — the **Hindi/Braj Siṅhāsan Battīsī** and its **Persian (Kissa-i-Sinhasan Battisi)**, **Urdu**, **Marathi**, **Tamil**, **Telugu**, **Bengali** and other renderings made the cycle one of the most widely circulated tale-books of the subcontinent. **[regional]**
- **Modern echoes** — the frame furnished popular Doordarshan-era television (the serial *Singhasan Battisi*) and countless comic-book and children's retellings, fixing "the thirty-two talking statues of Vikramaditya's throne" in modern imagination — companion in the public mind to *Vikram aur Betāl*. **[folk]**
