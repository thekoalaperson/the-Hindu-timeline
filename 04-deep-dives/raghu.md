# Raghu — the King Who Gave His Name to the Dynasty

> Cosmic address: a Sūryavaṃśa (Solar-dynasty) cakravartin of the **Tretā Yuga**, of the Ikṣvāku line seated at **Ayodhyā** in Kosala, in the present 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, 28th Mahāyuga. **Raghu** is the king so illustrious that the senior Solar branch is permanently renamed the **Raghuvaṃśa** ("House of Raghu") after him — the very title of Kālidāsa's dynastic mahākāvya, whose name for the whole line eclipses even Ikṣvāku's. He is a first-tier *cakravartin* (universal sovereign) of the Ikṣvāku stock: son of **Dilīpa** (begotten by the grace of the divine cow **Nandinī**, see [Dilīpa & Nandinī](dilipa-nandini.md)), grandfather of **Aja**, and great-grandfather of **Daśaratha** — hence Rāma's great-great-grandfather. His two emblematic deeds are the **digvijaya** (the four-quarter world-conquest) and the linked **Viśvajit-sacrifice / Kautsa** episode, in which he gives away every possession and then storms heaven's treasurer Kubera for the gold to satisfy a brahmin's fee — the tradition's supreme parable of royal *dāna* (open-handed giving). His career fills cantos **3–5** of Kālidāsa's *Raghuvaṃśa*; the Purāṇas (Viṣṇu IV, Bhāgavata IX) place him tersely in the king-list.

---

## I. Birth and accession

- **Son of Dilīpa and Sudakṣiṇā** — Raghu is born to King Dilīpa after Dilīpa's long service to the wish-cow Nandinī (Vasiṣṭha's *kāmadhenu*) ends his childlessness; the very name *Raghu* ("the swift") is given for his future fleetness in conquest. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 3.1–21; [Dilīpa & Nandinī](dilipa-nandini.md))
- **Indra steals the sacrificial horse** — When Dilīpa undertakes his hundredth Aśvamedha (which would make him Indra's equal), Indra carries off the consecrated horse; the boy-prince Raghu pursues, confronts Indra himself, and though worsted wins Indra's admiration and a boon. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 3.38–66)
  - *Variant (Kālidāsa's literary expansion):* the horse-theft and Raghu's duel with Indra is Kālidāsa's poetic elaboration; the Purāṇic king-lists give Raghu only as a name in the succession Dilīpa → Raghu → Aja. [late text]
- **Anointed king** — Dilīpa installs the young Raghu on the throne of Kosala and retires to the forest in the *vānaprastha* manner of the Solar kings. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 3.69–70)

## II. The Digvijaya — the four-quarter world-conquest

- **The horse loosed for the Aśvamedha** — As the centerpiece of his sovereignty Raghu mounts a *digvijaya*, a campaign of conquest in all four directions, his army following the freed sacrificial horse. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 4.24–88)
- **East** — He subdues the kings of the eastern (Suhma, Vaṅga) regions along the Gaṅgā delta and the sea-coast, planting pillars of victory on the islands of the Ganges' mouths. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 4.34–37)
- **South** — Crossing the Kāverī and the Malaya hills he humbles the Pāṇḍya and Kerala kings and reaches the southern ocean; the Malaya and Dardura mountains and the betel-and-sandal country submit. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 4.46–55)
- **West** — He defeats the Pārasīkas (Persians) and the Yavanas of the west by land, fighting the horse-riding peoples, and rests his troops in the saffron-fields of the western hills. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 4.60–67)
- **North** — He crosses the Indus / Vaṅkṣu (Oxus) country, conquers the Hūṇas and Kāmboja horse-breeders, and climbs into the Himālaya, subduing the mountain Utsava-saṃketa tribes; the Kailāsa region and the Lauhitya bow to him. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 4.66–84)
  - *Variant (geography, [scholarly]):* Kālidāsa's list — Hūṇas, Pārasīkas, Yavanas, Kāmbojas — reflects the political horizon of the Gupta age (c. 4th–5th c. CE) projected back onto a Tretā-Yuga king; the catalogue is read as literary geography, not a Purāṇic itinerary. [late text]
- **Return and the great sacrifice** — Laden with the tribute of the whole earth, Raghu returns to Ayodhyā and performs the conquest's culminating rite. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 4.86–88)

## III. The Viśvajit sacrifice — giving away all wealth

- **The Viśvajit ("all-conquering") sacrifice** — At the climax of his conquests Raghu performs the **Viśvajit**, a great Vedic rite whose vow requires the sacrificer to **give away the whole of his accumulated wealth** as *dakṣiṇā* (priestly fee). (*Raghuvaṃśa* 5.1–2)
- **Total renunciation of treasure** — Raghu duly distributes everything — the tribute of the digvijaya, his gold, his stores — to the brahmins and suppliants, retaining for himself only earthen vessels, so that the conqueror of the earth is left with nothing. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 5.1–3)
- **The point of the episode** — The emptying is deliberate: Raghu's glory is shown to lie in *tyāga* (renunciation, giving) rather than in hoarding the fruits of conquest — the Raghuvaṃśa's ideal of the king who possesses the world in order to give it away. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 5; [scholarly])

## IV. The Kautsa episode — the assault on Kubera

- **Kautsa arrives for guru-dakṣiṇā** — Just after the treasury has been emptied, the young brahmin **Kautsa**, a pupil of the sage **Varatantu**, comes to Raghu to beg the gold he needs as his teacher's fee: **fourteen crore (140 million) gold coins**, one crore for each of the fourteen branches of learning (*vidyā*) he has mastered. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 5.1, 5.20–24)
- **Raghu's shame and resolve** — Mortified that a suppliant should leave him empty-handed, Raghu — though he has only clay pots left — refuses to send Kautsa away and resolves to wrest the gold from **Kubera**, lord of wealth, the very next dawn. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 5.24–26)
- **The threatened assault on Kubera** — Raghu has his chariot readied to march against Kubera's realm; rather than face the invincible Solar king in war, **Kubera rains a shower of gold** into Raghu's treasury overnight, filling it. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 5.27–30)
  - *Variant (mechanism):* in Kālidāsa the gold-rain falls on Kubera's own initiative, forestalling the attack; the episode is a literary set-piece dramatizing Raghu's *dāna* and the impossibility of his refusing a worthy beggar — it is not narrated in the Purāṇic king-lists. [late text]
- **The double gift** — Raghu offers Kautsa the entire gold-rain; Kautsa, taking only the fourteen crore he asked for, refuses the surplus, and the two part each enriched by the other's restraint — Kautsa blessing Raghu with the boon of a worthy son. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 5.31–34)

## V. Aja, succession, and the close of Raghu's reign

- **Birth of Aja** — In fulfillment of Kautsa's blessing Raghu fathers **Aja**, his heir, who will wed **Indumatī** at her *svayaṃvara* and father **Daśaratha**. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 5.36; 6–8; [lineage](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md))
- **Abdication and the forest** — In the manner of the Ikṣvākus Raghu in old age hands the kingdom to Aja and withdraws to ascetic life, dying as a *yogin* fixed in contemplation. (*Raghuvaṃśa* 8.11–25)
- **The dynasty bears his name** — From Raghu the whole senior Solar line is styled **Raghuvaṃśa**; Rāma himself is *Rāghava* ("descendant of Raghu"), the patronymic carrying Raghu's fame into the [Rāmāyaṇa](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md). (*Raghuvaṃśa*; *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX)

## VI. Placement in the line and the "two clocks"

- **Genealogical placement** — In the Solar king-list: **… → Dilīpa II → Raghu → Aja → Daśaratha → Rāma →** Kuśa & Lava. Full tables in [../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md). (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* IV.2–4; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.6–12)
  - *Variant (which Dilīpa):* the Purāṇas distinguish an earlier **Dilīpa I** (descendant of Aṃśumān, in the Bhagīratha line) from the **Dilīpa** who is Raghu's father; Kālidāsa's Dilīpa-and-Nandinī tale belongs to Raghu's father (see [Dilīpa & Nandinī](dilipa-nandini.md)). [disputed]
- **Scriptural clock** — On the scriptural reckoning Raghu is a Tretā-Yuga king of the present Mahāyuga, four generations above Rāma, whose reign the tradition dates to the Tretā–Dvāpara junction.
- **Historical clock** — Kālidāsa's *Raghuvaṃśa*, the text that fixes Raghu's fame, is a **5th-century-CE** Sanskrit mahākāvya; its geography of Hūṇas, Pārasīkas, and Yavanas is Gupta-age literary world-building retrojected onto the ancient king. The two clocks must be kept distinct: Raghu the Tretā cakravartin of sacred history vs. Raghu the hero of a Gupta-classical court poem. [scholarly]
