# The Satyanārāyaṇa Vrata Kathā — and its Bengali Double, Satya Pīr

> Cosmic address: a *vrata-charter tale especially commended for Kali Yuga* — this is the observance’s devotional applicability, not a date for all its embedded stories. Chapter 1.18 permits any day with devotion; full moon ([Pūrṇimā](../00-time-and-cosmology/hindu-calendar-and-panchanga.md)) and saṁkrānti are specifically Sādhu’s recurring practice in 4.43; its kings (Ulkāmukha, Tuṅgadhvaja) sit in no genealogy. Historically [scholarly] both the Sanskrit kathā and its Bengali twin **Satya Pīr** belong to the early-modern centuries.
> Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Festival entry: [Festivals & Vratas — Pūrṇimā vratas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md) · Text profile: [Skanda Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md) · [Khaṇḍa map](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana-khanda-map.md)

This is the most-performed pūjā narrative of North and West India — the five-chapter **Satyanārāyaṇa Vrata Kathā** recited at full-moon household worship of **Viṣṇu as Satyanārāyaṇa** ("Nārāyaṇa who is Truth," or "the Lord of Truth"; see [Viṣṇu's forms](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishnu-forms-and-vyuhas.md)). Its plot is a relay of proof-stories: a starving brahmin, a woodcutter, the merchant **Sādhu** with his wife **Līlāvatī** and daughter **Kalāvatī**, and King **Tuṅgadhvaja** — each prospers by the vrata, and each who slights it (defers it, lies before the god, abandons the prasāda, spurns the prasāda) is struck until the slight is repaired. In **Bengal** the same deity circulates as **Satya Pīr**, a Muslim-styled fakir worshipped by Hindus and Muslims alike with the same flour-milk-banana offering (**śirṇi**), sung in a large **pāñcālī** literature (from the late 16th c. or earlier, with dated manuscripts from the 17th, burgeoning in 19th-c. print). Per house policy the Sanskrit kathā and the Bengali cycle are catalogued together here as one merged topic — two dresses of one figure — with their disagreements attributed, never fused.

---

## The text and its status

- **What it charters** — The kathā warrants the **Satyanārāyaṇa Pūjā**: evening worship (*niśāmukhe*) on any day with devotion (1.18–19); Pūrṇimā and saṁkrānti are Sādhu’s later recurring practice (4.43), with kinsmen and brahmins assembled, the story heard in full, and a **sapāda** (1¼-measure) prasāda of **wheat flour, sugar/jaggery, banana, milk and ghee** offered and shared (1.20–23). The five chapters are themselves the rite's core — the tales of Kalāvatī and Tuṅgadhvaja make *hearing the kathā and eating the prasāda* non-negotiable parts of the vrata. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-khaṇḍa appendix, 1.17–24, 4.24–40, 5.2–6)
- **Attribution and the interpolation flag** — The kathā is printed in pothīs as **Skanda Purāṇa, Revā-khaṇḍa**, chapters 1–5 (every chapter colophon in the SanskritDocuments witness reads *iti śrīskandapurāṇe revākhaṇḍe*). It is not in the Veṅkaṭeśvara Press (Bombay) Skanda; Tagare's introduction calls it the "spurious Satya-nārāyaṇa-māhātmya", printed in the Revā-khaṇḍa of the Gurumaṇḍala edition (pp. 1122–33) but absent from the Veṅkaṭeśvara one (Tagare, *Skanda-Purāṇa* pt. 1, intro. xvii–xviii [scholarly]). Bühnemann (1988: 134–43) finds it in the 1912 Bengali-script Vaṅgavāsī reprint, told only *after* the Revā-khaṇḍa proper closes. Hazra (1940: 157, 169) reports that the Vaṅgavāsī editor took the Veṅkaṭeśvara text as his base and added chapters and verses from Bengal manuscripts. (Both are known here only from Wikipedia's *Satyanarayana Puja* summary, not read directly; that summary also has Hazra saying the "entire Revā Khaṇḍa" is Vaṅgavāsī-only, which conflicts with Tagare and is probably garbled.) Sanskrit Wikisource hosts the five chapters as Revā-khaṇḍa adhyāyas 233–237. The older Śaiva *Skandapurāṇa* (the ~810 CE Nepalese palm-leaf; see [text profile](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md)) is a different text with no Revā-khaṇḍa, so its silence settles little. The Revā-khaṇḍa itself is of disputed placement (it also circulates under the Vāyu Purāṇa; [khaṇḍa-map](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana-khanda-map.md)). **[late text] [interpolation]**
  - *Variant (Bhaviṣya P., Pratisarga-parvan 3.2.24–29):* a second Sanskrit recension, in six adhyāyas, whose colophons place it in a *kaliyugīya-itihāsa-samuccaya* ("collection of Kali-age histories"). It shares whole verses with the Skanda text (Bhav. 3.2.24.7, 10 = Skanda app. 1.4, 1.7) but differs in plot: the Kāśī brahmin is named **Śatānanda** in the story itself (3.2.25.3); a king **Candracūḍa of Kedāra-Maṇipūraka**, driven out by Vindhya-dwelling mlecchas, learns the vrata in Kāśī from the now-prosperous brahmin (called **Sadānanda** in this chapter, 3.2.26.10–13), receives a sword from the god (26.18), wins back his kingdom and worships monthly on Pūrṇimā on the Narmadā bank (3.2.26.1–21); the woodcutters are **Bhilla/Niṣāda** wood-carriers (3.2.27.1), taught by the same brahmin through Candracūḍa's story (27.6–20); and the merchant (*sādhur lakṣapatir vaṇik*, 3.2.28.3) of Ratnapura vows at Candracūḍa's pūjā (3.2.28.1–14), not at Ulkāmukha's. The ritual is much more elaborate (a plantain-pillared *toraṇa* with five kalaśas and five banners, 3.2.26.15; cf. 3.2.24.25–34). There is **no Tuṅgadhvaja/cowherd chapter**. Pratisarga-parvan as a whole is "a modern fabrication" (Kane, *HDS* V.2: 896–97 [scholarly]). [late text] [disputed]
  - *Variant (Itihāsa-samuccaya colophon):* a Sanskrit manuscript dated Śaka 1772 (1850 CE) closes with *iti śrī itihāsasamuccaye satyanārāyaṇavratakathā caturtho 'dhyāyaḥ samāptā* (its closing colophon is to the fourth adhyāya) and is catalogued as a selection "from the Mahābhārata" (Penn Libraries Ms. Coll. 390 Item 324, f. 22r). Haraprasād Śāstrī (1928: lxv–lxvi, via Wikipedia) notes that the kathā is not in the Mahābhārata itself. [late text]
  - *Variant (Brahma Purāṇa attribution):* one Bengali manuscript (an anonymous *Satyanārāyaṇ pustak*, Dhaka University K-434, dated c. 1750) names the Brahma Purāṇa as its source, and Stewart could find no such passage in standard editions (Stewart 2019: 211 n. 40). [regional] [disputed]
- **The frame** — Like a proper Purāṇic māhātmya, the kathā is spoken under Vyāsa's heading (*śrīvyāsa uvāca*) and opens in **Naimiṣāraṇya**: **Śaunaka** and the sages ask **Sūta** by what vrata or austerity a desired fruit is won; Sūta answers with what **Nārāyaṇa** once told **[Nārada](narada.md)**. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 1.1–3) [late text]
- **Genre kin** — Structurally it belongs with the vrata-māhātmya genre catalogued at [Ekādaśī-māhātmyas](ekadashi-mahatmyas-catalog.md) and the [Śivarātri hunter legend](shivaratri-hunter-legend.md): stories whose theological engine is the *objective* power of the observance — kept, it prospers anyone; broken, it strikes anyone, king or merchant.

## Chapter 1 — Nārada's petition; Viṣṇu prescribes the vrata

- **Nārada tours the mortal world** — Roaming the lokas, Nārada reaches the earth (Mṛtyuloka) and is grieved to see beings suffering the miseries of their karma; he goes to **Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa** in Viṣṇuloka and asks by what small means (*laghūpāya*) such beings can be freed. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 1.4–12)
- **The prescription** — Viṣṇu declares the **Satyanārāyaṇa vrata**, "rare in heaven and on earth" (*svarge martye ca durlabham*, 1.14), now revealed for love of his devotee: whoever, in the **evening**, with brahmins and kinsfolk, worships Satyanārāyaṇa with the sapāda offering of banana, ghee, milk, wheat flour (rice flour where wheat is lacking) and sugar or jaggery, **hears the kathā**, and distributes the prasāda, gains wealth, progeny, and in the end the highest goal. The kathā frames this as the fit rite **for the Kali age** — maximal fruit for minimal means. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 1.13–24) [late text]

## Chapter 2 — The poor brahmin of Kāśī and the woodcutter

- **The starving brahmin** — A poor brahmin of **[Kāśī](../06-sacred-geography/sapta-puri-and-sacred-cities.md)** wanders begging; Satyanārāyaṇa himself, **disguised as an aged brahmin**, asks him his sorrow and teaches him the vrata. The next day his begging brings in plenty; he performs the vrata with his kin, keeps it monthly, and is released from poverty into wealth and finally mokṣa — the kathā's first proof. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 2.1–13)
  - *Textual naming distinction:* chapter 2 leaves the brahmin unnamed, but the SanskritDocuments five-chapter witness names **Śatānanda** in its own rebirth coda (5.19). The Bhaviṣya recension names him Śatānanda in the story itself (Bhav. P. 3.2.25.3), and its later chapters call him Sadānanda (3.2.26.10–19, 27.6) and place him in "Viṣṇudāsa's āśrama" (3.2.27.2, possibly an epithet, "servant of Viṣṇu"), so the name is textual, not merely oral.
- **The woodcutter** — A **wood-seller** (the text's word is *kāṣṭhakretā*, 2.17, literally "wood-buyer", but in context he sells wood; 5.20 calls him a wood-carrying *bhilla*) arrives thirsty, leaves his load outside and comes to the brahmin's house during the pūjā (2.17–18). He asks what it is, drinks water and **eats the prasāda** (2.21), and vows to spend that day's earnings on the vrata. That day his wood sells at **double price** (2.24); he buys the ingredients, performs the vrata with his kin, prospers with wealth and sons, and at death reaches **Satyapura** (2.25–27). The rite is thus shown open to the humblest laborer, not brahmins alone. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 2.16–27)
  - *Variant (Bhaviṣya P. 3.2.27.1–28):* the thirsty Niṣāda only drinks water at the brahmin's āśrama and is taught the rite through Candracūḍa's story (27.3–20); he and his fellow Bhillas vow a pūjā from their wood earnings, receive **fourfold** price (*caturguṇaṃ dhanam*, 27.24), and their wives join the pūjā, after which all take the prasāda (27.25–26). [late text]

## Chapter 3 — King Ulkāmukha; the merchant Sādhu, Līlāvatī and Kalāvatī

- **King Ulkāmukha** — The truthful, self-controlled king **Ulkāmukha** keeps the vrata: his wife performs it on the bank of the **Bhadraśīlā** river (3.3), and the king, called the *vratin* (3.5), says it is done with his kin "for the gaining of sons and the like" (3.7). (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 3.1–7)
  - *Variant (Bhaviṣya P. 3.2.28.1–14):* the merchant instead comes upon King **Candracūḍa** of Maṇipūra worshipping with his subjects, and he vows a golden banner if he is granted a son or a daughter. [late text]
- **The merchant's conditional vow** — The merchant **Sādhu** (sādhu-vaṇik), trading by boat, sees the king's pūjā and asks about it; childless, he vows to perform the vrata **if** he gains offspring. His wife **Līlāvatī** bears a daughter, **Kalāvatī**, and when Līlāvatī reminds him, Sādhu defers the vow "at her marriage." At the wedding (to a merchant's son from Kāñcana, who later goes with him on the trading journey) he **forgets again**, the Lord is angered, and he sails for trade. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 3.4–22)
  - *Variant (Bhaviṣya P. 3.2.28.3, 23):* the merchant of Ratnapura is *sādhur lakṣapatir vaṇik*, read as the name **Lakṣapati** (so Bühnemann, via Wikipedia) or as the epithet "owner of a lakh" (the HinduMonastery English renders "very wealthy"); the son-in-law is the merchant **Śaṅkhapati** of Kāñcanapura (*vaṇik śaṅkhapatiḥ śrutaḥ*). The Skanda text leaves the son-in-law unnamed. [late text]
- **The framed theft** — At **Ratnasārapura** "near the *sindhu*" (3.23: the word can mean "sea" or "river", and the verse does not decide; contrast the Bhaviṣya's explicit Narmadā below), in the city of King **Candraketu**, the Lord, seeing the broken promise, curses the merchant (3.24–25). A thief fleeing the king's men drops the stolen royal treasure where Sādhu and his son-in-law are staying (3.26–27). The two are bound as the thieves and **imprisoned** without inquiry; through the god's māyā no one hears their words, and Candraketu confiscates their goods (3.28–31). (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 3.22–31)
  - *Variant (Bhaviṣya P. 3.2.28.29–41):* the trading city is on the **Narmadā** and the king is not named. Royal officers who cannot find the thieves go to the river to drown themselves, see the stolen pearl necklace on the merchant's neck and seize him. [late text]
- **The women's vrata and the dream** — At home a separate theft empties the house, and Līlāvatī and Kalāvatī fall into destitution (3.32–33); Kalāvatī, begging, sees the Satyanārāyaṇa pūjā at a brahmin’s house, hears the kathā, prays, **eats prasāda**, and reports it when she returns at night; Līlāvatī performs the vrata, praying for husband and son-in-law. That night Satyanārāyaṇa commands King Candraketu **in a dream** to release the two merchants; the king frees them and sends them home with **doubled wealth**. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 3.32–51)
  - *Variant (Bhaviṣya P. 3.2.29.13–22):* Kalāvatī pays for the women's vrata with five *niṣkas* that **Śīlapāla Gupta** (a merchant in the HinduMonastery English) gives her, saying he is settling a debt owed to her father; and the king's chief minister has had the same dream. [late text]

## Chapter 4 — The lie to the sannyāsī and the abandoned prasāda

- **"Leaves and creepers"** — Shortly after the merchants set out from Candraketu's city (*kiyad dūre gate*, 4.2; the messenger home comes only at 4.20–22), Sādhu is tested. Satyanārāyaṇa, as a staff-bearing ascetic (*daṇḍin*, 4.3, 4.5; "sannyāsī" is the translators' gloss), asks what his boat holds. The two merchants, puffed up (*mattau*), laugh and ask whether he wants to carry off money, and Sādhu answers that the boat holds only "**latā-patra**, creepers and leaves" (4.3–4). "Let your word come true," says the ascetic, and the boat, now riding high, holds only creepers (4.4–7). At the **son-in-law's urging** (4.8–9) Sādhu begs forgiveness at the ascetic's feet and promises worship; his wealth is restored (4.10–19). (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 4.1–19)
- **The abandoned prasāda** — A messenger brings word of the arrival. Līlāvatī performs the Satya-pūjā, then hurries to the shore, telling Kalāvatī to come quickly; Kalāvatī completes the vrata but **leaves the prasāda uneaten** (4.23–25), and the Lord, angered, sinks the boat with her husband and the wealth **in the water** (4.25–26). (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 4.20–28)
- **The ākāśavāṇī and restoration** — Sādhu resolves on worship and prostrates; the Lord's words, heard "from the sky" (*gaganamaṇḍalāt*, 4.39), declare the cause, the abandoned prasāda (4.34–39). Kalāvatī returns home, eats the prasāda, and comes back to find her husband (4.40). Sādhu thereafter performs the worship on Pūrṇimā and saṁkrānti, and at death attains **Satyapura**, the Lord's realm. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 4.29–44)
  - *Variant (Bhaviṣya P. 3.2.29.34–67):* the leaves-and-creepers test (the god comes as a *tāpasa*, 3.2.29.33–36) and the sinking of the boat (here **Śaṅkhapati's** boat, 3.2.29.59) follow the same line. Repenting, the merchant vows a pūjā of a lakh of *mudrās* (3.2.29.52, 67); Līlāvatī hands the unfinished worship to her daughter and goes to the boat, and Kalāvatī, *avajñāya prasādam*, hurries after her (3.2.29.56–58). [late text]

## Chapter 5 — King Tuṅgadhvaja and the cowherds

- **The spurned prasāda** — King **Tuṅgadhvaja**, returning from the hunt, sees **cowherds (gopas)** with their kin worshipping Satyadeva at the foot of a **banyan** (*vaṭamūla*, 5.3); out of pride (*darpeṇa*, 5.4) he neither goes near nor bows. The cowherds set the prasāda before him and he **leaves it** (5.4–5). His **hundred sons** and his wealth and grain are lost (5.6). (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 5.1–6)
  - *Variant (Bengali printed Revā-khaṇḍa texts):* the king of this tale is **Vaṁśadhvaja**. One of the earliest printed Bengali editions of the Sanskrit (Vyāsadeva, *Satyanārāyaṇa nāmaka granthaḥ*, Calcutta c. 1861) closes with "the tale of King Vaṁśadhvaj", and Bengali tellings call it the *Vaṁśadhvaj gop saṁvād* or *Vaṁśadhvajoddhār*. Stewart notes the fourth tale names the king "variously … Tungadhvaj or Vamsadhvaj" and appears only in elaborate Bengali productions (Stewart 2019: 204 and n. 19 [scholarly]). Bühnemann's reading of the Vaṅgavāsī Skanda also reportedly gives Vaṁśadhvaja (via Wikipedia). [regional]
  - *Variant (Bhaviṣya P. 3.2.24–29):* there is no cowherd/king tale; the recension ends with the merchant's family going to heaven and a phala-śruti (3.2.29.67–70). [late text]
- **Repair and restoration** — Concluding that Satyadeva has destroyed all this (5.6), the king goes back to the cowherds and, with devotion and faith, performs the pūjā with them "according to rule" (5.7–8). By Satyadeva's grace he again has wealth and sons, and at death attains **Satyapura** (5.9). The verses do not narrate his eating the prasāda; it is implied by the rite as prescribed (1.23). The chapter closes the kathā's ledger: the vrata's power binds the highest as surely as it lifts the lowest. (Skanda P. [attrib.], Revā-kh. app. 5.6–9)
- **The rebirth coda** — The checked Sanskrit five-chapter witness explicitly gives a *janmāntara* coda (5.18–24): **Śatānanda → Sudāmā; wood-bearing Bhilla → Guha; Ulkāmukha → Daśaratha; Sādhu → Moradhvaja; Tuṅgadhvaja → Svāyambhuva; cowherds → inhabitants of Vraja**. The same verses stand in Sanskrit Wikisource's Revā-khaṇḍa adhyāya 237 (19–24). This is an attested passage in this witness, not merely an unspecified oral claim. Preserve edition differences: 5.21 prints Śrīraṅganātha worship with an alternative Rāmacandra reading. These identifications do not establish a consistent linear yuga date for the preceding tales. Traditional attribution does not itself settle placement in older Skanda recensions. The Bhaviṣya recension has no such coda. [late text]

## Full-story verification and adaptation anchors — 23 September 2026

This expansion checks the **complete five-chapter Sanskrit witness**, not only an English summary. Verse numbers below are local to each chapter. The narrative is paraphrased independently; modern explanatory prose and ritual manuals are not copied. Research used two independent Luna passes, followed by primary-text review; a 17 October 2026 re-check added verse loci throughout and collated the Bhaviṣya Pratisarga recension (3.2.24–29, Sanskrit Wikisource). Preserve the Bengali cycle below as a separate attributed tradition.

- **Witness and limits** — [SanskritDocuments, complete Sanskrit kathā](https://sanskritdocuments.org/doc_vishhnu/satyanArAyaNavratakathA.html), chapter lengths 24, 27, 51, 44 and 24 verses; the text prints the traditional Skanda/Revā colophons. [Lalitha Peetham’s complete devotional English retelling](https://www.lalithapeetham.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/07/Sri-Satyanarayna-Katha.pdf) and [Lakshmi Narayan’s bilingual puja/kathā booklet](https://lakshminarayanlenasia.com/downloads/Shri-Satyanarayan-Vrat-Katha.pdf) provide comparison witnesses. Their English headings, names and explanatory additions are not substitutes for the Sanskrit wording. This audit confirms the story; it does not independently establish the precise date of composition or every historical claim elsewhere in this article.
- **Chapter 1: frame, appearance and complete prescription** — Vyāsa introduces the sages’ inquiry; Sūta tells Nārada’s meeting with the Lord (1.1–16). The witnessed form is **white-complexioned, four-armed, with conch, discus, mace, lotus and forest garland** (1.7); this should not be silently replaced with another form’s visual specification. The prescription includes devotion, evening observance with brahmins and kin, the sapāda offering and flour/substitute choices, dakṣiṇā, hearing the kathā, feeding the gathering, accepting prasāda, music/dance and returning home remembering the Lord (1.17–24). It does not prescribe a full-moon-only restriction or a universal strict fast.
- **Chapter 2: the passing of the vrata** — After teaching the hungry Kāśī brahmin, the elderly divine visitor disappears. The man spends a sleepless night resolving to perform the observance, receives abundant alms next day, worships with relatives and continues monthly (2.7–13). The sages ask how the rite spread (2.14–16). The thirsty woodcutter leaves his load outside, receives water **and prasāda**, then vows to use **that day’s** sale proceeds; he receives double price, buys the ingredients and worships with relatives (2.17–27). Do not transpose this first acceptance of prasāda into Kalāvatī’s later omission.
- **Chapter 3: promise, family and two separate thefts** — Preserve the riverbank encounter, childlessness, birth of Kalāvatī, Līlāvatī’s reminder, postponement until marriage, the search for a groom from **Kāñcana**, and a second neglect at the wedding (3.1–21). The trading destination is **Ratnasārapura near the *sindhu*** ("sea" in the usual rendering; the word can also mean a river), associated with **Candraketu** (3.22–31); do not confuse it with the **Ratnapurī** named as home in 4.20. One thief abandons stolen royal property by the merchants, leading to their imprisonment without inquiry and confiscation. A separate theft empties the household at home; the women suffer hunger and beg (3.26–33). Kalāvatī hears the vrata and accepts prasāda before reporting it at night (3.34–37). Līlāvatī performs the observance with relatives, asks forgiveness and reunion (3.38–40). The dream commands release **next morning**, return of confiscated wealth, and warns the king of consequences for disobedience (3.41–43). Release includes removal of bonds, grooming, clothing/ornaments, reassuring words and **double** restitution (3.44–51).
- **Chapter 4: the ascetic is not the end of the story** — Preserve the staff-bearing ascetic’s question, the merchant’s dismissive lie about leaves/creepers, its realization, the merchant’s collapse and the **son-in-law’s advice** to seek forgiveness (4.1–11). Repentance, restored cargo and renewed worship precede continued travel and the messenger home (4.12–22). Līlāvatī completes worship before departing; Kalāvatī completes the vrata but leaves without taking prasāda (4.23–25). The Sanskrit explicitly describes **submergence in water** (4.26), then absence from sight (4.30, 4.38): these are compatible descriptions, not grounds for declaring either “sinks” or “disappears” false. No storm, wreck, physical rescue, or confirmed drowning death is stated.
- **Chapter 4: grief and return, without invented action** — Līlāvatī holds her grieving daughter; Kalāvatī takes her husband’s **pādukā** and resolves to follow him (4.29–33). The text does **not** narrate a completed sati, pyre or leap into water. Sādhu calls his people together, resolves to worship and bows (4.34–36). The divine voice explains the missed prasāda; Kalāvatī returns home, eats it and comes back to see her husband (4.37–40). A family return and recurring worship conclude the chapter (4.41–44). Preserve the distinct chain: message → joy → omission → loss → explanation → acceptance → restoration.
- **Chapter 5: exact scale and close** — Tuṅgadhvaja returns from a hunt; cowherds with their relatives worship under a **banyan**. He neither approaches nor bows and leaves the offering placed before him (5.1–5). **One hundred sons**, wealth and grain are lost, not one invented prince (5.6). He returns to worship with the cowherds, and family/prosperity are restored (5.7–9). The phala-śruti speaks of relief, release, hearing and devotion, commends the vrata especially for Kali, and leads into the witness’s rebirth coda (5.10–24). Present the promised fruits as this devotional text’s teaching, not a diagnosis of modern people’s suffering.
- **Chronology boundary** — “Especially fruitful in Kali” (1.24; 5.14–16) describes the observance. It does not assign all narrated kings to Kali or resolve the rebirth coda into a single chronological genealogy. Do not insert calculated years, named mahāyugas or unsupported dynastic ancestry into an adaptation.
- **Adaptation boundary** — The five chapters form the selected complete narrative. Cinematic camera, performance, transitions and sound may be invented; causal events, number of sons, recurring vessel, identity of the three kings, promises and repair acts remain grounded. A narrative film is distinct from a verbatim Sanskrit recital and from an exhaustive ritual manual. Original Hindi dialogue is dramatic paraphrase, not newly discovered scriptural quotation.

## The Bengali double — Satya Pīr and the pāñcālī literature

- **One deity, two dresses** — In Bengal (and adjacent Odisha, where he is **Satya Pīr / Satyanārāyaṇa** both), the god of this vrata circulates as **Satya Pīr** — imagined as a **Muslim fakir**, staff and beads in hand, who compels worship with an offering of **śirṇi** (Persian *shīrīnī*, "sweets"): the same flour-milk-banana-sugar mixture as the Sanskrit kathā's prasāda. Hindus address him as Satyanārāyaṇa, Muslims as a pīr; the texts often keep the ambiguity deliberately. Stewart notes that the tales also live in Assamese, Odia and Sanskrit (Stewart 2019: 195). [regional] [folk] [scholarly]
- **The pāñcālī corpus** — Satya Pīr is sung in Bengali **pāñcālī** narrative poems by poets **Hindu and Muslim alike**. Stewart's survey found "more than seven hundred fifty manuscripts and one hundred sixty printed titles composed by more than a hundred authors" (Stewart 2019: xiv). Stewart places the first known works (Phakir Rām, Ghanarām, Rāmeśvar, Ayodhyārām) "in the late sixteenth century, perhaps sooner" (Stewart 2019: 195), though elsewhere he dates Ghanarām to the early 18th century (2019: 78); Banglapedia credits a *Satya-Pīr* to Shaikh Faizullah, c. 1545–75. The **oldest dated manuscripts** are 17th-century: Śaṅkarācārya c. 1655 and c. 1695, **Phakir Rām Kavibhūṣaṇ** c. 1679–88 (61 extant MSS), Rāmeśvar Dvija c. 1680. Ghanarām Cakravartī (early 18th c.) wrote the *Satyanārāyaṇ ras sindhu*, and the literature boomed in cheap print from the mid-19th to the early 20th century (Stewart 2019: 78, 195, 203 n. 17). **Rāmeśvar** (printed as Bhaṭṭācārya and as Bandyopādhyāy, *Satyapīrer kathā*) and the court poet **Bhāratcandra Rāy** (*Satyanārāyaṇ vratkathā* and *Satyapīr kathā*; his version of the tale is "considered especially elegant though short") are among the named authors (Stewart 2019: 16, 211–12 nn. 41–42). (Bengali Satya Pīr pāñcālīs; Stewart [scholarly]) [regional] [folk]
- **Shared story-blocks** — The pāñcālī vrata-cycle carries the same proof-tales as the Sanskrit kathā, and Stewart describes the brahmin, the **woodcutters (kāṭhuriyā)** and the merchant as a set "ossified into a fixed set and sequence" in that order (Stewart 2019: 203). The **merchant cycle** is always the longest: the trader's deferred vow, imprisonment abroad, the denial of the cargo before the disguised god, and the daughter's slighted śirṇi with the sinking boat. Where the Sanskrit god tests as a sannyāsī, the Bengali god tests as a **fakir demanding śirṇi**. In the Bengali brahmin's tale, the god first appears as a *yavana* pīr and the brahmin refuses him until he reveals his Viṣṇu form (Stewart 2000, via Wikipedia). A cowherd/king tale (Tuṅgadhvaja or Vaṁśadhvaja) is added only "in extremely elaborate productions" (Stewart 2019: 204). (Satya Pīr pāñcālīs, merchant/kāṭhuriyā tales) [regional] [folk]
  - *Variant (origin tale, Kṛṣṇahari Dās strand):* some pāñcālīs give Satya Pīr a birth-story. In **Kṛṣṇahari Dās**'s *Baḍa Satya Pīr o Sandhyāvatī Kanyār puthi*, Alla sends Satya Pīr, and he is born from the unwed princess **Sandhyāvatī** (a heavenly Cāndbibi reborn to Queen Priyavatī), made pregnant by a heaven-sent flower while bathing. He comes to subdue her own father, King Mādhāi, called the demon-king **Mayadānav** (Maidānav) of Mālañcā, who jails fakirs, so that "the God of the hindu will be the pir of the musalmān" in the Kali age (Stewart 2019: ch. 1, 1–13). [folk] [regional]
  - *Variant (heroine tales):* a second Satya Pīr tale-family — translated by Tony K. Stewart as "fabulous females" romances — centers on resourceful heroines who invoke the pīr to rescue ruined husbands and kingdoms; these adventure pāñcālīs share the deity and the śirṇi but not the vrata-kathā plot. [scholarly] [folk]
  - *Variant (Sultanate origin claim):* popular tradition ties the cult's origin to the Bengal Sultanate era, sometimes to the court or family of **Ḥusain Shāh of Gauḍa** (r. 1494–1519). Banglapedia records the view that the sultan started the movement, adding "there is no evidence to support this view". Stewart treats Satya Pīr's biography as fiction (Stewart 2019, via Wikipedia), and the abstract of a 27 October 2016 talk by him says the figure appears nowhere in Persian chronicles or copperplate inscriptions, only as a mythic figure in British gazetteers (read only through a podcast-listing search summary; venue not verified). [disputed] [oral tradition]
- **Which came first?** — Both directions have been argued. **Satya Pīr first:** Haraprasād Śāstrī (1928) called the kathā "a very modern work" of Islamic origin, still known as Satya Pīr Pūjā. H. R. Divekar (1976) found it in no printed Skanda and derived it from a *Saccā Pīr* cult; the Gita Press *Kalyāṇa* translator admitted taking it from Bengali books. Asim Roy (1983) called the Skanda mention an interpolation written "to dislodge the worship of Satya-pir", and H. K. Sarma (1992) agrees (all via Wikipedia's summary). **Satyanārāyaṇa first:** S. C. Mitra (1919) took the Purāṇic inclusion as proof of older Hindu worship and noted that the pīr identification is purely Bengali (via Wikipedia). D. N. Neogi (*Sacred Tales of India*, 1916: 95–100) recorded both theories (via Wikipedia's *Satya Pir* article). **Tony K. Stewart** calls Satya Nārāyaṇa a 17th- or early-18th-century Sanskritization of Satya Pīr (Stewart 1995, via Wikipedia). He also writes that the lateness and fluidity of the Skanda and Bhaviṣya texts "opens the distinct possibility that the stories circulated first in Bangla, then subsequently were incorporated into the purāṇik text, before being reintroduced in Bangla", while leaving that speculation outside his inquiry (Stewart 2019: 211). He dates the conscious Sanskritizing of the rite to the late 19th and early 20th century (Stewart 2019: 206–11), by editing the Sanskrit text and "correcting" the theology (Stewart 2000, via Wikipedia). **[scholarly] [disputed]**
- **The company he keeps** — Satya Pīr belongs to the wider family of shared Hindu–Muslim cult figures catalogued in this corpus: [Bonbibī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/bonbibi.md) of the Sundarbans, [Rāmdev Pīr](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ramdev-pir.md) and [Guggā/Zāhir Pīr](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/gugga-gogaji.md) in the west, [Jhulelal/Zinda Pīr](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/jhulelal.md) in Sindh; and his pāñcālīs stand beside the Bengali [maṅgal-kāvyas](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md) of [Manasā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/manasa.md) and [Dharma Ṭhākur](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dharma-thakur.md), which share the master-plot of a deity compelling reluctant merchants to worship. [regional] [scholarly]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural/cyclic frame** — The kathā dates itself only functionally: Viṣṇu reveals the vrata as the fit rite **for Kali Yuga** (1.24; 5.14–16; Bhav. P. 3.2.24.17 *kalau pratyakṣaphaladaḥ*). The text permits any day (1.18); the customary full-moon performance on the [ritual calendar](../00-time-and-cosmology/hindu-calendar-and-panchanga.md) follows Sādhu's Pūrṇimā-and-saṁkrānti practice (4.43), and in the Bhaviṣya, Candracūḍa's monthly Pūrṇimā worship (3.2.26.21). Its kings Ulkāmukha and Tuṅgadhvaja appear in no vaṁśa list; the Naimiṣāraṇya frame borrows the standard Purāṇic recital setting. The rebirth coda (5.18–24) [late text] reaches back toward Tretā and Dvāpara figures (Guha, Daśaratha, Sudāmā, Moradhvaja), yet it also makes Tuṅgadhvaja "Svāyambhuva", the name of the first Manu, so it cannot be read as one linear chronology.
- **Academic/historical frame [scholarly]** — The kathā is **not in the Veṅkaṭeśvara Press Skanda Purāṇa** and appears in Bengal-derived editions after the Revā-khaṇḍa proper (Tagare; Bühnemann; Hazra; see above). Its Bhaviṣya twin sits in the Pratisarga-parvan, which Kane judged "a modern fabrication" (*HDS* V.2: 896–97) [late text] [interpolation]. The **Satya Pīr pāñcālīs** begin in the late 16th century, "perhaps sooner", with dated manuscripts from c. 1655 (Stewart 2019: 195, 203 n. 17). The Sanskrit kathā in its current five-chapter form is placed in the same early-modern window, by Stewart as a 17th–early-18th-century Sanskritization, with the direction of borrowing still argued [disputed]. Mass diffusion came with **19th–20th-century printed pothīs** and vernacular translations (Hindi, Marathi, Gujarati, Telugu — where the pūjā, with rava-śīrā prasāda, is a fixture of housewarmings and weddings), making this the most widely performed household kathā of modern Hinduism.
