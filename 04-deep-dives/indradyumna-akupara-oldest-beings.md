# Indradyumna and Akūpāra — is anyone older than Mārkaṇḍeya?

> Where this sits: a *cirajīvin* parable from the **Mārkaṇḍeya-samāsyā** of the Mahābhārata's [Vana Parva](./mahabharata/03-vana-parva.md), narrated to Yudhiṣṭhira during the forest exile — the story that gives Sanskrit tradition its "chain of ever-older animals." Parent: [04-deep-dives](./README.md) · Companion node: [Mārkaṇḍeya](./markandeya.md). Keep the **two clocks** distinct: the inset events are dated only as *purā* ("of old"), stretching across yugas through beings that outlive pralayas; the *telling* is fixed to the Dvāpara–Kali cusp of the Pāṇḍavas' exile.

The **Indradyumna-upākhyāna** (Mahābhārata, Critical Edition **3.191**; Ganguli, Vana Parva Section **CXCIX**, within the Vana 191–199 arc) answers a question the epic's frame keeps posing: **who is the oldest living thing, and how long can a mortal's fame endure?** A royal sage cast out of heaven — because the memory of his charity has faded from the earth — must find one being old enough to remember him. He is passed up a ladder of increasing antiquity: **the sage Mārkaṇḍeya → an owl → a crane → a tortoise**, each disclaiming sufficient age and naming an elder. The tortoise **Akūpāra**, oldest of all, finally recognizes him — because the very lake Akūpāra lives in was scooped out by the hooves of the cattle Indradyumna once gave away. The parable's punch is double: even the deathless Mārkaṇḍeya is *not* the oldest being, and a man's true monument is not his name but the good he did, still legible in the shape of the land.

---

## I. The frame — Mārkaṇḍeya, the deathless narrator

- **The exile audience** — During the Pāṇḍavas' twelve-year forest exile, the *cirajīvin* ("long-lived") sage **Mārkaṇḍeya** visits Yudhiṣṭhira at Kāmyaka and delivers a long cycle of instructive tales (the **Mārkaṇḍeya-samāsyā-parva**, Vana Parva ~182–232). The Indradyumna story is one such inset, told to illustrate the vastness of time and the fragility of earthly renown. (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)
- **Mārkaṇḍeya as the point of comparison** — Mārkaṇḍeya is the standing benchmark of longevity in the epic: the ṛṣi who survives the dissolution and beholds the infant [Kṛṣṇa on the banyan leaf](./markandeya.md) between world-cycles. That he becomes the *first* rung of the ladder — and confesses he is not old enough — is the tale's deliberate shock. (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva; see [Mārkaṇḍeya](./markandeya.md)) [scholarly]
- **The fallen king arrives** — A royal sage named **Indradyumna**, once a sovereign of great sacrifices, appears at Mārkaṇḍeya's hermitage. He explains that he had dwelt in the heavenly worlds by the merit of his gifts and rites, but has now **fallen back to earth**: the celestials told him his store of merit was spent because **"there is no one left on earth who remembers thy deeds."** His restoration was made conditional — *find a living witness who knows thee, and thou mayst return.* (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)
  - *Note on the theology of fame:* the premise is that heavenly tenure is sustained by **kīrti** — one's fame surviving in the mouths and memory of the living below. When the last rememberer dies, the merit lapses and the soul drops from *svarga*. This makes the tale a meditation on *puṇya* and its exhaustion (cf. [karma, puṇya and prāyaścitta](../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md)). [scholarly]

## II. The ladder of ever-older witnesses

- **Mārkaṇḍeya disclaims and points onward** — The sage answers that **he does not know Indradyumna**, but that on a certain mountain lives an **owl named Prāvārakarṇa**, older than himself; the king should ask the owl, who may remember. (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)
- **The owl Prāvārakarṇa** — Carried by Mārkaṇḍeya (in the telling, Indradyumna becomes a horse to bear the sage, or they travel together — versions differ), they reach the aged owl **Prāvārakarṇa** ("mantle-eared"). The owl, questioned, also confesses **he does not know the king**, but names one older still: a **crane named Nāḍījaṅga** dwelling by a lake. (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)
- **The crane Nāḍījaṅgha** — The crane **Nāḍījaṅgha** ("reed-shanked"), by his lake, likewise does not recognize Indradyumna, and directs the party to the **oldest being he knows: the tortoise Akūpāra**, who lives in the depths of the same or a neighbouring lake and has seen far more than any bird. (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)
  - *Cross-reference [scholarly]:* the name **Nāḍījaṅgha** attaches elsewhere in the epic to a crane friend of Brahmā, better known by his epithet **Rājadharman**, killed in the Gautamī cycle of the [Śānti Parva](./mahabharata/12-shanti-parva.md). That is a **different tale**; the shared name is not the same character. Do not merge them.
- **The structure is a rising staircase of antiquity** — sage, then owl, then crane, then tortoise — each older than the last, each ignorant of the king, each humbly naming an elder. The suspense is built entirely by the accumulating failure to be remembered. (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)

## III. Akūpāra remembers — the charity that dug the lake

- **The tortoise recognizes the king** — Summoned from the water and asked, the ancient tortoise **Akūpāra** at last **weeps and says he knows Indradyumna well** — indeed the king is famous to him beyond all others. (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)
- **The proof written in the land** — Akūpāra explains the astonishing reason: **this very lake was made by Indradyumna's charity.** At his great sacrifices the king gave away **countless cattle** to the brāhmaṇas; the ceaseless passage and treading of those numberless cows over the ground **scooped out the earth into this broad lake**, in which the tortoise has lived ever since. The king's generosity did not merely earn fame — it **reshaped the physical world**, and that geography is a witness no forgetting can erase. (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)
- **Restoration to heaven** — The moment the oldest being on earth affirms that his deeds are still legible, a **celestial car (vimāna) descends** and a voice declares that Indradyumna's fame yet abides; he is taken back up to the heavenly worlds from which he fell. His merit is renewed not by re-earning it but by the discovery that it never truly lapsed. (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)
  - *Etiological charter [regional]:* the episode is read as the origin-legend of the **Indradyumna lake (Indradyumna-saras / Indradyumna-tīrtha)**, a tīrtha whose bowl-shape "the cows' feet dug" — one of several sacred tanks bearing the king's name across Purāṇic geography.

## IV. What the parable argues

- **Kīrti outlasts the man, but not forever** — The tale sits between two claims it holds in tension: earthly glory is **so long-lived** that it can outlast the sage who saw the deluge, yet it is **still finite**, dependent on a chain of mortal (and near-immortal) memory that can fail. It is neither pure consolation nor pure *vanitas*. (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva) [scholarly]
- **Dāna as the durable deed** — Crucially, what survives is not the king's *name* recited but the **consequence of his giving** — a lake, a changed landscape. The epic's charity-ideology (*go-dāna*, the gift of cattle, as the supreme gift) is dramatized as literally world-shaping. (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva; cf. [Dāna-upākhyānas](./dana-upakhyanas-shveta-and-the-mongoose.md)) [scholarly]
- **Humility of the very old** — Every elder in the chain answers with the same self-effacement — "I do not know him; ask one older" — so that antiquity itself is portrayed as humble, not proud. The tortoise, the eldest, is the one who weeps. (Mahābhārata CE 3.191 / Ganguli Vana CXCIX)
- **Placed against the Mārkaṇḍeya cycle** — Framing this beside Mārkaṇḍeya's own deathlessness lets the epic say something it cannot say plainly: **even the survivor of pralaya is a latecomer** measured against the tortoise, and time's depth exceeds any single witness. (Mahābhārata, Mārkaṇḍeya-samāsyā)

## V. The Skanda Purāṇa retelling (Kaumārika-khaṇḍa)

- **A Purāṇic duplicate at the Mahīsāgara-saṅgama** — The **Skanda Purāṇa, Kaumārika-khaṇḍa** (of the Māheśvara-khaṇḍa), chapters **7–11**, retells the Indradyumna story within the sthala-māhātmya of the **Mahīsāgara-saṅgama / Kumārikā-kṣetra** (the pilgrimage complex at the Mahī-river's sea-mouth). The Purāṇa embeds the Mahābhārata parable into a *tīrtha* frame, tying the fallen king's redemption to a specific sacred confluence. (Skanda Purāṇa, Kaumārika-khaṇḍa 7–11) [late text] [regional]
- **Same skeleton, localized flesh** — The rising ladder of witnesses — sage, owl, crane, tortoise — and Akūpāra's recognition are preserved; the Purāṇic version foregrounds the **kṣetra's** power to restore lapsed merit and folds the tale into its catalogue of who was redeemed at the confluence. As with all such retellings, **catalogue the variant, do not collapse it into the epic**. (Skanda Purāṇa, Kaumārika-khaṇḍa; see [Mahīsāgara-saṅgama / Kumārikā-kṣetra](../06-sacred-geography/mahisagara-sangama-kumarika-kshetra.md)) [late text]
- **Dating [scholarly]** — the Skanda Purāṇa's khaṇḍas are heterogeneous and generally late; the Kaumārika-khaṇḍa's redaction is conventionally placed **c. 700–1200 CE**, centuries after the epic's Vana Parva — a Purāṇic re-use of an older epic *upākhyāna*, not an independent witness.

## VI. The "oldest animals" motif and the name Akūpāra

- **A pan-folkloric type** — The device of a quest passed from animal to ever-older animal — culminating in the eldest, who alone holds the needed knowledge — is a widespread narrative type (the **"oldest animals"** motif; cf. the eldest animals of *Culhwch ac Olwen* in Welsh tradition, ATU 726). The Indradyumna episode is Sanskrit literature's classic instance and a likely conduit for the motif in later Indian story-collections. (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva) [scholarly]
- **Why the tortoise is eldest** — The tortoise's primacy is not arbitrary. **Akūpāra** ("unbounded, boundless") is also a standing epithet of the **cosmic world-tortoise** that bears the earth (allied to the Kūrma imagery of the [Samudra Manthana](./samudra-manthana.md)). Making the *oldest terrestrial being* a tortoise quietly invokes the cosmological tortoise on whose back the world rests — antiquity and world-support fused in one animal. [scholarly]
- **The owl and crane as longevity emblems** — The owl (nocturnal, long-lived in folk belief) and the crane/heron (a byword for patient age and, ironically, false piety in the fable tradition) are conventional emblems of great age, giving the ladder its intuitive plausibility to the audience. [folk]

## VII. Disambiguation — which Indradyumna?

- **This Indradyumna is *not* the Jagannātha king** — The corpus carries several kings named Indradyumna, and they must be kept apart:
  - *The fallen sacrificer of the "oldest beings" tale (this file):* a *rājarṣi* cast from heaven for want of a rememberer; his monument is the cattle-dug **Indradyumna lake**. His story is about **time, memory, and dāna** — no Viṣṇu-image, no Puri. (Mahābhārata 3.191; Skanda P., Kaumārika-khaṇḍa 7–11)
  - *The Mālava/Avanti king who founds Jagannātha Purī:* **Indradyumna** who seeks the hidden **Nīla Mādhava**, receives the floating **dāru-brahma** log, and installs the unfinished wooden images at Puruṣottama-kṣetra — a Vaiṣṇava sthala-legend. See [Jagannātha Purī / Puruṣottama-kṣetra](../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md) and [Kāñcī–Kāverī Jagannātha legend](./kanchi-kaveri-jagannatha-legend.md). (Skanda Purāṇa, Puruṣottama Māhātmya)
  - *The Pāṇḍya king reborn as the elephant Gajendra:* **Indradyumna**, cursed by Agastya for silent inhospitality into an elephant, later liberated as [Gajendra](./gajendra-moksha.md). (Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.4)
- **Same name, three unrelated arcs** — the antiquity-parable, the Purī foundation, and the Gajendra rebirth share only the royal name **Indradyumna** ("Indra's splendour"); none of their plots overlaps. This file treats **only the first**. [scholarly]

## VIII. The two clocks

- **Scriptural/cyclic frame** — The inset is dated only as *purā*, "of old": Indradyumna's reign and sacrifices lie in an unmeasured deep past, and the chain of witnesses reaches into a time before Mārkaṇḍeya himself — beings whose lifespans span **manvantaras and survive pralayas**. The *telling*, however, is fixed: Mārkaṇḍeya narrates it to Yudhiṣṭhira during the exile, at the close of **Dvāpara Yuga**, on the eve of Kurukṣetra (Kali reckoned traditionally from 3102 BCE). (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva)
- **Academic/historical frame** — [scholarly] The Vana Parva's Mārkaṇḍeya-samāsyā is a didactic accretion within the epic's long redaction, conventionally placed **c. 400 BCE – 400 CE**; the Critical Edition (Pune, 1933–66) fixes the episode as **3.191**. The Skanda Purāṇa's Kaumārika-khaṇḍa retelling is a later, regional re-use, **c. 700–1200 CE**. Never collapse the *purā* of the tale into either dating.
