# Keshi the Horse-Demon and the Keshava-Stuti

> *Where this sits: the last wave of demons the tyrant [Kaṃsa](./kamsa-vadha.md) sends against the boy [Kṛṣṇa](./krishna-lila.md) in Vraja — the bull-demon **Ariṣṭa** and the giant horse-demon **Keśin** — and the etiology of the name **Keśava**, sealed by **Nārada's** prophetic hymn after Keśin's death. Set in the closing [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md) of our 28th Mahāyuga.*
>

This file is a dedicated deep-dive on the **two paired demons that close the Vraja cycle** — the bull-demon **Ariṣṭāsura** (Bhāgavata X.36) and the horse-demon **Keśin** (Bhāgavata X.37) — and on the **Keśava-stuti**, the prophecy [Nārada](./narada.md) speaks over Krishna once Keśin is dead, previewing the hero's whole future career. The slaying of Keśin is the named occasion for one of Krishna's most-used epithets, **Keśava**, "the slayer of Keśin." The principal source is the **[Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/bhagavata-purana-skandha-map.md) X.36–37**, with the **Harivaṃśa** (Viṣṇu Parva) and **Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Book 5** as parallels. For the wider boyhood arc see [Kṛṣṇa Bālya-līlā](./krishna-balya-lila.md); for the catalog of all Vraja demons see [Vraja Boyhood Demons](./vraja-boyhood-demons.md).

---

## The Frame: Kaṃsa's Last Wave

- **The order from Mathurā** — Forewarned that the boy reared in Vraja is the foretold eighth child who will kill him, Kaṃsa dispatches a final relay of *asuras* to Vṛndāvana after the earlier waves (Pūtanā, Tṛṇāvarta, the elephant, the calf, the heron, etc.) have all failed (Bhāgavata X.36; Harivaṃśa; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5). These last two — **Ariṣṭa** and **Keśin** — immediately precede the Mathurā summons.
- **Position in the cycle** — In the Bhāgavata the pair stand at the very seam between the pastoral *līlā* and the martial career: X.37 closes with Nārada's prophecy and Kaṃsa's resolve to lure the brothers to the arena (see [Kaṃsa-vadha](./kamsa-vadha.md)). They are the *bridge* demons. [textual ordering]

## Ariṣṭāsura — the Bull-Demon (Bhāgavata X.36)

- **The charging bull** — Kaṃsa sends **Ariṣṭa** (Ariṣṭāsura), an *asura* in the form of a colossal **bull** (*vṛṣabha*), who rampages into the cowherd settlement, tearing up the earth with his hooves and horns, his hump shaking, terrifying the *gopas* and cattle and causing cows to miscarry from fear (Bhāgavata X.36; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5; Harivaṃśa).
- **The slaying** — Krishna challenges him, seizes the charging bull by the horns, **hurls him back eighteen steps**, and when Ariṣṭa charges again, grips him, throws him down, and wrings his neck — pressing and twisting until the demon vomits blood and dies, "like a wet cloth wrung out" (Bhāgavata X.36; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5).
- **Why it matters** — The death of the bull-demon is the *trigger* for Nārada's visit: in the Bhāgavata, Nārada comes to Kaṃsa directly after Ariṣṭa's fall to reveal the secret of Krishna's birth, which sets the Keśin mission in motion (Bhāgavata X.36).
  - *Variant (regional, Braj):* local tradition fixes Ariṣṭa's death at **Arīṭ / Ariṣṭavana** (modern Radha-kund / Arita-grama near Govardhana); the killing of the bull-demon is given as the etiological reason Krishna must later **bathe to purge the sin of "cow-slaughter"** (gohatyā), which in turn produces the holy **Rādhā-kuṇḍa and Śyāma-kuṇḍa** ponds. [regional] [late text]

## Keśin — the Horse-Demon (Bhāgavata X.37)

- **The monstrous horse** — At Kaṃsa's command **Keśin** (Keśī), an *asura* in the shape of a gigantic **horse** (*hayarūpa*), descends on Vṛndāvana — "swift as the mind," his mane streaming (the *keśa*, "mane/hair," that names him), neighing so that the worlds tremble, tearing clouds apart and scattering the *gopas* (Bhāgavata X.37; Harivaṃśa; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5).
- **Krishna's challenge** — Krishna alone stands his ground and calls the demon out; Keśin rushes him with open jaws and lashing forelegs (Bhāgavata X.37).
- **The arm down the throat** — As Keśin opens his mouth to devour him, Krishna **thrusts his left arm into the horse's gaping throat**; the arm swells like white-hot iron, choking the demon; Keśin's teeth fall out, he sweats and writhes, his eyes roll, and he voids his bowels as Krishna's arm **bursts the demon from within**, splitting him in two (Bhāgavata X.37; Viṣṇu Purāṇa 5; Harivaṃśa). This — the **arm rammed down the horse-demon's throat** — is the defining image of the episode.
- **Iconography** — Krishna grappling a rearing horse with one arm down its mouth is a recognizable sculptural and miniature subject (often labeled *Keśi-mardana*), the more so because the demon gives the god a name. [scholarly]

## The Name Keśava

- **The naming** — In Vaiṣṇava etymology one of the standard glosses of **Keśava** is *"Keśinaṃ avadhīt iti Keśavaḥ"* — "he who slew Keśin." The slaying of the horse-demon is thus the named occasion for one of Krishna's most-recited epithets (Bhāgavata X.37; and the *nāma*-tradition broadly).
  - *Variant (alternate etymologies):* the same name is independently derived elsewhere as **"he of beautiful (or long, flowing) hair"** (*keśa* = hair) — the Viṣṇu-Sahasranāma commentaries (Śaṅkara) gloss *Keśava* as the lord of fine locks, or as **ka + a + īśa**, lord of Brahmā (*ka*) and Viṣṇu/Śiva (*a, īśa*). The Keśin-slayer reading is the *narrative* etiology; the hair-reading is the older lexical one. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Earlier in the avatāra line** — The epithet attaches to Viṣṇu/Krishna across texts, but the Vraja narrative makes Keśin's death its *story-cause*; the name is invoked through the Gītā and the Mahābhārata (Arjuna repeatedly addresses Krishna as **Keśava**).

## Nārada's Keśava-Stuti — the Prophecy (Bhāgavata X.37)

After Keśin falls, the sage **Nārada** appears, unseen by others, and addresses Krishna with a hymn of praise that doubles as a **table of contents of the rest of his life** — naming deeds not yet done (Bhāgavata X.37):

- **Salutation to the Lord hidden as a cowherd** — Nārada hails Krishna as the Supreme Self veiled in the play of a *gopa*, having just slain Keśin (Bhāgavata X.37).
- **The prophecy of Kaṃsa-vadha** — Nārada foretells that within days Krishna will go to Mathurā, kill the wrestlers Cāṇūra and Muṣṭika, the elephant **Kuvalayāpīḍa**, and **Kaṃsa himself**, restoring Ugrasena (Bhāgavata X.37; cf. [Kaṃsa-vadha](./kamsa-vadha.md)).
- **The Mathurā and Dvārakā deeds** — He previews the killing of **Kālayavana**, the founding of **Dvārakā** in the sea, the slaying of **Jarāsandha** and **Śiśupāla** and **Naraka**, the lifting of the Pārijāta, and the marriages — the dynastic career to come (Bhāgavata X.37). [The compression of so many future deeds into one hymn is the Bhāgavata's signal that the *līlā* now turns to history.]
- **The Kurukṣetra role** — Nārada foresees Krishna as the charioteer and counselor who will **unburden the earth of its warrior-kings** through the great war — the avatāra's stated purpose (*bhāra-haraṇa*) (Bhāgavata X.37).
- **A demon as devotee's instrument** — Nārada's hymn frames even the demons' assaults as serving the divine plan; he then departs, and Kaṃsa's plot moves to the arena (Bhāgavata X.37).
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa / Viṣṇu Purāṇa):* the **older texts narrate Keśin's death but lack the developed Nārada Keśava-stuti**; the prophetic hymn previewing the whole future career is the Bhāgavata's devotional and structural overlay, used to knit the Vraja and Mathurā books together. [scholarly]

## Two Clocks

- **Scriptural time:** Ariṣṭa, Keśin and Nārada's prophecy fall **late in [Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md)** of the current 28th Mahāyuga, at the close of Krishna's Vṛndāvana boyhood, immediately before he departs for Mathurā (traditionally a generation before the onset of Kali Yuga, **3102 BCE**).
- **Text-critical time:** the **oldest connected form is the Harivaṃśa** (core c. 1st–2nd c. BCE, growing into the early CE centuries), restated in the **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** (~5th c. CE), and most elaborated — with Nārada's prophetic *stuti* and the Ariṣṭa→Nārada→Keśin sequencing — in the **Bhāgavata** (~6th–10th c. CE). The skeleton — bull-demon wrung, horse-demon split by the arm, the name Keśava — is constant across all three; the prophecy is the Bhāgavata's addition. [scholarly] (See [Harivaṃśa](./harivamsha.md).)
