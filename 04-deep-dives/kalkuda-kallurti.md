# Kalkuda and Kallurti — the mutilated sculptor of the Karkala Bahubali and his avenging sister

> Parent: [04 · Deep Dives](README.md) → a **Tuḷu paddana** of the coastal daiva-cult: the deified master-sculptor **Kalkuḍa** and his fierce sister **Kallurṭi**.
> Cosmic address: **outside the Sanskritic yuga-clock**. This is a [regional] hero-cult of **Tuḷu Nāḍu** (Dakṣiṇa Kannaḍa–Uḍupi), whose origin-paddana is anchored not to a yuga but to a **datable historical monument** — the Kārkaḷa Gommaṭeśvara consecrated **[scholarly] 1432 CE**. Two clocks meet here: a documented Jain colossus of Kali-Yuga history, and a timeless daiva propitiated at the all-night Bhūta-Kōla.
> Kindred nodes: [Panjurli & Guliga — the Tuḷu daivas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/panjurli-guliga-tulu-daivas.md) · [Tuḷu paddanas & the Bhūta cult](../90-literature-corpus/regional-and-oral-epics.md) · [Bāhubali / Gommaṭa](rishabha-bharata-jada.md).

Among the **daivas (bhūtas)** of Tuḷu Nāḍu — the deified spirits answered to through the possession-rite of the **Bhūta-Kōla** — the pair **Kalkuḍa** and **Kallurṭi** are unique in being tied to a monument that still stands and can be dated. Kalkuḍa is the **maimed master-sculptor** who is said to have raised the towering monolith of **Bāhubali (Gommaṭeśvara)** at **Kārkaḷa**, and who was crippled by his royal patron precisely so that his hands could never repeat the feat elsewhere. His sister **Kallurṭi** avenges the atrocity — burning the palace and bringing ruin on the king's line — and brother and sister die into godhood, worshipped ever after as a **paired daiva**: the wronged artisan and the wrathful woman who answered for him. The paddana is a folk charter about the **cost of genius under a jealous patron**, and it doubles as the oral memory of the Karkala–Venur statue rivalry. Because it names a king, a place, and a dated statue, it sits at the exact seam where **oral tradition meets documented history**. **[folk] [oral tradition] [regional]**

## The historical armature — the Kārkaḷa and Vēṇūr Bāhubalis

The paddana hangs on real monuments and a real dynasty; the ballad's power comes from grafting a folk atrocity onto verifiable stone.

- **The Kārkaḷa Gommaṭeśvara** — a single-block granite monolith of the Jain saint **Bāhubali** (Gommaṭa), c. **41–42 ft (≈12.7 m)** tall, standing in the *kāyotsarga* posture on **Gommaṭa-beṭṭa** hill at Kārkaḷa. It was consecrated in **[scholarly] 1432 CE** by **Vīra Pāṇḍya** (Vīra Pāṇḍya Bhairarasa Woḍeyar) of the **Sāntara / Bhairava dynasty** of Kārkaḷa. It is the **second-largest** of the coast's Bāhubali monoliths, after the c. 57-ft image at **Śravaṇabeḷagoḷa** (981 CE). (Epigraphic/temple record.) **[scholarly]**
- **The Vēṇūr (Yēṇūr) Bāhubali** — a smaller monolith, c. **35 ft**, raised at **Vēṇūr** on the Phalguṇī river by the **Ajila** chieftain **Tiṃmaṇṇa Ajila** in **[scholarly] 1604 CE**. In the paddana it becomes the *second* statue that the crippled Kalkuḍa is compelled to attempt — the very duplication his maiming was meant to prevent. (Epigraphic record; Kalkuḍa paddana.) **[scholarly] [folk]**
- **The subject of the image** — **Bāhubali**, in Jain sacred history the second son of the first *tīrthaṅkara* **Ṛṣabha (Ādinātha)**, who renounced after a duel with his brother **Bharata** and attained *kevala-jñāna* standing in year-long meditation (whence the vines climbing his legs in every icon). See [Ṛṣabha–Bharata–Bāhubali](rishabha-bharata-jada.md). The paddana treats the statue purely as the sculptor's masterwork, not as an object of the paddana's own devotion — a **Jain** monument narrated by a **daiva-cult**. **[regional]**
- **The craft-community frame** — Kalkuḍa is figured as a **Viśvakarma-caste sculptor-smith** (*śilpi / kammāra* lineage), and he is to this day a **patron-daiva of artisans** — smiths, carpenters, sculptors — of the coast. The Sanskritic patron of the crafts is the deva [Viśvakarma / Tvaṣṭṛ](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishvakarma-and-tvashtar.md); Kalkuḍa is his regional, historicised shadow. **[regional]**

## The paddana — Kalkuḍa, the maimed maker

*The sung ballad (paddana) exists in several Tuḷu recensions, collected by scholars such as B. A. Viveka Rai and Peter J. Claus; the core is stable, the details vary.*

- **The commission** — the king **Vīra Pāṇḍya** of Kārkaḷa summons the greatest living sculptor to raise a colossus of Gommaṭa greater than any before it. **Kalkuḍa** (in some tellings with kin — father or brother-artisans) undertakes the monolith, quarrying, carving, and finally raising the towering Bāhubali on the hill. (Kalkuḍa paddana.) **[folk] [oral tradition]**
- **The perfection and the flaw** — the statue is triumphantly set up; the king rewards the sculptor. But in the characteristic sting of the tale, the work is found to have a **single blemish** — variously a fault in a toe, a nail, or the proportions — and this becomes the pretext, or the goad, for the king's cruelty.
  - *Variant (Kalkuḍa paddana):* there is **no flaw at all** — the king mutilates the flawless sculptor purely to guarantee **exclusivity**, so that no rival court can ever obtain an equal Gommaṭa. [variant] **[folk]**
- **The maiming** — to ensure Kalkuḍa can **never duplicate the masterpiece for another patron**, Vīra Pāṇḍya has him **mutilated** — his **right hand** severed (and in fuller tellings a **leg** cut and an **eye** put out). The precise limbs differ by recension; the constant is that the **hands that made the god are destroyed by the man who commissioned it**. (Kalkuḍa paddana.) **[folk] [oral tradition]**
- **The forced second statue** — crippled and embittered, Kalkuḍa is nonetheless later pressed (by the same or a rival house) to raise **another** Bāhubali — the paddana's echo of the historical **Vēṇūr** image. Working maimed, he cannot equal the first; the second colossus comes out **smaller / flawed**, sealing his ruin and his rage. In some versions he refuses, or dies before completion, or curses the stone. [variant] **[folk] [oral tradition]**
- **The vow of vengeance** — broken in body, Kalkuḍa turns from maker to avenger. Unable to strike the king with a smith's arm he no longer has, he calls on his **sister** — and the wrath passes to her. His own death (by grief, wound, or self-willed end) transfigures him into a **daiva**. (Kalkuḍa paddana.) **[folk] [oral tradition]**

## Kallurṭi — the avenging sister

*Kallurṭi (Kallurṭi, Kallurti) — the fierce female daiva; in the paddana Kalkuḍa's sister, in wider cult one of the great wrathful mother-daivas of Tuḷu Nāḍu.*

- **The sister's rising** — hearing of her brother's mutilation, **Kallurṭi** takes on a terrible aspect and carries the vengeance he cannot. She confronts the court of Kārkaḷa (and/or the patrons of the second statue) and unleashes ruin on the royal house. (Kalkuḍa–Kallurṭi paddana.) **[folk] [oral tradition]**
- **The burning of the palace** — the signature act: Kallurṭi **sets fire to the king's palace** (in tellings, the whole citadel), consuming the line that maimed her brother — the retributive blaze that is the paddana's climax and the source of her fierce, fire-linked iconography. (Kalkuḍa–Kallurṭi paddana.) **[folk] [oral tradition]**
- **Death into godhood** — brother and sister both pass out of human life into the order of daivas, and are thereafter **installed and propitiated together** — the wronged sculptor and the woman who avenged him, a **paired daiva** of grievance answered. (Tuḷu Bhūta-Kōla tradition.) **[regional]**
- **Kallurṭi beyond the paddana** — in the wider cult **Kallurṭi is not only Kalkuḍa's sister** but a major independent wrathful female daiva, frequently paired in shrine and *nēma* with other male daivas (she recurs alongside guardian-daivas much as [Guliga](../08-beings-and-bestiary/panjurli-guliga-tulu-daivas.md) pairs with Panjurli). Her name is glossed from Tuḷu **kallu** ("stone / rock"), fitting both a sculptor's medium and a stone-born fierce goddess. Whether the Kalkuḍa-legend *created* Kallurṭi or *annexed* an older stone-goddess to the sculptor's story is **[disputed]**. **[folk] [regional]**

## Deification — the paired daiva and the Kōla

- **The Kalkuḍa–Kallurṭi nēma** — the pair are worshipped through the standard Tuḷu **Bhūta-Kōla / daiva-nēma**, the overnight possession-rite in which a costumed medium of the hereditary performer communities (Nalike, Parava, Pambada) is entered by the daiva, dances its story and wrath, and delivers binding **oracle (*nuḍi*)** to the village. The **paddana** is sung to summon and legitimate the possession. (Bhūta-Kōla liturgy; Claus.) **[regional] [oral tradition]**
- **Kalkuḍa, daiva of the artisans** — Kalkuḍa is specifically the tutelary of the **craft communities** — Viśvakarma smiths, sculptors, carpenters — invoked for skill in the workshop and for **justice against exploitation by patrons**, the very wrong the paddana enshrines. His shrines cluster around Kārkaḷa, Mūḍabidri, and the old Sāntara–Ajila country. **[regional]**
- **A charter of grievance** — like all paddanas, the tale functions as a **social charter**: it dignifies the sculptor-caste by seating its archetype among the gods, and it encodes a durable protest — the maker's right not to be crippled by the patron who profits from his genius. The daiva's continuing power is the memory of an **injustice that the human courts never redressed**. **[scholarly] [folk]**
- **Relation to the other coastal cults** — Kalkuḍa–Kallurṭi belong to the same order as Tuḷu Nāḍu's [Panjurli & Guliga](../08-beings-and-bestiary/panjurli-guliga-tulu-daivas.md) and the hero-cults of **Siri** and **Koṭi-Cennaya**, and are cousins to the Malayāḷi **Teyyam** deities of northern Kerala ([Teyyam & tōṟṟam myths](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/theyyam-deities-and-thottam-myths.md); [Kāṭivanūr Vīran](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/kathivanur-veeran.md)) — regional possession-cults in which **deified dead heroes and wronged persons** are made present in a dancing medium. **[regional]**

## Variants, name-collisions, and the two clocks

- *Variant — the king's identity:* the tyrant is most often named **Vīra Pāṇḍya** of Kārkaḷa, but recensions also cast the offending patron as a later **Ajila** lord of Vēṇūr (matching the 1604 statue), or leave him a generic "rāja." The paddana **conflates two building-events a century and a half apart** (Kārkaḷa 1432, Vēṇūr 1604) into one biography — a telescoping typical of oral memory. [variant] **[folk]**
- *Variant — the sculptor's kin:* some versions make the maimed maker not Kalkuḍa alone but a **father-and-son** or **brother** pair of sculptors, with Kalkuḍa as one member; the mutilation and the avenging sister remain constant. [variant] **[oral tradition]**
- **Name-collision — "Kallurṭi":** distinguish (a) **Kallurṭi the sister-daiva** of this paddana, from (b) **Kallurṭi the independent wrathful mother-daiva** worshipped across many Tuḷu shrines with no reference to Kalkuḍa. Tradition treats them as one being wearing two histories; scholarship keeps them analytically apart. [disambiguation] **[disputed]**
- **Name-note — "Kalkuḍa":** the sculptor-daiva is not to be confused with the many unrelated local *śilpi*-legends of maimed artists across India (the "blinded/handless builder" is a wandering folk motif, e.g. the tales around the Tāj and other monuments); the Tuḷu Kalkuḍa is a **specific, place-bound daiva** with an active cult, not a free-floating anecdote. [disambiguation]
- **The two clocks** — the **scriptural/cyclic** frame does not place Kalkuḍa–Kallurṭi on the yuga-spine at all; they belong to a heroic-historical past "in the time of the Kārkaḷa kings." The **academic/historical** frame supplies hard anchors: the **Kārkaḷa Bāhubali of 1432 CE** and the **Vēṇūr Bāhubali of 1604 CE** ([scholarly]), with the paddana itself recorded and studied only from the **19th–20th c.** onward (Viveka Rai, Claus). The cult is thus a rare daiva-legend that is genuinely **Kali-Yuga documented history**, not timeless myth. **[scholarly]**
