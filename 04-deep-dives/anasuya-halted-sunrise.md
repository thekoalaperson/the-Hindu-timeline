# Anasūyā and the halted sunrise — the pativratā's night and the Dattātreya boon

> Cosmic address: a *Vaivasvata-Manvantara* tale of the saptarṣi household, set in the deep dynastic past of the present **[7th — Vaivasvata — Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)**. This is the **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa's** distinct origin of the Trimūrti-as-sons boon — earned not by the chastity-test but by rescuing a faithful wife's cursed husband from a halted dawn (Pargiter, Cantos XVI–XVII).
> ⬆ Parent index: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Master index: [README](../README.md) · Canonical household: [Atri and Anasūyā](atri-anasuya.md) · [Dattātreya](dattatreya.md)

The **Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa** grounds the birth of **[Dattātreya](dattatreya.md)** in a story that most other Purāṇas do *not* tell: a chain of two faithful wives. A leprous, cruel brahmin named **Kauśika** is doomed by the curse of an impaled sage to die at sunrise; his devoted wife, refusing to lose him, **commands the sun never to rise** — and the world's clock stops. The gods, their sacrifices and seasons collapsing, are helpless until **Anasūyā**, Atri's wife, persuades the pativratā to relent, restores the dawn, and revives the dead husband young and whole. For this the gods grant Anasūyā a boon, and she asks that **Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva be born as her sons** — the origin, in this text, of **Soma, Dattātreya, and Durvāsas**. It is a *pativratā-māhātmya* (a hymn to the power of the faithful wife) that doubles as the charter-myth of the triple-deity avatar.

---

## Where it sits — the two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — The episode belongs to the primordial saptarṣi stratum of the present **[Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)**, when Atri (a mind-born son of Brahmā and a [Saptarṣi](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/saptarishi.md) of this age) and Anasūyā already dwelt in their hermitage — effectively the deep-past root of the [Lunar dynasty](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) via the son Soma. On the cosmic clock this is treated as timeless/early-yuga charter material, not a dated reign.
- **Academic / historical frame** — The *Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* is among the **oldest Purāṇas**; its narrative core (the frame of the wise Birds and the *pativratā-māhātmya*, distinct from the later-inserted *Devī-Māhātmya*) is dated **[scholarly] c. 3rd–6th century CE**, with F. E. Pargiter's English translation published **1904**. The two clocks are kept apart: the tale claims Manvantara-antiquity; the text that preserves it is mid-first-millennium CE.

---

## The narrative frame (Pargiter, Cantos XVI–XVII)

- **Told inside a father–son dialogue on yoga** — In the *Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* the story is spoken by the sage-son **Jaḍa** ("the dullard," secretly wise) to his father, who has asked to be instructed; Jaḍa opens his teaching on the might of dharma with the Anasūyā legend. It thus sits within the Purāṇa's larger *pativratā-māhātmya* — its exaltation of the faithful wife (*pativratā*) as a force able to bend nature itself. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI, Pargiter tr.)
- **A "greatness of the faithful wife" charter** — The whole point is etiological and moral: to show that *satya-saṃkalpa* born of wifely fidelity can override the sun's own course, and that even the Trimūrti must work *through* such a woman (Anasūyā) to undo what another such woman (Kauśika's wife) has done. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)

## The tale — the halted sunrise

- **Kauśika, the leprous brahmin of Pratiṣṭhāna** — In the city of **Pratiṣṭhāna** lives a brahmin **Kauśika**, afflicted with **leprosy**, cruel and imperious of temper, yet served with flawless devotion by his wife, who worships him as a god despite his disease and abuse. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)
  - *The wife's name (variant):* Pargiter's text leaves her **unnamed**, calling her only "the faithful wife." Later retellings and secondary sources name her **Śāṇḍilī** (also **Śīlavatī** or **Sumatī**). [regional] Do not confuse her with **Anasūyā**, who enters only as rescuer.
- **The demand for the courtesan** — Consumed by desire, Kauśika orders his wife to carry him at night to the house of a **courtesan (prostitute)** he lusts after. Obedient, she bears her diseased husband **on her shoulder** through the dark forest road. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)
- **The collision with the impaled sage** — On the road lies the sage **Māṇḍavya**, **impaled on a stake (śūla)** as punishment for a crime he did not commit, kept alive by his yogic power. In the dark Kauśika's foot **strikes / pushes the suffering ascetic aside**. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)
  - *Cross-tradition identification:* This impaled sage is standardly identified with **Aṇī-Māṇḍavya**, the ascetic wrongfully impaled for theft who, in the *Mahābhārata*, curses **Dharma** to be born a śūdra — i.e. as **[Vidura](characters/vidura.md)** (Ādi Parva). [scholarly] The Purāṇa gives only "Māṇḍavya"; the equation of the two impaled-Māṇḍavyas is a reader's inference, not stated in the text.
- **The curse of death at sunrise** — Māṇḍavya, in agony, curses the man who spurned him: *"he shall be bereft of life… at the sight of the sun indeed he shall perish"* — Kauśika is doomed to **die the instant the sun rises**. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI, Pargiter)
- **The wife stops the dawn** — Hearing the curse, the faithful wife utters her own vow against the sun: *"the sun verily shall not arise!"* By the accumulated power of her fidelity the decree holds — **the sun fails to rise**, and there is **continual night** across the worlds. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)

## Cosmic crisis and Anasūyā's intervention

- **The world-clock stops** — With no sunrise, night runs on "for many lengths of day"; the round of **sacrifices, oblations, seasons and rites** — all keyed to solar time — collapses, and gods and men alike are thrown into disorder. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)
- **The gods go first to Brahmā** — The terrified gods approach **Brahmā**, who cannot simply overrule the pativratā: *"the might of a faithful wife may not be lost."* He directs them instead to **propitiate Atri's wife Anasūyā, rich in austerities**, as the only one who can resolve it without wronging the first wife. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)
- **Anasūyā honours, not overpowers** — Anasūyā goes to the grieving wife and does not command her; she **honours her fidelity** and offers a bargain: *"while honouring that good lady, I will liberate the day"* — she will let the sun rise, and by her own power **restore Kauśika to life** so the wife loses nothing. The wife consents. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)
- **The dawn released at the tenth midnight** — Anasūyā **summons the sun**, "raising up the *arghya* oblation," at **midnight on the tenth night**; the sun rises, and by the curse Kauśika **dies** — whereupon Anasūyā's blessing raises him up again, **free of leprosy, young and whole**. Fidelity's decree is satisfied and the curse spent. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)

## The boon — the Trimūrti as her sons

- **The gods offer a boon** — Overjoyed that time is restored, the gods (with the Trimūrti) offer Anasūyā any boon for her deed. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)
- **Anasūyā's request** — She asks: *"Let Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva become sons to me, and let me, in company with my husband, attain religious devotion, to the end that I may be delivered from affliction."* The gods grant it and depart, "duly honouring the ascetic lady." (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI, Pargiter)
- **The three sons born (Canto XVII)** — In the next canto three sons are born to Anasūyā, "being portions of Brahmā, Śiva and Viṣṇu," apportioned by the three **guṇas**:
  - **Soma / Candra** — the **Brahmā-portion**, who rises as the Moon and becomes the progenitor of the [Lunar dynasty](soma-tara-budha.md). (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVII)
  - **Dattātreya** — the **Viṣṇu-portion**, "in whom **goodness (sattva)** predominated," the brāhman who remains as the archetypal avadhūta and teacher. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVII) — see [Dattātreya](dattatreya.md).
  - **Durvāsas** — the **Śiva-portion**, "in whom **darkness (tamas)** predominated," who departs to wander as the wrathful, curse-wielding ascetic. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVII) — see [Ambarīṣa–Durvāsā](ambarisha-durvasa.md).
- **Dattātreya's lake-test coda** — The same canto immediately gives Dattātreya's own testing of disciples: he **lives immersed in a lake** for a hundred divine years, then emerges to consort with women and **drink liquor**, yet his ascetics never forsake him — the avadhūta beyond convention. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVII)

## Two rival origins of the Datta boon (disambiguation)

The tradition preserves **two entirely different earnings** of the same Trimūrti-as-sons boon. They must not be merged — the corpus catalogs both:

- **The halted-sunrise version (this file) — Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa XVI–XVII.** The boon is won by **Anasūyā rescuing another woman's cursed husband** and restoring the sun. There is **no chastity-test, no naked-alms demand, no reduction of the gods to infants**. Anasūyā's virtue is proved by her *power over nature and her honouring of a fellow pativratā*, not by resisting seduction. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI–XVII) [scholarly]
- **The nagna-bhikṣā / chastity-test version — Bhāgavata, Padma, and sthala-māhātmyas.** Provoked (usually by **Nārada**) the three goddesses **Sarasvatī, Lakṣmī, Pārvatī** send their husbands to break Anasūyā's vow; the Trimūrti, disguised as mendicants, demand to be served food **naked (nagna)**; Anasūyā turns them into **crying infants**, serves them, and by the goddesses' plea they are reborn as her sons. (Bhāgavata 4.1.15–33; Mārkaṇḍeya *Anasūyā-cycle* as later elaborated; Padma Purāṇa) — this is the version carried in **[atri-anasuya.md](atri-anasuya.md)** and **[dattatreya.md](dattatreya.md)**.
- **How they relate** — Both are *pativratya* charter-myths reaching the same result (Soma/Datta/Durvāsas born of Anasūyā), and later Datta-Sampradāya tradition freely blends them or fuses the three into **one child, Dattātreya**, with three heads and six arms. But the *engine* differs: **compassion-and-cosmic-power** (Mārkaṇḍeya) versus **the tested-and-unbroken vow** (Bhāgavata/Padma). [disputed] The apportionment of deity-to-son is also handled differently, though both make **Dattātreya the Viṣṇu-portion**. (comparative Purāṇic)

## Motif parallels

- **The wife who stops the sun** — Anasūyā's cousin-figure is the first wife herself, whose word freezes the dawn: the same *satya-saṃkalpa* motif by which [Sāvitrī](savitri-satyavan.md) wins back Satyavān from Yama and by which chaste women in the Purāṇic *pativratā* corpus bend cosmic law. (comparative)
- **Halting light, damming rivers** — The Purāṇic Anasūyā cycle elsewhere credits her with **making a barren tract bloom** or **halting a river's flow** in drought by her vow; the sunrise-arrest here is the most cosmic member of that motif-family. [regional] — see [Atri and Anasūyā](atri-anasuya.md).
- **The impaled just man** — Māṇḍavya on the stake is the same "wrongly punished ascetic whose curse reshapes a lineage" motif that, via **Aṇī-Māṇḍavya**, seeds the birth of [Vidura](characters/vidura.md) in the *Mahābhārata*. [scholarly]
- **A pativratā rescuing a pativratā** — Uniquely, this tale sets one faithful wife's vow *against* the world's need and resolves it not by breaking her but by a **second faithful wife** who out-gives her — fidelity answered by fidelity, not by force. (*Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa* XVI)
