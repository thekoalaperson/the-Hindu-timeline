# Agastya — Sage of the South

> **Where this sits:** A deep-dive into **Agastya (Agasti)** — pot-born ṛṣi, Ṛgvedic seer, ocean-drinker, mountain-stiller, asura-digester, and the single figure who in Hindu memory **carries Vedic / Sanskritic civilization across the Vindhyas into the South**, where Tamil tradition adopts him as the father of its grammar. His episodes thread the [Ṛgveda](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md), the [Mahābhārata](mahabharata/) (esp. Vana Parva), the [Rāmāyaṇa](ramayana/) (Bāla, Araṇya, Kiṣkindhā, Yuddha & Uttara Kāṇḍas), the Purāṇas, and the [Tamil Sangam corpus](../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md).
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](./) · Cosmic address: Śveta-Varāha Kalpa › 7th ([Vaivasvata](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/)) Manvantara › 28th Mahāyuga, active from the [Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) (Rāma's era) with a Ṛgvedic seer-layer atop and a Tamil/Siddha afterlife reaching historical time.

Agastya is the rare figure who is simultaneously a **Vedic ṛṣi** (composer of Ṛgveda hymns), an **epic actor** (host and arms-giver to Rāma; pilgrimage-tale of the Pāṇḍavas), a **cosmic engineer** (he drinks the ocean and stops the Vindhya), and a **culture-hero of the South** (Tamil grammarian, Siddhar, irrigation-bringer). A traditional etymology reads his name as *aga* ("mountain," the Vindhya) + *√as/asta* ("to throw down / cast / halt") — *"the one who halted the mountain."* [traditional etymology] The epic passages below do not offer this derivation, and the name *Agastya* is already in the Ṛgveda (RV 1.170.3, 7.33.10), long before any Vindhya story is attested. The North remembers him as the carrier of Sanskrit and Vedic rite southward; the South remembers him as the founder of Tamil letters. Catalog all variants below.

> **Citation convention.** Mahābhārata: Ganguli section numbers (vulgate) first, then the BORI Critical Edition (CE) adhyāya.verse from GRETIL. In this stretch of the Vana Parva, Ganguli's section number runs two higher than the CE adhyāya (Ganguli 96 = CE 3.94). Rāmāyaṇa: vulgate / Southern sarga.verse (valmikiramayan.net) first, then the Baroda CE (GRETIL) in parentheses. Where a detail appears only in a CE starred passage (\*), the editors found it in only part of the manuscript tradition and kept it out of the constituted text.

## Dramatis personae & origins

- **Agastya (Agasti)** - a ṛṣi whom some lists count among the **[Saptarṣi](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)**. He is not in the usual seven of the Vaivasvata manvantara (see the Saptarṣi roster on that page). Brahmanical gotra theory sometimes adds him as an **eighth** gotra-founder (*gotrakārin*) beside the seven ([Wikipedia: Gotra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotra)). The standard study is J. Brough, *The Early Brahmanical System of Gotra and Pravara* (1953), not opened for this page. [scholarly] Iconography makes him short, bearded and pot-bellied: the small man who out-powers giants. Epithets: **Maitrāvaruṇi** (son of Mitra & Varuṇa; MBh CE 3.101.13), **Vāruṇi** (CE 3.102.13), **Kumbhayoni / Kumbhasambhava** ("pot-born"; Rām. CE 7.2.1), **Aurvaśeya** ("of Urvaśī," a lexical epithet). In the Ṛgveda he is also **Mānya / Māndārya** (RV 1.165.15). [scholarly identification]
- **Birth from the pot (kumbha)** - the gods **Mitra** and **Varuṇa**, aroused at the sight of the apsarā **Urvaśī**, let their seed fall into a water-jar (kumbha). Agastya, and in most accounts **Vasiṣṭha** too, is born from it. This makes Agastya the pot-brother of **[Vasiṣṭha](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md)**. The layers differ:
  - *Ṛgveda 7.33.10–13 (the oldest layer):* the hymn is about Vasiṣṭha. Verse 10 names Agastya as the one who "brought" Vasiṣṭha to the people (*agastyo yat tvā viśa ājabhāra*). Verse 11 calls Vasiṣṭha *maitrāvaruṇa*, born "from the mind of Urvaśī." Verse 13 says the two gods, at a sacrificial session, poured a common seed into a jar (*kumbhe retaḥ siṣicatuḥ samānam*). From its middle **Māna** rose, and from it, "they say," the seer Vasiṣṭha was born. Agastya is named outright only in v. 10. Reading "Māna" in v. 13 as Agastya is the tradition's interpretation, supported by his RV name *Mānya* (1.165.15). [scholarly]
  - *Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa 56–57 (Shastri numbering):* Rāma tells the full story. Varuṇa, refused by Urvaśī because she had already accepted Mitra, sheds his seed into a vessel made by Brahmā, and Mitra's seed lies in the same vessel. **Agastya is born first**, tells Mitra *"I am not your son,"* and leaves. **Vasiṣṭha** is born after him: he is Brahmā's son, who lost his first body to King **Nimi**'s curse and was told to re-enter the seed of Mitra and Varuṇa. This Nimi–Vasiṣṭha section is **not in the constituted text of the Baroda CE** of the Uttara Kāṇḍa. [late text]
  - *Purāṇic:* the Devī Bhāgavata hails him as "sprung from a water jar" and "son of Mitrāvaruṇa" (DBhP 10.6.7–19). [Purāṇic]
- **Lopāmudrā** - his wife, see §4 below. Their son is **Dṛḍhasyu**, also called **Idhmavāha** ("fuel-bearer"), born reciting the Vedas. (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva, Ganguli 99 / CE 3.97.22–24)
- **His brother** - the Rāmāyaṇa gives Agastya a younger brother with his own hermitage on the road to Agastya's, but never names him (Rām. 3.11.37–72 / CE 3.10). The name **Sudarśana** comes from later tradition. The translator's note at valmikiramayan.net (3.11.72) gives it, but the verse does not. [late tradition]

## 1. Drinking the ocean dry — exposing the Kālakeyas

- **The asura menace** - the **Kālakeya (Kāleya) dānavas** fought under **Vṛtra** in the Kṛta age. When Indra kills Vṛtra with the vajra made from **Dadhīca**'s bones, they flee into the ocean, Varuṇa's abode. There they decide that the world rests on tapas, so they must destroy those who practise it. (Mahābhārata, **Vana Parva**, Tīrtha-yātrā Parva, Ganguli 100–101 / CE 3.98–99)
- **The night killings** - by day they hide in the sea. By night they devour ascetics: **188 brahmins and nine other ascetics at Vasiṣṭha's āśrama**, a hundred at **Cyavana**'s, and twenty air-and-water-fasting brahmacārins at **Bharadvāja**'s. At dawn only bones are found, heaped "like conch shells," and sacrifice ceases across the world. (Ganguli 102 / CE 3.100.1–11)
- **Viṣṇu's counsel** - the gods take refuge with **Nārāyaṇa**. He names the Kālakeyas and explains that they cannot be destroyed while the sea shelters them: *"who but Agastya can dry up the ocean?"* (*agastyena vinā ko hi śakto 'nyo 'rṇavaśoṣaṇe*). With Brahmā's leave the gods go to Agastya's hermitage. They praise him for three past rescues: he cast **Nahuṣa** down from heaven (§8), stopped the **Vindhya** (§2), and protected creatures when the world was covered in darkness. (Ganguli 102–103 / CE 3.100.17–3.101.16)
- **The deed** - Agastya goes to the sea with the gods and sages, while gandharvas, yakṣas, kinnaras, nāgas and men gather to watch. He announces *"for the good of the world I drink the abode of Varuṇa,"* drinks it **in anger while all the worlds look on**, and leaves the great ocean **waterless**. The gods attack the exposed dānavas, who were **already "burnt by the tapas of the sages"** and so are cut down. A remnant **splits the earth and escapes to Pātāla**. (Ganguli 104–105 / CE 3.102.14–3.103.12) The "single draught from the cupped palm" of popular retellings is not in this passage.
- **The water never returned** - the gods ask Agastya to give the water back. He replies: *"That water has been digested by me (jīrṇaṃ tad dhi mayā toyam). Think of some other way to fill the ocean."* (Ganguli 105 / CE 3.103.15–16) The gods and Viṣṇu go to Brahmā. He tells them that after a long time the ocean will regain its natural state through King **Bhagīratha** and his kinsmen (CE 3.104.1–2). Lomaśa then narrates the Sagara–Bhagīratha cycle (Ganguli 106–109 / CE 3.104–108; see [Gaṅgā-avataraṇa](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md)). At the end of that cycle Bhagīratha brings the Gaṅgā to the sea and fills it, and Lomaśa sums up that the Gaṅgā "was brought down to earth for the filling of the ocean" (*pūraṇārthaṃ samudrasya*, Ganguli 109 / CE 3.108.16–18). So in the Mahābhārata, Agastya's feat is the stated reason for the Gaṅgā's descent to refill the sea.
  - *Note (digestion motif):* the stomach as a cosmic furnace recurs. The same "I have digested it" that answers for the ocean also answers for the asura Vātāpi (§3), with the same verb (*jīrṇa*). [interpretive note]

## 2. Humbling the Vindhya mountain

- **The jealous mountain (Mahābhārata)** - the Sun circles golden **Meru** from rising to setting. The **Vindhya** asks the Sun to circle it in the same way. The Sun answers that the path is not his choice but was assigned to him by the one who made this world. In anger the Vindhya **starts to grow**, to block the paths of the **Sun, the Moon and the stars**. It ignores the gods, so they go to Agastya: *"none but you can stop him."* (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva, Ganguli 104 / CE 3.102.1–9)
- **The compact (Mahābhārata)** - Agastya comes to the mountain **with his wife** (*sadāraḥ*) and asks it for a passage, because he has business in the south. He tells it to **wait until he returns**, and after that it may grow as it likes. "And to this day," says Lomaśa, "Vāruṇi has not returned from the southern country." (Ganguli 104 / CE 3.102.10–13) The Mahābhārata does not have the mountain bowing or prostrating. It has only the compact. The gods' hymn earlier puts it in one line: the Vindhya "does not grow, not transgressing your word." (CE 3.101.15)
  - *Rāmāyaṇa:* Rāma, approaching the hermitage, says it belongs to the sage "obeying whose command the Vindhya ceases to grow" (*saṃdeśaṃ pālayaṃs tasya vindhyaśailo na vardhate*). This is a single verse; the episode is not narrated. (Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya 3.11.85 / CE 3.10.83) The translator's note on this verse at valmikiramayan.net, in which the mountain lowers itself so the aged couple can climb over, is commentary, not Vālmīki.
  - *Variant (Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.2–7):* the sage **Nārada** stirs the Vindhya's pride by telling it of Meru's arrogance as the mountain the Sun circles, and the mountain swells to stop the Sun (10.2–3). The gods go to Śiva, then to Viṣṇu (10.4–5). Viṣṇu sends them to **Agastya at Kāśī**, a devotee of the Devī (10.6). Grieved to leave the city, Agastya takes leave of Viśveśvara, Kālabhairava and Sākṣi-Gaṇeśa and sets out with Lopāmudrā. When the Vindhya sees him, it trembles, shrinks and **falls prostrate before him "like a stick" (sāṣṭāṅga)**. Agastya tells it to stay so until he comes back, because he cannot climb its heights. He crosses, visits **Śrīśaila**, and settles at **Malayācala**. The Devī comes to dwell on the Vindhya as **Vindhyavāsinī**. (DBhP 10.7.1–21) [Purāṇic] This is where the popular picture of the bowing mountain and the sage leaving Kāśī comes from. The Skanda Purāṇa's Kāśī-khaṇḍa is said to frame the same departure from Kāśī, but that text was not checked for this page.
- **Why he goes south** - in every version, the errand that stops the mountain is also the errand that **carries him permanently across the Vindhya** into the Deccan and Tamil land. The Mahābhārata's "he has not returned to this day" (CE 3.102.13) is the hinge of his whole southern career.
  - *Variant (motive):* some tellings make the journey itself the purpose. In these, gods crowding north to Śiva's wedding at Kailāsa tip the earth, and the sage is sent south to weigh it down (see §6). [folk / temple legend] No Sanskrit Purāṇic source for this was found for this page.
  - *Variant (name etymology):* tradition explains *Agastya* by this act ("mountain-stiller"). As noted above, the name is older than the story. [traditional etymology]

## 3. Digesting the asura Vātāpi (the Ilvala trick)

> Two primary versions survive, and they differ in frame and ending. The Mahābhārata version is a **wealth-errand that ends with Ilvala paying up**. The Rāmāyaṇa version is a **demon-slaying in which Ilvala is burnt to death**. They are kept apart below.

- **The brothers' scheme (Mahābhārata)** - in the city of **Maṇimatī** lives the daitya **Ilvala**, with his younger brother **Vātāpi**, "of Prahlāda's line." Ilvala once asked a brahmin for "a son equal to Indra," was refused, and was enraged. (The vulgate adds that "from that day" he became a killer of brahmins, starred \*0479.) Anyone Ilvala calls by name comes back to life, even from Yama's realm. So he cooks Vātāpi as a meat dish and feeds him to a brahmin guest, then calls him. Vātāpi bursts **laughing out of the guest's side**. (Ganguli 96 / CE 3.94.4–10) The CE text uses both animals: Ilvala makes Vātāpi a **goat** (*chāgaṃ kṛtvā*, CE 3.94.8), and when the kings later see the dish he is a **ram** (*meṣabhūtaṃ mahāsuram*, CE 3.97.3). Ganguli's translation has "ram" throughout, and the Rāmāyaṇa says ram (*meṣa*).
- **The wealth-errand (Mahābhārata)** - Agastya needs wealth to meet Lopāmudrā's condition (§4). He visits three kings in turn: **Śrutarvan**, **Bradhnaśva**, and the Ikṣvāku **Trasadasyu**, son of Purukutsa. Each king shows that his income only just equals his expenditure, and Agastya will take nothing that would harm others. So the kings take him to the rich dānava **Ilvala**. (Ganguli 98 / CE 3.96)
- **The meal (Mahābhārata)** - the kings despair when they see the cooked Vātāpi. Agastya tells them, *"Do not grieve, I will eat the great asura,"* and eats **all of it**. Ilvala calls his brother, and **only wind comes out of the sage**. Ilvala sees that Vātāpi has been digested and is dejected. (CE 3.97.4–7, constituted text) The vulgate text that Ganguli translates adds a scene. The wind roars "like a thundercloud." Ilvala keeps calling *"Come out, Vātāpi!"* Agastya laughs: *"How can he come out? I have already digested that great asura"* (CE starred \*0490). A further passage, in only some manuscripts, has Agastya strike his belly three times and say *"eṣa jīrṇo 'si vātāpe mayā lokasya śāntaye"* ("you are digested, Vātāpi, by me, for the peace of the world"). (CE starred \*0489) The popular formula **"Vātāpi jīryatām"** / *"jīrṇam jīrṇam vātāpi jīrṇam"* is not found in either constituted text in that form. It is a later traditional saying. The translator's note at valmikiramayan.net (3.11.64) records that mothers repeat it over a fed child. [folk / later formula]
- **Ilvala pays (Mahābhārata)** - Ilvala, with folded hands, asks what they want. Ilvala says he will give if the sage can say what Ilvala means to give, and Agastya tells him what he had already resolved to give: to each king **ten thousand cows and as much gold**, and to Agastya **twice that, a golden chariot, and two horses swift as thought**. Ilvala checks, finds that the chariot really is gold, and pays. The horses (**Virāva and Surāva** in Ganguli; **Vivāja and Suvāja** in CE) carry everything to Agastya's hermitage in the blink of an eye. In the constituted text and in Ganguli, **Ilvala is not killed**. (Ganguli 99 / CE 3.97.8–15) A starred passage found in only some manuscripts adds that Ilvala followed Agastya to kill him and was burnt to ash by the sage's *huṃkāra* (CE \*0493).
  - *Variant (Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya 3.11.55–66 / CE 3.10.53–64):* **Rāma** tells this version himself, to Lakṣmaṇa, as they near the hermitage of Agastya's brother. Here Ilvala, **disguised as a brahmin and speaking refined speech** (*saṃskṛtaṃ vadan*, which commentators also read as "speaking Sanskrit"), invites brahmins to a **śrāddha** and feeds them Vātāpi as a **ram**. "Thousands of brahmins" die this way (3.11.60 / CE 3.10.58). There is no wealth-errand. The gods ask Agastya to end the menace, and Ilvala invites him to a śrāddha. After the meal, Ilvala pours the water for washing the hands and calls his brother out. Agastya laughs: *"Where is the strength for him to come out? I have digested that rākṣasa, your ram-shaped brother, and he has gone to Yama's abode"* (3.11.64 / CE 3.10.62). Ilvala rushes at him and is **burnt to death by the sage's fire-like eye** (*cakṣuṣānalakalpena nirdagdho nidhanaṃ gataḥ*, 3.11.66 / CE 3.10.64).
  - *Note (twin-of-the-ocean):* "digested" (*jīrṇa*) is the same answer Agastya gives about the ocean (§1). [interpretive note]

## 4. Lopāmudrā — his wife and intellectual equal

- **Created to order** - Agastya sees his **ancestors (pitṛs)** hanging **head-down in a pit**. They tell him they will be saved only if he begets a son. He finds no woman worthy to bear him, so he **takes the finest part of each creature** and makes from them an excellent woman. He gives her to the childless king of **Vidarbha**, who is performing austerities for offspring. She is born as his daughter, and the **brahmins name her Lopāmudrā**. (Mahābhārata, Vana Parva, Ganguli 96 / CE 3.94.11–21) The epic gives no etymology. The popular gloss, "she for whose making the creatures suffered loss (*lopa*) of their beauty," is a later explanation of the name. [late gloss]
- **The renunciant marriage** - when she comes of age, Agastya asks for her. The king faints: he does not want to give her, but he fears the sage's curse. **Lopāmudrā herself** tells her parents not to grieve and to give her away. Agastya asks her to put aside her costly clothes, and she wears **bark and deerskin**, "equal to her husband in vows." They practise severe tapas at **Gaṅgādvāra**. (Ganguli 97 / CE 3.95.1–12)
- **Her condition for a child** - after a long time he calls her to bed. She replies that a husband marries for offspring, but she wants him to come to her **on a bed like the one in her father's palace**, both of them adorned with garlands and ornaments, and not in ascetic rags. He says he has no such wealth. She says his tapas could produce it in a moment. He refuses to spend his tapas that way, and she agrees he should not. So he sets out to seek wealth by fair means. (Ganguli 97 / CE 3.95.13–24) This sends him to the three kings and to **Ilvala** (§3).
- **The Ṛgvedic dialogue** - Lopāmudrā is herself a **Ṛgvedic ṛṣikā**. In **Ṛgveda 1.179** (vv. 1–2 hers by the Anukramaṇī), she complains of "many autumns" of toil that wear down the body's beauty, and says that even the ancient truth-keeping seers came together with their wives. Verse 4 names her (*lopāmudrā vṛṣaṇaṃ nī riṇāti*). Verse 6 sums up: "Agastya, digging with spades, desiring offspring, progeny, strength, the mighty seer nourished **both colours (*ubhau varṇau*)**." This is usually read as balancing tapas with householder life. [scholarly reading] See also [Ṛgvedic women seers](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/rigvedic-women-seers-brahmavadinis.md).
- **The son** - Agastya brings everything Lopāmudrā asked for, and she asks for a mighty child. He offers her the choice: **a thousand sons, or a hundred each worth ten, or ten each worth a hundred, or one worth a thousand** (*sahasraṃ … śataṃ vā daśasaṃmitam / daśa vā śatatulyāḥ syur eko vāpi sahasravat*). She chooses **one son equal to a thousand**: "one learned, good son is better than many bad ones." (Ganguli 99 / CE 3.97.17–20)
- **Birth of Dṛḍhasyu** - after conceiving her, Agastya goes into the forest. The embryo grows for **seven years**. **Dṛḍhasyu** is born blazing, **reciting the Vedas with their Upaniṣads and Aṅgas**. As a child he carries loads of sacrificial firewood (*idhma*) to his father's house, so he is called **Idhmavāha**. The ancestors gain the worlds they wished for. (Ganguli 99 / CE 3.97.21–25) Lomaśa shows the Pāṇḍavas this very āśrama, on the **Bhāgīrathī**. (CE 3.97.26–27)

## 5. Agastya in the Rāmāyaṇa — host, arms-giver, and gatekeeper of the South

> Sarga numbers below are vulgate / Southern-recension (valmikiramayan.net), the convention this corpus uses throughout. Critical Edition (Baroda, on GRETIL) numbers follow in parentheses.

- **Before Rāma is born — the curse on Tāṭakā and Mārīca** - Agastya is in the story two books early, and he is its *cause*. Brahmā gives the yakṣa **Suketu** a daughter, **[Tāṭakā](characters/tataka.md)**, who marries **Sunda, son of Jambha**, and bears **Mārīca**. **Sunda is killed.** The verse says only "when Sunda was slain" (*sunde tu nihate*); the translator at valmikiramayan.net adds "by sage Agastya's curse," and the revenge that follows implies Agastya. When Tāṭakā and Mārīca rush at the sage in revenge, he curses **Mārīca to become a rākṣasa** and Tāṭakā to become a deformed **man-eater**. Enraged, she lays waste the Malada-Karūṣa country where Agastya once walked. (Rāmāyaṇa, **Bāla 1.25.5–14**; CE 1.24.5–12) Agastya's curse thus produces both Rāma's first kill and the golden deer of the Araṇya. See also [Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha](vishwamitra-vasishtha.md).
- **The hermitage in Daṇḍaka** - in the **[Araṇya Kāṇḍa](ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md)**, Sutīkṣṇa directs Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa first to the āśrama of **Agastya's (unnamed) brother**, a night's stay, and then on to Agastya's own. Rāma says this is the sage who "by his own force held back death" and **made the southern quarter a refuge**. "From then on the night-walkers keep the peace" here. The southern quarter itself is "famed in the three worlds by his name." (Rām. 3.11.37–86; CE 3.10.52, 79–83) This is Agastya as the *pioneer* who tames the wilderness ahead of Rāma.
- **Rāma tells the Vātāpi story himself** - the Ilvala–Vātāpi episode (§3) is **not only a Mahābhārata tale**. Rāma narrates it to Lakṣmaṇa on the road, in his own voice, to explain whose forest they are entering. Its frame (a śrāddha, not a wealth-errand) and its ending (Ilvala burnt, not paying tribute) both differ from the Mahābhārata. (Rām. **3.11.55–66**; CE 3.10.53–64)
- **The divine arsenal** - Agastya receives Rāma as the guest of honour and then gives him the weapons. The text itemises them: a great **bow of Viṣṇu**, adorned with gold and diamonds and **made by Viśvakarman**; an unfailing, sun-bright **best of arrows "given by Brahmā"** (*brahmadattaḥ*); **two inexhaustible quivers** full of fire-bright arrows; and a **gold-adorned sword in a great golden sheath**. Between the arrow and the quivers stands "given to me by Mahendra" (*datto mama mahendreṇa*). Its verb is singular, so it can be read with the arrow, although the valmikiramayan.net translator applies it to the quivers and the sword. The verse names no separate donor for the sword. With this bow, Agastya says, Viṣṇu once killed the great asuras. (Rām. **3.12.32–36**; CE 3.11.29–33) Agastya then directs the exiles to **Pañcavaṭī**, two yojanas off, on the Godāvarī (Rām. 3.13.13ff; CE 3.12.13). The whole episode makes Agastya the South's gatekeeper, who *equips* the northern prince to fight the southern demon-king.
  - *Where the gifts reappear:* Khara is killed by an arrow "**given by Maghavan (Indra)**, king of the gods" (Rām. 3.30.25; CE 3.29.25). In the vulgate, Agastya himself is among the sages who gather to praise Rāma after that fight (3.30.33–34). The corresponding CE verse (3.29.29) does not name him. Rāvaṇa is killed by the arrow "which the blessed sage **Agastya** gave him earlier, **given by Brahmā**." The next verse adds that **Brahmā made it long ago for Indra**, and that it was given to the lord of the gods (Rām. **6.108.3–5**; CE 6.97.4–5). So the epic itself joins Brahmā and Indra in that weapon's history.
- **The Āditya-Hṛdaya** - in the **[Yuddha Kāṇḍa](ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md)**, Rāma stands exhausted and anxious before the final duel. Agastya, who has come with the gods to watch, approaches and teaches him the **Āditya-Hṛdayam**, the hymn to the Sun. Recited three times, it will bring victory (6.105.2–5, 25–30). (Rāmāyaṇa, **Yuddha Kāṇḍa 105** in the vulgate numbering of valmikiramayan.net; other printed editions number the sarga differently.) It is a Sūrya-hymn, fittingly given by the sage whose mountain-errand freed the Sun's path (cf. §2).
  - *Note on its textual status [scholarly]:* the Āditya-Hṛdayam is **not in the constituted text of the Baroda Critical Edition**. In the GRETIL CE text, the duel runs straight from 6.95.26 to 6.96.1 with no Agastya hymn between them. The editors thus treat it as a later addition to the Yuddha Kāṇḍa. It is still one of the most recited passages attributed to Vālmīki, and the corpus records both facts without choosing between them. (Baroda Critical Edition, *Yuddhakāṇḍa*, ed. **P. L. Vaidya**, 1971)
  - *Kiṣkindhā Kāṇḍa:* Sugrīva briefs the southern search party. They will see Agastya, sun-bright, seated on the peak of **Mount Malaya**. Agastya placed the **Mahendra** mountain in the ocean, and on Mount **Kuñjara** stands "Agastya's abode, built by Viśvakarman." (Kiṣkindhā 4.41.15–16, 19–20, 34–35; CE 4.40.16, 20, 34) Vālmīki thus already sets Agastya on the southern mountain that Tamil tradition calls Potiyil (§6).
  - *Variant (Uttara Kāṇḍa):* in the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md), Agastya ("the pot-born," *kumbhayoni*) visits the crowned Rāma. In answer to Rāma's question, he tells the **back-story of Rāvaṇa and the rākṣasas**, from Pulastya's line onward (Uttara 1ff; CE 7.2.1ff), and so serves as the epic's southern historian. He also tells the tale of **[Śveta, the corpse-eating king](shveta-corpse-king-and-agastya.md)** (Uttara 77–78 Shastri; CE 7.68–69), who gave Agastya a divine ornament. Next comes the **[Daṇḍa and Arajā](danda-araja-and-dandakaranya.md)** story, which explains why the Daṇḍaka forest is empty (Uttara 79–81). [late text — the Uttara Kāṇḍa is widely held to be a later stratum]

## 6. The southward spread of Vedic culture & Tamil tradition

- **Carrier of the Veda across the Vindhya** - across Purāṇic and regional tradition, Agastya is *the* sage who **brings Vedic rite, Sanskrit, settlement, agriculture and irrigation** into the Deccan and Tamilakam. The North stresses the spread of Veda and Sanskrit; the South stresses irrigation, agriculture and the **enrichment of the Tamil language**. ([Central Institute of Classical Tamil PDF](https://kashitamil.bhu.edu.in/Tamilar%20Marabil%20Agathiyar-ENGLISH.pdf)) The Sanskrit epics already state the core of this: "the southern quarter was made a refuge by him" (Rām. 3.11.81) and "Vāruṇi has not returned from the south" (MBh CE 3.102.13).
- **Founder of Tamil grammar — the Agattiyam** - Tamil tradition makes **Agattiyaṉ (Agastya)** the author of the **Agattiyam / Akattiyam**, held to be the **earliest Tamil grammar**. It is now lost apart from a few disputed sūtras quoted by medieval commentators. The first full account of the three Sangams is in **Nakkīrar's commentary on the *Iṟaiyaṉār Akapporuḷ*** (c. 8th c.). It names Agattiyaṉ among the 549 members of the **First Sangam** at (Then) Madurai, alongside gods such as Śiva and Murukaṉ, and makes the *Agattiyam* that Sangam's grammar. Secondary summaries disagree on whether it makes him the Sangam's head: Wikipedia's Agastya article says the commentary "names Agathiyar as the head of the First Tamil Sangam," while its Tamil Sangams article says only that "some are of the opinion" that he headed it. See the [Tamil canon node](../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md). [late text / disputed — the Agattiyam is non-extant; the attribution is traditional, not philological; the Sangam narrative's historicity is itself contested]
  - *Variant (the twelve disciples):* Tamil tradition also says Agastya taught the *Agattiyam* to **twelve pupils**, and various traditions count **Tolkāppiyar**, author of the surviving *Tolkāppiyam*, among them ([Wikipedia: Agattiyam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agattiyam)). Jean-Luc Chevillard writes the history of this list in "The Pantheon of Tamil Grammarians: a short history of the myth of Agastya's twelve disciples" (2009). Only the bibliographic record of that article was seen for this page. [late text / scholarly]
  - *Variant (Buddhist Tamil framing):* Tamil Buddhist texts make Akattiyaṉ **learn Tamil from Avalokita (Avalokiteśvara)**, the Bodhisattva, which turns the grammarian-sage to Buddhist use. Wikipedia, summarising Anne E. Monius (*Imagining a Place for Buddhism*, 2001), names the Buddhist grammar **Vīracōḻiyam** and the **Maṇimēkalai** among the texts that make him a student of the Bodhisattva. Neither text was checked for this page. The Maṇimēkalai's prologue (below) mentions Agastya only for the Kāvēri. [late text / sectarian]
- **The Pothigai / Podhigai abode** - Tamil tradition places Agastya's southern seat in the **Pothigai (Potiyil) Hills** of the southern Western Ghats, the source of the **Tāmraparṇī** river. The peak **Agastyārkūḍam / Agasthyamala**, on the Kerala–Tamil Nadu border, is named for him. (Tamil tradition) The Sanskrit texts put him on the **Malaya** range: Vālmīki's Kiṣkindhā (CE 4.40.16, §5) and the Devī Bhāgavata, where he settles at **Malayācala** (DBhP 10.7).
- **The Kāvēri from his water-jar** - the prologue (*patikam*) of the Tamil Buddhist epic **Maṇimēkalai** says that the Cōḻa king **Kāntaṉ (Kāntamaṉ)** asked Agastya for water to make the solar line prosper. Agastya **let his water-jar overturn**, and the stream flowed east to the sea as the **Kāvēri**. ([Maṇimēkhalai, Prologue — ancient-buddhist-texts.net](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/English-Texts/Manimekhalai/00-Prologue.htm)) Later Kāvēri-māhātmya retellings expand this: a wind, or Gaṇeśa as a crow, upsets the kamaṇḍalu. [regional] See [sacred rivers & descent myths](../06-sacred-geography/sacred-rivers-and-descent-myths.md).
- **Tamil Siddhar & Siddha medicine** - in **Śaiva / Siddha** tradition, Agastya (**Agathiyar**) is the foremost of the **eighteen Siddhars**, founder of **Siddha medicine**, alchemy and yoga. He is credited with a vast, largely apocryphal corpus of Tamil technical and medical texts. (Tamil Siddha tradition) [folk / late text]
- **Tilting the earth southward** - a widespread Tamil temple legend says the gods and sages crowding to Śiva's Himālayan wedding **tipped the earth northward**. Śiva sent the sage south so that his weight would **rebalance it**, and granted him a vision of the wedding from where he stood. Several Agastya shrines tell this as their local origin story. It unites the Vindhya-crossing and the southern migration into one cosmic load-balancing act. [folk / temple legend — no Sanskrit Purāṇic source located for this page]
- **Pan-South/Southeast-Asian afterlife** - Agastya images spread with Hindu culture: the paunchy, bearded sage, often in the **southern niche** of Śaiva shrines. In Java there is the Old Javanese **Agastya-parva** (an 11th-c. version survives), the **Dinoyo inscription (760 CE)**, which records that a wooden Agastya image was remade in stone, and the southern cella of the main Śiva shrine at **Prambanan**, dedicated to Agastya. **Borobudur** carves the **Agastya-jātaka** ([Wikipedia: Agastya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agastya)) [scholarly]. This is the seventh story of Āryaśūra's *Jātakamālā*. In it the Bodhisattva is born into a brahmin family, renounces the household life, and lives as a forest ascetic who honours every guest with what he has (*Jātakamālā* 7, GRETIL). That story is a Buddhist tale of generosity (*dāna*), not part of the Vedic sage's mythology.

## 7. Vedic seer & ritual roles (the Ṛgvedic Agastya)

- **Author of Ṛgveda hymns** - by the Anukramaṇī, Agastya Maitrāvaruṇi is the ṛṣi of **Ṛgveda 1.165–1.191**. This includes the **Indra–Maruts–Agastya** dialogues (1.165, 1.170–1.171), in which he makes peace between Indra and the storm-gods. In 1.170.3, Indra addresses him as **"brother Agastya"** (*bhrātar agastya*), asking why, though a friend, he slights him. Many of his hymns close with the refrain *vidyāmeṣaṃ vṛjanaṃ jīradānum* (e.g. 1.165.15), "may we find refreshment, a community, and lively waters." The same verse calls the poet **Māndārya Mānya**. The cycle ends with **1.191**, a charm against venomous creatures. (RV text: GRETIL) — links to the [Veda corpus](../90-literature-corpus/shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md).
- **The Lopāmudrā sūkta** - **Ṛgveda 1.179** (see §4) is the husband–wife dialogue on tapas and progeny. The Anukramaṇī divides it between Lopāmudrā (vv. 1–2), Agastya (vv. 3–4) and a disciple (vv. 5–6). [scholarly]
- **Vasiṣṭha's companion in RV 7.33** - the Vasiṣṭha hymn 7.33.10 names Agastya as the one who brought the newborn Vasiṣṭha "to the people." This is the oldest textual link between the two pot-born seers (see Dramatis personae).
- **Not (usually) a [Chiranjīvi](the-chiranjivis.md)** - unlike Vyāsa or Aśvatthāmā, Agastya is not in the standard list of immortals. Yet he persists across the yugas: Vedic seer, Tretā host of Rāma, his āśrama still shown to the Pāṇḍavas in the Dvāpara, timeless Tamil Siddhar. This gives him a comparable **deathless, recurring presence** across the timeline. Vālmīki calls him "long-lived" (*dīrghāyus*, Rām. 3.11.86). [note]

## 8. Nahuṣa cast down, and other Mahābhārata episodes

- **The fall of Nahuṣa** - when the mortal king **[Nahuṣa](nahusha.md)** rules heaven in Indra's absence, he makes the ṛṣis carry his palanquin. The Mahābhārata tells his fall three times, and the accounts differ on who speaks the curse. They are kept apart:
  - *Udyoga Parva 5.17 (CE), told by Agastya to Indra:* the weary ṛṣis carrying Nahuṣa ask him whether the mantras for consecrating cows, laid down by Brahmā (*brahmaṇā proktāḥ*), are authoritative, and he says "no." In the dispute that follows he **touches Agastya's head with his foot**. Agastya curses him: *"Fall, sinner, … for ten thousand years you will roam the earth in the form of a great serpent; when they are complete you will regain heaven"* (CE 5.17.11–15). Some manuscripts add that he will be freed on seeing his descendant **Yudhiṣṭhira** (starred \*0114).
  - *Vana Parva 3.176–178 (CE), Nahuṣa's own account (the python episode):* a thousand brahmarṣis bore his palanquin. He touched Agastya with his foot, and **an unseen speaker** cried *"dhvaṃsa sarpa"* ("fall, serpent!"). Falling from his vimāna, Nahuṣa begged "that brahmin" for an end to the curse, and was promised that **Yudhiṣṭhira** would free him (CE 3.178.36–41). Earlier in the episode he calls his fate "Agastya's curse" (*śāpād agastyasya*, CE 3.176.14). The "unseen" speaker matches the Anuśāsana version below. This sets up the [ajagara](nahusha.md) dialogue with Yudhiṣṭhira and Bhīma.
  - *Variant (Anuśāsana Parva 13.102–103 CE):* the sage **Bhṛgu** hides in **Agastya's matted hair**, so that Nahuṣa, whose boon lets him sap the power of anyone he looks at, cannot see him. Agastya lets himself be yoked and endures Nahuṣa's goad patiently. When Nahuṣa kicks him on the head with his **left foot**, **Bhṛgu, not Agastya**, pronounces the serpent-curse. Then, at the merciful Agastya's request, Bhṛgu names Yudhiṣṭhira as the deliverer (CE 13.103.12–28).
- **Burning the dānavas** - in the Anuśāsana Parva's string of tales of brahmin power, Vāyu tells how the dānavas had defeated the gods and stolen the sacrifices. Agastya, angered, **blazed like the fire at the end of time** and burnt them out of the sky. The survivors fled to the **southern quarter**. Agastya refused to burn the asuras who were on earth, such as **Bali** at his aśvamedha, because doing so would deplete his tapas. (MBh 13.140.1–14 CE) [late text]
- **The stolen lotus** - on a group pilgrimage of ṛṣis and royal sages led by Indra, **Agastya's lotus**, which he has drawn from Brahmasaras, disappears. Each sage swears an oath of innocence (the *bisa-stainya* oaths). The thief turns out to be **Indra**, who took it only to hear the sages declare dharma, and he returns it. (MBh 13.96.1–49 CE) [late text]

## 9. Agastya the star (Canopus)

- **Canopus** - Sanskrit astronomy and astrology name the bright southern star **Canopus** *Agastya*. Varāhamihira's **Bṛhat-saṃhitā** gives it a chapter, the **Agastya-cāra** (ch. 12). It opens by recalling that he drank the ocean and stopped the Vindhya (BS 12.1–6). The rising of "the sage called Agastya" makes waters clear and wholesome (12.7–12). The text then prescribes an **arghya** that the king offers toward the south before dawn, with seasonal flowers, fruits, jewels, gold and cloth, and it lists the rewards for each class (12.13–18). (Bṛhatsaṃhitā, GRETIL) [scholarly / jyotiṣa]
- **In the Purāṇic hymn** - the gods' praise of Agastya in the Devī Bhāgavata links the sage and the star: "at your rising the waters of the ocean become clear … at your rising the kāśa flower blooms." (DBhP 10.6.7–19) The star's rising at the end of the rains thus belongs to the same sage who drank the ocean and went south. [Purāṇic]
