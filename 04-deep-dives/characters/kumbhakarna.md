# Kumbhakarṇa

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Kumbhakarṇa**, the colossal rākṣasa brother of Rāvaṇa who asked Brahmā for sleep when Sarasvatī took over his speech, was roused during the war and killed by Rāma (by Lakṣmaṇa in the *Mahābhārata*). Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). His war and death sit in the [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md); his birth and boon belong to the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md) back-story.
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* this falls in Tretā Yuga of the present 28th Mahāyuga, 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara; the academic baseline is the *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, with Rāma's traditional dating debated and the epic's textual core usually placed c. 5th–4th c. BCE onward. [scholarly] Vālmīki is cited by the vulgate sarga.verse used at valmikiramayan.net (Araṇya, Sundara, Yuddha) and by the Uttara sargas as in M. N. Dutt's translation, with Baroda Critical Edition numbers marked **CE** where given; the two numberings differ.

## Birth, name, and parentage
- **Son of Viśravas and Kaikasī** - second-born of the brahmin-sage [Viśravas](./vishravas.md) (son of Pulastya, a Prajāpati) and the rākṣasī [Kaikasī](./kaikasi.md), daughter of Sumālin, hence half-brahmin, half-rākṣasa. Rāvaṇa is born first, then Kumbhakarṇa, "whose size has no measure here," then **Śūrpaṇakhā**, and **Vibhīṣaṇa** is the youngest (*Uttara* 9; CE 7.9.25–27). See [Rāvaṇa](./ravana.md) for the shared lineage.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna):* Kubera gives Viśravas three rākṣasīs; **Puṣpotkaṭā** bears Kumbhakarṇa and Rāvaṇa, Mālinī bears Vibhīṣaṇa, Rākā bears Khara and Śūrpaṇakhā (BORI 3.259.5–8).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa):* the mother is named **Keśinī** (7.1.43 in GRETIL's numbering). [late text]
- **The name "pot-eared"** - *kumbha* ("pot, pitcher") + *karṇa* ("ear"), "one with ears like pots." This is a lexical gloss: Vālmīki explains Daśagrīva's name at his birth but gives none for Kumbhakarṇa (CE 7.9.25–26).
- **Hunger and menace** - two passages, in two books:
  - *Uttara 9:* as the brothers grow up, Kumbhakarṇa roams the three worlds terrifying them and devouring great sages (CE 7.9.29). At the boon scene the gods list his victims as seven apsarases of Nandana, ten of Indra's attendants, sages and men (CE 7.10.33).
  - *Yuddha 61 (Vibhīṣaṇa's account):* "as soon as he was born," hungry, he ate thousands of creatures (61.13; CE 6.49.13). When Indra struck him with the vajra he tore a tusk from Airāvata and drove it into Indra's chest (61.15–17; CE 6.49.15–17).

## The sleep — Vālmīki's two accounts

Vālmīki tells the origin of Kumbhakarṇa's sleep **twice**, in two different books, and the two accounts do not agree. Kept apart they are legible; fused into one composite they produce the muddle most retellings carry.

- **Joint tapas of the three brothers** - the three brothers go to the hermitage at **Gokarṇa** to perform austerities for **Brahmā** (CE 7.9.37). Kumbhakarṇa sits among five fires in the hot season, holds the *vīrāsana* posture in the rains, and stands in water through the cold season, for ten thousand years, and the text calls him "intent on dharma" while he does it (CE 7.10.3–5). Rāvaṇa's penance is the more drastic one: fasting for ten thousand years, he offers nine of his heads into the fire (CE 7.10.10–12).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* the penance is on **Gandhamādana**, where Kumbhakarṇa lies on the bare ground with restrained food and vows (BORI 3.259.13–17).
- **Uttara 10 — the boon layer: Sarasvatī on his tongue** - As Brahmā turns to Kumbhakarṇa, the gods beg him not to grant a boon to this devourer and ask him instead to give him "delusion under the guise of a boon" (*varavyājena moho*, CE 7.10.31–34). Brahmā thinks of **Sarasvatī** and tells her to "be the speech" of the rākṣasa; she enters him (CE 7.10.35–38). Asked to choose, he says he wishes "to sleep for many years" (*svaptuṃ varṣāṇy anekāni*, CE 7.10.39). Brahmā grants it and Sarasvatī leaves him. Then Kumbhakarṇa, troubled, asks himself what word has just fallen from his mouth (CE 7.10.41). The vulgate adds that he suspects the gods stupefied him (Dutt, Uttara §X). The text never says what he meant to ask for, and it attaches **no waking clause** (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 10).
- **Uttara 13 — the sleep arrives** - some time later, a sleep "sent forth by the lord of the worlds" (*lokeśvarotsṛṣṭā*) comes over him in embodied form. He asks Rāvaṇa for a dwelling, and craftsmen build one a yojana wide and two long. There he sleeps for many thousands of years without waking, while Rāvaṇa ravages gods and sages (CE 7.13.1–8).
- **Yuddha 61 — the curse layer: Vibhīṣaṇa's account** - Narrating to Rāma on the battlefield why the sleeper can be woken at all, **Vibhīṣaṇa** gives a different mechanism: it is **not a boon but a curse**. Indra and the gods complain to Brahmā that Kumbhakarṇa devours creatures, assaults the gods, wrecks hermitages and takes others' wives (61.18–21). Brahmā, frightened at the sight of him, declares that Viśravas surely made him for the ruin of the worlds and that from now on he will sleep like the dead (61.24; CE 6.49.23). **Rāvaṇa pleads** that a god does not cut down a golden tree just as it fruits, nor curse his own great-grandson, and asks Brahmā to fix a time for sleep and waking (61.26–27; CE 6.49.24–25). Brahmā commutes the sentence: **six months asleep, one day awake**, and on that day he will roam the earth devouring (61.28–29; CE 6.49.26–27). The famous cycle belongs here, to a separate sarga, and not to the boon.
  - *Consistency within Yuddha:* the war narrative itself treats the sleep as a curse. Rāvaṇa orders him woken as one "overpowered by Brahmā's curse" (60.13; CE 6.48.9), and he sleeps on "in the grip of the curse" (60.48; CE 6.48.42).
- **Which layer is older** - most text-critical scholarship treats the *Uttara Kāṇḍa* (with the *Bāla*) as a later addition to the epic's core books. On that view the Yuddha curse would be the earlier account and the Uttara boon the later back-story, which reverses the order a reader meets them in (see R. P. Goldman, *The Rāmāyaṇa of Vālmīki*, vol. I: *Bālakāṇḍa*, Princeton 1984, Introduction). [scholarly]
- **How long he sleeps — the Yuddha figures disagree** - the numbers are cataloged here, not reconciled:
  - Rāvaṇa at the first council: he "had been sleeping six months and has now woken" (12.11).
  - Rāvaṇa ordering the waking: he "sleeps nine, seven, ten or eight months," and he fell asleep after the council "nine days ago" (60.16–17). The CE reads "nine, six, seven or eight months" and lacks the nine-days verse (CE 6.48.11–12).
  - Vibhīṣaṇa: six months asleep, one day awake (61.28).
- **The mispronunciation puns are in neither passage** - Vālmīki has the slip but never names an intended boon, and the *Mahābhārata* has no slip at all: there Kumbhakarṇa "chose a great sleep, his mind seized by *tamas*" (*sa vavre mahatīṃ nidrāṃ tamasā grastacetanaḥ*, BORI 3.259.28). The word-play explanations are later retelling:
  - *Variant (widely cited):* he meant to ask for **Nirdevatvam** (a world emptied of gods) and his twisted tongue produced **Nidrāvatvam** ("the state of sleep"). Vettam Mani's *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia* (s.v. Kumbhakarṇa) gives this and cites "Vana Parva, Chapter 275 and Uttara Rāmāyaṇa", but neither the BORI Rāmopākhyāna nor the critical Uttara contains the pun. [folk]
  - *Variant (also common):* he meant **Indrāsana** (Indra's throne) and said **Nidrāsana** ("sleep-seat"). [folk]
  - *Variant:* some retellings give the interval as six months asleep and six months awake (the *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia*: he "would sleep for six months of the year continuously") rather than the one day of Yuddha 61.28. [disputed]
- **Strength by nature, not by boon** - the Uttara boon gives him only the sleep. Vibhīṣaṇa says Kumbhakarṇa is mighty "by his very nature," while the strength of the other rākṣasa chiefs comes from boons (61.12; CE 6.49.12). He has conquered Yama and Indra, and the gods could not kill him, taking him for Death himself (61.9–11).

## Character — the virtuous giant
- **The first war-council** - He speaks early, at Rāvaṇa's opening council, just after waking from a six-month sleep (12.10–11), and he **rebukes his brother for policy, not for sin**. Rāvaṇa should have weighed the matter when he carried Sītā off, and consultation would have suited the start of the affair, not its end. A king who acts on settled judgment has nothing to regret afterwards, and Rāvaṇa is lucky Rāma did not kill him, as poisoned food kills the eater (12.27–34). In the same breath he pledges to **undo the dishonour by killing the enemy**, boasting he would face Indra, Sūrya, Agni, Vāyu, Kubera or Varuṇa, and tells Rāvaṇa to drink at ease, since with Rāma dead Sītā will be his (12.35–40). The speech is the exact measure of him: clear-eyed about the blunder, wholly unrepentant about the crime.
  - *Textual note (Critical Edition):* this council scene (vulgate 12) is not in the Baroda constituted text, which passes from Vibhīṣaṇa's plea to return Sītā (CE 6.9) straight to Rāvaṇa's rebuke of him (CE 6.10). Yet the CE keeps Kumbhakarṇa's later reference to "the fault we saw before, when the counsel was decided" (CE 6.51.2). [scholarly]
- **Wise counsellor, reluctant warrior** - when roused for the war, he laughs at Rāvaṇa's lament. The danger foreseen at the council has arrived, he says, as the swift fruit of a sinful deed (*phalaṃ pāpasya karmaṇaḥ*, 63.2–3; CE 6.51.2–3). He lectures on statecraft, on timing, the three ends of life, the means of policy and flattering counsellors (63.4–20). He ends by saying that **Vibhīṣaṇa's earlier advice**, which was to return Sītā, was the beneficial course (63.21). He does not himself call for her return or speak of Rāma's divinity. That belongs to the variants below (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa* 63).
  - *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa):* he reminds Rāvaṇa that he had already told him Rāma is the supreme Nārāyaṇa and Sītā is Yogamāyā. He learned it from **Nārada**, whom he met one night at Viśālā, and he urges Rāvaṇa to give up enmity and worship Rāma (*Yuddha* 7.56–70). [late text]
  - *Variant (Kamba Rāmāyaṇam, Tamil):* on waking he tells Rāvaṇa that dharma has fled from him. The way to survive is to **release Sītā, bow at Rāma's feet and be reconciled with Vibhīṣaṇa**; failing that, Rāvaṇa should throw all his forces in at once (*Kumpakaruṇaṉ vataip paṭalam* vv. 79–89, Tamil Wikisource text). He acknowledges Rāma as the Primal One, "the Vedic being who took a king's form," in his dying words (v. 354; see *Later retellings*). [late text]
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās, Rāmcaritmānas, Laṅkākāṇḍa):* he rebukes Rāvaṇa: "having carried off the Mother of the world (*jagadambā*), you fool, you now want your welfare" (dohā 62). He urges him to drop his pride and worship Rāma (*bhajahu rāma hoihi kalyānā*), recalls the knowledge Nārada once gave him, and asks to be embraced before he goes out to see Rāma (chaupāīs after dohā 62, ramcharit.in numbering). [late text]
- **Mahodara's ruse rejected** - Mahodara calls him rash and proposes a deception instead of battle. A few champions would return smeared with blood, Rāvaṇa would proclaim that Rāma has been eaten, and Sītā, believing herself widowed, would yield (64.19–34; CE 6.52). Kumbhakarṇa rounds on Mahodara as one of the flatterers whose pleasant counsel has emptied the treasury and destroyed the army, and goes out to fight (65.1–8; CE 6.53.1–8). On the way he says the forest-roaming monkeys have done him no wrong: Rāma is the root of the attack, and Rāma is the one he will kill (65.44–45).
- **Loyalty over judgment** - the canonical resolution: though he condemns the cause, he fights for his brother. "Rāma will kill you only after killing me" (63.41), and he leaves Rāvaṇa with the promise of Rāma's head (63.36), the tragic warrior who dies for a war he knows is wrong (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa* 63).

## The awakening — rousing the sleeper
- **Summoned in extremity** - after Prahasta falls and Rāvaṇa himself is defeated and sent home alive by Rāma (59), Rāvaṇa recalls Brahmā's warning about men and orders his brother woken (60.5–21; CE 6.48.5–16). He calls him a fool who is always asleep, given to vulgar pleasure (60.19; CE 6.48.14), and asks what use such strength is if it will not help in a calamity like this (60.21).
- **The herculean wake-up** - in a cave a yojana across, the rākṣasas pile up meat "like Mount Meru," pots of blood and meats of many kinds (the CE reads liquors), sandal paste and garlands. They blow conches and beat drums, ten thousand of them at once. They strike him with mountain-tops, pestles and maces, drive horses, camels, donkeys and elephants over him with sticks and goads, beat a thousand drums, pull his hair and bite his ears. Through all of it he sleeps on under the curse. Finally **a thousand elephants run over his body** (*vāraṇānāṃ sahasraṃ*), and only then does he feel the touch; hunger brings him to his feet (60.23–55; CE 6.48.16–48).
  - *Textual note:* the "**one thousand elephants**" is Vālmīki's own figure (60.54; CE 6.48.47), not a later exaggeration.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* Rāvaṇa himself has him woken "with great effort" by loud instruments, then tells him, "Fortunate are you, whose sleep is such that you know nothing of this dreadful time" (BORI 3.270.19–22).
- **Mountain of food on waking** - he eats the meat, drinks the blood, the fat and the wine (60.62–63; CE 6.48.54). He asks why he was woken, since no small cause would justify it (60.66–69), and the minister **Yūpākṣa** tells him of the siege, Hanumān's burning of the city and Rāvaṇa's humiliation (60.70–76). He vows to kill the army and the two princes before even seeing Rāvaṇa, until Mahodara persuades him to hear the king first (60.78–82). Before setting out he drinks two thousand pots of liquor (60.92); the CE reads one thousand (CE 6.48.82).
- **The sortie** - Rāvaṇa decks him with jewels. He takes an iron spike and, as he leaves, grows to a body a hundred bows broad and six hundred high (65.18–41). Omens attend him: a vulture settles on his spike, and his left eye and arm throb (65.47–52). Rāma, seeing him, asks who he is. Vibhīṣaṇa suggests the fleeing monkeys be told he is only a **machine** (*yantra*) (61.33; CE 6.49.31), and Aṅgada rallies them by calling him a mere bogey (66.6–7).

## The battle and death — the climax
- **Devastation of the vānara army** - he scatters the monkeys with his spike and mace and stuffs them into his mouth by handfuls; some come out again through his nostrils and ears (67.5–7, 35–36). Maddened by the smell of blood, he eats rākṣasas as well as monkeys and bears (67.95, 123). Lakṣmaṇa notices that he can no longer tell friend from foe and has the monkeys swarm onto his body to weigh him down, but he shakes them off (67.128–133) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa* 67).
- **Duels with the vānara chiefs** - **Hanumān** pelts him from the sky and strikes him with a peak, and Kumbhakarṇa drives his spike into Hanumān's chest so that he vomits blood (67.15–20). He fells **Nīla, Ṛṣabha, Śarabha, Gavākṣa and Gandhamādana** (67.22–30) and knocks **Aṅgada** senseless (67.43–50). When he hurls the spike at **Sugrīva**, Hanumān catches it and breaks it over his knee (67.62–64). He then stuns Sugrīva with a peak of Mount Malaya and carries him into Laṅkā (67.67–83), while Hanumān holds back so as not to shame the king by rescuing him (67.73–81). Reviving, Sugrīva **tears off his ears with his nails and his nose with his teeth**, rips his flanks with his feet, and springs free (67.87–89; CE 6.55.67–69). **Lakṣmaṇa** pierces him and his golden armour with arrows. Kumbhakarṇa praises the young man's courage and **passes him by** to get at Rāma (67.101–116).
- **Rāma severs his limbs one by one** - Rāma's arrows charged with the Raudra spell strike his chest (67.117). Kumbhakarṇa answers that he is not Virādha, Kabandha, Khara, Vālin or Mārīca, and that Rāma should not despise him for his lost ears and nose, whose loss gives him no pain (67.149–152; CE 6.55.103–106). With the **Vāyavya** weapon Rāma cuts off the arm holding the hammer (67.157; CE 6.55.111). With an arrow charged with **Indra's** spell he cuts off the other arm with the tree it grips (67.161; CE 6.55.115). Two crescent-headed arrows take the **feet** (67.163; CE 6.55.117). When the limbless giant charges with open mouth, Rāma fills it with arrows (67.166; CE 6.55.119) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa* 67).
- **The decapitation** - finally Rāma looses an arrow bearing the **Aindra** weapon and shears off the head, "as Indra once cut off Vṛtra's" (67.167–170; CE 6.55.120–123). The **head** falls on Laṅkā, smashing houses and gateways and throwing down the high rampart (67.172; CE 6.55.124). The **body** falls into the sea, crushing crocodiles, great fish and serpents, and sinks into the earth (67.173; CE 6.55.125).
  - *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa):* the feet fall at Laṅkā's gate. The head falls there too and blocks the gate, and the body falls into the ocean, grinding the crocodiles (*Yuddha* 8.23, 8.28–29). [late text]
  - *Variant (Kamba Rāmāyaṇam):* the dying Kumbhakarṇa **asks** Rāma to cut off his head and sink it in the dark sea, so that sages and gods will not look on "a face without a nose." Rāma grants it and sinks the head with a wind-weapon (vv. 357–359). [late text]
  - *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas):* the head falls **before Rāvaṇa**. The headless trunk keeps running, and the earth sinks under it, until Rāma cuts it in two (between dohās 70 and 71). [late text]
- **Rāvaṇa's grief** - told of the death, Rāvaṇa faints, and his sons Devāntaka, Narāntaka, Triśiras and Atikāya weep for their uncle (68.6–7). He laments the fall of the "right arm" that let him fear no god (68.12; CE 6.56.7). He has no use for the kingdom, he says, and asks what he will do with Sītā (68.17). Vibhīṣaṇa's words, he admits, have come true (68.21–23). His sons then go out to fight (69) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa, ch. 68*).

## Family — wife and sons
- **Wife — Vajrajvālā (Vajrajwala)** - his consort, married to him by Rāvaṇa in the round of dynastic matches that also gave Saramā to Vibhīṣaṇa and Śūrpaṇakhā to Vidyujjihva (CE 7.12.1–2, 20–22). The text calls her *vairocanasya dauhitrī*, the **daughter's daughter of Virocana** (CE 7.12.21). Dutt's vulgate translation reads the same: "the grand-daughter of Virochana (on the maternal side)" (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 12, Dutt §XII).
  - *Variant:* Vettam Mani's *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia* (s.v. Vajrajvālā) makes her "a daughter of Mahābali", that is, Bali's daughter, which would make her Virocana's granddaughter through a son. It cites the "Uttara Rāmāyaṇa", but the Uttara's own wording points to descent through a daughter. [later reference works]
  - *Variant (Śiva Purāṇa):* **Karkaṭī**, the widow of Virādha (who was also slain by Rāma), says Kumbhakarṇa came to her on the Sahya mountain, took her by force and returned to Laṅkā; their son is the demon **Bhīma** (*Śiva Purāṇa*, Koṭirudra-saṃhitā 20, J. L. Shastri tr.). [late text]
  - *Variant (later lists):* further consorts named **Tāritamālā** and **Aśvanī** appear in modern genealogical summaries, with no primary text located. [folk]
- **Sons — Kumbha and Nikumbha** - both fight and die in the Laṅkā war. **Kumbha**, an archer, wounds Mainda and Aṅgada, then wrestles **Sugrīva**, who kills him with a blow of the fist (76.36–94; CE 6.63). **Nikumbha**, seeing his brother fall, attacks **Hanumān** with an iron bar; Hanumān pins him and tears off his head (77.1–22; CE 6.64.1–23).
- **Other sons in later texts** - these appear only in later texts:
  - **Bhīma**, the son by Karkaṭī. He grows up on the Sahya mountain, learns that Rāma killed his father, and is reduced to ashes by Śiva "with only a Huṃ sound," which is the origin of the Bhīmaśaṅkara jyotirliṅga (*Śiva Purāṇa*, Koṭirudra-saṃhitā 20–21; 21.43 in Shastri's translation). See [12 Jyotirliṅgas](../../06-sacred-geography/12-jyotirlingas.md). [late text]
  - **Mūlaka**, born under the inauspicious Mūla asterism. Kumbhakarṇa throws him away, honey-bees rear him, and he grows into a tormentor of the worlds who is killed by Sītā with Rāma's help (*Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa*, Rājya-kāṇḍa, as summarized in the *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia*, s.v. Mūlaka). [late text]
  - **Aśvanikumbha** appears in some modern rosters, with no primary text located. [folk] [open-ended roster]

## Elsewhere in Vālmīki
- **Śūrpaṇakhā's introduction** - naming her brothers to Rāma in the forest, she calls him "the mighty Kumbhakarṇa, ever deep in sleep" (*pravṛddhanidraḥ sadā*, Araṇya 17.22; CE 3.16.19). See [Śūrpaṇakhā](./shurpanakha.md).
- **Trijaṭā's dream** - among the omens of Laṅkā's fall, she sees Kumbhakarṇa riding a **camel** to the south, the direction of Yama, and later dressed in red and plunging into a pool of cow-dung with the other rākṣasa chiefs (Sundara 27.30, 27.38; CE 5.25.21, 5.25.25). See [Trijaṭā](./trijata.md).
- **The campaign against Indra's heaven** - he brings up the rear when Rāvaṇa marches out, first against Madhu and then on to Indra's world (CE 7.25.30–35). In the battle he fights blindly with teeth, arms, feet, spears and arrows, attacking whoever is near, until the Rudras and Ādityas hack at him with their weapons (CE 7.28.32–34).

## Past life and theological frame
- **The gatekeeper Vijaya** - in the [Bhāgavata Purāṇa](../../90-literature-corpus/README.md) frame, Rāvaṇa and Kumbhakarṇa are the second birth of **Jaya and Vijaya**, the two doorkeepers of Vaikuṇṭha. The Four Kumāras cursed them for barring their way, and in mercy limited the exile to three births (7.1.38 in GRETIL's numbering). Their first birth is as Hiraṇyakaśipu and Hiraṇyākṣa (7.1.39). They are next born as "Rāvaṇa and Kumbhakarṇa, tormentors of all the worlds," and Rāma kills them "for their release from the curse" (7.1.43–44). The third pair are the cousins Śiśupāla and Dantavakra (7.1.45–46). See [Jaya–Vijaya](../jaya-vijaya.md) for the full curse arc. [late text]
  - *Which twin:* the *Bhāgavata* speaks only of "the two." Making Kumbhakarṇa **Vijaya** is a later assignment, and secondary works disagree on which twin is which. [disputed]
  - *Variant (popular retellings):* Viṣṇu offers the pair seven births as devotees or three as enemies, and they choose enmity. This choice is **not in** the *Bhāgavata* (see [Jaya–Vijaya](../jaya-vijaya.md)). [folk]
- **Devotional reading** - the *Bhāgavata* itself frames the death as release. Born again as rākṣasas through a brahmin's curse, Kumbhakarṇa and Rāvaṇa lie on the battlefield with their hearts pierced by Rāma's arrows, and with minds fixed on him they give up their bodies as in their former birth (7.10.36–37). [late text] Later retellings make the release visible:
  - *Rāmcaritmānas:* his radiance (*tej*) enters Rāma's mouth, to the astonishment of gods and sages, and Rāma gives "even this vile demon" his own abode (between dohās 70 and 71, and dohā 71). [late text]
  - Folk and Vaiṣṇava tellings likewise stress that he died facing Rāma and was freed. [folk]

## The Mahābhārata's Rāmopākhyāna
Mārkaṇḍeya's short Rāma story in the *Āraṇyaka Parvan* gives a self-contained version of Kumbhakarṇa that differs from Vālmīki at nearly every point (BORI numbering). See [Variant & Regional Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).
- **Character and boon** - son of Puṣpotkaṭā, the strongest of all the brothers, a battle-loving master of illusion (*māyāvī raṇaśauṇḍaś ca*, 3.259.7, 11). Offered a boon, he "chose a great sleep, his mind seized by *tamas*." There is no Sarasvatī and no plot by the gods (3.259.28).
- **Waking and sortie** - after Prahasta and Dhūmrākṣa fall, Rāvaṇa has him woken and sends him out with Dūṣaṇa's younger brothers **Vajravega and Pramāthin** (3.270.17–29).
- **Death by Lakṣmaṇa** - he eats the monkeys Panasa, Gavākṣa and Vajrabāhu (3.271.4). Sugrīva breaks a *śāla* tree over his head, and Kumbhakarṇa seizes him and carries him off (3.271.6–9). The ear-and-nose mutilation appears here only in a passage the BORI editors relegated to the apparatus (*1284), where Sugrīva tears off his ears, lip and nose. **Lakṣmaṇa**'s arrow then pierces armour and body. Kumbhakarṇa drops Sugrīva and charges with a rock, and Lakṣmaṇa cuts off his raised arms with razor-arrows. He grows four arms, then many heads, arms and feet, and Lakṣmaṇa burns him with the **brahmāstra** (3.271.10–17). Hanumān and Nīla kill his two escorts (3.271.24–25).
- **A tīrtha** - bathing at the **Kumbhakarṇāśrama** wins honour on earth (BORI 3.82.136).

## Later retellings
- **Vibhīṣaṇa comes to his brother** - absent from Vālmīki, this battlefield meeting is shared by several later retellings, and each gives it a different turn:
  - *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa:* Vibhīṣaṇa, mace in hand, bows at his feet. Kumbhakarṇa embraces him and tells him to live under the shelter of Rāma's feet: Nārada told him long ago that Vibhīṣaṇa is a great devotee. Then he sends him away, because in his drunkenness he can no longer tell his own side from the enemy's (*Yuddha* 8.9–16). [late text]
  - *Rāmcaritmānas:* Vibhīṣaṇa falls at his feet and names himself, and Kumbhakarṇa lifts and embraces him, glad that he is Rāma's devotee. He calls him the ornament of the rākṣasa race and urges him to worship Rāma without guile, since he himself, in death's grip, can no longer tell friend from foe (after dohā 63, dohā 64). [late text]
  - *Kamba Rāmāyaṇam:* Vibhīṣaṇa comes to win him over to Rāma, and Kumbhakarṇa, weeping, refuses. He will not leave the brother who reared him and has now arrayed him for war, and he will not live on bowing to the enemy who is to take his elder's life. He asks Vibhīṣaṇa to perform the funeral rites afterwards and sends him back (vv. 154–166). [late text]
- **The dying requests (Kamban)** - with his limbs gone, he asks Rāma, scion of the king who weighed himself on the scales for a dove, to protect Vibhīṣaṇa. Rāvaṇa will kill Vibhīṣaṇa if he can, so he must never be parted from Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa or Hanumān (vv. 353–356). Then he makes the request about his head (v. 357). Kamban also expands Lakṣmaṇa's fight with him into a long duel (vv. 208–253), where Vālmīki has Kumbhakarṇa simply pass Lakṣmaṇa by. [late text]
- **Jain retelling (Vimalasūri's *Paumacariya*)** - the Jain poet puts his doubts about the popular Rāmāyaṇa in the mouth of King Śreṇika questioning Gautama. Among the absurdities he rejects are Kumbhakarṇa's six-month sleep, through which elephants could trample him unnoticed, and his devouring of whatever was near on waking (V. M. Kulkarni, *Studies in Jain Literature*). Kumbhakarṇa and the other rākṣasa heroes appear as pious men (Kulkarni, *The Story of Rāma in Jain Literature*, p. 80). He survives the war: after Rāvaṇa's death he is released with Indrajit and Meghavāhana and takes up asceticism (canto 75; Kulkarni, p. 40). See [Jain Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md). [Jain] [scholarly]

## Regional and modern afterlife
- **The third effigy** - Kumbhakarṇa is one of the three figures, with Rāvaṇa and Meghanāda, burned at Rāmlīlā on Daśaharā. Of the three, his plain speech and loyalty draw the most sympathy in retellings. [folk]
- **Idiom of the sleeper** - "Kumbhakarṇa's sleep" / *Kumbhakarṇa nidrā* is a living idiom across Indian languages for one who sleeps prodigiously or is oblivious to crisis. [folk]
- **Iconography** - the awakening and the battle are favorite subjects of Rāmāyaṇa painting. Examples are the Metropolitan Museum's folio "The Awakening of Kumbhakarna in the Golden City of Lanka" (India, ca. 1605; acc. 2002.504) and its Malwa folio "The Demon Kumbhakarna Is Defeated by Rama and Lakshmana" (ca. 1670; acc. 2001.541). [scholarly]
