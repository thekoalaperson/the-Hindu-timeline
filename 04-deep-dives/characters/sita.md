# Sītā

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Sītā** (Jānakī, Vaidehī, Maithilī, Bhūmijā) — the furrow-born princess of Mithilā, wife of Rāma, *aṃśa* of Lakṣmī, and in Śākta tradition the supreme Śakti herself. Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). Her life threads the whole [Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md): birth & the bow-trial in the [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md), exile in the [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/2-ayodhya-kanda.md), abduction in the [Araṇya Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md), the Aśoka grove in the [Sundara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/5-sundara-kanda.md), the agni-parīkṣā in the [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md), and banishment & return in the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md). Variant biographies sit in [Adhyātma & variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* her life falls in the Tretā Yuga of the present 28th Mahāyuga, 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara; the scholarly baseline is the Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa* (cited here by vulgate/Southern sarga throughout), with later Purāṇic and sectarian recensions adding the Māyā-Sītā and Śākta layers (c. 1st millennium CE onward). [scholarly]

## Names, identity, and divine nature
- **Sītā / "the furrow"** - the name itself is the Sanskrit word for the furrow (*sītā*) opened by the plough; she is named for the manner of her finding rather than given a personal birth-name, fusing her identity with agrarian fertility and the Earth's bounty (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa*).
- **Jānakī** - "daughter of Janaka," the most common epithet, after her adoptive father Janaka, king of Mithilā/Videha (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*).
- **Vaidehī / Maithilī** - "she of Videha" / "she of Mithilā," after her kingdom and capital (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*).
- **Bhūmijā / Pārthivī / Dharaṇī-sutā** - "Earth-born," "daughter of the Earth (Pṛthivī/Dharaṇī)," naming Bhūmi/Pṛthvī as her true mother — a relation made literal at her death-return (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa*). See [Pṛthu & Pṛthvī](../prithu-prithvi.md) for the Earth-goddess.
- **Lakṣmī-aṃśa** - the incarnate portion of Lakṣmī (Śrī), born to be consort to Viṣṇu's Rāma-avatāra; the pairing reproduces the eternal Viṣṇu–Lakṣmī bond on earth (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*; emphasized in Vaiṣṇava *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa* and *Rāmcaritmānas*).
- **Subject of the Sītā Upaniṣad** - a minor Upaniṣad attached to the *Atharvaveda* exalts Sītā as Prakṛti/Māyā and the primordial Śakti — *mūla-prakṛti* — from whom creation unfolds, well beyond the role of consort. [late text]

## Birth — ayonijā, born of the furrow
- **Found in the ploughed field** - King Janaka, ploughing a sacrificial field (for a yajña / to bring rain), turns up an infant girl in the furrow; he adopts her as his own daughter and names her Sītā for the furrow that yielded her (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa*, Janaka's own testimony to Viśvāmitra).
- **Ayonijā — "not womb-born"** - because she arises from the earth and not from a human womb, Sītā is *ayonijā*, a being of unmediated purity and auspiciousness; this non-uterine origin is theologically central, marking her as a manifestation rather than an ordinary birth (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*; expanded in Purāṇic and Upaniṣadic readings).
- **Daughter of Bhūmi/Pṛthvī** - the Earth-goddess is understood as her mother; the furrow is the Earth offering up her own child, foreshadowing the final reabsorption (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa*).
- **Sister Ūrmilā; cousins Māṇḍavī & Śrutakīrti** - Janaka's household includes Ūrmilā (Janaka's own daughter, later wife of Lakṣmaṇa) and the daughters of his brother Kuśadhvaja, Māṇḍavī and Śrutakīrti (wed to Bharata and Śatrughna) — the four brides of the four sons of Daśaratha (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa*).
- *Variant — daughter of Rāvaṇa & Mandodarī (Jain & Adbhuta traditions):* in Saṅghadāsa's Jain *Vasudevahiṇḍī*, Guṇabhadra's *Uttarapurāṇa* (9th c. CE), and the *Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*, Sītā is born to Rāvaṇa and his queen Mandodarī; astrologers foretell that this first child will destroy Rāvaṇa's lineage, so the infant is cast out / buried in a distant land (Mithilā), where Janaka discovers her. [disputed]
  - *Sub-variant (Uttarapurāṇa):* the ascetic Manivatī, wronged by Rāvaṇa, vows revenge and is reborn as Rāvaṇa's daughter Sītā — the agent of his doom by design.
- *Variant — born from a drop of blood / sealed jar:* some retellings have Mandodarī conceive after drinking blood (of sages Rāvaṇa had collected), the child sealed in a jar and buried, then ploughed up by Janaka. [folk]

## The bow-trial of Mithilā — a vīrya-śulka, not a swayaṃvara
- **Not a suitor-choice assembly** - Vālmīki's Mithilā contest is a standing **vīrya-śulka** — a bride-price of valour, a condition posted by Janaka years before Rāma arrives, which any man may attempt at any time and which suitors have been failing for years. It is **not a *swayaṃvara***: there is no convened assembly of kings, and Sītā makes no choice. The "Sītā-svayaṃvara" title is later tradition's, and it changes what the scene is about — from a test that Rāma passes to a woman who elects. [scholarly]
- **The bow-test of Mithilā** - Janaka's standing condition is that whoever can string the colossal divine bow of Śiva (Pināka / Hara-dhanu), held in trust by the house of Mithilā, shall win Sītā; no king, deva, or asura had ever even lifted it (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa*). The suitors who fail lay siege to Mithilā for a year in resentment before Rāma ever comes (Bāla 66).
- **Rāma breaks the bow** - escorted by Viśvāmitra, the young Rāma lifts the bow, bends it to string, and snaps it in two with a thunderous report — winning Sītā's hand (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa*). See [Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha](../vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) for the sage who brings Rāma to Mithilā.
- **Quadruple wedding** - Janaka offers Sītā to Rāma and his three sisters/cousins to the three other princes; Daśaratha's four sons wed in one ceremony at Mithilā (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa*).
- *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas / popular tradition):* Sītā first sees Rāma in a garden before the trial and silently chooses him, praying to Gaurī (Pārvatī) for him as husband; the bow-breaking then fulfils her vow. This is where the *swayaṃvara* reading comes from — Tulsīdās gives her the choice Vālmīki does not. [late text]
- *Variant (Adbhuta / Śākta framing):* the contest is read as the Goddess electing her own divine counterpart, the bow a symbol of the cosmic order only the Lakṣmī–Viṣṇu pair can wield.

## Exile — into the Daṇḍaka forest
- **Insistence on accompanying Rāma** - when Kaikeyī's boon sends Rāma into 14 years of forest exile, Sītā refuses to remain in palace comfort, declaring a wife's place is at her husband's side; her speech on conjugal dharma is one of the Rāmāyaṇa's set-pieces (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa*).
- **Forest life with Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa** - the three live as ascetics through the Citrakūṭa and Daṇḍaka wilds, visiting sages' hermitages (Atri & Anasūyā, Agastya, etc.); Anasūyā gifts Sītā celestial garments and unguents that never fade or soil — the ornaments she will later cast down as a trail (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā 117–119; Araṇya 52–54*). See [Agastya](../agastya.md) and [Atri & Anasūyā](../atri-anasuya.md).
- **The counsel on ahiṃsā (Araṇya 9)** - Leaving Sutīkṣṇa's hermitage, Sītā puts to Rāma the sharpest ethical challenge anyone in the epic puts to him. He has promised the sages he will clear the Daṇḍaka of rākṣasas: but the demons **have not attacked him**, and to bear arms against those who have not wronged you, she says, is the third of the great faults born of desire. She tells him the parable of an ascetic to whom Indra entrusted a sword for safekeeping, who wore it constantly, grew cruel through the mere carrying of it, and lost his austerity — *the weapon makes the man*. "Do not kill the rākṣasas of Daṇḍaka without enmity," she says; a kṣatriya's bow in a forest of hermits is fire brought near dry wood (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya* **9**).
  - Rāma's answer — his keystone statement that a promise given to the helpless outranks Lakṣmaṇa, outranks Sītā, outranks his own life — is **Araṇya 10**, and it belongs with her challenge: the two sargas are a single exchange. She has spoken to him "in adoration," and he does not dismiss her; he argues.
- **Śūrpaṇakhā and the chain of vengeance** - the rākṣasī Śūrpaṇakhā's advances are rebuffed and she is mutilated by Lakṣmaṇa; her complaint to her brother Rāvaṇa, inflamed by her praise of Sītā's beauty, sets the abduction in motion (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa*).

## Abduction — the golden deer and the Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā
- **The golden deer (Mārīca)** - the rākṣasa Mārīca, on Rāvaṇa's order, takes the form of a dazzling golden deer; Sītā, enchanted, begs Rāma to capture it, drawing him away from the hermitage (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa*).
- **The false cry and Lakṣmaṇa's departure** - the dying Mārīca mimics Rāma's voice crying for help; Sītā, fearing for Rāma, drives a reluctant Lakṣmaṇa to go after him, leaving her unguarded (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa*).
- **Rāvaṇa's abduction** - disguised as a mendicant, Rāvaṇa seizes Sītā and carries her off in a **golden chariot yoked to mules** — never called the Puṣpaka in Vālmīki; the vulture-king **Jaṭāyu** fights to stop him, shatters the chariot and kills the mules, and is mortally wounded, after which Rāvaṇa completes the flight **carrying her bodily** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya* 49–52).
- **She does not go quietly** - Vālmīki gives her four sustained speeches of defiance, and they are the record of her agency: she denounces Rāvaṇa in flight and calls the trees and the river Godāvarī to witness (Araṇya **47–49**); she refuses him again on arrival, telling him to his face that he cannot escape Rāma (**53**); and at the twelve-month ultimatum she places a **blade of grass between them** and answers his every argument point by point, fearless and haggard with grief (**56**). The grass-blade is contempt made visible — she will not address him directly.
- **Sītā's dropped tokens** - en route she casts down her ornaments — Anasūyā's gifts — bundled in her upper cloth onto the Ṛṣyamūka hilltop where five vānaras sit, to leave a trail; Sugrīva keeps them, and they are the clue that points the search toward Laṅkā (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya* 54; *Kiṣkindhā* 6). It is Sītā's own doing, and it is what makes the alliance possible.
- *Variant — the Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā:* in later and popular retellings (*Rāmcaritmānas* and folk tradition, not the Vālmīki critical text), Lakṣmaṇa draws a protective line around the hut that Sītā must not cross; Rāvaṇa-as-mendicant tricks her into stepping over it to give alms. [late text][folk]

## In Laṅkā — the Aśoka grove
- **Captive in the Aśokavanikā** - Rāvaṇa confines Sītā in the Aśoka grove (Aśoka-vana / Aśokavāṭikā), guarded by rākṣasīs, refusing his court and palace; she sits beneath a śiṃśapā tree in grief and fasting (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Sundara Kāṇḍa*).
- **Steadfast rejection of Rāvaṇa** - Rāvaṇa alternately woos and threatens her under a **twelve-month ultimatum** set at Araṇya 56, of which "two months remain" when Hanumān finds her; Sītā never wavers. At **Sundara 22** she tells him what her fidelity costs him: *she could burn him to ashes herself by the power of her austerity, and does not only because Rāma has not commanded it and she will not spend her tapas* — a threat, not a plea.
  - **Why Rāvaṇa cannot force her — two curses, not one** - The corpus and the retellings routinely fuse three separate things. (1) **Nalakūbara's curse** over the apsarā **Rambhā**: if Rāvaṇa ever again forces an unwilling woman, his head will split into **seven** pieces (Uttara **26**). (2) **Brahmā's curse** over the apsarā **Puñjikasthalā**: the same condition, but **a hundred** pieces — and *this* is the one **Rāvaṇa himself names**, in his own voice, in his own war-council, as the reason he cannot compel her (Yuddha **13.11–15**). (3) **Vedavatī's curse** is a different thing altogether: not a restraint on his hands but a sentence of death, that she would be reborn to destroy him. Sītā's bodily inviolability rests on (1) and (2); her identity as his doom rests on (3). See [Rāvaṇa §V](./ravana.md).
- **Hanumān's meeting, and the sign only Rāma knows** - Hanumān, leaping to Laṅkā, finds Sītā in the grove, gives Rāma's signet ring as proof, and offers to carry her back; she refuses, holding that Rāma himself must come and win her honourably — and that she will not willingly touch another man's body. She sends back her **cūḍāmaṇi** and, with it, a **private token no one else could know**: the **Kākāsura** story, of the crow (a son of Indra) that pecked her at Citrakūṭa, and how Rāma loosed a blade of grass charged with the brahmāstra that pursued it through the three worlds until it surrendered its right eye (*Sundara* **38**). Vālmīki gives her **two** such private proofs — the crow episode and the **manaḥśilā tilaka** Rāma once drew on her cheek (Sundara 40, relayed at 67) — not one. See the [Sundara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/5-sundara-kanda.md).
- **Vedavatī / earlier-birth link** - Sītā is identified in places with **Vedavatī**, daughter of the sage **Kuśadhvaja, son of Bṛhaspati** — a chaste ascetic doing tapas to win Viṣṇu as husband, who cut off the hair Rāvaṇa had defiled by seizing, entered the fire, and vowed to be **reborn to cause his death** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa* **17**). This dovetails with the Māyā-Sītā theology below. Full treatment of the sarga, its stratum and the rebirth-identification: [Vedavatī](./vedavati.md).
  - *Name-collision, flagged:* Vedavatī's father **Kuśadhvaja son of Bṛhaspati** is **not** the Kuśadhvaja who is Janaka's brother and the father of Māṇḍavī and Śrutakīrti (Bāla 71–73). Two unrelated men of the same name stand at either end of Sītā's two biographies, and the coincidence is easy to mistake for a link. [scholarly] See [Bharata, Śatrughna & their wives](./bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md).

## Mercy in victory, and the fire ordeal
- **She spares the rākṣasīs who tormented her (Yuddha 113)** - Sent by Rāma with the news of victory, Hanumān stands before her with joined hands and asks her leave to **kill the demonesses** who had threatened and abused her in the grove — and describes at length exactly how he would do it. Sītā *reflects and ponders*, and then refuses. The epic calls her, in that moment, **kṛpaṇā** and **dīna-vatsalā** — moved to pity, tender toward the wretched. They acted, she says, under their king's orders and are not to be blamed; and a noble soul takes no account of injuries done by others. Her one act of power in the whole war is an act of clemency (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha* **113.29–43**).
- **The first ordeal after Laṅkā's fall** - after Rāvaṇa is slain, Rāma receives Sītā coldly, voicing public doubt over her chastity during captivity; wounded, Sītā asks Lakṣmaṇa to build a pyre and enters the flames to prove her purity (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa* 116–118).
- **Agni returns her unharmed** - the fire-god Agni rises bodily from the pyre bearing an unscathed Sītā, testifying to her unblemished virtue; Rāma accepts her, saying (in the canonical apologia) that he never doubted her but staged the ordeal for the world's eyes (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa*).
- **Theological function** - the fire ordeal becomes the hinge for the entire Māyā-Sītā doctrine: if the true Sītā was hidden in fire all along, the ordeal is the moment of her restoration, not a test (see below).
- *Variant (Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa, Śākta):* the fire ordeal is reframed — Sītā's divinity as Śakti makes the test a revelation of her supremacy rather than a trial of her honour.

### The Māyā-Sītā / Chāyā-Sītā tradition — catalog of variants
- *Variant (Kūrma Purāṇa):* as Rāvaṇa approaches, Sītā prays to Agni, who creates **Māyā-Sītā**, an exact illusory double; Rāvaṇa abducts only the phantom while Agni hides the true Sītā in his keeping. At the agni-parīkṣā, the illusion enters the fire and dissolves, and Agni restores the real, untouched Sītā to Rāma. [late text]
- *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, c. 14th c., part of Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa):* foreknowing the abduction, **Rāma instructs Sītā to enter fire and leave her chāyā (shadow-form) outside the hut**; Māyā-Sītā is abducted and dwells in Laṅkā for a year while the true Sītā stays hidden in fire. The agni-parīkṣā is the moment the real Sītā emerges and the shadow is withdrawn — recasting the "ordeal" as a reunion. [late text]
- *Variant (Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, c. 9th–11th c.):* the Māyā-Sītā motif protects Sītā's purity from Rāvaṇa's touch; after the fire, the illusory Sītā is **not destroyed but reborn as Padmāvatī (Veṅkaṭeśvara's consort at Tirupati)**. [late text]
  - *Sub-variant (Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa):* similarly continues the Māyā-Sītā into the Padmāvatī / Tirupati cycle, linking the Rāmāyaṇa to South Indian Vaiṣṇava temple lore.
- *Variant (general Vaiṣṇava reading):* the doctrine exists to resolve the theological discomfort that Lakṣmī-incarnate could be defiled — the Goddess herself is never truly in Rāvaṇa's power, only her projected image. [scholarly]

## Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa — Sītā as Mahākālī (Śākta variant)
- **The thousand-headed Rāvaṇa** - beyond the familiar ten-headed Rāvaṇa, a far mightier brother, **Sahasra-Rāvaṇa** (thousand-headed), rules a farther Laṅkā; he strikes Rāma unconscious on the field, a foe beyond Rāma's power (*Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*). [late text]
- **Sītā becomes Mahākālī** - to save Rāma, Sītā assumes the terrifying form of **Mahākālī**, severs Sahasra-Rāvaṇa's thousand heads in an instant, and rampages over the rākṣasas — the climactic feminine-Śakti inversion of the epic (*Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*).
- **The gods calm her; Rāma's divine vision** - Brahmā, Śiva, and the devas pacify her fury; Brahmā restores Rāma and grants him sight of Sītā's true nature — her Śakti surpassing even Viṣṇu's — establishing the *Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa* as a Śākta scripture with Sītā as supreme deity (*Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*, 27 sargas / ~1,355 verses). [late text]

## Banishment — the second exile and Lava-Kuśa
- **The slander — reported by the courtier Bhadra** - during Rāma's reign in Ayodhyā, Rāma asks his companion **Bhadra** what the city says of him, and Bhadra reports the talk: that the king has taken back a wife who lived in another man's house. In the **vulgate text no washerman appears at all** — the *rajaka* scolding his own wife is **Padma-Purāṇa and folk elaboration**, so universal that it has displaced the text's own scene. To satisfy public opinion, Rāma resolves to send the pregnant Sītā away (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa* 43–45; the washerman: *Padma Purāṇa* and later retelling). [folk]
- **Abandoned near Vālmīki's hermitage** - Lakṣmaṇa, on Rāma's order, leaves Sītā in the forest near the āśrama of the sage **Vālmīki**, who shelters her; the banishment is done without the second ordeal she had already passed (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa*).
- **Birth of the twins Kuśa & Lava** - Sītā bears twin sons at the hermitage; Vālmīki rears them and teaches them the *Rāmāyaṇa* itself, which they later recite, note-perfect, before Rāma's court (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa*).
- **The Aśvamedha and recognition** - at Rāma's horse-sacrifice the boys sing the epic; Rāma recognizes his sons and summons Sītā to publicly reaffirm her purity once more (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa*).
- *Variant (Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa & later texts):* a "second agni-parīkṣā" is demanded; Sītā refuses to undergo a humiliating re-trial, having already proved herself, and chooses the Earth instead. [late text]

## Return to the Earth — the final departure
- **The oath to Mādhavī (Bhūmi)** - summoned to swear her chastity before the assembly, Sītā instead calls on her mother the Earth (Bhūmi/Mādhavī) to take her back if she has been ever true to Rāma alone (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa*).
- **The Earth opens** - the ground splits, a golden throne rises bearing Bhūmi-devī, who embraces Sītā and draws her down into the earth — her ultimate vindication and release from an unjust world (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa*). She returns to the element that bore her, closing the furrow-to-earth arc.
- **Reunion in death / Vaikuṇṭha** - Rāma, bereft, soon renounces the world; in Vaiṣṇava theology Sītā as Lakṣmī rejoins Viṣṇu-Rāma in Vaikuṇṭha, the earthly separation healed in the eternal pairing (*Uttara Kāṇḍa*; Vaiṣṇava commentary).
- *Variant (sectarian / Śākta):* her return to Earth is read not as defeat but as the Goddess withdrawing into Prakṛti, the source from which she emerged — sovereign to the last.

## Cultural and theological significance
- **The dharmic ideal and its critique** - Sītā is upheld in classical and devotional tradition as the model of wifely fidelity (*pativratā*) and forbearance; modern and feminist readings instead foreground the injustice of her ordeals and banishment, making her a figure of dignified protest. [scholarly]
- **Worship** - venerated with Rāma (Rāma-Sītā / Sītā-Rāma), and independently as a goddess; her birth is celebrated as **Sītā Navamī / Jānakī Navamī**, and her wedding as **Vivāha Pañcamī** (*regional festival calendars*). See the [festivals/calendar materials](../../README.md).
- **Open-ended roster note** - regional and folk Rāmāyaṇas (Tamil Kamban, Telugu, Bengali Krittibāsī, Thai Ramakien, Southeast Asian, tribal and Dalit retellings) multiply Sītā's biography in ways too numerous to fully catalog here; the variants above are the principal scriptural and pan-Indian ones. [folk][open-ended]
