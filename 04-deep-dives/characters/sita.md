# Sītā

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Sītā** (Jānakī, Vaidehī, Maithilī, Bhūmijā) — the furrow-born princess of Mithilā, wife of Rāma, *aṃśa* of Lakṣmī, and in Śākta tradition the supreme Śakti herself. Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). Her life threads the whole [Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md): birth & the bow-trial in the [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md), exile in the [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/2-ayodhya-kanda.md), abduction in the [Araṇya Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md), the Aśoka grove in the [Sundara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/5-sundara-kanda.md), the agni-parīkṣā in the [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md), and banishment & return in the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md). Variant biographies sit in [Adhyātma & variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* her life falls in the Tretā Yuga of the present 28th Mahāyuga, 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara; the scholarly baseline is the Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa*, with later Purāṇic and sectarian recensions adding the Māyā-Sītā and Śākta layers (c. 1st millennium CE onward). [scholarly]
> Numbering: Vālmīki is cited by the vulgate/Southern sarga and verse — the valmikiramayan.net text for Bāla–Yuddha, Hari Prasad Shastri's translation for the Uttara Kāṇḍa — with the Baroda Critical Edition (CE, GRETIL text) in square brackets where it was checked.

## Names, identity, and divine nature
- **Sītā / "the furrow"** - the name itself is the Sanskrit word for the furrow (*sītā*) opened by the plough; Janaka says she was named for the plough-furrow she rose from (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla* 66.13–14 [CE 1.65.14]), fusing her identity with agrarian fertility and the Earth's bounty.
- **Jānakī** - "daughter of Janaka," the most common epithet, after her adoptive father Janaka, king of Mithilā/Videha (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*).
- **Vaidehī / Maithilī** - "she of Videha" / "she of Mithilā," after her kingdom and capital (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*).
- **Bhūmijā / Pārthivī / Dharaṇī-sutā** - "Earth-born," "daughter of the Earth (Pṛthivī/Dharaṇī)," naming Bhūmi/Pṛthvī as her true mother. Sītā herself throws this birth at Rāma during the fire ordeal — he has not honoured her birth from the earth (*Yuddha* 116.15) — and it is made literal at her departure, when the goddess Mādhavī/Dharaṇī takes her back (*Uttara* 97). See [Pṛthu & Pṛthvī](../prithu-prithvi.md) for the Earth-goddess.
- **Lakṣmī-aṃśa** - the incarnate portion of Lakṣmī (Śrī), born to be consort to Viṣṇu's Rāma-avatāra. In Vālmīki the identification is explicit at one point: Brahmā's hymn after the fire ordeal, *sītā lakṣmīr bhavān viṣṇuḥ* — "Sītā is Lakṣmī, you are Viṣṇu" (*Yuddha* 117.28 [CE 6.105.25]); Anasūyā earlier compares her to Śrī adorning Viṣṇu (*Ayodhyā* 118.20). The identification becomes pervasive in the Vaiṣṇava *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa* and *Rāmcaritmānas*.
- **Subject of the Sītā Upaniṣad** - a minor Upaniṣad attached to the *Atharvaveda* exalts Sītā as Prakṛti/Māyā and the primordial Śakti — *mūla-prakṛti* — from whom creation unfolds, well beyond the role of consort. [late text]

## Birth — ayonijā, born of the furrow
- **Found in the ploughed field** - King Janaka, ploughing and clearing a field (*kṣetraṃ śodhayatā* — translators and commentators take it as preparing ritual ground), turns up an infant girl in the furrow; he raises her as his own daughter and names her Sītā (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla* 66.13–15 [CE 1.65.14–15], Janaka's own testimony to Viśvāmitra). The drought-and-rain motive often attached to the ploughing is not in Vālmīki. [folk]
  - *Sītā's own telling (Ayodhyā 118.27–33 [CE 2.110.26–32]):* the king was scattering handfuls of seed with plough in hand when she came up "splitting the earth," her limbs covered in dust; the childless Janaka took her on his lap and called her his daughter, a voice from the sky confirmed she was his daughter "by dharma," and he gave her into the care of his senior queen. Vālmīki does not name that queen; the name Sunayanā belongs to later tradition.
- **Ayonijā — "not womb-born"** - the word is Vālmīki's own: Janaka calls her *ayonijā* (*Bāla* 66.15 and 66.26 [CE 1.65.15, 27]), and Sītā says her father could find no fitting husband for a girl not born of a womb (*Ayodhyā* 118.37). Arising from the earth and not from a human womb marks her as a manifestation rather than an ordinary birth; Purāṇic and Upaniṣadic readings expand the point.
- **Daughter of Bhūmi/Pṛthvī** - the Earth-goddess is understood as her mother; the furrow is the Earth offering up her own child, foreshadowing the final reabsorption (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa* 97).
- **Sister Ūrmilā; cousins Māṇḍavī & Śrutakīrti** - Janaka's household includes Ūrmilā — Janaka's own daughter, whom Sītā calls "my younger sister" (*Ayodhyā* 118.53), later wife of Lakṣmaṇa (*Bāla* 71.20–22) — and the daughters of his brother Kuśadhvaja, Māṇḍavī and Śrutakīrti (wed to Bharata and Śatrughna, *Bāla* 72–73) — the four brides of the four sons of Daśaratha. See [Ūrmilā](./urmila.md).
- *Variant — daughter of Rāvaṇa & Mandodarī, cast out on a prophecy (Jain):* in Saṅghadāsa's Prakrit *Vasudevahiṇḍī* and Guṇabhadra's *Uttarapurāṇa* (9th c. CE), Sītā is born to Rāvaṇa and his queen Mandodarī; astrologers foretell that the child will ruin Rāvaṇa, so the infant is abandoned and buried in a distant land (in the *Uttarapurāṇa*, in a casket in the ground of Mithilā), where Janaka discovers her. [disputed]
  - *Sub-variant (Uttarapurāṇa):* the ascetic Manivatī, whose austerity Rāvaṇa had disturbed, vows revenge and is reborn as Rāvaṇa's daughter Sītā — the agent of his doom by design.
- *Variant — born of the sages' blood (Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa, canto 8):* Rāvaṇa bleeds the forest sages with arrow-points and collects the blood in a pitcher — the pitcher in which the sage Gṛtsamada had charged milk with mantras so that Lakṣmī would be born as his daughter. Rāvaṇa leaves it with Mandodarī as deadlier than poison; neglected by him, she drinks it to die, conceives instead, and, unable to explain a pregnancy in his absence, flies to Kurukṣetra and buries the embryo there. Janaka later ploughs that very field for a sacrifice and finds Sītā. The frame is Nārada's curse on Lakṣmī (cantos 5–7) and Rāvaṇa's boon from Brahmā that he will die if he ever desires his own daughter and she refuses him. No astrologers figure in this version. [late text]

## The bow-trial of Mithilā — a vīrya-śulka, not a swayaṃvara
- **Two accounts inside Vālmīki** - Janaka's account to Viśvāmitra presents a standing **vīrya-śulka** — "valour is the bride-price" (*Bāla* 66.14b–17a [CE 1.65.15–17]) — a condition posted years before Rāma arrives, which suitors have been failing; nothing in *Bāla* 66–67 shows Sītā choosing. But Sītā's own account to Anasūyā calls the same event a **svayaṃvara** three times — Anasūyā's question (*Ayodhyā* 118.24), Janaka's resolve "I shall hold a svayaṃvara for my daughter" (118.38), and Sītā's closing line, "thus was I given to Rāma at that svayaṃvara" (118.54) [CE 2.110.23, 37, 52; Anasūyā uses the word again in her reply, CE 2.111.2] — and has Janaka announce the condition to the kings in assembly (*samavāye narendrāṇām*, 118.41). The word is therefore not a later invention. What neither Vālmīki account gives her is a choice: in both she is the prize of a test, and the later tradition that makes her choose (below) changes what the scene is about. [scholarly]
- **The bow-test of Mithilā** - Janaka's standing condition is that whoever can string the colossal divine bow of Śiva shall win Sītā. Śiva had brandished it at Dakṣa's sacrifice and then left it in the keeping of **Devarāta**, sixth king from Nimi (*Bāla* 66.8–12 [CE 1.65.8–13]). The visiting kings could neither grasp nor even shift it (66.18b–19a); the rejected suitors then besieged Mithilā for a full year until Janaka's resources were exhausted and the gods, propitiated by his austerity, gave him an army that scattered them (66.20–24).
  - *Intra-Vālmīki variant:* in Sītā's telling the bow, with two inexhaustible quivers, was a gift to Janaka from **Varuṇa** at a great sacrifice, not Śiva's bow held in trust (*Ayodhyā* 118.39 [CE 2.110.38]). The two accounts are both Vālmīki's and are not harmonised in the text.
- **Rāma breaks the bow** - escorted by Viśvāmitra, the young Rāma grasps the bow at its middle "as if in play," strings it, and draws it until it snaps in the middle with a thunderous report (*Bāla* 67.15–17); in Sītā's telling he bends it "in the blink of an eye" (*Ayodhyā* 118.47–49). Rāma declines her hand until his father's consent is known (118.51). See [Viśvāmitra & Vasiṣṭha](../vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) for the sage who brings Rāma to Mithilā.
- **Quadruple wedding** - Janaka gives Sītā to Rāma and Ūrmilā to Lakṣmaṇa (*Bāla* 71.20–22), and on Viśvāmitra's proposal Kuśadhvaja's daughters to Bharata and Śatrughna (72); the four sons of Daśaratha wed in one ceremony at Mithilā. At the rite Janaka hands her over with the words *iyaṃ sītā mama sutā sahadharmacarī tava* — "this is Sītā, my daughter, your partner in dharma" (73.26–27).
- *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas, Bāla Kāṇḍa, the flower-garden episode):* Sītā first sees Rāma in a garden before the trial and silently chooses him, praying to Gaurī (Pārvatī) for him as husband; the bow-breaking then fulfils her vow. Tulsīdās gives her an inner choice that neither Vālmīki account gives her. [late text]
- *Variant (Kamban, Tamil Irāmāvatāram, c. 12th c.):* earlier still, as Rāma walks through Mithilā with Viśvāmitra, he and Sītā see each other and fall in love before any trial — "the lord looked, and she looked" (*aṇṇalum nōkkiṉāṉ; avaḷum nōkkiṉāḷ*, Pāla kāṇṭam, *Mithilaik kāṭcip paṭalam* v.35). [regional]
- *Note (Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa):* the *Adbhuta* skips the bow-trial and the wedding entirely, opening its Rāma story with Paraśurāma's confrontation on the road home (canto 9). Readings of the contest as the Goddess electing her own divine counterpart are a Śākta interpretation, not this text's narrative. [scholarly]

## Exile — into the Daṇḍaka forest
- **Insistence on accompanying Rāma** - when Kaikeyī's boon sends Rāma into 14 years of forest exile, Sītā refuses to remain in palace comfort. She is "angry out of love" (*Ayodhyā* 27.1): a wife alone shares her husband's fortune (27.4), and she will walk ahead of him treading down the thorns (27.6). She reveals that brāhmaṇas reading her marks, and a female mendicant in her mother's presence, had foretold in her father's house that she would live in a forest (29.8–13). When he still refuses, she taunts him — "What did my father think, getting as son-in-law a woman in a man's form?" (30.3) — likens herself to Sāvitrī following Satyavān (30.6), and asks whether he means to hand his own wife over to others like an actor (30.8). Her speeches on conjugal dharma are among the Rāmāyaṇa's set-pieces (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā* 26–30).
- **Forest life with Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa** - the three live as ascetics through the Citrakūṭa and Daṇḍaka wilds, visiting sages' hermitages (Atri & Anasūyā, Agastya, etc.). Anasūyā gives Sītā a garland, garments, ornaments and a celestial unguent as gifts of love (*Ayodhyā* 118.18–21), and asks for the story of her marriage, which Sītā tells (118.24–54; the visit spans *Ayodhyā* 117–119). Vālmīki does not say that the ornaments Sītā later drops at Ṛṣyamūka are Anasūyā's gifts; that link is a reader's inference. See [Agastya](../agastya.md), [Atri & Anasūyā](../atri-anasuya.md) and [the forest sages](./sharabhanga-sutikshna-and-the-forest-sages.md).
- **The counsel on ahiṃsā (Araṇya 9)** - Leaving Sutīkṣṇa's hermitage, Sītā puts to Rāma the sharpest ethical challenge anyone in the epic puts to him. He has promised the sages he will kill the rākṣasas of the Daṇḍaka (9.10–11): but the demons **have not attacked him**, and violence against others' lives *without enmity* (*nirvairam*), she says, is the third of the faults born of desire, after falsehood and going to another's wife (9.3–9). She tells him the parable of an ascetic to whom Indra, disguised as a soldier, entrusted a sword for safekeeping; carrying it everywhere, the hermit grew cruel, lost his resolve for austerity and went to hell (9.16–22) — keeping weapons close is like keeping fire close (9.23), as a bow and fuel near a fire make it blaze (9.15). Killing the offenceless forest-dwellers is not desirable (9.24–25); a kṣatriya's bow in the forest is for protecting the afflicted (9.26). She closes by calling her words a woman's fickleness — who could teach him dharma? — and asks him to decide with his brother (9.33) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya* **9**).
  - Rāma's answer — he praises her counsel as worthy of her family (10.2), and then gives his keystone statement that he would sooner give up his life, or her, or Lakṣmaṇa, than a promise made, above all to brāhmaṇas (10.18b–19a) — is **Araṇya 10**, and it belongs with her challenge: the two sargas are a single exchange. She has spoken to him "in devotion to her husband" (10.1), and he does not dismiss her; he argues.
- **Śūrpaṇakhā and the chain of vengeance** - the rākṣasī Śūrpaṇakhā's advances are rebuffed and she is mutilated by Lakṣmaṇa; her complaint to her brother Rāvaṇa, inflamed by her praise of Sītā's beauty, sets the abduction in motion (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa*). See [Śūrpaṇakhā](./shurpanakha.md).

## Abduction — the golden deer and the Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā
- **The golden deer (Mārīca)** - the rākṣasa Mārīca, on Rāvaṇa's order, takes the form of a dazzling golden deer; Lakṣmaṇa at once suspects it is Mārīca, but Sītā, enchanted, sets his warning aside and begs Rāma to capture it, drawing him away from the hermitage (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya* 43). See [Mārīca](./maricha.md).
- **The false cry and Lakṣmaṇa's departure** - the dying Mārīca mimics Rāma's voice crying for help. Sītā, fearing for Rāma, drives a reluctant Lakṣmaṇa after him in words of startling harshness: he is an enemy in a friend's guise who wants Rāma to perish for her sake (*Araṇya* 45.5–7); he followed Rāma into the forest covertly, to get her, or as Bharata's agent (45.21–24); she will drown herself in the Godāvarī, hang herself, take poison or enter fire before she touches another man (45.36–37). Lakṣmaṇa answers that she is a deity to him, calls the forest-beings to witness, and leaves her unguarded (45.28–31, 40). See [Lakṣmaṇa](./lakshmana.md).
- **Rāvaṇa's abduction** - disguised as a mendicant, Rāvaṇa seizes Sītā by the hair and the thighs (*Araṇya* 49.17) and carries her off in a magical (*māyāmaya*), golden chariot yoked to braying asses (*khara*, often translated "mules") (49.19) — it is not called the Puṣpaka in this scene. The vulture-king **Jaṭāyu** fights to stop him, shatters the chariot, kills the goblin-faced asses and the charioteer, and is mortally wounded, after which Rāvaṇa completes the flight holding her against his side (*Araṇya* 49–52, the fight at 51). See [Jaṭāyu & Sampāti](./jatayu-and-sampati.md).
- **She does not go quietly** - Vālmīki gives her four sustained speeches of defiance, and they are the record of her agency: at the hermitage, once he reveals himself, she denounces him and refuses his offers (*Araṇya* **47–48**); as she is seized she calls on the trees of Janasthāna and the river Godāvarī to tell Rāma (49); in the air she taunts him as a thief who dares not face her husband in fair fight (**53**); and in Laṅkā, placing a **blade of grass between them**, she answers his every argument point by point, fearless and haggard with grief (**56.1** ff.), after which he sets the twelve-month limit, on pain of being cut up for his breakfast (56.24–25). She repeats the grass-blade gesture when he comes to her in the Aśoka grove (*Sundara* 21.2). The grass-blade is contempt made visible — she will not address him directly.
- **Sītā's dropped tokens** - en route she bundles her ornaments in her silk upper cloth and drops them among five vānaras on a hilltop, to leave a trail for Rāma (*Araṇya* 54.1–3); Sugrīva keeps them and later shows them to Rāma, and, in the vulgate, Lakṣmaṇa can recognise only the anklets, because he had always bowed at her feet (*Kiṣkindhā* 6.22; the verse is not in the Critical Edition, which has Rāma alone lament over the ornaments [CE 4.6.13–19]). It is Sītā's own doing, and it is what makes the alliance possible. See [Sugrīva & Vālī](./sugriva-and-vali.md).
- *Variant — the Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā:* the protective line that Sītā must not cross, which Rāvaṇa-as-mendicant tricks her into crossing to give alms, is not in Vālmīki. Tulsīdās's *Rāmcaritmānas* does not narrate it in the Araṇya Kāṇḍa either; it is alluded to only in Mandodarī's taunt to Rāvaṇa in the Laṅkā Kāṇḍa (cited as Laṅkā 35.1 in the secondary literature; the verse was not checked here). The full episode belongs to later popular retellings, such as the *Rādheśyām Rāmāyaṇa*, and to folk tradition. [late text][folk]
- *Variant (Kamban, Āraṇiya kāṇṭam, Irāvaṇaṉ cūḻccip paṭalam vv.72–73):* Rāvaṇa does not touch her — mindful of Brahmā's words — but digs up the ground a yojana deep and carries it off, hermitage and all, on his chariot. [regional]

## In Laṅkā — the Aśoka grove
- **Captive in the Aśokavanikā** - Rāvaṇa confines Sītā in the Aśoka grove (Aśoka-vana / Aśokavāṭikā), guarded by rākṣasīs, after she refuses his palace (*Araṇya* 55–56); there Hanumān finds her beneath a śiṃśapā tree, wasted by grief and fasting (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Sundara Kāṇḍa*).
- **Steadfast rejection of Rāvaṇa** - Rāvaṇa alternately woos and threatens her under the **twelve-month ultimatum** set at *Araṇya* 56.24; when he comes to her in the grove he tells her two months of it remain (*Sundara* 22.8–9). She tells him to turn his mind back to his own wives and seek Rāma's friendship (21.3 ff.). At **Sundara 22.20** she tells him what her fidelity costs him: *she could burn him to ashes herself by her own power, and does not only because Rāma has not commanded it and she will not spend her tapas* — a threat, not a plea.
  - **Why Rāvaṇa cannot force her — two curses, not one** - The corpus and the retellings routinely fuse three separate things. (1) **Nalakūbara's curse** over the apsarā **Rambhā**: if Rāvaṇa ever again forces an unwilling woman, his head will split into **seven** pieces (*Uttara* **26** [CE 7.26.42–44, *saptadhā*]). (2) **Brahmā's curse** over the apsarā **Puñjikasthalā**: the same condition, but **a hundred** pieces (*śatadhā*) — and *this* is the one **Rāvaṇa himself names**, in his own voice, in his own war-council, as the reason he cannot compel her (*Yuddha* **13.11–15**, the curse at 13.14). (3) **Vedavatī's curse** is a different thing altogether: not a restraint on his hands but a sentence of death, that she would be reborn to destroy him. Sītā's bodily inviolability rests on (1) and (2); her identity as his doom rests on (3). See [Rāvaṇa §V](./ravana.md).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna):* Brahmā tells Rāma that he protected Sītā through **Nalakūbara's** curse, but in the hundred-piece form — the Mahābhārata gives one curse where Vālmīki has two (*Mbh* 3.275.32–33, BORI CE).
- **Despair, and Trijaṭā's dream** - after Rāvaṇa's visit Sītā resolves to die (*Sundara* 26) and, remembering the two-month limit, resolves to hang herself with her own long braid (28.17–18), until auspicious omens stop her; the rākṣasī Trijaṭā meanwhile tells the guards her dream of Rāma's victory (27). See [Trijaṭā](./trijata.md).
- **Hanumān's meeting, and the sign only Rāma knows** - Hanumān, leaping to Laṅkā, finds Sītā in the grove and gives her Rāma's signet ring as proof (*Sundara* 36). He offers to carry her back on his back; she refuses, holding that Rāma himself must come and win her honourably, and that out of devotion to her husband she will not willingly touch another man's body — Rāvaṇa's touch was forced on her, when she was helpless (37.60–61). She sends back her **cūḍāmaṇi** and, with it, a **private token no one else could know**: the **Kākāsura** story, of the crow, "said to be a son of Indra" (38.28), that pecked her at Citrakūṭa, and how Rāma loosed a blade of darbha grass charged with the brahmāstra (38.30) that pursued it through the three worlds until it gave up its right eye to save its life (38.37) (*Sundara* **38**). Vālmīki gives her **two** such private proofs — the crow episode and the **manaḥśilā tilaka** Rāma once painted on her cheek when the mark on her forehead was rubbed away (*Sundara* 40.4–5, relayed at 67) — not one. See the [Sundara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/5-sundara-kanda.md).
- **The war seen from the grove** - Rāvaṇa has the sorcerer Vidyujjihva conjure a severed head and bow of Rāma and shows them to her; she faints, and then begs Rāvaṇa to kill her too (*Yuddha* 31–32). The kindly rākṣasī **Saramā**, one of her guards (Vibhīṣaṇa's wife, according to some commentators), tells her it was an illusion and that Rāma is alive (33). Later, on Rāvaṇa's order, she is flown over the battlefield in the Puṣpaka to see Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa lying bound by Indrajit's arrows; she recalls the soothsayers' promise that she would never be widowed, and Trijaṭā persuades her they are alive (47–48). See [Saramā](./sarama.md) and [Indrajit](./indrajit-meghnad.md).
- **Vedavatī / earlier-birth link** - Sītā is identified in places with **Vedavatī**, daughter of the sage **Kuśadhvaja, son of Bṛhaspati** — a chaste ascetic doing tapas to win Viṣṇu as husband, who cut off the hair Rāvaṇa had defiled by seizing, entered the fire, and vowed to be **reborn, not of a womb (*ayonijā*), to cause his death** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa* **17** [CE 7.17.25–27]). This dovetails with the Māyā-Sītā theology below. Full treatment of the sarga, its stratum and the rebirth-identification: [Vedavatī](./vedavati.md).
  - *Name-collision, flagged:* Vedavatī's father **Kuśadhvaja son of Bṛhaspati** is **not** the Kuśadhvaja who is Janaka's brother and the father of Māṇḍavī and Śrutakīrti (Bāla 71–73). Two unrelated men of the same name stand at either end of Sītā's two biographies, and the coincidence is easy to mistake for a link. [scholarly] See [Bharata, Śatrughna & their wives](./bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md).

## Mercy in victory, and the fire ordeal
- **She spares the rākṣasīs who tormented her (Yuddha 113)** - Sent by Rāma with the news of victory, Hanumān stands before her with joined hands and asks her leave to **kill the demonesses** who had threatened and abused her in the grove — and describes at length exactly how he would do it (113.29–38). Sītā reflects, and then refuses. Who would be angry with servant-women who act on their king's orders? Her suffering, she says, came from her own fate and past deeds (113.40–42). She cites the old maxim a bear once spoke to a tiger: the good do not repay evil with evil, for good conduct is their ornament (113.44–45). And she adds: a noble person must show compassion to sinners and good people alike, even to those who deserve death, for no one is without fault (*na kaścin nāparādhyati*, 113.46). Her one act of power in the whole war is an act of clemency (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha* **113.29–46** [CE 6.101.23–37]). The vulgate introduces her reply with the epithets **kṛpaṇā** and **dīna-vatsalā** — pitiable herself, and tender toward the wretched (113.39). The Critical Edition lacks them and reads simply "Vaidehī, daughter of Janaka" (CE 6.101.29).
- **The first ordeal after Laṅkā's fall** - after Rāvaṇa is slain, Rāma receives Sītā coldly before the assembled host (*Yuddha* 115 [CE 6.103]). The war, he says, was fought to restore his honour, not for her. A woman under suspicion is as unwelcome to him as a lamp to a man with sore eyes (115.17); she has lived in another man's house and been held in Rāvaṇa's lap (115.19–20), and she may go where she likes, even set her mind on Lakṣmaṇa, Bharata, Śatrughna, Sugrīva or Vibhīṣaṇa (115.18, 22–23). Her reply (116) is no plea. He speaks to her "like a common man to a common woman" (116.5); her body was in another's power, but her heart, which was hers to rule, was always his (116.8–9); why did he not renounce her when Hanumān came, and spare everyone the war (116.11–13)? He has disregarded her birth from the earth and the hand he took when they were children (116.15–16). She asks Lakṣmaṇa to build a pyre; he looks to Rāma, reads assent in his face, and builds it (116.18–21). Sītā circles Rāma, calls on Agni as witness that her heart has never left him, and enters the flames (116.23–29) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa* 115–118 [CE 6.103–106]).
- **Agni returns her unharmed** - Brahmā's hymn proclaims Rāma as Viṣṇu and Sītā as Lakṣmī (117.28); the fire-god Agni then rises bodily from the pyre bearing an unscathed Sītā and testifies that she has never been unfaithful "by word, mind, thought or glance," ordering Rāma to take her back without a harsh word (118.1–10). Rāma accepts her and explains that he knew her heart, and that Rāvaṇa could no more violate her than the ocean its shore, but that the world would have called him foolish and lustful had he taken her back untested; he let her enter the fire to convince the three worlds (118.13–20) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Yuddha Kāṇḍa*).
- **Theological function** - the fire ordeal becomes the hinge for the entire Māyā-Sītā doctrine: if the true Sītā was hidden in fire all along, the ordeal is the moment of her restoration, not a test (see below).
- *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna — no fire):* in the Mahābhārata's retelling the old minister Avindhya brings her out; Rāma tells her she is free to go, since a man who knows dharma could not keep a woman who had been in another's hands, any more than an oblation a dog had licked (*Mbh* 3.275.10–13, BORI CE). She falls like a felled plantain. Then, before the gods and Daśaratha, she swears by the wind, fire, water, sky and earth that they should take her life if she has sinned, and Vāyu, Agni, Varuṇa and Brahmā each testify to her purity (3.275.21–34). The **constituted text has no pyre**. The fire-entry appears only in a passage found in some manuscripts and relegated by the editors to an appendix (after 3.275.16; appendix passage 28, marked `@028` in the GRETIL text), and that passage repeats almost word for word the *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*'s lines (*Adhyātma, Yuddha* 12.76–83). The Rāmopākhyāna has no banishment either. See [Vana Parva](../mahabharata/03-vana-parva.md). [scholarly]
- *Note (Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa):* the *Adbhuta* compresses the whole Laṅkā war, and with it the ordeal, into some twenty verses (canto 16). Its Śākta revelation of Sītā comes later, in the war against the thousand-headed Rāvaṇa (below), not in a reframed fire ordeal.

### The Māyā-Sītā / Chāyā-Sītā tradition — catalog of variants
- *Variant (Kūrma Purāṇa, Uttara-bhāga 33.112–140 in the GRETIL text):* when Rāvaṇa comes in an ascetic's guise, Sītā takes refuge in the household fire (*āvasathya*) and praises Agni in an eight-verse hymn (the *vahnyaṣṭaka*, 33.117–124). Agni appears, creates a **Māyā-Sītā** (*māyāmayī sītā*) "for Rāvaṇa's destruction," and vanishes with the true Sītā; Rāvaṇa carries off only the phantom (33.126–128). At the ordeal the illusory Sītā enters the fire and is burnt, and Agni shows Rāma the real one (33.130–131). Agni adds that Janaka had won this daughter by worshipping Pārvatī, and that he had kept her at Bhavānī's side (33.136–138), a Śaiva colouring of the story. [late text]
- *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, framed as Śiva's narration to Umā; traditionally counted as part of the Brahmāṇḍa Purāṇa; c. 14th c. per Raghavan):* foreknowing the abduction, **Rāma tells Sītā to leave a shadow (*chāyā*) of her own form in the hut and stay invisible in the fire for a year** (*Araṇya* 7.1–4); it is the Māyā-Sītā who sees the golden deer, is abducted, and dwells in Laṅkā. After the victory Rāma, wanting to give up the Māyā-Sītā and recover the Jānakī "standing in the fire," knowingly speaks harsh words to the shadow "made for a purpose"; she enters the pyre (*Yuddha* 12.67–83), and the real Sītā is restored — recasting the "ordeal" as a reunion. [late text]
- *Variant (Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, ch. 14 in the Kamakoti summary's numbering; extant text largely 15th–16th c. on an older core, per Rocher and Hazra):* Agni substitutes a shadow-Sītā for the real one, and swaps them back at the fire ordeal. Afterwards the **Chāyā-Sītā — identified with Vedavatī — goes to Puṣkara to do tapas and is reborn in the Dvāpara age as Draupadī**. Source used: a published chapter summary (Kamakoti), not the Sanskrit. [late text] See [Draupadī](./draupadi.md).
  - *Sub-variant (Devī Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.16, Vijñānānanda's translation):* tells the same story at greater length. The Chāyā-Sītā performs austerities at Puṣkara and becomes Svarga-Lakṣmī; she asks Mahādeva five times for a husband and so is granted five; she is born from Drupada's sacrificial altar as Draupadī, and is called Trihāyaṇī as the one woman of three ages — Vedavatī, Sītā and Draupadī. Checked through quoted extracts; the full page was not reachable. [late text]
- *Variant (Tirupati temple tradition — the Veṅkaṭācala-māhātmya):* it is **Vedavatī**, the one who had stood in for Sītā, who is promised marriage to Viṣṇu in the Kali age and is reborn as **Padmāvatī**, consort of Veṅkaṭeśvara at Tirupati (per Doniger's summary, cited in the secondary literature). The Padmāvatī link belongs to this tradition, not to the Brahmavaivarta or Devī Bhāgavata. [regional][late text]
- *Variant (general Vaiṣṇava reading):* the doctrine exists to resolve the theological discomfort that Lakṣmī-incarnate could be defiled — the Goddess herself is never truly in Rāvaṇa's power, only her projected image. [scholarly]

## Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa — Sītā as Mahākālī (Śākta variant)
- **The thousand-headed Rāvaṇa** - after the return to Ayodhyā, Sītā tells the court that the ten-headed Rāvaṇa had an **elder brother with a thousand heads** who rules at **Puṣkara** (canto 17). Rāma marches against him, and the war of cantos 18–22 ends with Rāma struck down and lying unconscious on the field (22.48–50) — a foe beyond Rāma's power (*Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*). [late text]
- **Sītā becomes Mahākālī** - at the gods' plea, Sītā assumes the terrifying form of **Mahākālī**, kills the thousand-headed Rāvaṇa with his sons and army single-handed, and dances the dance of destruction over the field — the climactic feminine-Śakti inversion of the epic (*Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*, canto 23).
- **The gods calm her; Rāma's divine vision** - Brahmā and the devas pray to her and assure her that Rāma will revive (canto 24). When he wakes and asks who she is, **it is Sītā herself who reveals her cosmic form** to him (canto 25), and Rāma praises her in long hymns (cantos 25–26), establishing the *Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa* as a Śākta scripture with Sītā as supreme deity (*Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*, 27 sargas / ~1,355 verses). [late text]

## Banishment — the second exile and Lava-Kuśa
- **The occasion — her own wish** - pregnant, Sītā tells Rāma that her one desire is to visit the hermitages of the sages on the Gaṅgā and spend even a night among them (*Uttara* 42 [CE 7.41.23]). That wish becomes the pretext for her exile.
- **The slander — reported by the courtier Bhadra** - during Rāma's reign in Ayodhyā, Rāma asks his companion **Bhadra** what the city says of him, and Bhadra reports the talk in the streets and markets: that the king has taken back a wife whom Rāvaṇa had held in his lap and kept in his house (*Uttara* 43 [CE 7.42.4–20]). In the **vulgate text no washerman appears at all**. To satisfy public opinion, Rāma consults his brothers and orders Lakṣmaṇa to leave the pregnant Sītā near Vālmīki's hermitage under cover of the promised visit (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa* 43–45).
  - *Variant (Padma Purāṇa, Pātāla-khaṇḍa 55 and 57):* Rāma's spies overhear a **washerman** refusing to take back his wife, who had stayed a day in another man's house: he is "not Rāma," to accept a wife who stayed in another's house (55.68–73). Chapter 57 explains him as a curse. As a girl in Janaka's garden, Sītā had caught a pair of talking parrots and kept the pregnant hen against her pleas. The dying hen cursed her to be parted from her husband while pregnant, and the grieving cock drowned himself to be reborn in Ayodhyā as the washerman Krodhana. This washerman has since displaced the vulgate's own scene in popular memory. [late text][folk]
- **Abandoned near Vālmīki's hermitage** - Lakṣmaṇa takes her across the Gaṅgā by boat and only then tells her the truth (*Uttara* 47). Her message to Rāma (48) is measured and devastating. She asks what sin she committed to be cast off when she is chaste. She will not drown herself in the Gaṅgā, lest the royal line end with her. Rāma knows she is pure, and has abandoned her for fear of dishonour; let him treat his subjects as he treats his brothers. She asks Lakṣmaṇa to bear witness that she is far gone in pregnancy. The sage **Vālmīki** receives and shelters her (49). See [Vālmīki](./valmiki-ratnakara-conversion.md).
- **Birth of the twins Kuśa & Lava** - Sītā bears twin sons at the hermitage on a rainy-season night when Śatrughna, on a journey, is lodging there; Vālmīki performs the protective rite with kuśa grass and names the elder **Kuśa**, for the mantra-charged kuśa grass he is to be wiped with, and the younger **Lava**, for the cut lower part of the grass (*lava*) with which the elder women are to dry him (*Uttara* 66 [CE 7.58.4–7]). Vālmīki rears them and teaches them the *Rāmāyaṇa* itself, which they later recite, note-perfect, before Rāma's court (93–94).
- **The Aśvamedha and recognition** - at Rāma's horse-sacrifice — performed with a **golden image of Sītā** as his consort (*Uttara* 91) — the boys sing the epic; Rāma recognizes his sons and sends for Sītā to swear to her purity before the assembly (95); Vālmīki brings her in and himself vouches for her (96) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa*). What Rāma demands is a sworn oath (*śapatha*), not a second fire.
- *Variant (Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa, c. late 17th–18th c. per Raghavan):* this text centres the years *after* the coronation. Its Vilāsa-kāṇḍa shows Rāma and Sītā enjoying their reign, travelling on pilgrimages and arranging the marriages of Kuśa and Lava; the IITK summary of Raghavan's study reads it as a householder ideal, with Rāma a caring husband who looks after Sītā and the children. [late text]

## Return to the Earth — the final departure
- **The oath to Mādhavī (Bhūmi)** - summoned to swear her chastity before the assembly, Sītā comes dressed in ochre, palms joined, eyes lowered, and the oath she swears is itself her call to the Earth: *yathāhaṃ rāghavād anyaṃ manasāpi na cintaye | tathā me mādhavī devī vivaraṃ dātum arhati* — "as I have never thought even in my mind of anyone but Rāghava, so may the goddess Mādhavī open a way for me" (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara* 97 [CE 7.88.9–10]).
- **The Earth opens** - a celestial jewelled throne rises from the ground borne on the heads of mighty nāgas; the goddess Dharaṇī takes Sītā in her arms, welcomes her, seats her on it, and she descends into the nether world under an unbroken rain of flowers while the gods cry "well done" (*Uttara* 97 [CE 7.88.11–16]) — her ultimate vindication and release from an unjust world. She returns to the element that bore her, closing the furrow-to-earth arc.
- **After her** - Rāma, in grief and fury, demands that the Earth — his mother-in-law, he says — give Sītā back or face his wrath, until Brahmā calms him, reminds him that he is Viṣṇu, and promises that he will be reunited with her in heaven (*Uttara* 98; this sarga is absent from the Critical Edition, where 7.88 ends with her descent and 7.89 opens at the end of the sacrifice). He never takes another wife; at every sacrifice a golden image of Jānakī takes her place, and he goes on performing horse-sacrifices for ten thousand years (99 [CE 7.89.3–5]). At his own departure the epic has him enter the form of Viṣṇu (110) without narrating any reunion with Sītā. So the vulgate carries only Brahmā's one-line promise; **reunion in Vaikuṇṭha** as a scene — Sītā as Lakṣmī rejoining Viṣṇu-Rāma, the earthly separation healed in the eternal pairing — is Vaiṣṇava theology and commentary.
- *Variant (sectarian / Śākta):* her return to Earth is read not as defeat but as the Goddess withdrawing into Prakṛti, the source from which she emerged — sovereign to the last.
- *Variant (Jain):* in Hariṣeṇa's *Kathākośa*, when Sītā steps into the fire of her ordeal the whole place turns into a lake; a Jain nun appears, and Sītā and those around her become Jain ascetics (per secondary summary). [disputed]
- *Variant (Mahābhārata):* the Rāmopākhyāna ends with the reunion and the return to Ayodhyā; there is no second exile and no descent into the earth (*Mbh* 3.275).

## Cultural and theological significance
- **The dharmic ideal and its critique** - Sītā is upheld in classical and devotional tradition as the model of wifely fidelity (*pativratā*) and forbearance; modern and feminist readings instead foreground the injustice of her ordeals and banishment, making her a figure of dignified protest. [scholarly] The text itself gives the protest a voice: her reproach at *Ayodhyā* 30, her refusal of Rāvaṇa at *Araṇya* 56 and *Sundara* 22, and her answers to Rāma at *Yuddha* 116 and *Uttara* 48.
- **Worship** - venerated with Rāma (Rāma-Sītā / Sītā-Rāma), and independently as a goddess; her birth is celebrated as **Sītā Navamī / Jānakī Navamī** (Vaiśākha śukla navamī), and her wedding as **Vivāha Pañcamī** (Mārgaśīrṣa śukla pañcamī) (*regional festival calendars*). See the [festivals/calendar materials](../../README.md).
- **Open-ended roster note** - regional and folk Rāmāyaṇas (Tamil Kamban, two of whose departures are noted above; Telugu, Bengali Krittibāsī, Thai Ramakien, Southeast Asian, tribal and Dalit retellings) multiply Sītā's biography in ways too numerous to fully catalog here; the variants above are the principal scriptural and pan-Indian ones. [folk][open-ended]
