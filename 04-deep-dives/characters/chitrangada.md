# Chitrāṅgadā of Maṇipura — Warrior-Princess and Wife of Arjuna

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Citrāṅgadā**, daughter of King **Citravāhana of Maṇipura**, the warrior-princess [Arjuna](./arjuna.md) weds during his twelve-year *tīrtha*-exile on the **matrilineal pact** that their son inherits her throne — that son being [Babhruvāhana](./babhruvahana.md), the boy-king who later slays and revives his own father at the horse-sacrifice. She is one of Arjuna's four wives alongside [Subhadrā](./subhadra.md), Draupadī, and the Nāga princess Ulūpī (mother of [Irāvān/Aravān](./aravan-iravan.md)). Up to [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md).
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* this falls in the present 28th Mahāyuga of the 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara, in late Dvāpara Yuga, during the years before the Mahābhārata war (Kali Yuga begins 3102 BCE at the war's close); the BORI Critical Edition of the *Mahābhārata* is the scholarly textual baseline. [scholarly]

## Disambiguation — not the Kuru prince Citrāṅgada

- **Two distinct epic figures share the name** — this article is **Citrāṅgadā** (feminine, long final *-ā*), the **princess of Maṇipura** and wife of Arjuna; she must not be confused with **Citrāṅgada** (masculine), the **eldest son of King Śāntanu and Satyavatī** of the Kuru line (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva*).
- **The Kuru prince Citrāṅgada** — Śāntanu's heir, elder brother of Vicitravīrya and half-brother of Bhīṣma; a proud warrior who **was slain young by a Gandharva of the same name** (Citrāṅgada) at Kurukṣetra in a three-year duel, dying childless and leaving the throne to Vicitravīrya — the dynastic crisis that later forced the *niyoga* births of Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Pāṇḍu (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, Sambhava Parva*). See [Bhīṣma](./bhishma.md) and [Ambikā & Ambālikā](./ambika-ambalika.md).
- **Why the confusion arises** — the names are near-identical in transliteration (often both rendered "Chitrangada"), and both stand near Arjuna's lineage (one an ancestor of the Kuru house, one Arjuna's wife). They are **unrelated persons, three generations and one gender apart** — keep them separate. [scholarly]

## Identity, name, and lineage

- **Daughter of Citravāhana** — Citrāṅgadā is the only child of **Citravāhana**, king of **Maṇipura (Maṇalūra / Maṇipūra)**, a realm Arjuna reaches in the eastern/southern leg of his pilgrimage (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva ~207*).
- **The name** — "Citrāṅgadā" glosses as "she of the **bright/variegated bracelets** (*citra* + *aṅgada*, armlet)"; in many transliterations she is **Chitrangada / Chitrangadaa**, and she is styled **Maṇipura-rāja-kanyā** (princess of Maṇipura) (*Mahābhārata*).
- **A warrior-princess** — she is presented not as a cloistered maiden but as her father's **sole heir reared to rule**, a kṣatriya woman of the single-child royal line of Maṇipura; the later vernacular and theatrical tradition amplifies this into an explicitly martial, hunter/warrior identity (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva*; later tradition). [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Tagore's* Chitrangada *):* Rabindranath Tagore's dance-drama *Chitrangada* (1892) makes her a **woman raised as a man / a warrior who knows no womanly arts**, granted beauty by Madana (Kāma) for a year to win Arjuna, then choosing to be loved as a warrior-equal — a modern reinterpretation, not the epic text. [late text]

## The marriage and the matrilineal pact

- **Arjuna's tīrtha-yātrā** — Arjuna is in the **twelfth year of his self-imposed exile-pilgrimage**, the penance for breaching the brothers' compact over Draupadī (he had entered Yudhiṣṭhira's chamber to fetch arms while Draupadī was there); on this circuit he weds Ulūpī, then comes to Maṇipura (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva ~206–207*). See [Arjuna](./arjuna.md) §62.
- **Arjuna sees Citrāṅgadā** — at Maṇipura Arjuna beholds Citrāṅgadā, is smitten, and goes to her father Citravāhana to **ask for her in marriage** (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva ~207*).
- **Citravāhana's condition — the matrilineal heir-pact** — the king discloses that his house follows a **putrikā-dharma / single-child succession**: an ancestor was granted by Śiva the boon that **each generation would bear but one child, who inherits the throne**. Citrāṅgadā being his only offspring is his **putrikā** (appointed daughter who functions as a son for lineage). He gives her **only on the condition that the son she bears shall remain in Maṇipura as Citravāhana's own heir** — that son belongs to her father's line, not to the Kurus (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva ~207*).
- **Arjuna accepts** — Arjuna agrees to the pact and weds her; he **dwells at Maṇipura for three years** until a son is born (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva ~207–208*).
- **Birth of Babhruvāhana** — Citrāṅgadā bears **Babhruvāhana**, who is **left in Maṇipura** to be reared as its crown-prince under his grandfather, per the pact; Arjuna then **resumes his pilgrimage**, departing without wife or son for Hastināpura (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva ~208*). See [Babhruvāhana](./babhruvahana.md).
- **A wife who does not migrate** — unlike Subhadrā (taken to Indraprastha) or Draupadī, **Citrāṅgadā remains in her own kingdom**; the marriage is uxorilocal and her son matrilocal — she never joins the Pāṇḍava household (*Mahābhārata*). [scholarly]

## Matrilineal kingship — what Citrāṅgadā illustrates

- **The putrikā device** — Citrāṅgadā is a textbook case of the **putrikā** ("appointed daughter"): where a king has no son, a daughter's son is contracted to continue the **maternal grandfather's** line rather than the father's — succession runs **through the daughter**, an inversion of the epic's usual patriliny (*Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva ~207*; Dharmaśāstra on *putrikā*). [scholarly]
- **Contrast with Arjuna's other unions** — Subhadrā's and Draupadī's sons are **Kuru/Pāṇḍava** heirs; Ulūpī's son Irāvān is a Nāga-realm child; **only Babhruvāhana is contractually bound to a non-paternal throne** — making Maṇipura the epic's clearest surviving matrilineal-succession kingdom (*Mahābhārata*). See [Irāvān/Aravān](./aravan-iravan.md).
- **Regional resonance — Northeast / Maṇipur identification** — the modern state of **Manipur** claims this episode as its origin-story, identifying Citrāṅgadā's Maṇipura with itself (a claim also made for sites in Odisha and the south); scholars treat the epic Maṇipura's exact location as **disputed**, but the matrilineal-pact reading is widely noted as preserving a memory of bilateral/matrilineal custom on India's frontiers (*regional tradition; scholarly note*). [regional] [disputed]
- **Why it matters for the dynasty** — because Babhruvāhana stays in Maṇipura, the **Kuru throne does not pass through Citrāṅgadā's line**; the surviving Pāṇḍava heir is **Parīkṣit**, through Subhadrā → Abhimanyu. Citrāṅgadā's branch remains a separate eastern kingship (*Mahābhārata*). See [Subhadrā](./subhadra.md).

## Reunion at the horse-sacrifice (Āśvamedhika Parva)

- **The wandering horse reaches Maṇipura** — years later, during Yudhiṣṭhira's post-war **Aśvamedha**, the consecrated stallion (guarded by Arjuna) enters Maṇipura, where the now-grown **Babhruvāhana** comes out peaceably in homage; Arjuna, bound to fight any realm the horse enters, **rebukes his son as unwarriorlike** and demands battle (*Mahābhārata, Āśvamedhika Parva ~79*).
- **The Nāga princess Ulūpī goads the son** — **Ulūpī**, Arjuna's serpent-wife and Babhruvāhana's step-mother, rises from the earth and **incites the boy to fight his father**; in the ensuing duel **Babhruvāhana slays Arjuna** (*Mahābhārata, Āśvamedhika Parva ~80–81*). See [Irāvān/Aravān](./aravan-iravan.md) for the Ulūpī complex.
- **Citrāṅgadā's lament** — hearing that her son has killed his father, **Citrāṅgadā rushes to the field, grief-stricken, and bitterly reproaches Ulūpī** for engineering a son's parricide; she prepares to die beside Arjuna (*Mahābhārata, Āśvamedhika Parva ~81*).
- **The revival and disclosure** — Ulūpī summons the Nāga **Saṃjīvana / Nāgamaṇi** gem and **restores Arjuna to life**, revealing the whole episode was contrived to **expiate the Vasus' curse** for Arjuna's unfair slaying of Bhīṣma (from behind Śikhaṇḍī) — the curse could be discharged only by Arjuna falling to his own son. Reconciled, Arjuna later invites **Citrāṅgadā, Babhruvāhana, and Ulūpī to Hastināpura** for the sacrifice (*Mahābhārata, Āśvamedhika Parva ~81–82*). See [Babhruvāhana](./babhruvahana.md) for the full duel-and-revival.
  - *Variant (South-Indian / Yakṣagāna* Babruvāhana *cycle):* the popular drama heightens the scene — Babhruvāhana **beheads** Arjuna, and the twin grief of Citrāṅgadā and Ulūpī before the gem-revival is the dramatic climax — beyond Vyāsa's terser core (*regional folk tradition*). [folk] [regional]

## Later, regional, and literary variants

- *Variant (Tagore's dance-drama) [late text]:* in *Chitrangada* (1892, later the dance-drama *Chitrangada, the Warrior Princess*), she is a king's daughter **raised as a son and trained in arms**, who through Madana's boon takes on borrowed beauty to win Arjuna and finally wins him as a warrior-equal — a modern feminist re-imagining widely staged in Bengal.
- *Variant (Manipuri tradition) [regional]:* Manipuri folk and dance traditions claim Citrāṅgadā as a daughter of their land and weave her into local Rās/ballad repertoire, treating the epic episode as ancestral history.
- *Variant (location dispute) [disputed]:* candidate identifications for the epic Maṇipura include modern **Manipur (Northeast)**, **Maṇipūr in Odisha (near Puri)**, and sites in the **south (Tamil Nadu)**; the *Mahābhārata*'s itinerary places it on Arjuna's eastern-then-southern circuit, but no identification is secure.
- *Variant (one wife of many) [scholarly]:* the epic treats Citrāṅgadā soberly as one of Arjuna's four named wives; she has no independent narrative beyond the marriage-pact and the Aśvamedha reunion in the Critical Edition — the warrior-princess colouring is largely post-epic and theatrical.
