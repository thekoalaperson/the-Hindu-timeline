# Lakṣmaṇa

> The peerless **devoted brother** of the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md) — third son of **Daśaratha**, twin of **Śatrughna**, husband of **Ūrmilā**, and the inseparable shadow of **[Rāma](../ramayana/README.md)** through the fourteen-year exile. Traditionally the **aṃśa (partial incarnation) of Śeṣa / Ananta**, the cosmic serpent on whom **Viṣṇu** reclines — so that where Viṣṇu (as Rāma) goes, Śeṣa (as Lakṣmaṇa) follows. The slayer of **Indrajit/Meghanāda**, the mutilator of **Śūrpaṇakhā**, the drawer of the (post-Vālmīki) **Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā**, and the brother whose self-sacrifice precedes Rāma's own departure.
> Sits in the **[Tretā-yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** of the current [28th Mahāyuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md). His arc runs across the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md), especially the [Araṇya-](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md), [Yuddha-](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md), and [Uttara-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md).
> Up: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md) · Antagonists: [Rāvaṇa](./ravana.md) · Variant tellings: [Adhyātma & variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).

**Lakṣmaṇa** (Sanskrit *Lakṣmaṇa*, "endowed with auspicious marks / lakṣmī") is the son of King **Daśaratha** of Ayodhyā by his queen **Sumitrā**, and the twin of **Śatrughna**. Of Rāma's three brothers he is the one bound to Rāma by a love so total that the tradition treats the two as a single being split in two: **Viṣṇu and his own bed-serpent Śeṣa**, descended together. Lakṣmaṇa chooses exile over comfort, renounces sleep, food, and his own wife's company for Rāma's sake, fights as Rāma's right arm in every battle, kills the otherwise-invincible **Indrajit**, and — in the Uttara-kāṇḍa — is taken up bodily by Indra from the bank of the Sarayū before Rāma's own departure, so that even at the end he goes first to prepare the way. He is the archetype of *bhrātṛ-bhakti* (fraternal devotion) and *sevā* (selfless service), and is worshipped beside Rāma and Sītā in nearly every Rāma temple.

---

## I. Birth, lineage, and the Śeṣa-aṃśa

- **Parentage** - Third-born son of **Daśaratha**; mother **Sumitrā** (Daśaratha's third queen). Twin brother **Śatrughna** was born of the same mother. Half-brothers: **Rāma** (by Kausalyā) and **Bharata** (by Kaikeyī) (Rām. Bāla-kāṇḍa 18).
- **The pāyasa division** - All four sons are conceived after Daśaratha's **putrakāmeṣṭi / aśvamedha** sacrifice (conducted via [Ṛṣyaśṛṅga](../rishyashringa.md)), when a divine being emerges from the fire with **pāyasa (sacred porridge)**. Kausalyā and Kaikeyī each receive a portion; **Sumitrā receives two half-portions** (one from each), and so bears **two** sons — explaining why Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna are twins and why each is half-attached to one of the elder brothers (Rām. Bāla-kāṇḍa 16). This is why **Lakṣmaṇa attaches to Rāma** and **Śatrughna to Bharata**.
- **Śeṣa / Ananta incarnation** - Pan-tradition theology makes Lakṣmaṇa the **aṃśa of Śeṣa-nāga (Ananta)**, the thousand-hooded serpent who is Viṣṇu's couch and canopy. As Rāma is Viṣṇu, Lakṣmaṇa-as-Śeṣa must accompany and shelter him — a theological grounding of his inseparability (Purāṇic; Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa; widely held).
  - *Variant (Vaiṣṇava):* Bharata and Śatrughna are correspondingly identified as aṃśas of Viṣṇu's **conch (Śaṅkha)** and **discus (Sudarśana cakra)** — so the four brothers embody Viṣṇu's body and his four attributes. [devotional]
  - *Variant (Jain Rāmāyaṇa, Paümacariya):* Lakṣmaṇa is the **Vāsudeva** of the age and **Rāma (Padma) the Baladeva**; crucially, in Jain telling it is **Lakṣmaṇa, not Rāma, who slays Rāvaṇa** — and being a Vāsudeva (a "violent" hero) he descends to hell, while the non-violent Rāma attains liberation. See [variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md). [variant]
- **Marriage to Ūrmilā** - At Mithilā, alongside Rāma's wedding to **Sītā**, Lakṣmaṇa weds **Ūrmilā** (Janaka's second daughter, Sītā's younger sister); Bharata weds **Māṇḍavī** and Śatrughna weds **Śrutakīrti** (Janaka's nieces) — a fourfold wedding binding Ayodhyā and Mithilā (Rām. Bāla-kāṇḍa 73).
- **Sons** - **Aṅgada** (not to be confused with the vānara Aṅgada) and **Candraketu**; in the Uttara-kāṇḍa Rāma settles Aṅgada over a kingdom (commonly **Aṅgadīyā / Kārupada**) and Candraketu over **Candrakāntā / Mallya** (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa).

## II. Choosing exile — the vow of service

- **Refusing to stay behind** - When Kaikeyī's boons force Rāma into fourteen years' forest exile, Lakṣmaṇa first reacts with **fury**, urging Rāma to seize the throne by force; Rāma restrains him. Lakṣmaṇa then **insists on accompanying** Rāma and Sītā, declaring he will go ahead to clear the path, build the hut, gather food, and stand guard while they rest (Rām. Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa 31).
- **The role he chose** - Throughout the exile Lakṣmaṇa functions as **servant, sentinel, hunter, and builder** — never as an equal seeking comfort. He sleeps little, eats only Rāma's leavings (in devotional retellings), and keeps perpetual watch. This self-erasure is the heart of his character.
- **The boon of sleeplessness (Nidrā / Ūrmilā-nidrā)** - On the first night of exile, the goddess **Nidrā (Sleep)** appears to Lakṣmaṇa, who begs to stay **awake for the entire fourteen years** to guard Rāma and Sītā. Nidrā grants it on one condition: **someone must sleep his share**. Lakṣmaṇa names his wife **Ūrmilā**, who in Ayodhyā gladly accepts and **sleeps continuously for fourteen years** — the celebrated **Ūrmilā-nidrā**, held up as a quiet sacrifice equal to Lakṣmaṇa's own (folk/devotional expansion; not in Vālmīki, prominent in regional and Hindi traditions).
  - *Variant:* In some tellings Nidrā simply **transfers** to Ūrmilā automatically; in others Lakṣmaṇa's sleeplessness is left unexplained in Vālmīki and the Ūrmilā device is a later poetic harmonization (notably elaborated by Maithilīśaraṇ Gupta in *Sāket*). [folk] [late text] — the whole *Ūrmilā-nidrā* tradition, stratum by stratum, and what Vālmīki's four verses actually give her: **[Ūrmilā](./urmila.md)**.
  - *Variant (function in plot):* this same sleeplessness becomes the **precondition for killing Indrajit** (see §V) — a neat narrative payoff in conditions that name "one who has not slept." [folk]

## III. Śūrpaṇakhā and the spark of war (Araṇya-kāṇḍa)

- **The mutilation** - The rākṣasī **Śūrpaṇakhā** (sister of [Rāvaṇa](./ravana.md)) lusts after Rāma; he deflects her to Lakṣmaṇa, who deflects her back; when she lunges to attack/devour **Sītā**, Rāma orders Lakṣmaṇa to disfigure her. Lakṣmaṇa **cuts off her nose** (and, in many versions, **ears** as well) (Rām. Araṇya-kāṇḍa 18).
  - *Variant (Vālmīki):* the standard wound is the **nose** (and often the ears); some recensions specify both nose and ears. [variant]
  - *Variant (the playful deflection):* in several retellings (and in Tulsīdās's [Rāmcaritmānas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md)) Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa tease Śūrpaṇakhā, bouncing her between them, before the mutilation — emphasizing her humiliation. [folk]
- **Consequence** - Śūrpaṇakhā flees to her brothers **Khara and Dūṣaṇa**, then to Rāvaṇa; Lakṣmaṇa and Rāma annihilate Khara, Dūṣaṇa, and **14,000 rākṣasas** at **Janasthāna**; the insult and slaughter drive Rāvaṇa to plot Sītā's abduction — making Lakṣmaṇa's blade the **immediate cause of the whole war**.

## IV. The golden deer and the Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā

- **The golden deer** - **Mārīca**, disguised as a **golden deer**, lures Rāma away on Sītā's insistence. Struck down, Mārīca cries out **"Hā Sītā! Hā Lakṣmaṇa!"** in Rāma's voice. Sītā, panicking, accuses Lakṣmaṇa of wanting Rāma dead so as to take her; stung, Lakṣmaṇa **leaves against his judgment** to find Rāma (Rām. Araṇya-kāṇḍa 43–45).
- **The Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā** - Before leaving, Lakṣmaṇa is said to **draw a protective line** around the hut, warning Sītā **not to cross it**; the disguised mendicant **Rāvaṇa** cannot enter while she stays within, so he provokes her to **step over** the line to give alms, and seizes her.
  - *Variant (crucial — textual status):* the **Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā is NOT in Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa**. It is a **later accretion**, famous through the **[Rāmcaritmānas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md)** of Tulsīdās and other regional/folk tellings, and absorbed into popular and idiomatic Hindi ("crossing the Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā" = transgressing a moral boundary). [late text] [folk]
  - *Variant:* some traditions make it a **mantra-charged barrier of fire/protection** rather than a mere scratched line. [folk]
- **The blameless guard** - Vālmīki, lacking the rekhā, places the "fault" entirely on the **deception (Mārīca's false cry)** and Sītā's accusation; the rekhā tradition redistributes responsibility, making Sītā's **crossing** the proximate cause — a much-debated shift in moral framing. [disputed]

## V. War in Laṅkā — Indrajit and the Sañjīvanī (Yuddha-kāṇḍa)

- **Right arm in battle** - Lakṣmaṇa fights as Rāma's foremost warrior, slaying numerous rākṣasa champions, including (per Vālmīki) **Atikāya** and others, and matching Indrajit repeatedly.
- **The Nāgapāśa / serpent-arrows** - **Indrajit (Meghanāda)**, Rāvaṇa's son, binds **both Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa** with the serpent-weapon **Nāgapāśa**; they are freed when **Garuḍa**, Viṣṇu's eagle and natural enemy of serpents, arrives and the snakes flee (Rām. Yuddha-kāṇḍa).
- **Two herb-flights, two different fellings** - Vālmīki has **two** distinct crises and **two** distinct mountains, which retellings collapse into one:
  - **First (Yuddha 73–74)** — **Indrajit**, invisible in the sky after his oblation, looses the **Brahmāstra**; it fells **Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa together** with sixty-seven crores of vānaras. **Jāmbavān** sends **Hanumān** to the Himālaya for four herbs — *mṛta-sañjīvanī, viśalya-karaṇī, suvarṇa-karaṇī, sandhānī* — on a blazing all-herb peak that Vālmīki places **between Mount Ṛṣabha and Kailāsa and never names** (74.29–33); unable to pick them out, he brings the mountain, and the scent revives the whole army.
  - **Second (Yuddha 100–101)** — much later, it is **Rāvaṇa**, not Indrajit, who fells Lakṣmaṇa, and with a **śakti fashioned by Maya** the dānava. The vānara physician **Suṣeṇa** prescribes, and Hanumān flies a second time — to **Mount Mahodaya (Oṣadhi)**, a different peak — and Lakṣmaṇa is revived.
  - **In Vālmīki, Indrajit never fells Lakṣmaṇa with a śakti**; and **"Droṇagiri" and "Gandhamādana" are not the epic's names for either herb-mountain**. Both are later tradition, near-universal in retelling. [folk] See [Indrajit](./indrajit-meghnad.md).
- **The slaying of Indrajit** - Completion of the fire-rite at the **Nikumbhilā** sanctuary inside Laṅkā would make Indrajit, as **Vibhīṣaṇa** states it, "difficult to be overcome in battle even by the celestials including Indra," and invisible on the field; the counter is to strike **before the oblations are finished** (*Yuddha* 84.14–23). Vibhīṣaṇa — Rāvaṇa's defector brother, and the only man in Rāma's camp who knows the ground — leads the strike-party himself and shows Lakṣmaṇa the black-cloud banyan Indrajit must reach; Lakṣmaṇa, with Vibhīṣaṇa and Hanumān, **breaks the rite** and kills him in the ensuing duel, beheading him with the **Aindrāstra** loosed on an oath sworn by Rāma's virtue (*Yuddha* 86–87, 90). Full treatment: [Nikumbhilā](../../06-sacred-geography/nikumbhila.md).
  - *Variant (the presiding goddess):* commentarial, southern and tantric tradition gives the altar a goddess of its own — **Nikumbhilā-devī**, identified with **Bhadrakālī** and, most widely in the south, with **Pratyaṅgirā** — and reads the rite as her invocation rather than a fire-oblation. Vālmīki names **no deity** for it. [late text] [regional]
  - *Variant (the "impossible man" condition):* a widespread later tradition holds that Indrajit could be killed **only by a man who had not slept, not eaten, and not seen his wife's face for fourteen years** — conditions **Lakṣmaṇa alone met** (sleepless by Nidrā's boon, fasting/eating only Rāma's leavings, and parted from Ūrmilā the whole exile), which is **why Lakṣmaṇa and not Rāma** is his slayer. This dovetails with the Ūrmilā-nidrā motif (§II). [folk] — note: **not in Vālmīki**; there the Nikumbhilā-rite disruption is the operative cause.
  - *Variant:* some texts credit a specific weapon (an **Indrāstra** or arrow charged by Lakṣmaṇa's lifelong **brahmacarya/dharma**) as the lethal instrument. [variant]

## VI. After the war — return and refusal

- **Coronation** - With Rāvaṇa dead and Sītā recovered, the party returns to Ayodhyā; **Rāma is crowned**. Lakṣmaṇa, offered the position of **Yuvarāja (crown prince)**, traditionally **declines** in deference to **Bharata** as the elder, embodying the same self-effacement as throughout (Uttara-kāṇḍa tradition).
- **Continued service** - Lakṣmaṇa remains Rāma's closest aide during the reign; in the Uttara-kāṇḍa it is **Lakṣmaṇa** whom Rāma, with a heavy heart, charges to **escort the pregnant Sītā to the forest** (to Vālmīki's hermitage) after the public slander — a duty Lakṣmaṇa performs in anguish, unable to disobey his brother (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa). See [Uttara-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md).

## VII. His end — the Sarayū, Durvāsas, and Kāla (Uttara-kāṇḍa)

- **Kāla / Yama's secret visit** - Near the end of Rāma's reign, **Kāla (Time / Death, in the guise of an ascetic — often identified with Yama)** comes to Rāma to signal that his earthly span is ending, demanding a **strictly private audience**: whoever **interrupts or enters** must be **put to death**. **Lakṣmaṇa** is set to guard the door under that oath (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa).
- **Durvāsas's threat** - The volatile sage **[Durvāsas](../ambarisha-durvasa.md)** arrives and demands immediate audience with Rāma, threatening to **curse Rāma, Ayodhyā, and the whole dynasty** if refused. Caught between two dooms, **Lakṣmaṇa chooses to sacrifice himself**: he interrupts the meeting to announce Durvāsas, accepting the death-penalty so that the kingdom is spared the curse (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa).
- **The sentence — and the bodily ascent** - To honour the oath without literally executing his brother, Rāma (counselled by **Vasiṣṭha**) treats **banishment as equivalent to death** (*tyāga* of the virtuous = death). Lakṣmaṇa goes to the **Sarayū**, stands on the bank and restrains his breath in yoga — and then, in Vālmīki's own words, **"becoming invisible to men, the mighty Lakṣmaṇa was borne away in the body by Śakra, who carried him to the celestial region."** He does **not** drown, and he does not leave his body behind: **Indra takes him up bodily, alive** (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 106). Popular retelling substitutes a *jala-samādhi* in the river, assimilating his end to Rāma's; the text keeps them different.
  - *Variant (Vaiṣṇava gloss):* on reaching heaven he **resumes his form as Śeṣa**, the quarter of Viṣṇu's essence, foreshadowing Rāma's own return to Vaikuṇṭha shortly after. [late text]
  - *Variant:* some tellings name the door-guarding oath and the sage differently, or have Durvāsas curse **Lakṣmaṇa** ("you shall ascend to heaven alive"), folding the curse and the sentence into one. [variant]
- **Precursor to Rāma's departure** - Lakṣmaṇa's going **breaks Rāma**; bereft of his Śeṣa, Rāma soon installs his sons, divides the kingdom, and makes the **mahāprasthāna** into the Sarayū himself, the brothers reunited as Viṣṇu and his serpent (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 108–110). Thus, true to character, **Lakṣmaṇa goes first.**

## VIII. Character, worship, and themes

- **Bhrātṛ-bhakti incarnate** - The supreme model of devotion to an elder sibling; his quick temper (toward Kaikeyī, toward Sītā's doubters, toward injustice) is the foil to Rāma's calm — together they form a complete dharmic actor.
- **Renunciation of the three comforts** - Sleep (to Nidrā/Ūrmilā), food (eating only after/with Rāma), and conjugal life (parted from Ūrmilā) — the triad that later tradition turns into the very key that unlocks Indrajit's death.
- **Worship** - Almost universally depicted **at Rāma's right** (with Sītā and Hanumān) in Rāma temples; rarely worshipped in isolation, fitting his self-subordinating nature.
- **Ūrmilā's neglected sacrifice** - Modern and feminist retellings (e.g. Gupta's *Sāket*) recover **[Ūrmilā](./urmila.md)** as the silent co-sufferer whose fourteen-year sleep mirrors Lakṣmaṇa's fourteen-year vigil. [scholarly]
