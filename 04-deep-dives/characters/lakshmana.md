# Lakṣmaṇa

> The peerless **devoted brother** of the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md) — son of **Daśaratha** by Sumitrā, twin of **Śatrughna**, husband of **Ūrmilā**, and the inseparable shadow of **[Rāma](./rama.md)** through the fourteen-year exile. Vālmīki calls him a **portion of Viṣṇu**; later Vaiṣṇava tradition, stated outright in the *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*, makes him the **aṃśa (partial incarnation) of Śeṣa / Ananta**, the cosmic serpent on whom **Viṣṇu** reclines — so that where Viṣṇu (as Rāma) goes, Śeṣa (as Lakṣmaṇa) follows. The slayer of **Indrajit/Meghanāda**, the mutilator of **Śūrpaṇakhā**, the drawer of the (post-Vālmīki) **Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā**, and the brother whose self-sacrifice precedes Rāma's own departure.
> Sits in the **[Tretā-yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** of the current [28th Mahāyuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md). His arc runs across the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md), especially the [Araṇya-](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md), [Yuddha-](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md), and [Uttara-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md).
> Up: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md) · Antagonists: [Rāvaṇa](./ravana.md) · Variant tellings: [Adhyātma & variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).
> Citation key: Vālmīki is cited by **vulgate (Southern) sarga.verse**, as on valmikiramayan.net, with the **Baroda Critical Edition** in brackets as **CE** (GRETIL text); "vulgate only" marks verses the CE leaves out of its constituted text. The Uttara-kāṇḍa is cited by CE, with the vulgate sarga added where checked (against H. P. Shastri's translation on wisdomlib).

**Lakṣmaṇa** (Sanskrit *Lakṣmaṇa*, "endowed with auspicious marks / lakṣmī") is the son of King **Daśaratha** of Ayodhyā by his queen **Sumitrā**, and the twin of **Śatrughna**. Of Rāma's three brothers he is the one bound to Rāma by a love so total that Vālmīki calls him Rāma's "second life-breath outside the body," and later tradition treats the two as a single being split in two: **Viṣṇu and his own bed-serpent Śeṣa**, descended together. Lakṣmaṇa chooses exile over comfort, keeps watch while Rāma sleeps, leaves his wife behind for fourteen years, fights as Rāma's right arm in every battle, kills the otherwise-invincible **Indrajit**, and — in the Uttara-kāṇḍa — is taken up bodily by Indra from the bank of the Sarayū before Rāma's own departure, so that even at the end he goes first. He is the archetype of *bhrātṛ-bhakti* (fraternal devotion) and *sevā* (selfless service), and is worshipped beside Rāma and Sītā in nearly every Rāma temple.

## I. Birth, lineage, and the Śeṣa-aṃśa

- **Parentage** - Son of **Daśaratha** by **Sumitrā**, born after Rāma (by Kausalyā) and Bharata (by Kaikeyī); **Śatrughna** is his full brother by the same mother (Rām. Bāla 18.10–14 [CE 1.17.6–9]). Vasiṣṭha names the four on the twelfth day (Bāla 18.21–22 [CE 1.17.11–12]).
  - *Variant (the birth-star, vulgate only):* Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna are born under **Āśleṣā** (*sārpe*, the serpent-asterism) with the sun in Cancer (Bāla 18.15). The verse is not in the CE's constituted text. [interpolation]
- **The pāyasa division** - All four sons are conceived after Daśaratha's **aśvamedha and putrakāmeṣṭi**, conducted by [Ṛṣyaśṛṅga](../rishyashringa.md), when a being sent by Prajāpati rises from the fire with **pāyasa (sacred porridge)**. In Vālmīki **Daśaratha himself** divides it: half to Kausalyā, half of the remainder (a quarter) to Sumitrā, half of what is left (an eighth) to Kaikeyī, and then, "after reflection," the last eighth to **Sumitrā again**. Sumitrā therefore receives two portions and bears two sons (Bāla 16.27–29 [CE 1.15.25–26]).
  - *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa; Kālidāsa):* Daśaratha gives halves only to Kausalyā and Kaikeyī, and **each queen gives Sumitrā half of her own share**. The *Adhyātma* (Bāla 3.10–12) and the *Raghuvaṃśa* (10.55–57) both tell it this way. The *Adhyātma* then explains the brothers' pairing by these shares: Lakṣmaṇa goes with Rāma and Śatrughna with Bharata, *pāyasāṃśānusārataḥ*, "according to their portions of the pāyasa" (Bāla 3.42). [late text]
- **"A second life outside his body"** - Vālmīki explains the attachment by temperament, not by porridge. From childhood Lakṣmaṇa was devoted to Rāma, *bahiḥprāṇa ivāparaḥ*, "like a second life-breath outside his body." Rāma could not fall asleep without him and would not eat a delicacy unless Lakṣmaṇa shared it. When Rāma rode out to hunt, Lakṣmaṇa followed behind him with his bow. Śatrughna was bound to Bharata in the same way (Bāla 18.28–32 [CE 1.17.15–19]).
- **A portion of Viṣṇu (Vālmīki)** - Vālmīki speaks of Lakṣmaṇa only as a share of Viṣṇu, never as Śeṣa. At birth the twins are *viṣṇor ardhasamanvitau*, "endowed with half [a share] of Viṣṇu" (Bāla 18.14 [CE 1.17.9]). Struck down by Rāvaṇa's śakti, Lakṣmaṇa "remembered himself as an inconceivable portion of Viṣṇu" (Yuddha 59.112, 122 [CE 6.47.104, 115]). When Indra carries him to heaven the gods worship *viṣṇoś caturbhāgam*, "the quarter of Viṣṇu," come home (Uttara CE 7.96.18). The serpent **Ananta** appears in the Uttara only in Kāla's account of creation, as a being Viṣṇu brought forth through māyā (CE 7.94.5), and the text does not equate him with Lakṣmaṇa. The *Bhāgavata* likewise has Hari becoming four sons *aṃśāṃśena*, "by portion and sub-portion," with no serpent (BhP 9.10.2).
- **Śeṣa / Ananta incarnation** - The identification is post-Vālmīki. The **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** states it outright: Vasiṣṭha reveals to Daśaratha that "Śeṣa is Lakṣmaṇa, who has followed Rāma" (*śeṣas tu lakṣmaṇo rājan rāmam evānvapadyata*, Bāla 4.17). In later Vaiṣṇava theology it is standard: Lakṣmaṇa is the **aṃśa of Śeṣa-nāga (Ananta)**, the thousand-hooded serpent who is Viṣṇu's couch and canopy, and so he must accompany and shelter Rāma. [late text]
  - *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa):* in the next verse **Bharata and Śatrughna** are born as the **conch and discus** (*śaṅkha-cakre*) of the mace-bearer, and Sītā as Yogamāyā (Bāla 4.18). The four brothers thus become Viṣṇu with his couch-serpent and two of his weapons. [late text]
  - *Variant (Jain Rāmāyaṇa, Paümacariya):* Lakṣmaṇa is the **Vāsudeva** of the age and **Rāma (Padma) the Baladeva**; crucially, in Jain telling it is **Lakṣmaṇa, not Rāma, who slays Rāvaṇa** — and being a Vāsudeva (a "violent" hero) he descends to hell, while the non-violent Rāma attains liberation. See [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md) and [variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md). [variant]
- **With Viśvāmitra; Tāṭakā** - When Viśvāmitra asks for Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa goes with them. In the vulgate, after Rāma's arrows cut off the arms of the yakṣī **[Tāṭakā](./tataka.md)**, Lakṣmaṇa in anger cuts off **her ears and the tip of her nose**, before Rāma kills her (Bāla 26.17–18).
  - *Variant (Critical Edition):* the CE has Rāma only **threaten** to send her back "with ears and nose cut off," and then shoot her through the breast. Lakṣmaṇa's cut is not in its constituted text (CE 1.25.11–14). [interpolation]
- **Marriage to Ūrmilā** - At Mithilā, alongside Rāma's wedding to **Sītā**, Janaka gives Lakṣmaṇa his own second daughter **[Ūrmilā](./urmila.md)** (*lakṣmaṇāgaccha bhadraṃ te ūrmilām udyatāṃ mayā*, "Come, Lakṣmaṇa, bless you: take the hand of Ūrmilā, whom I offer"). Bharata weds **Māṇḍavī** and Śatrughna weds **Śrutakīrti**, daughters of Janaka's brother Kuśadhvaja, in a fourfold wedding that binds Ayodhyā and Mithilā (Bāla 73.30 [CE 1.72.18]). See [Bharata, Śatrughna and their wives](./bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md).
- **Sons** - **Aṅgada** (not to be confused with the vānara [Aṅgada](./angada.md)) and **Candraketu**. Late in his reign Rāma asks for a land where they may rule without harming other kings or hermitages. Bharata proposes **Kārāpatha**, and Rāma founds **Aṅgadīyā** there for Aṅgada, in the west. **Candrakāntā** is founded for Candraketu in the **Malla country** (*mallabhūmi*), to the north. Lakṣmaṇa escorts Aṅgada and lives a year at Aṅgadīyā before he returns to Ayodhyā (Uttara CE 7.92.2–13; vulgate 7.102, where the spelling of the land's name differs, Shastri's translation giving "Karupatta").

## II. Choosing exile — the vow of service

- **The first fury** - When Kaikeyī's boons force Rāma into fourteen years' forest exile, Lakṣmaṇa reacts with **fury**. He urges Rāma to seize the throne by force, vowing to empty Ayodhyā with his arrows if it opposes Rāma and to kill everyone who sides with Bharata (Ayodhyā 21.10–11 [CE 2.18.10–11]).
  - *Variant (vulgate only):* the vulgate goes further. If their father has turned enemy at Kaikeyī's urging, Lakṣmaṇa says, "let him be imprisoned, or even killed" (Ayodhyā 21.12). The CE's constituted text lacks the verse. [interpolation]
  - Rāma answers him with the doctrine of obedience to a father's word (Ayodhyā 21.28–ff, 22 [CE 2.18.25–35, 2.19]). Lakṣmaṇa rages once more, this time against "fate," before he yields (Ayodhyā 23 [CE 2.20]).
- **Refusing to stay behind** - Lakṣmaṇa then **insists on accompanying** Rāma and Sītā. "Make me your attendant," he says. He will walk ahead carrying bow, spade and basket to show the way, bring roots and fruit, and "do everything for you, whether you are awake or asleep" (*ahaṃ sarvaṃ kariṣyāmi jāgrataḥ svapataś ca te*). Rāma sends him to fetch the divine bows and armour that Varuṇa gave Janaka (Ayodhyā 31.22–29 [CE 2.28.8–14]; the "attendant" verse 31.22 is vulgate only).
- **Sumitrā's charge** - His mother sends him off with the most quoted farewell of the book: *rāmaṃ daśarathaṃ viddhi māṃ viddhi janakātmajām / ayodhyām aṭavīṃ viddhi gaccha tāta yathāsukham* — "Know Rāma as Daśaratha, know Janaka's daughter as me, know the forest as Ayodhyā; go, my son, and be happy" (Ayodhyā 40.9 [CE 2.35.8]). See [Sumitrā](./sumitra.md). Ūrmilā is not in the scene (see [Ūrmilā](./urmila.md)).
- **The vigil at Śṛṅgaverapura** - Vālmīki's own source for the sleepless Lakṣmaṇa is the night at Śṛṅgaverapura, on the bank of the Gaṅgā. The Niṣāda chief [Guha](./guha.md) offers to keep watch so that Lakṣmaṇa can rest. Lakṣmaṇa refuses: while Rāma and Sītā lie on the bare ground, how could he sleep, or live, or take any pleasure? He stays awake all night (Ayodhyā 51.3–9 [CE 2.45.9]). Guha later retells the night to Bharata (Ayodhyā 86 [CE 2.80.10]).
- **The role he chose** - Throughout the exile Lakṣmaṇa functions as **servant, sentinel, hunter, and builder** — never as an equal seeking comfort. He builds the leaf-huts at Citrakūṭa and Pañcavaṭī (Ayodhyā 56; Araṇya 15) and keeps watch. Devotional retellings add that he ate only Rāma's leavings. This self-erasure is the heart of his character.
- **The second fury — Bharata at Citrakūṭa** - From a tree-top Lakṣmaṇa sees Bharata's army, with its Kovidāra banner, approaching Citrakūṭa. He takes it for an attack, calls Bharata an enemy who deserves death, and threatens to kill him and Kaikeyī as well. Rāma rebukes him: Bharata comes out of love (Ayodhyā 96.11–30; 97).
- **The boon of sleeplessness (Nidrā / Ūrmilā-nidrā)** - On the first night of exile, the goddess **Nidrā (Sleep)** appears to Lakṣmaṇa, who begs to stay **awake for the entire fourteen years** to guard Rāma and Sītā. Nidrā grants it on one condition: **someone must sleep his share**. Lakṣmaṇa names his wife **Ūrmilā**, who in Ayodhyā accepts and **sleeps continuously for fourteen years** — the celebrated **Ūrmilā-nidrā**, held up as a quiet sacrifice equal to Lakṣmaṇa's own. The episode is **not in Vālmīki** and **not in Tulsīdās's *Rāmcaritmānas***. Its identified home is the oral *Rām-kathā*, most concretely the **Telugu women's songs** of the *Ūrmilādevi Nidra* cycle. [folk] [oral tradition] [regional]
  - *Variant:* In some tellings Nidrā simply **transfers** to Ūrmilā automatically; in others Lakṣmaṇa names her. Vālmīki gives only the one sleepless night with Guha, and the Nidrā device reads as a later poetic harmonization. Maithilīśaraṇ Gupta's *Sāket* (1931), the modern epic built around Ūrmilā's **separation**, made her the epic's "forgotten" wife in North India. It is not the source of the sleep-boon. [folk] [late text] — the whole *Ūrmilā-nidrā* tradition, stratum by stratum, and what Vālmīki's four verses actually give her: **[Ūrmilā](./urmila.md)**.
  - *Variant (function in plot):* this same sleeplessness becomes the **precondition for killing Indrajit** (see §V) — a neat narrative payoff in conditions that name "one who has not slept." [folk]

## III. Śūrpaṇakhā and the spark of war (Araṇya-kāṇḍa)

- **The mutilation** - The rākṣasī **[Śūrpaṇakhā](./shurpanakha.md)** (sister of [Rāvaṇa](./ravana.md)) lusts after Rāma. Already in Vālmīki the brothers make sport of her: Rāma, jesting, sends her to Lakṣmaṇa as the unmarried one, and Lakṣmaṇa mocks her and sends her back. When she lunges to devour **Sītā**, Rāma orders Lakṣmaṇa to disfigure her, and Lakṣmaṇa draws his sword and **cuts off her ears and nose** (*uddhṛtya khaḍgaṃ ciccheda karṇanāsaṃ mahābalaḥ*) (Araṇya 17–18; 18.21 [CE 3.17.21]).
  - *Variant (the playful deflection):* Tulsīdās's [Rāmcaritmānas](../ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) and other retellings play up the bouncing between the brothers before the mutilation, which sharpens her humiliation. The jest itself is Vālmīki's (Araṇya 18.1–17). [late text]
  - *Variant (Jain tradition):* there is no mutilation. Lakṣmaṇa instead kills Śūrpaṇakhā's son **Śambūka** without knowing it, while testing a sword he has found, and her grief starts the war (*Paümacariya* tradition, as given in [Śūrpaṇakhā](./shurpanakha.md); not checked against the Prakrit here). [variant]
- **Consequence** - Śūrpaṇakhā flees to her brother **Khara**. When Khara, **Dūṣaṇa** and **Triśiras** march on Pañcavaṭī with **14,000 rākṣasas**, Rāma sends **Lakṣmaṇa** into a mountain cave with Sītā to guard her. Lakṣmaṇa takes his bow and does so, and **Rāma alone** destroys the whole army at **Janasthāna** (Araṇya 24.11–15 [CE 3.23.11]; 25–30). See [Khara and the Janasthāna command](./khara-and-the-janasthana-command.md). Śūrpaṇakhā then goes to Rāvaṇa. The insult and the slaughter drive Rāvaṇa to plot Sītā's abduction, which makes Lakṣmaṇa's blade the **first link** in the chain that leads to the war.
- **Ayomukhī (vulgate only)** - After the abduction, in the Krauñca forest, the rākṣasī **Ayomukhī** seizes Lakṣmaṇa and offers herself to him. Lakṣmaṇa cuts off her nose, ears and breasts, and she flees (Araṇya 69.11–18). The CE goes straight from the Krauñca forest to [Kabandha](./kabandha.md), so the episode is not in its constituted text (CE 3.65). [interpolation]

## IV. The golden deer and the Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā

- **The golden deer** - **[Mārīca](./maricha.md)**, disguised as a **golden deer**, appears at the hermitage. Lakṣmaṇa sees through it at once: "I think this deer is that rākṣasa Mārīca," he says, reminding Rāma of the kings Mārīca had killed out hunting by such disguises (Araṇya 43.5–7 [CE 3.41.4]). Rāma goes after the deer on Sītā's insistence anyway. Struck down, Mārīca cries out **"Hā Sītā! Hā Lakṣmaṇa!"** in Rāma's voice (Araṇya 44).
- **Sītā's accusation** - Sītā, panicking, accuses Lakṣmaṇa of wanting Rāma dead for her sake, of following Rāma into the forest to get her, or of being Bharata's agent (Araṇya 45.5–7, 21–25 [CE 3.43.22]). Stung, Lakṣmaṇa calls the forest-dwellers to witness that the charge is false. He commends her to the keeping of "all the forest deities" (*rakṣantu tvāṃ viśālākṣi samagrā vanadevatāḥ*), says the omens make him fear he will not see her again, and goes (Araṇya 45.30–34 [CE 3.43.30]).
- **The Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā** - Before leaving, Lakṣmaṇa is said to **draw a protective line** around the hut, warning Sītā **not to cross it**; the disguised mendicant **Rāvaṇa** cannot enter while she stays within, so he provokes her to **step over** the line to give alms, and seizes her.
  - *Variant (crucial — textual status):* the **Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā is NOT in Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa**, where Lakṣmaṇa's only safeguard is to entrust Sītā to the forest deities. The *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa* also has him leave her in the care of the forest goddesses (*vanadevībhyaḥ samarpya*, Araṇya 7.36). Even the **[Rāmcaritmānas](../ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md)** never narrates the line in its Araṇya-kāṇḍ. It only presupposes it in the Laṅkā-kāṇḍ, where **Mandodarī** taunts Rāvaṇa: *rāmānuja laghu rekha khacāī / sou nahiṃ nāghehu asi manusāī*, "Rāma's younger brother drew a little line, and even that you could not cross — such is your manhood" (caupāī beginning *kaṃta samujhi mana tajahu kumatihī*). The episode in full belongs to later popular tellings, such as Rādheśyām Kathāvācak's Hindi *Rāmāyaṇ*. It has entered idiomatic Hindi: "crossing the Lakṣmaṇa-rekhā" means transgressing a moral boundary. [late text] [folk]
  - *Variant:* some traditions make it a **mantra-charged barrier of fire/protection** rather than a mere scratched line. [folk]
  - *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa — the shadow Sītā):* before the deer appears, Rāma has the real Sītā enter the fire for a year and leaves a **Māyā-Sītā** in her place. Lakṣmaṇa is not told. Returning, Rāma reflects that "Lakṣmaṇa does not know of the Māyā-Sītā I made," and so he grieves like an ordinary man for the sake of the deception (Araṇya 7.1–4; 8.3). [late text]
- **The blameless guard** - Vālmīki, lacking the rekhā, places the "fault" entirely on the **deception (Mārīca's false cry)** and Sītā's accusation; the rekhā tradition redistributes responsibility, making Sītā's **crossing** the proximate cause — a much-debated shift in moral framing. [disputed]
- **Rebuked for leaving** - Meeting Lakṣmaṇa on the path, Rāma blames him for leaving Sītā alone because of an angry woman's words. Lakṣmaṇa's defence that he was driven away does not satisfy him (Araṇya 59). In the search that follows, Lakṣmaṇa is the steadier of the two, restraining Rāma's grief and talking him out of his threat to destroy the worlds (Araṇya 64–66; his counsel is 65–66). The brothers then cut off the arms of the headless [Kabandha](./kabandha.md), Rāma the right and Lakṣmaṇa the left (Araṇya 70).

## Kiṣkindhā — the anklets and the bowstring

- **Meeting Hanumān** - At Ṛṣyamūka it is Lakṣmaṇa who answers [Hanumān](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/hanuman.md)'s questions, tells their story, and asks for Sugrīva's help (Kiṣkindhā 4).
- **"I know only her anklets" (vulgate only)** - Sugrīva shows the brothers the ornaments Sītā dropped during the abduction. Rāma shows them to Lakṣmaṇa, and Lakṣmaṇa answers that he does not know the armlets or the earrings, *nūpure tv abhijānāmi nityaṃ pādābhivandanāt*: "but the anklets I know, from bowing daily at her feet" (Kiṣkindhā 6.22–23). This is the most quoted line about his reverence for Sītā, and it is not in the CE, whose sarga 4.6 ends with Rāma's lament (CE 4.6.18–23). [interpolation]
- **The rainy season and the bowstring** - When the monsoon ends and Sugrīva, lost in pleasure, forgets his promise, Rāma sends Lakṣmaṇa with a warning: "the path by which Vālin went has not closed; keep your compact, Sugrīva, and do not follow Vālin's road." Lakṣmaṇa is ready to kill Sugrīva then and there, and Rāma has to restrain him (Kiṣkindhā 30–31 [CE 4.29.48; 4.30.4–7]). In the vulgate, at the threshold of the women's quarters, he twangs his bowstring in fury, and the terrified Sugrīva sends **[Tārā](./tara-vanara-queen.md)** out to calm him (Kiṣkindhā 33; vulgate only, since the CE has him walk straight in, CE 4.32). He then rebukes Sugrīva to his face, repeating Rāma's warning, and Tārā's reasoning softens him; Sugrīva then sets the search in motion (Kiṣkindhā 34–35 [CE 4.33.18; 4.34]). See [Sugrīva and Vālin](./sugriva-and-vali.md).

## V. War in Laṅkā — Indrajit and the Sañjīvanī (Yuddha-kāṇḍa)

- **Right arm in battle** - Lakṣmaṇa fights as Rāma's foremost warrior and kills many rākṣasa champions. The greatest before Indrajit is **Atikāya**, Rāvaṇa's son by Dhānyamālinī, whose armour turns every arrow. **Vāyu** comes to Lakṣmaṇa and tells him that only the **Brahmāstra** can pierce it, and with that weapon Lakṣmaṇa takes off Atikāya's head (Yuddha 71.102–111 [CE 6.59.96–104]).
- **The Nāgapāśa / serpent-arrows** - **[Indrajit (Meghanāda)](./indrajit-meghnad.md)**, Rāvaṇa's son, invisible in the sky, binds **both Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa** with serpent-arrows, and they fall in a bath of blood (Yuddha 45). They are freed when **Garuḍa**, Viṣṇu's eagle and natural enemy of serpents, arrives and the snakes flee at his touch (Yuddha 50).
- **Felled by Rāvaṇa: the first śakti** - The first time Lakṣmaṇa meets Rāvaṇa in battle, he breaks Rāvaṇa's bow. Rāvaṇa answers with a **śakti given him by Brahmā (Svayambhū)**, which pierces Lakṣmaṇa's chest. Rāvaṇa then tries to carry the fallen prince away and cannot. He who could lift Himavat, Mandara and Meru cannot lift Lakṣmaṇa, because Lakṣmaṇa "remembered himself as an inconceivable portion of Viṣṇu." **Hanumān**, out of love, lifts him easily and carries him to Rāma, and the śakti returns to Rāvaṇa's chariot. Lakṣmaṇa recovers without any herb (Yuddha 59.107–122 [CE 6.47.101–115]).
- **Two herb-flights, two different fellings** - Vālmīki has **two** distinct herb crises, which retellings collapse into one:
  - **First (Yuddha 73–74 [CE 6.60–61])** — **Indrajit**, invisible in the sky after his oblation, looses the **Brahmāstra**; it fells **Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa together** with sixty-seven crores of vānaras. **[Jāmbavān](./jambavan.md)** sends **Hanumān** to the Himālaya for four herbs — *mṛta-sañjīvanī, viśalya-karaṇī, suvarṇa-karaṇī, sandhānī* — on a blazing peak **between Mount Ṛṣabha and Kailāsa**. Vālmīki gives it no proper name, only *oṣadhi-parvata*, "the herb-mountain" (74.29–33 [CE 6.61.31]). Unable to pick out the herbs, Hanumān brings the mountain, and their scent revives the whole army.
  - **Second (Yuddha 100–101 [CE 6.88–89])** — much later, it is **Rāvaṇa**, not Indrajit, who fells Lakṣmaṇa, and with a **śakti fashioned by Maya** the dānava (100.30 [CE 6.88.30]). Over his body Rāma laments that "wives may be found in every land, and kinsmen in every land, but I see no land where a brother born of the same womb is found" (*deśe deśe kalatrāṇi…*, 101.15, vulgate only). The vānara physician **[Suṣeṇa](./nala-nila-and-sushena.md)** sends Hanumān back to "the herb-mountain that Jāmbavān described to you before," and to its southern peak (101.31–32 [CE 6.89.14–15]). Hanumān again cannot tell the herbs apart and again brings the summit, and Lakṣmaṇa is revived.
  - **The second mountain is the first.** Vālmīki's wording sends Hanumān back to the **same** herb-mountain. The name "Mahodaya" sometimes given to it is not in the Sanskrit of either flight, in the vulgate text printed on valmikiramayan.net or in the CE; both have only the common noun. It appears only in the valmikiramayan.net translator's parenthesis "Oushadhi (Mahodaya)".
  - **In Vālmīki, Indrajit never fells Lakṣmaṇa with a śakti**; and **"Droṇagiri" and "Gandhamādana" are not the epic's names for either herb-mountain**. Both are later tradition, near-universal in retelling. The popular version, in which **Meghanāda's** *vīraghātinī* ("hero-slaying") śakti fells Lakṣmaṇa, is the *Rāmcaritmānas*'s (Laṅkā-kāṇḍ, *bīraghātinī chāṛisi sāṃgī / tejapuṃja lachimana ura lāgī*). [late text] [folk] See [Indrajit](./indrajit-meghnad.md).
- **Lakṣmaṇa against dharma** - When Indrajit kills an illusory Sītā before the army and Rāma faints at the news, it is Lakṣmaṇa who rails at his brother's faith. Righteousness, he argues, has not saved the righteous Rāma from a single calamity, while the wicked Rāvaṇa prospers. Wealth and power are what count, and the mistake was to give up the kingdom. He vows to level Laṅkā with his arrows (Yuddha 83.14–44). It is the most sustained piece of *arthaśāstra* reasoning put in his mouth, and Vibhīṣaṇa's news that the Sītā was a phantom cuts it short (Yuddha 84).
- **The slaying of Indrajit** - Indrajit has gone to the fire-rite at the **Nikumbhilā** sanctuary inside Laṅkā. **[Vibhīṣaṇa](./vibhishana.md)** warns that if the rite is completed he will be unconquerable even by the gods with Indra, and invisible on the field (*Yuddha* 84.14–23). Vibhīṣaṇa then gives the exact terms of **Brahmā's boon**: "the enemy who strikes you down, armed as you are, before you reach Nikumbhilā and before you have fed the fire, he shall be your death" (*nikumbhilām asaṃprāptam ahutāgniṃ ca yo ripuḥ / tvām ātatāyinaṃ hanyād indraśatro sa te vadhaḥ*, Yuddha 85.14–15 [CE 6.72.13]). Vibhīṣaṇa, Rāvaṇa's defector brother and the only man in Rāma's camp who knows the ground, leads the strike-party himself. He shows Lakṣmaṇa the dark banyan, like a black cloud, that Indrajit must reach (Yuddha 87.2–3). Lakṣmaṇa, with Vibhīṣaṇa and Hanumān, **breaks the rite** and kills Indrajit in the duel that follows. The last arrow carries the **Aindrāstra**, Indra's missile, and an oath: "If Rāma son of Daśaratha is righteous and true to his word, and unrivalled in valour, then slay this son of Rāvaṇa." It strikes off Indrajit's head (*Yuddha* 86–87, 90.71–74 [CE 6.78.30–32]). Full treatment: [Nikumbhilā](../../06-sacred-geography/nikumbhila.md).
  - *Variant (the presiding goddess):* commentarial, southern and tantric tradition gives the altar a goddess of its own — **Nikumbhilā-devī**, identified with **Bhadrakālī** and, most widely in the south, with **Pratyaṅgirā** — and reads the rite as her invocation rather than a fire-oblation. Vālmīki names **no deity** for it. [late text] [regional]
  - *Variant (the "impossible man" condition):* a widespread later tradition holds that Indrajit could be killed **only by a man who had not slept, not eaten, and not seen his wife's face for fourteen years** — conditions **Lakṣmaṇa alone met** (sleepless by Nidrā's boon, fasting/eating only Rāma's leavings, and parted from Ūrmilā the whole exile), which is **why Lakṣmaṇa and not Rāma** is his slayer. This dovetails with the Ūrmilā-nidrā motif (§II). [folk] — note: **not in Vālmīki**. There the operative condition is Brahmā's boon (Yuddha 85.14–15), which turns on interrupting the Nikumbhilā rite, not on Lakṣmaṇa's austerities.
  - *Variant (the weapon):* Vālmīki itself names the **Aindrāstra** and Rāma's truth as the lethal charge (above). Later tellings that make Lakṣmaṇa's lifelong **brahmacarya** or his fourteen-year austerities the power in the arrow are the "impossible man" tradition again. [folk]
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna):* in the epic's own retelling Lakṣmaṇa's war is different. He kills **[Kumbhakarṇa](./kumbhakarna.md)**: he cuts off the giant's multiplying arms and burns him with the **Brahmāstra** (MBh 3.271.10–17). When Indrajit's arrows bind both brothers, Vibhīṣaṇa rouses them with the *prajñāstra* and Sugrīva draws out the arrows with the herb *viśalyā*. There is no flight to a mountain (MBh 3.273.5–7). Kubera sends water by a guhyaka so that they can see invisible beings. Lakṣmaṇa washes his eyes with it, attacks Indrajit "before he had done his rites" (*akṛtāhnikam*), and kills him with three arrows: one for each arm, the third for the head (MBh 3.273.9–24). No Nikumbhilā is named. See [Vana Parva](../mahabharata/03-vana-parva.md). [variant]
  - *Variant (Jain):* the Jain Vāsudeva Lakṣmaṇa kills **Rāvaṇa** himself, with the discus. See §I and [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md). [variant]
- **The pyre** - After Rāvaṇa's death Rāma receives Sītā with harsh words. Sītā asks Lakṣmaṇa to build her a pyre. Lakṣmaṇa looks at Rāma in anger, reads assent in his face, and builds it (Yuddha 116.18–21 [CE 6.104.21]).

## VI. After the war — return and refusal

- **Coronation** - With Rāvaṇa dead and Sītā recovered, the party returns to Ayodhyā and **Rāma is crowned**. Rāma then asks Lakṣmaṇa to rule the earth with him as **Yuvarāja (crown prince)**. Lakṣmaṇa will not accept, though Rāma entreats him in every way, and so Rāma consecrates **Bharata** as Yuvarāja instead (Yuddha 128.93–94 [CE 6.116.79]). The text gives no reason for the refusal. The usual explanation is the self-effacement he shows throughout, and deference to Bharata as the elder.
- **Counsel in the reign** - When Rāma thinks of a *rājasūya*, Bharata dissuades him. Lakṣmaṇa proposes the **aśvamedha** instead, as the sacrifice that cleanses every sin, and tells the story of **Indra freed from the sin of killing Vṛtra** by a horse-sacrifice (Uttara CE 7.74.8–19; 7.75–77).
- **Continued service** - Lakṣmaṇa remains Rāma's closest aide during the reign. After the public slander it is Lakṣmaṇa whom Rāma charges to **take the pregnant Sītā to the forest** and leave her near Vālmīki's hermitage (Uttara CE 7.44–45). At the Gaṅgā he breaks down. Death, he tells her, would be better than this deed the world will condemn. He sinks to the ground before her with joined palms, then tells her the truth and directs her to Vālmīki, his father's friend. When she has given him her last messages for the king, he cannot speak, circles her weeping, and recrosses the river (Uttara CE 7.46.4–18; 7.47.1–15). On the way back the charioteer Sumantra comforts him with a prophecy he once heard **Durvāsas** give Daśaratha: that Rāma would one day abandon **both Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa** (Uttara CE 7.49.10–16; 7.50.10–17). It foreshadows Lakṣmaṇa's own end. See [Uttara-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md).

## VII. His end — the Sarayū, Durvāsas, and Kāla (Uttara-kāṇḍa)

- **Kāla's secret visit** - Near the end of Rāma's reign, **Kāla (Time)** comes to the palace gate **in the form of an ascetic** (*tāpasarūpeṇa*). He is a messenger of **Brahmā** (Pitāmaha), and he tells Rāma that he is "your son from a former existence, born of māyā, Kāla who gathers up all things" (Uttara CE 7.93.1; 7.94.1–2; vulgate 7.103–104). He demands a **strictly private audience**: whoever hears or sees their talk **must be put to death** by Rāma. Rāma agrees, dismisses the doorkeeper, and sets **Lakṣmaṇa** to guard the door under that oath (CE 7.93.12–15). His message is Brahmā's: Rāma's term on earth is over, and he may return to the gods' world or stay, as he wishes (CE 7.94.3–19).
  - *Note:* *kāla* means both Time and Death, and translators often render him "Death" (so Shastri, vulgate 7.103); popular retellings make him **Yama**. Vālmīki's Kāla is not Yama but Brahmā's messenger and Viṣṇu's own māyā-born son. [late text]
- **Durvāsas's threat** - The volatile sage **[Durvāsas](../ambarisha-durvasa.md)**, son of Atri, arrives demanding to see Rāma at once. When Lakṣmaṇa asks him to wait a moment, he threatens to **curse the land, the city, Rāma, Bharata, and all their descendants**. Lakṣmaṇa weighs the two dooms: "Let it be my death alone, not the ruin of all" (*ekasya maraṇaṃ me 'stu mā bhūt sarvavināśanam*). He goes in and announces the sage (Uttara CE 7.95.5–9; vulgate 7.105). What Durvāsas wants turns out to be a meal, after a thousand-year fast; he eats and leaves (CE 7.95.13–15).
- **The sentence — and the bodily ascent** - Lakṣmaṇa cheerfully urges Rāma to kill him and keep the vow, "for those who break a vow go to hell" (CE 7.96.1–4). Rāma consults his counsellors. **Vasiṣṭha** tells him to give Lakṣmaṇa up rather than let the vow fail, since dharma itself would perish with it (CE 7.96.8–11). Rāma pronounces the sentence as **banishment**: "I send you away, Saumitri, lest dharma be overturned; for the good, abandonment and death are the same" (*tyāgo vadho vā vihitaḥ sādhūnām ubhayaṃ samam*, CE 7.96.13). Weeping, Lakṣmaṇa goes straight to the **Sarayū** without entering his own house. There he sips water, joins his palms, closes off all his senses, and stops his breath. The gods and Indra shower him with flowers. Then Śakra takes up the mighty Lakṣmaṇa **in his body** (*saśarīram*), invisible to all men, and enters heaven with him (CE 7.96.14–17; vulgate 7.106). He does **not** drown, and he does not leave a body behind: **Indra takes him up bodily, alive**. Popular retelling substitutes a *jala-samādhi* in the river, assimilating his end to Rāma's; the text keeps them different.
  - *Vālmīki's own gloss:* the gods in heaven rejoice and worship him as "**the quarter of Viṣṇu**" come home (*viṣṇoś caturbhāgam āgatam*, CE 7.96.18).
  - *Variant (Vaiṣṇava gloss):* on reaching heaven he **resumes his form as Śeṣa**, foreshadowing Rāma's own return to Vaikuṇṭha shortly after. This is the *Adhyātma* identification (§I) read into the ending. [late text]
  - *Variant:* some tellings name the door-guarding oath and the sage differently, or have Durvāsas curse **Lakṣmaṇa** ("you shall ascend to heaven alive"), folding the curse and the sentence into one. [variant]
  - *Variant (Jain, Raviṣeṇa's Padmapurāṇa):* in the Jain Rāma story Lakṣmaṇa's death has nothing to do with an oath. Two gods, testing the brothers' love, falsely announce in Ayodhyā that Rāma has died. Lakṣmaṇa falls lifeless at the news. Rāma, unable to accept the death, carries the body about with him, lamenting like a madman (Padmapurāṇa/Padmacarita 115–116, per the Bhāratīya Jñānapīṭha edition's summary; the Prakrit Paümacariya was not checked here). In Jain doctrine a Vāsudeva goes to hell (see §I). [variant]
- **Precursor to Rāma's departure** - Lakṣmaṇa's going **breaks Rāma**. That same day he resolves to install Bharata and go to the forest himself (CE 7.97.1–2). In the end he installs his sons Kuśa and Lava, divides the kingdom, and walks into the **Sarayū**. Brahmā hails him from the sky, "Come, Viṣṇu," and Rāma enters Viṣṇu's own radiance in his body, **with his younger brothers** (*saśarīraḥ sahānujaḥ*, CE 7.100.1–10; vulgate 7.108–110). Thus, true to character, **Lakṣmaṇa goes first.** That the brothers are reunited there as Viṣṇu and his serpent is the Vaiṣṇava reading; Vālmīki speaks only of Viṣṇu's form. [late text]

## VIII. Character, worship, and themes

- **Bhrātṛ-bhakti incarnate** - The supreme model of devotion to an elder sibling. His quick temper is the foil to Rāma's calm: against Daśaratha and Kaikeyī (Ayodhyā 21), against Bharata at Citrakūṭa (Ayodhyā 96), against Sugrīva (Kiṣkindhā 30–33), and against dharma itself (Yuddha 83). Together the two make a complete dharmic actor.
- **Reverence for Sītā** - He serves her as he serves Rāma. In the vulgate's anklet verse he knows her only by her feet (Kiṣkindhā 6.22–23). Accused by her, he says he cannot answer her because she is a deity to him, though he lashes out at women's nature before he goes (Araṇya 45 [CE 3.43.25–31]); he builds her pyre at her request (Yuddha 116), and in the end leaves her at the Gaṅgā in tears (Uttara CE 7.46–47).
- **Renunciation of the three comforts** - Sleep (to Nidrā/Ūrmilā), food (eating only after/with Rāma), and conjugal life (parted from Ūrmilā) — the triad that later tradition turns into the very key that unlocks Indrajit's death. Vālmīki's own warrant is slighter: the sleepless night with Guha (Ayodhyā 51) and the fourteen years apart from Ūrmilā, which the text never mentions.
- **Worship** - Almost universally depicted **at Rāma's right** (with Sītā and Hanumān) in Rāma temples; rarely worshipped in isolation, fitting his self-subordinating nature.
- **Ūrmilā's neglected sacrifice** - The Telugu songs and, in the modern period, Gupta's *Sāket* recover **[Ūrmilā](./urmila.md)** as the silent co-sufferer whose fourteen years apart — in the folk tradition, a fourteen-year sleep — mirror Lakṣmaṇa's fourteen-year vigil. [folk] [scholarly]
