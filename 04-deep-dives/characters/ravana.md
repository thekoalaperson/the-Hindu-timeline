# Rāvaṇa

> The ten-headed **rākṣasa** emperor of **Laṅkā** — supreme antagonist of the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md), abductor of **Sītā**, conqueror of the three worlds, and yet a **Śiva-devotee** who carries a golden liṅga on campaign, a Veda-learned brāhmaṇa's son, and (in later tradition) a vīṇā-player, Sāma-chanter and hymnist. A study in *tapas* misused: brilliance, learning, and devotion yoked to lust and pride.
> Sits in **[Tretā-yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** of the current [28th Mahāyuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md). His life is narrated across the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md) and his ancestry/back-story in the [Uttara-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md).
> Up: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md) · The curse-frame that includes him: [Jaya & Vijaya](../jaya-vijaya.md).

**Rāvaṇa** (Sanskrit *Rāvaṇa*, "He Who Makes (the Worlds) Cry Out" — caus. of √*ru*) is the ten-headed (**Daśagrīva, Daśānana**), twenty-armed king of the rākṣasas, ruler of golden **Laṅkā**. Born of a **brāhmaṇa father and a rākṣasī mother**, he is a hybrid: heir to the *tapas*-power and Vedic learning of the sage-line of **Pulastya**, and to the demonic ferocity of his mother's people. By terrific austerity he won near-invincibility from **Brahmā**, took Laṅkā from his half-brother **Kubera**, seized the flying chariot **Puṣpaka**, and humbled gods, gandharvas, and kings — until his abduction of **Sītā** brought down upon him the avatāra **[Rāma](./rama.md)**. His names — **Daśagrīva** (given by his father at birth), **Rāvaṇa** (given by Śiva at Kailāsa), **Laṅkeśvara, Paulastya, Daśakaṇṭha** — each mark a stage of that career. The Rāmāyaṇa is unusual in dignifying its villain: Vālmīki grants him grandeur and, after his death, Rāma's own respect.

*Reading the citations:* sarga numbers are the vulgate (Southern) numbering. Where a passage was checked against the **Baroda Critical Edition** it is given as "CE book.sarga.verse"; **[vulgate only]** marks a passage the CE does not print in its constituted text. The *Mahābhārata*'s own retelling (the [Rāmopākhyāna](../ramopakhyana.md), *Āraṇyaka-parvan* 258–275) and the Purāṇas are cited separately and never merged into Vālmīki.

## I. Ancestry and the Pulastya–Viśravas line (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa)

- **Pulastya** - "Son of Prajāpati" (Brahmā) in the Uttara's own words (Uttara 2; CE 7.2.4); counted among the mind-born sons of Brahmā and the **[Saptarṣi](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/saptarishi.md)** in the Purāṇic lists. He is Rāvaṇa's **paternal grandfather** (Pulastya → Viśravas → Rāvaṇa), and it is at his request that Kārtavīrya Arjuna later releases the captive Rāvaṇa (Uttara 33; CE 7.33.1–20). Full treatment: [Pulastya](./pulastya.md).
- **Viśravas (Viśrava)** - Son of Pulastya by the daughter of the royal sage **Tṛṇabindu**. At Tṛṇabindu's hermitage on the flank of Meru, the maidens who came to play there disturbed his tapas, and Pulastya declared that **any girl who came into his sight would conceive**. The others stayed away; Tṛṇabindu's daughter, who had **not heard** the warning, wandered in, heard him reciting the Veda, and found herself pregnant. Her father gave her to the sage, and the boy was named **Viśravas** because the Veda had been "heard" (*viśruta*) (Uttara 2; CE 7.2.5–28). By **Devavarṇinī**, Bharadvāja's daughter, Viśravas fathers **Vaiśravaṇa (Kubera)**, named by Brahmā (Uttara 3; CE 7.3.3–7); by Kaikasī he fathers Rāvaṇa's brood. The shared father makes **Kubera Rāvaṇa's elder half-brother**. Full treatment: [Viśravas](./vishravas.md); [Kubera](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/kubera.md).
  - *Variant (Purāṇic):* the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* names Kubera's mother **Iḍaviḍā (Ilavilā)** and Rāvaṇa's mother **Keśinī** (*BhP* 4.1.37, 7.1.43). [late text]
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna):* Kubera is Pulastya's own son; when Kubera leaves his father to attend on Brahmā, Pulastya in anger makes **half of himself** into a second son, **Viśravas**, to spite him (*MBh* 3.258.12–14). Kubera, seeking to placate this angry father, gives him **three rākṣasī attendants**, and each bears sons: **Puṣpotkaṭā** bears **Rāvaṇa and Kumbhakarṇa**, **Mālinī** bears **Vibhīṣaṇa**, and **Rākā** bears the twins **Khara and Śūrpaṇakhā** (*MBh* 3.259.1–8). There is no Kaikasī and no Sumālī. See [Rāmopākhyāna](../ramopakhyana.md).
- **Kaikasī** - Daughter of the rākṣasa **Sumālī** (son of **Sukeśa**). Sumālī, seeing Kubera pass in the Puṣpaka, sends her to woo Viśravas so that she may bear sons as splendid. She comes to him at a **"dreadful hour"** (*dāruṇā velā*) while he is at his agnihotra; Shastri's note reads it as dusk. For that reason, Viśravas tells her, **her sons will be cruel rākṣasas**; when she pleads, he concedes that **the last-born** will be righteous and worthy of his line (Uttara 9; CE 7.9.1–20). Full treatment: [Kaikasī](./kaikasi.md).
- **The four children of Viśravas & Kaikasī**, in the order Uttara-kāṇḍa 9 gives them -
  - **Rāvaṇa (Daśagrīva)** — born dark, with **ten heads, great fangs, copper lips and twenty arms**; jackals circle him, the sky rains blood, the sun dims and meteors fall. His father names him **Daśagrīva** (Uttara 9; CE 7.9.21–25).
  - **Kumbhakarṇa** — the giant brother, whose asked-for sleep belongs to Uttara 10 and whose six-month cycle belongs to Yuddha 61 (§III).
  - **Śūrpaṇakhā** — of misshapen face; the sister whose mutilation by Lakṣmaṇa ignites the war ([Araṇya-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md); [Śūrpaṇakhā](./shurpanakha.md)).
  - **Vibhīṣaṇa** — last-born, the righteous brother of Viśravas's promise, who defects to Rāma ([Vibhīṣaṇa](./vibhishana.md)). The rain of flowers, celestial drums and aerial "Excellent!" at his birth are **[vulgate only]**: Shastri's ch. 9 has them, CE 7.9.27 does not.
- **Maternal rākṣasa line** - Through Sumālī and Sukeśa, Rāvaṇa inherits the older rākṣasa dynasty once led by **Mālyavān, Sumālī, Mālī** — the original lords of Laṅkā driven out by Viṣṇu before Kubera held it (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 5–8). See [Sumālī & Mālyavān](./sumali-and-malyavan.md) and [rākṣasas](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/rakshasas.md).

## II. The ten heads — meanings and variants

- **Daśagrīva / Daśānana** - "Ten-Necks / Ten-Faces." The default Rāmāyaṇa portrayal: ten heads and twenty arms, a monstrous birth-form (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 9; CE 7.9.22). In the tapas he offers **nine** of them into the fire, one each thousand years, and Brahmā restores them (§III); in the last duel, every head Rāma cuts is replaced by another like it, **a hundred** in all (Yuddha 107; CE 6.96.20–24).
- *Variant (Mahābhārata):* the Rāmopākhyāna has the same offering — when a thousand years are complete Daśānana cuts off his head and offers it into the fire — and Brahmā promises that all the heads offered will be on his body again as he wishes (*MBh* 3.259.20–23).
- *Variant (Śiva Purāṇa):* in the Vaidyanātha legend the heads are offered to **Śiva**, not Brahmā: nine are cut, and Śiva appears before the tenth and restores them (*Śiva Purāṇa*, Koṭirudra-saṃhitā 28, as summarised in translation). [late text]
- *Variant (Jain, Vimalasūri's Paümacariya):* the ten heads are rationalised away. When he is young his mother gives him a **necklace of nine pearls** in which his face is reflected nine times, so that he shows ten faces and is called **Daśamukha** (as summarised from Ramanujan in Wikipedia, *Rama in Jainism*). The corpus's Jain page reads this as part of Vimalasūri's stated correction of the Brāhmaṇical poets, who give him ten literal heads. See [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md). [late text]
- *Variant (allegorical):* the ten heads represent **mastery of the four Vedas and six Śāstras** (Vedāṅgas) — i.e. Rāvaṇa as the supreme scholar. [folk/devotional]
- *Variant (allegorical):* the ten heads symbolize ten vices — **kāma, krodha, moha, lobha, mada, mātsarya** (lust, anger, delusion, greed, pride, envy) plus manas, buddhi, citta, ahaṅkāra — the inner enemies a devotee must conquer at **Daśaharā / Vijayadaśamī**. [folk]

## III. The tapas and Brahmā's boon (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 10)

- **The spur: his mother's taunt** - When Kubera comes in the Puṣpaka to visit their father, **Kaikasī** points him out to Daśagrīva: "see your brother, blazing with glory, and see what you are." Stung, Daśagrīva **vows to equal or surpass him**, and goes with his younger brothers to the hermitage of **[Gokarṇa](../../06-sacred-geography/gokarna-mahabaleshwar-atma-linga.md)** to win it by tapas (Uttara 9; CE 7.9.31–37). The *Mahābhārata* gives the same motive, the brothers' envy on seeing Kubera's splendour with their father (*MBh* 3.259.14–15).
- **The austerities** - Kumbhakarṇa stands among five fires in summer, sits in the vīrāsana in the rains, and stays in water through the winter; Vibhīṣaṇa stands on one leg for five thousand years and faces the sun for another five; Daśagrīva **fasts for ten thousand years** (Uttara 10; CE 7.10.2–10). Shastri's translation of the vulgate gives Kumbhakarṇa two thousand years; the CE gives ten thousand (CE 7.10.5).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* Daśagrīva stands on one foot for a thousand years, living on air amid five fires; Kumbhakarṇa sleeps on the ground; Vibhīṣaṇa eats one dry leaf; Khara and Śūrpaṇakhā wait on them (*MBh* 3.259.16–19).
- **The self-decapitation offering** - At the end of each thousand years Daśagrīva **cuts off one head and offers it into the fire**. After nine thousand years nine heads are gone; as he is about to cut the tenth, **Brahmā appears** with the gods, "pleased" (Uttara 10; CE 7.10.10–13).
- **The boon — invincibility with a fatal omission** - Rāvaṇa first asks for **immortality**: "for living beings there is no fear like death." In the CE he goes straight on, in the same speech, to ask (the vulgate inserts Brahmā's refusal first) to be **unslayable by suparṇas, nāgas, yakṣas, daityas, dānavas, rākṣasas and devas**, and adds, in his contempt, that **"all creatures beginning with men are straw to me"** (*tṛṇabhūtā hi me sarve prāṇino mānuṣādayaḥ*) (Uttara 10; CE 7.10.16–18). Men are the class he names and dismisses; the vānaras who will help kill him fall under "the rest." This omission is the hinge of the whole epic: only a **human (Rāma)** aided by **vānaras** can kill him.
  - **The rest of the grant** - Brahmā **restores the nine heads** that had been offered into the fire (CE 7.10.21–22). The vulgate adds a further boon, the power to **assume any form at will** — **[vulgate only]**: Shastri's ch. 10 has it, CE 7.10.19–22 does not.
  - *Roster note:* Uttara 10's own list has seven classes, and neither gandharvas nor piśācas is among them. The *Mahābhārata* list differs: **gandharvas, gods, asuras, yakṣas, rākṣasas, serpents, kiṃnaras and bhūtas**, and Brahmā himself states the exception, "**except from man**" (*ṛte manuṣyāt*). Brahmā's grant there also includes shape-shifting (*MBh* 3.259.24–27). The vulgate's Kailāsa sarga gives a third list when Rāvaṇa recites his immunities to Śiva (gods, gandharvas, dānavas, rākṣasas, guhyakas, nāgas) (Uttara 16, [vulgate only]).
- **Vibhīṣaṇa's boon** - Vibhīṣaṇa asks that his mind stay fixed on dharma whatever befalls him, and Brahmā, pleased that a rākṣasa's mind does not turn to adharma, **grants him immortality** unasked (Uttara 10; CE 7.10.23–30). The vulgate and the *Mahābhārata* add a second request, that the **brahmāstra** come to him without instruction (*MBh* 3.259.30–31).
- **Kumbhakarṇa's boon goes wrong** - Three different accounts, kept apart:
  - *Uttara 10 (CE 7.10.31–41):* the gods beg Brahmā not to give him a boon, since he has already **eaten seven apsarases and ten of Indra's attendants**, sages and men, and ask that he be given **delusion "under the guise of a boon."** Brahmā calls **Sarasvatī**, who enters Kumbhakarṇa's mouth; he asks "**to sleep for many years**" (*svaptuṃ varṣāṇy anekāni*), Brahmā grants it, and Kumbhakarṇa is left wondering what words came out of his mouth. No waking term is set.
  - *Yuddha 61 (Vibhīṣaṇa's account to Rāma):* no boon and no Sarasvatī. The new-born Kumbhakarṇa devours thousands of creatures and wounds Indra with a tusk torn from Airāvata, and Brahmā **curses** him to sleep like the dead. At Rāvaṇa's plea the term becomes **six months' sleep and one day's waking**.
  - *Mahābhārata:* he simply "chose a great sleep, his mind seized by tamas" (*MBh* 3.259.28).
  - *Variant (popular retellings):* the gloss that he **meant** to ask for *nirdevatvam* ("godlessness") or *Indrāsana* and said *nidrā* instead is in none of the three texts above. [later tradition] Full treatment: [Kumbhakarṇa](./kumbhakarna.md).

## IV. Conquest of the three worlds (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 11–34)

- **Taking Laṅkā — cession, then war** - Two distinct beats the retellings usually fuse. First, **Kubera cedes Laṅkā without a fight**. Once the boons are known, Sumālī and his ministers come up from Rasātala and urge Daśagrīva to reclaim the rākṣasas' city. He refuses at first — "the Lord of Wealth is our elder" — until **Prahasta** argues that brothers do not share power. **Prahasta** goes as envoy. Kubera consults their father, and **Viśravas**, who has already rebuked Daśagrīva, advises him to yield and move to **Kailāsa**. Rāvaṇa enters the empty city and is consecrated (Uttara 11; CE 7.11.1–41). Only later, after Rāvaṇa has ravaged Nandana and killed sages, does Kubera send an **envoy** urging him to abandon adharma. Rāvaṇa **cuts the envoy down with his sword and gives him to the rākṣasas to eat**, and marches on Kailāsa. The **yakṣa war** follows: Kubera is felled by Rāvaṇa's mace and carried to Nandana by his treasures, and the **Puṣpaka** is taken as spoil (Uttara 13–15; CE 7.13.9–39, 7.15.22–31).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* there is no cession; Rāvaṇa **drives Kubera out of Laṅkā in battle**, and Kubera withdraws to Gandhamādana. Vibhīṣaṇa follows Kubera and is made general of his yakṣa and rākṣasa armies (*MBh* 3.259.32–37).
- **The Puṣpaka Vimāna** - Kubera's **flying chariot**, which goes where its rider wills, with golden pillars, gateways of lapis and wish-granting trees (CE 7.15.29–31); it becomes Rāvaṇa's signature vehicle. After the war Rāma sends it back to Kubera. Kubera returns it, saying that it was **won by Rāma** and must carry him; Rāma then lets it go with the instruction to **come whenever he calls it to mind** (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 41; CE 7.40.2–11). See [Artifacts — vimānas](../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/README.md).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* as Rāvaṇa seizes the Puṣpaka, Kubera **curses** that it will not carry him but **will carry the one who kills him** (*MBh* 3.259.34–35).
- **Marriages** - Rāvaṇa gives his sister **Śūrpaṇakhā** in marriage to the Kālakeya dānava **Vidyujjihva**. Hunting, he meets the dānava **Maya**, son of Diti, wandering with his daughter **Mandodarī**, born of the apsaras **Hemā**. He marries her by the fire, and Maya, unaware of the curse on Viśravas's sons, adds the **infallible śakti** with which Rāvaṇa later fells Lakṣmaṇa (Uttara 12; CE 7.12.1–19). Mandodarī's brothers are **Māyāvī and Dundubhi** (CE 7.12.12). The names match the two demons **Vālī** kills in the Kiṣkindhā, though there Māyāvī is called Dundubhi's son, not his brother (CE 4.9.4), and the epic does not link the pairs. Their son is named **Meghanāda** for the thunder-like cry he gives at birth (CE 7.12.26–28). Full treatment: [Mandodarī](./mandodari.md).
- **Digvijaya — conquest of the directions** - Rāvaṇa answers Kubera's envoy with a vow to conquer the three worlds and **send the four Lokapālas to Yama's abode** (CE 7.13.37). In the campaign that follows he subdues yakṣas, nāgas, and kings, many of whom simply concede defeat (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 11–34). His son **Meghanāda** captures **Indra** with the invisibility-māyā Śiva had given him, and Brahmā names him **Indrajit** ("Conqueror of Indra"). To free Indra, Brahmā grants Indrajit a qualified immortality: he will die only if he goes into battle before completing his offering to the fire (Uttara 29–30; CE 7.29.22–28, 7.30.5–14). Full treatment: [Indrajit](./indrajit-meghnad.md).
- **Marutta's sacrifice** - At **Uśīrabīja** Rāvaṇa finds King **Marutta** sacrificing with the gods, his officiant **Saṃvarta** being Bṛhaspati's brother. The gods, rather than face him, hide in animal shapes: **Indra a peacock, Dharmarāja (Yama) a crow, Kubera a chameleon, Varuṇa a swan**. Marutta takes up his bow, but Saṃvarta forbids him to fight while consecrated for a Śaiva rite. Rāvaṇa's herald Śuka proclaims the victory, and Rāvaṇa **devours the sages** at the rite before he leaves. Afterwards each god blesses the creature that sheltered him: the peacock's tail takes Indra's thousand eyes; the crow is spared disease and dies only if men kill it; the swan turns moon-white; the chameleon turns gold (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 18; CE 7.18.1–32).
- **The Yamaloka war** - Goaded by **Nārada**, who tells him that mortals, already in Death's power, are not worth tormenting, Rāvaṇa turns south and storms Yama's realm (Uttara 20; CE 7.20). He fights Yama for **seven nights** (*saptarātram*, CE 7.22.13). When Yama at last raises the **kāla-daṇḍa** (the rod of Death, which cannot fail), **Brahmā appears and forbids the stroke**: if Rāvaṇa died, Brahmā's boon would be a lie, and if he did not, the rod would be. Either way Brahmā's word would fail. Yama withholds the weapon and vanishes from the field, leaving Rāvaṇa to proclaim his name and depart in the Puṣpaka. His "defeat of Death" is thus a stopped fight, not a kill (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 21–22; CE 7.22.31–42).
- **The nāgas and the Nivātakavacas** - He descends into Rasātala and subdues the nāga city **Bhogavatī**. Against the **Nivātakavacas** he fights for **more than a year** (*sāgraḥ saṃvatsaraḥ*) with no victory on either side. **Brahmā** then brokers a friendship sworn before the fire, and Rāvaṇa stays a year as their guest and learns **ninety-nine** māyās (*māyānāṃ śatam ekonam*) (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 23; CE 7.23.3–14). The same campaign carries him on against **Varuṇa's** sons and grandsons (CE 7.23.16–45).
- **The Kālakeya slaughter** - In the same sweep he takes the Kālakeya stronghold **Aśmanagara** and kills four hundred daityas (CE 7.23.15). Among them is **Vidyujjihva**, the husband he had himself given **Śūrpaṇakhā**. When she reproaches him, he pleads that in the frenzy of battle he could not tell his own from the enemy. He makes amends by posting her to Daṇḍaka with her maternal cousin **Khara**, with Dūṣaṇa and fourteen thousand rākṣasas (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 24; CE 7.24.17–35). See [Khara & the Janasthāna command](./khara-and-the-janasthana-command.md).
- **Madhu, and the death of Sumālī** - **Vibhīṣaṇa** rebukes him for the abducted women and tells him that the rākṣasa **Madhu** has carried off **Kumbhīnasī**: the daughter of Mālyavān's daughter, and so, by dharma, their sister. Rāvaṇa marches on Madhupura with Indrajit and Kumbhakarṇa, but spares Madhu at Kumbhīnasī's plea and takes him as an ally for the war on heaven (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 25; CE 7.25.17–48). That forbearance leaves Madhu's son **Lavaṇa** alive for Śatrughna to kill generations later. His maternal grandfather **Sumālī**, who set the whole dynastic project in motion, is struck down and burned to ash by **Sāvitra**, the eighth of the Vasus, in the war on Indraloka (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 27; CE 7.27.26–42).
- **Lifting Mount Kailāsa** - After the yakṣa war, the Puṣpaka stops dead over the reed-forest where Skanda was born. **Nandī** bars the way, since Śiva is at play on the mountain. Rāvaṇa laughs at his monkey-face, and Nandī curses him: **monkeys of Nandī's own form and strength will be born to destroy Rāvaṇa's race**, though, Nandī adds, Rāvaṇa is "already slain by his own deeds." Rāvaṇa then puts his arms under the mountain to **uproot** it. **Śiva**, laughing, presses it down with his great toe, crushing the arms beneath it, and Rāvaṇa's roar fills the three worlds. Pleased with his valour, Śiva releases him and names him **Rāvaṇa**, "he who made the worlds cry out" (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 16; CE 7.16.1–29). Full treatment: [Rāvaṇānugraha](../ravana-kailasa-ravananugraha.md).
  - *Recension note:* the CE has only the roar and the naming. The vulgate adds three things: on his ministers' advice he praises Śiva with hymns and lamentation for **a thousand years** before he is released; he asks for and receives a further span of life; and Śiva gives him the sword **Candrahāsa** with a warning that it will return if he slights it (Shastri ch. 16). **[vulgate only]**
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* the name has no Kailāsa story; he is called Rāvaṇa simply because he "made the worlds cry out" in his raids (*MBh* 3.259.40).
  - *Variant:* the **Rāvaṇānugraha** iconography, and the elaboration of the thousand-year lament into the singing of the *Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra*, are Purāṇic and Tamil Śaiva developments on the Uttara 16 base. [late text/regional]
- **Curbed by mightier kings** - The Rāmāyaṇa concedes Rāvaṇa was not unbeaten. On the Narmadā, while Rāvaṇa is worshipping his golden liṅga, **Kārtavīrya Arjuna**, the thousand-armed Haihaya king of Māhiṣmatī, dams the river with his arms, sporting with his women. The flood sweeps away the offerings. In the fight that follows, Arjuna stuns Rāvaṇa with his mace, **binds him with his thousand arms** and carries him off to the city. He frees him only when **Pulastya** comes to ask it, and the two swear friendship before the fire (Uttara 31–33; CE 7.32.1–7, 7.32.59–72, 7.33.1–20). The **vānara king Vālī**, set upon from behind at his twilight worship, tucks Rāvaṇa under his arm and carries him round **all four oceans** while finishing his sandhyā. Rāvaṇa, amazed, asks for a friendship sworn before the fire (Uttara 34; CE 7.34.1–42). A man and a monkey, the two classes his boon left out, are the ones who subdue him.
- **Wives and sons** - Chief queen **Mandodarī** (above), who warns him to make peace and laments him at the end (§VI). Another consort, **Dhānyamālinī**, draws him away when he threatens Sītā in the Aśoka grove (Sundara 22; CE 5.20.36–38). She is also the mother of **Atikāya** (Yuddha; CE 6.59.29). Sons include **Meghanāda/Indrajit**, **Akṣa (Akṣakumāra)**, **Atikāya**, **Narāntaka**, **Devāntaka**, **Triśiras**, most slain in the [Yuddha-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md), and Akṣa by Hanumān in the Sundara. The *Sundara-kāṇḍa* describes a vast harem.

## V. The curses that doomed him — Vedavatī, Rambhā/Nalakūbara, Puñjikasthalā, Jaya-Vijaya

- **Vedavatī → Sītā** - A brahmarṣi's daughter (her father **Kuśadhvaja**, son of Bṛhaspati, was killed by the daitya Śambhu for refusing her to anyone but **Viṣṇu**) is doing *tapas* to win Viṣṇu as husband. Rāvaṇa mocks Viṣṇu and **seizes her by the hair**. She **cuts off the hair he touched** and prepares to enter the fire. Strictly, she **declines to curse him**: a woman cannot kill him, she says, and a curse would spend her tapas. Instead she vows to be **reborn, not from a womb, as the daughter of a righteous man, for his destruction**. The sarga itself identifies her rebirth: she rises from the furrow and men call her **Sītā** (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 17; CE 7.17.6–31). Full treatment: [Vedavatī](./vedavati.md).
  - *Variant (Sītā as Rāvaṇa's own daughter):* a separate strand, not a version of the Vedavatī story, makes Sītā the exposed infant of Rāvaṇa and Mandodarī. It is found in the Jain texts (Guṇabhadra's *Uttarapurāṇa*, Saṅghadāsa's *Vasudevahiṇḍī*), the Śākta *Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*, and Southeast-Asian retellings. See [Mandodarī](./mandodari.md) and [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md). [late text/regional]
- **Rambhā & Nalakūbara's curse** - On the night march to Indraloka, Rāvaṇa stops the apsaras **Rambhā** on her way to **Nalakūbara**, Kubera's son. She pleads that she is betrothed to his brother's son and so is "**by dharma your daughter-in-law**." He brushes the plea aside and **rapes** her. Nalakūbara, told of it, takes water and curses that **if Rāvaṇa ever again forces an unwilling woman, his head will split into seven pieces** (*mūrdhā tu saptadhā*). The Uttara adds that, hearing the curse, **Rāvaṇa lost all desire for unwilling women** (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 26; CE 7.26.19–47). The Uttara does not name Sītā here, but this is in effect its explanation of why the captive is never forced. The CE has no second curse alongside it, since it lacks the Puñjikasthalā confession below.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* the Rāmopākhyāna knows the same curse but with a different term. A well-wisher reassures Sītā that she is protected by **Nalakūbara's curse**, laid on Rāvaṇa for violating Rambhā, his brother's son's bride (*MBh* 3.264.58–59). After the war **Brahmā** tells Rāma that the curse was his own provision for her safety: if Rāvaṇa forced any unwilling woman, **his body would split into a hundred pieces** (*śatadhā*) (*MBh* 3.275.32–33). The MBh thus pairs Nalakūbara's name with the hundredfold term that the vulgate Yuddha gives to Brahmā's separate Puñjikasthalā curse.
- **Brahmā's curse over Puñjikasthalā** - A **second and separate** curse to the same effect, and the one Rāvaṇa himself invokes. In the vulgate, when **Mahāpārśva** urges him to take Sītā by force (Yuddha 13.1–8), Rāvaṇa confesses a secret. He once saw the apsaras **Puñjikasthalā** on her way to Brahmā's abode, stripped her and forced her. Brahmā then cursed that if he ever again forced a woman, **his head would burst into a hundred pieces** (*śatadhā*). Fear of that curse, he says, is why he has not taken Sītā by force (Rām. Yuddha-kāṇḍa 13.10–15). **[vulgate only]**: vulgate Yuddha 10–15 have no counterpart in the CE, where Vibhīṣaṇa's first counsel (CE 6.9) runs straight into Rāvaṇa's rebuke (CE 6.10 = vulgate 16). The CE's only Puñjikasthalā is Hanumān's mother Añjanā in a former life (CE 4.65.7–8). The two curses are distinct, with distinct terms, not two names for one.
- **Nandī's curse** - At Kailāsa, mocked for his monkey-face, Nandī curses that **monkeys of his own form and strength will be born to destroy Rāvaṇa's race** (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 16; CE 7.16.14–15; §IV above).
- **Anaraṇya's curse** - The Ikṣvāku king **Anaraṇya** of Ayodhyā, felled by a blow of Rāvaṇa's palm, says that Time, not Rāvaṇa, has defeated him. Dying, he swears by his gifts, sacrifices and just rule that **a king born in his own Ikṣvāku line will take Rāvaṇa's life**; drums sound and flowers fall from the sky as he speaks. This is the Solar-dynasty warrant for Rāma (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 19; CE 7.19.16–25). Full treatment: [Anaraṇya & Rāvaṇa's curse](../../03-lineages-vamsha/anaranya-ravana-curse.md).
- **The abducted women's collective curse** - On the way home from Varuṇa's city he kills the kin of every beautiful girl or woman he sees and loads them into the Puṣpaka: daughters of nāgas, yakṣas, men, rākṣasas, daityas and dānavas. The chaste wives among them curse him together: since he delights in other men's wives, **he will die because of a woman** (*strīkṛtenaiva vadhaṃ prāpsyati*) (Rām. Uttara-kāṇḍa 24; CE 7.23.46, 7.24.1–16). Read together, the Uttara's warrants name different instruments: a woman (the captives; Vedavatī's vow), a king of Anaraṇya's line, monkeys (Nandī), and his own lust (Nalakūbara, and in the vulgate Brahmā). All of them converge on the war.
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* the Rāmopākhyāna adds Kubera's curse on the Puṣpaka (§IV; *MBh* 3.259.34–35) and keeps the Nalakūbara curse (§V above), but none of the Uttara's other curses.
  - *Variant (cow/sage curses):* regional tellings multiply further minor curses earned during his conquests. [folk]
- **The Jaya–Vijaya frame** - The *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* overlays a theological cause. Viṣṇu's Vaikuṇṭha gatekeepers, cursed by the Kumāras (*BhP* 3.15–16) to three demonic births (*BhP* 7.1.37–38), are born second as the rākṣasas **Rāvaṇa and Kumbhakarṇa**, sons of Viśravas by **Keśinī**, "tormentors of all the worlds," and Rāma kills them "to free them from the curse" (*BhP* 7.1.43–44). The cycle runs Hiraṇyakaśipu/Hiraṇyākṣa → Rāvaṇa/Kumbhakarṇa → Śiśupāla/Dantavakra. Pierced through the heart by Rāma's arrows, they leave the body absorbed in him, so that Rāvaṇa's death is a cursed soul's return to God by *vairānubandha* (devotion-through-enmity) (*BhP* 7.1.45–46, 7.10.35–37). Full treatment: [Jaya & Vijaya](../jaya-vijaya.md).
  - *Variant:* the Rāmāyaṇa itself does **not** know this frame — it gives Rāvaṇa a self-contained Pulastya ancestry; the gatekeeper-overlay is retrofitted by the Purāṇas. [late text]
  - *Pairing note:* later tradition usually makes **Jaya → Rāvaṇa, Vijaya → Kumbhakarṇa**; the *Bhāgavata* verses cited speak of the two jointly and do not assign them one by one. [later tradition]

## VI. The war and death (Rām. Yuddha-kāṇḍa)

- **The abduction** - Provoked by Śūrpaṇakhā's mutilation and inflamed by her account of Sītā's beauty, Rāvaṇa has **[Mārīca](./maricha.md)** take the golden-deer form to lure Rāma away (Araṇya 35–44). He then approaches Sītā **disguised as a wandering mendicant**, in ochre robe, with staff and water-pot (Araṇya 46; CE 3.44.1–7). He throws off the disguise and **seizes her by the hair with his left hand and by the thighs with his right**. The vehicle is a **golden chariot yoked to mules** (*kharayuktaḥ*), never named Puṣpaka (Araṇya 49; CE 3.47.15–18). **[Jaṭāyu](./jatayu-and-sampati.md)** shatters it, and Rāvaṇa completes the flight carrying her bodily (Rām. Araṇya-kāṇḍa 51–52). See [Araṇya-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md).
- **Sītā in Aśoka-vana** - Held captive but unviolated. The Uttara attributes this to Nalakūbara's curse, and the vulgate Yuddha, in Rāvaṇa's own voice, to Brahmā's (§V). In Laṅkā he gives her **twelve months**, after which, he says, his cooks will cut her up for his breakfast (Araṇya 56; CE 3.54.22). When he comes to her in the Sundara he tells her that **two months** of the term remain (Sundara 22; CE 5.20.8). **Hanumān** finds her in the [Sundara-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/5-sundara-kanda.md) and burns Laṅkā.
- **Counsel refused** - Saramā tells Sītā that his own **mother** and the aged minister **Aviddha** had urged him to give her back, and that he would not hear it (Yuddha 34; CE 6.25.20). Counselling the same, the righteous brother **Vibhīṣaṇa** is spurned and exiled; he joins Rāma and reveals Rāvaṇa's secrets ([Yuddha-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md)).
- **The illusory head** - To break Sītā, he has the sorcerer **Vidyujjihva** fashion a severed **head and bow of Rāma** by māyā, and throws them down before her (Yuddha 31–32; CE 6.22.6–43).
- **The first duel, and Rāma's mercy** - In their first encounter Rāma cuts his bow, kills his horses and charioteer, and shatters his great diadem. Then, finding him exhausted, he refuses to kill him: since Rāvaṇa is weary, he will not put him in Death's power with his arrows, and the king goes back into Laṅkā (CE 6.47.127–133). The vulgate adds Rāma's instruction to go, take breath, and come back in his chariot with his bow (Yuddha 59.140–144).
- **The fall of his champions** - Kumbhakarṇa is roused and slain; **Indrajit**, master of the **Brahmāstra** and invisibility (and the Nāgāstra that fells Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa), is finally killed by **Lakṣmaṇa** — breaking Rāvaṇa's last great hope ([Yuddha-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md)).
- **Rāma vs. Rāvaṇa — the final duel** - The fight runs through day and night (the vulgate: for seven days). Each head Rāma cuts is replaced by another like it, a hundred in all, and Rāma wonders why the arrows that killed Mārīca, Khara and Virādha now fail (Yuddha 107; CE 6.96.20–31). **Mātali**, Indra's charioteer, reminds him of the **Brahmā-weapon** (Yuddha 108.1–2; CE 6.97.1–2). Rāma takes up the arrow **made by Brahmā for Indra**, which the sage **Agastya** had given him long before at his hermitage in the forest. Wind is in its feathers, fire and sun in its point, its shaft is made of ether, and it is as heavy as Meru and Mandara (Yuddha 108.3–13; CE 6.97.3–13; the gift itself, Araṇya 12, CE 3.11.29–34). Rāma charges it by the Vedic rite and looses it. It falls on Rāvaṇa's **chest and splits his heart**, enters the earth, and, its work done, **returns silently to Rāma's quiver** (Yuddha 108.14–20; CE 6.97.14–19). There is no amṛta and no navel anywhere in the sarga.
  - *Recension note:* earlier on the field **Agastya** teaches Rāma the **Āditya-hṛdaya** hymn to the sun (Yuddha 105). **[vulgate only]**: the CE does not print the sarga, and in CE Yuddha Agastya never comes onto the field; in the battle he is named only as the giver of the arrow (CE 6.97.4).
  - *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas):* **Vibhīṣaṇa** tells Rāma that **nectar dwells in the pool of Rāvaṇa's navel** (*nābhikuṃḍa piyūṣa basa yākeṃ*) and that it keeps him alive. Rāma looses **thirty-one arrows**: one dries up the navel and the rest strike the heads and arms (Tulsīdās, *Rāmcaritmānas*, Laṅkā-kāṇḍ, after dohā 101(kha) and dohā 102). This is the famous popular image, and it is **not Vālmīki's** — his weapon takes the heart. [late text]
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata):* Rāma charges a gold-feathered arrow with the brahmāstra. Its fire consumes Rāvaṇa **with his chariot, horses and charioteer**, so that his flesh and blood are destroyed and **not even ash is seen** (*MBh* 3.274.24–31). [variant]
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata Purāṇa):* the arrow **splits his heart** and he falls from his aerial car (*vimāna*) vomiting blood from his ten mouths (*BhP* 9.10.23); compare 7.10.37, "their hearts pierced by Rāma's arrows."
  - *Variant (Jain):* **Lakṣmaṇa**, not Rāma, kills Rāvaṇa, as the Vāsudeva must kill the Prativāsudeva, and both then go to hell (Vimalasūri, *Paümacariya*, and the Jain tradition after him). See [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md). [late text]
- **Mandodarī's lament** - Over his body Mandodarī asks how a mere man could kill him. She decides it was his own senses, once conquered by his tapas, taking their revenge, or else Indra come in Rāma's form. She reproaches him for desiring Sītā, who was no better than her in birth, beauty or courtesy (CE 6.99.1–28). The vulgate expands the lament and has her recognise Rāma as **Viṣṇu**, eternal, bearing conch, discus and mace, with the gods around him in vānara form (Yuddha 111.14–17). **[vulgate only]**
- **A dignified end** - Rāma orders the **funeral rites** with the words "**enmities end at death; our purpose is accomplished; let his rites be done; he is mine as much as yours**" (*maraṇāntāni vairāṇi … mamāpy eṣa yathā tava*). The two recensions differ on what precedes it. In the CE, **Vibhīṣaṇa first refuses** to cremate a cruel, lawless violator of other men's wives, and Rāma answers that Rāvaṇa was a mighty hero, unbeaten by the gods, before speaking the line above (CE 6.99.29–40). The vulgate has the line twice. The first time it follows a **eulogy** by Vibhīṣaṇa: his brother kept the sacred fires, practised great austerities, had gone to the end of the Veda and excelled in rites (*eṣo 'hitāgniś ca mahātapāś ca vedāntagaḥ*) (Yuddha 109.21–25). The second time it answers the same refusal the CE has (Yuddha 111.95–102). [vulgate for the eulogy]
  - *Variant (Lakṣmaṇa's lesson):* Rāma sends Lakṣmaṇa to the dying Rāvaṇa to learn statecraft and wisdom from the fallen sage-king. This is a beloved episode, but it is found in neither Vālmīki nor the Mahābhārata. [folk/regional]
- **Aftermath** - Vibhīṣaṇa is crowned king of Laṅkā; Sītā's chastity is affirmed (the **Agni-parīkṣā**); Rāma flies home in the recovered **Puṣpaka** for the coronation that founds **Rāma-rājya** (return narrated in [Yuddha-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md)).

## VII. The other Rāvaṇa — devotee, scholar, artist

- **Parama-Śiva-bhakta** - Rāvaṇa is among the great devotees of **[Śiva](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/README.md)**. In Vālmīki the devotion appears in two places. On campaign a **golden liṅga** is carried wherever he goes, and on the Narmadā he sets it on an altar of sand and worships it with perfumes and flowers (Uttara 31; CE 7.31.38–39). After the Kailāsa episode he has a name given by Śiva himself (§IV). The Śaiva *Rāvaṇānugraha* ("Śiva's grace to Rāvaṇa") is a major iconographic theme; see [Rāvaṇānugraha](../ravana-kailasa-ravananugraha.md).
- **Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra** - The thunderous Sanskrit hymn (in *pañcacāmara* metre) praising Śiva's cosmic dance is **traditionally attributed to Rāvaṇa** and sung, in that tradition, while he was pinned beneath Kailāsa. The nearest the epic comes is the vulgate's thousand years of hymns under the mountain (Uttara 16, [vulgate only]). The Tamil Śaiva telling adds that he **tore off one of his heads and strung a vīṇā from it with his own sinews**, giving its name to the bowed **rāvaṇahattha**. See [Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra](../stotras/shiva-tandava-stotra.md). [late attribution; Tamil Śaiva tradition]
- **The Ātma-liṅga at Gokarṇa** - Pleased, Śiva grants Rāvaṇa the **Ātma-liṅga** to carry to Laṅkā, on condition it never touch the ground. **Gaṇeśa**, as a boy, tricks him into setting it down at **Gokarṇa** (Karnataka), where it roots forever as **Mahābaleśvara** (Gokarṇa-māhātmya; *Skanda Purāṇa*, Sahyādri-khaṇḍa). Vālmīki's Gokarṇa is the place of the brothers' tapas (§III), with no liṅga story. See [Gokarṇa](../../06-sacred-geography/gokarna-mahabaleshwar-atma-linga.md). [regional Sthala-purāṇa]
  - *Variant (Śiva Purāṇa, Vaidyanātha):* a separate telling. After the head-offering, Śiva gives Rāvaṇa a liṅga that will fix itself wherever it is set down. On the way, by Śiva's māyā, Rāvaṇa is seized by the need to relieve himself and hands the liṅga to a **cowherd boy**, who cannot bear its weight and puts it down. It becomes **Vaidyanātha**, most often identified with Deoghar (Jharkhand), though Parli and Baijnath also claim it (*Śiva Purāṇa*, Koṭirudra-saṃhitā 28, as summarised in translation). See [12 Jyotirliṅgas](../../06-sacred-geography/12-jyotirlingas.md). [late text]
- **Learned brāhmaṇa** - By his father's line a brāhmaṇa. In Vālmīki his learning is attested chiefly by Vibhīṣaṇa's eulogy: keeper of the sacred fires, great ascetic, **one who had gone to the end of the Veda** (*vedāntaga*), foremost in rites (Yuddha 109.23, [vulgate only]). In the *Mahābhārata* all of Viśravas's rākṣasa children are "**knowers of the Veda**" (*sarve vedavidaḥ*) (*MBh* 3.259.13). The fuller reputation is later. It makes him master of the **four Vedas and six Vedāṅgas**, a great **Sāma chanter** (the CE Sundara, Yuddha and Uttara have no reference to his Sāma-singing), and author of works on **Ayurveda (Arka-prakāśa), astrology, and the Śaiva āgamas** — even **Rāvaṇa-saṃhitā** astrological texts bear his name. [later tradition; late attributions]
- **Yajña-officiant for Rāma** - In a striking irony of some retellings, Rāma asks the dying or living Rāvaṇa (the supreme brāhmaṇa-scholar) to **officiate the pre-war yajña** to consecrate the bridge / worship Śiva at Rāmeśvaram. [folk]
- **Regional veneration** - Rāvaṇa is honored, not burned, in pockets of India and Sri Lanka. **Bisrakh** (Gautam Buddh Nagar district, near Greater Noida, **Uttar Pradesh**) claims to be his birthplace and holds yajñas instead of burning the effigy. **Mandsaur** (Madhya Pradesh), claimed as Mandodarī's home, mourns him as the region's son-in-law. **Ravangram** (Vidisha, Madhya Pradesh) calls him "Rāvaṇa Bābā." At **Mandore** (Jodhpur, Rajasthan), believed to be the site of his wedding, a Brahmin community honours him as son-in-law. In Gadchiroli (Maharashtra) the Gond community calls itself Rāvaṇavaṃśī. Shrines also exist in Kanpur (Uttar Pradesh), Kakinada (Andhra Pradesh) and Kolar and Mandya (Karnataka). In Sri Lankan tradition he is a king/ancestor figure. [regional]
