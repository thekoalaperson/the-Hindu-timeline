# Tāṭakā (Tāḍakā) — Rāma's First Slaying

> The **yakṣiṇī-turned-rākṣasī** of the [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md) — a beautiful nature-spirit cursed into a monstrous flesh-eater for ravaging the lands of sage [Agastya](../agastya.md), mother of [Mārīca](./maricha.md) and Subāhu, and the **first being Rāma kills** under the tutelage of [Viśvāmitra](../vishwamitra-vasishtha.md). Her death is the epic's first *dharma*-debate (may a kṣatriya slay a woman?) and the gateway to Rāma's mastery of the divine *astras*.
> Up: [Epic Characters](README.md) · Section: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md).

**Tāṭakā** (Sanskrit *Tāṭakā*; also **Tāḍakā**) is a **yakṣiṇī** (female nature-spirit) of immense strength, daughter of the yakṣa **Suketu**, wife of **Sunda**, and mother of **[Mārīca](./maricha.md)** and **Subāhu**. Cursed by the sage **[Agastya](../agastya.md)** into rākṣasa-hood after her family's assault on him, she lays waste the **Malada–Karūṣa** country, blocking the road Viśvāmitra travels with the boy princes. On Viśvāmitra's command and dharma's authority, **[Rāma](../ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md)** kills her with arrows — his maiden slaying — and is rewarded with the full arsenal of celestial weapons. Her tale sits in **[Tretā-yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** of the current 28th Mahāyuga (*itihāsa*-time); academic clocks date the Rāmāyaṇa quite differently — two clocks throughout. [scholarly]

---

## I. Birth, beauty, and the strength of a thousand elephants (Rām. Bāla 24–25)

- **Daughter of Suketu** - Tāṭakā is born to the **yakṣa Suketu**, a powerful and righteous nature-spirit who, being childless, won her from Brahmā through austerities; Brahmā granted him a daughter endowed with the **strength of a thousand elephants** in lieu of a son (Vālmīki, Bāla 25).
- **A yakṣiṇī, not born a demon** - She begins as a **beautiful yakṣiṇī**, lovely in form — her monstrousness is acquired by curse, not native. The text is emphatic that she was *once* fair, which sharpens the tragedy and the *dharma*-problem (Vālmīki, Bāla 25).
- **Marriage to Sunda** - When grown, Suketu gives her in marriage to the yakṣa (in some readings asura) **Sunda**, son of Jharjha; their sons are **Mārīca** and **Subāhu**, who will themselves attack Viśvāmitra's later sacrifice (Vālmīki, Bāla 25). See [Mārīca](./maricha.md).

---

## II. The curse of Agastya (Rām. Bāla 25)

- **Sunda's death** - **Sunda** is destroyed — in Vālmīki's telling, killed by the sage **[Agastya](../agastya.md)**'s power (in some readings reduced to ashes by Agastya's curse) after he and his sons trouble the ṛṣi (Vālmīki, Bāla 25).
- **Tāṭakā's revenge** - Maddened by grief, **Tāṭakā together with her son Mārīca rushes to devour Agastya**, charging the hermitage to avenge Sunda (Vālmīki, Bāla 25).
- **The double curse** - Agastya curses both: **Mārīca becomes a rākṣasa**, and **Tāṭakā loses her beauty, becoming a hideous man-eating monster** of distorted face and fearsome form — condemned to a deformed, cannibal existence (Vālmīki, Bāla 25).
- **The ravaged country** - In fury at her transformation, she devastates the once-prosperous **Malada and Karūṣa** region near Agastya's grove, turning fertile land into a haunted wasteland that travellers shun for generations (Vālmīki, Bāla 24–25).
  - *Variant (curse-agent):* most retellings keep **Agastya** as the curser; a few loose popular versions blur Sunda's killer and Tāṭakā's curser, but Vālmīki ties both to Agastya. [folk]
  - *Variant (cause of Sunda's death):* some tellings say Sunda was slain by **Indra's thunderbolt** or in conflict with the gods, with Agastya cursing only after Tāṭakā's attack on his āśrama; details shift across recensions. [disputed]

## III. The forest and Viśvāmitra's charge (Rām. Bāla 24–26)

- **Crossing into her wasteland** - Leading Rāma and **Lakṣmaṇa** from Ayodhyā toward his **Siddhāśrama** sacrifice, [Viśvāmitra](../vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) brings the boys to the desolate **Tāṭakā forest** (*Tāṭakā-vana*) and recounts her story (Vālmīki, Bāla 24).
- **The command to kill** - Viśvāmitra orders Rāma to **slay Tāṭakā** for the welfare of brahmins and cattle, since she pollutes the land and devours all who enter (Vālmīki, Bāla 25–26).
- **Rāma's scruple** - Rāma voices the central hesitation: she is a **woman**, and the kṣatriya code forbids killing one. He recalls his father's instruction to obey the sage, weighing filial command against the prohibition on slaying a female (Vālmīki, Bāla 26).
- **The dharma-resolution** - Viśvāmitra and Rāma reason that **the protection of the world overrides the protection of an individual evildoer**: a being who harms the innocent must be slain regardless of sex, citing precedents (e.g. Indra's slaying of **Mantharā**, Viṣṇu's killing of Bhṛgu's wife) where even women were justly killed for the common good. The *āpad-dharma* of a ruler is to destroy a destroyer (Vālmīki, Bāla 26). [scriptural]

---

## IV. The slaying — Rāma's first kill (Rām. Bāla 26)

- **Summoned by sound** - Rāma twangs his bowstring to challenge her; the noise enrages Tāṭakā, who **charges invisibly, hurling rocks and raising a storm of dust** at the brothers (Vālmīki, Bāla 26).
- **Lakṣmaṇa's mutilation, then the kill** - The brothers first **sever her arms, ears, and nose** to repel her without killing; when she persists, raining stones by her illusory powers, **Rāma kills Tāṭakā with an arrow to the chest/heart** as she rushes them (Vālmīki, Bāla 26).
- **The first slaying** - This is **Rāma's maiden act of killing** — the inaugural deed of the avatāra's mission, foreshadowing the slaughter of Rāvaṇa's host. The forest is at once purified (Vālmīki, Bāla 26).
- **The gods' rejoicing** - **Indra and the devas applaud**; Indra urges Viśvāmitra to now bestow the divine weapons on Rāma, the worthy slayer, before they proceed (Vālmīki, Bāla 26).
  - *Variant (disfigure-first, kill-by-night):* several regional and televised retellings stage it as Rāma **only disfiguring her by day** and slaying her fully when she attacks again **invisibly at night** — dramatizing his reluctance to kill a woman until forced. [regional]
  - *Variant (Lakṣmaṇa's role):* in some versions Lakṣmaṇa performs the maiming of nose and ears while Rāma delivers the death-shot. [folk]

## V. Tulsīdās' Rāmcaritmānas (Bāla Kāṇḍa)

- **Devotional framing** - In Tulsīdās' **[Rāmcaritmānas](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)** (Bāla Kāṇḍa), the episode is told briefly and devotionally: **Tāḍakā** (Tāṛkā) rushes upon Rāma, and he kills her with a single arrow, the act framed less as moral dilemma than as the Lord beginning his work of clearing the earth of demons. [late text]
- **Liberation by the Lord** - Consistent with bhakti theology, the Mānas tradition stresses that **death at Rāma's hand is itself a grace** — Tāṭakā, slain by the Supreme, is delivered from her cursed demon-state, a redemptive reading of the killing. [folk/devotional]
- **Compressed debate** - Tulsīdās largely **omits the long Vālmīki dharma-argument** about killing a woman, since for him Rāma's command-obedience to Viśvāmitra and divine identity settle the matter; the scruple is muted. [late text]

## VI. The reward: divine weapons and the wider arc

- **The astra-arsenal** - Pleased by the slaying, Viśvāmitra **confers on Rāma the full array of celestial astras and their saṃhāra (recall) mantras** — the missiles of Śiva, Viṣṇu, Indra, Varuṇa, Agni, Vāyu, the daṇḍa, dharma, kāla, and brahmaśiras weapons — which appear as embodied beings pledging service (Vālmīki, Bāla 27–28). Tāṭakā's death is thus the **initiation into divine weapon-craft**. See [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md).
- **Her sons' continuing role** - Tāṭakā dies, but her sons live on: at Siddhāśrama Rāma uses the **Mānava astra** to hurl **[Mārīca](./maricha.md)** a hundred yojanas into the sea (sparing him — he returns as the golden deer in the Araṇya Kāṇḍa) and the **Āgneya astra** to **kill Subāhu** (Vālmīki, Bāla 30). The Tāṭakā line is the first demon-family Rāma confronts.
- **Structural function** - The episode forms a deliberate trio with the **astra-gift** and the **Subāhu/Mārīca** combat: a test (Tāṭakā), an empowerment (the weapons), and an application (defending the sacrifice) — Rāma's military and moral apprenticeship in miniature.

## VII. Meaning, motifs, and reception

- **The first dharma-knot** - Tāṭakā's killing is the Rāmāyaṇa's opening ethical puzzle: it establishes that **dharma is contextual** — the rule "do not kill a woman" yields to the higher duty of protecting the innocent from a proven predator, a model of *āpad-dharma* (duty in extremity). [scholarly]
- **Beauty cursed to monstrosity** - Like **Surpaṇakhā** and other rākṣasīs, Tāṭakā embodies the once-fair female nature-being **deformed by transgression against a sage** — the yakṣiṇī collapsing into the rākṣasī, the wild punished into horror.
- **The avatāra's first blood** - That the perfect man's career of slaying begins with a **woman, and reluctantly**, is pointed: Rāma kills only on sagely command and only the irredeemably harmful, distinguishing the dharmic warrior from the demon he destroys.
- **Place lore** - Local tradition associates the **Tāṭakā-vana** with regions of Bihar/eastern India along the route from Ayodhyā toward Mithilā; the identification is traditional rather than fixed. [folk]
