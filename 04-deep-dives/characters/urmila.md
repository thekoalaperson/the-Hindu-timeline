# Ūrmilā — Janaka's Second Daughter, and the Wife Left in Ayodhyā

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Ūrmilā** — younger daughter of **Janaka** of Mithilā, sister of [Sītā](./sita.md), wife of [Lakṣmaṇa](./lakshmana.md), and mother of Aṅgada and Candraketu. Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). Vālmīki gives her four verses and no scene; the fourteen-year **Ūrmilā-nidrā** for which she is famous belongs to the folk and modern strata, and both layers are kept separate below. Her marriage sits in the [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md); the tradition that grew around her belongs to the [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/2-ayodhya-kanda.md)'s silence.
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* she belongs to the **Tretā Yuga** of the present 28th Mahāyuga, 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara; the scholarly baseline is Vālmīki (cited by vulgate/Southern sarga), over which Telugu women's song, North Indian *Rām-kathā*, and Maithilīśaraṇ Gupta's *Sāket* (1931) layer in that order. [scholarly]

## Name and identity

- **Ūrmilā (ऊर्मिला)** — glossed from *ūrmi* ("wave, surge"), "she of the waves" or "the enchantress"; the **second daughter of Janaka (Sīradhvaja)** of Mithilā and the younger sister of Sītā, married to Lakṣmaṇa in the fourfold Mithilā wedding (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa* 71–73).
- **Janaka's own daughter, not a niece** — Ūrmilā and Sītā are Janaka's daughters; **Māṇḍavī** and **Śrutakīrti**, who marry Bharata and Śatrughna on the same day, are the daughters of Janaka's younger brother **Kuśadhvaja** and so their first cousins (Bāla 71–73). See [Bharata, Śatrughna & their wives](./bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md) and the [Videha–Mithilā line](../../03-lineages-vamsha/videha-mithila-line-janaka.md).
- **Her mother is unnamed in Vālmīki** — the epic never names Janaka's queen; later Maithilī and *Rāmcaritmānas* tradition calls her **Sunayanā**. [regional] [late text]
- **Born, not found** — the asymmetry that governs everything written about the two sisters afterwards: Sītā is *ayonijā*, raised from the furrow at the plough's tip, while Ūrmilā is Janaka's daughter in the ordinary way. Only Sītā is the *vīrya-śulka* ("valour is her bride-price") of the bow-trial; Ūrmilā is given by Janaka's own decision, outside any contest (Bāla 66, 71). [scholarly]
- **Note on namesakes** — an **Ūrmilā** appears elsewhere in the Bāla Kāṇḍa as the mother of the gandharvī **Somadā**, who serves the ascetic Cūlī; that figure is unrelated to Janaka's daughter (Bāla 33; see [Kuśanābha's daughters & Brahmadatta](../../03-lineages-vamsha/kushanabha-daughters-and-brahmadatta.md)). [scholarly]

## What Vālmīki actually gives her

Four passages, and no scene of her own. Everything else told about Ūrmilā is supplied by later tellers working in the space this leaves.

- **The betrothal (Bāla 71.20–22)** — reciting the Videha genealogy to Daśaratha, Janaka bestows **Sītā on Rāma and Ūrmilā on Lakṣmaṇa**, calling Ūrmilā "the second," her equal in virtue and in beauty, and repeating the gift **three times** in the formal manner of a vow (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa* 71).
- **The wedding (Bāla 73.30, 73.39)** — at the altar Janaka calls the second brother forward — *"Come, Lakṣmaṇa, take the palm of Ūrmilā, whom I have determined to give you, into your own; let the hour not lapse"* — and the four couples circumambulate the fire three times under **Vasiṣṭha**, with Viśvāmitra and Śatānanda (Bāla 73). See [Janakpur–Mithilā](../../06-sacred-geography/janakpur-mithila-sita-janmabhumi.md) for the **Vivāha Pañcamī** observance built on this day.
- **Sītā names her to Anasūyā (Ayodhyā 118.53)** — telling her own story in the forest, Sītā ends the marriage account with her sister: *"My younger sister Ūrmilā, virtuous and lovely to look on, was given by my father himself to be Lakṣmaṇa's consort"* (*Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa* 118). This is the **only time Ūrmilā is named after the wedding** in the whole of Vālmīki — and it is Sītā, in exile, who names her.
- **Her sons (Uttara 102)** — deep in Rāma's reign, Rāma resolves to crown Lakṣmaṇa's two sons, **Aṅgada** and **Candraketu**; on Bharata's proposal the region of **Kārupatha** is chosen and two cities founded, **Aṅgadīyā** for Aṅgada in the western quarter and **Candrakāntā** among the Mallas in the northern (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 102). **Vālmīki does not name their mother in the passage**; that they are Ūrmilā's is an inference from the marriage, universal in tradition and nowhere stated in the text. [scholarly]
  - *Note on namesakes:* this Aṅgada, son of Lakṣmaṇa, is not the vānara prince [Aṅgada](./angada.md), son of Vālī. [scholarly]

## The silence of the Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa

- **No farewell** — Lakṣmaṇa's leave-taking before the exile is a scene Vālmīki writes at length, and Ūrmilā is not in it: he bows to Kausalyā, then clasps the feet of his own mother **[Sumitrā](./sumitra.md)**, who gives him her charge and sends him with *gaccha, gaccha* — "go, go" (*Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa* 40.4–9). The wife is neither consulted, nor refused, nor mentioned.
- **The asymmetry that drives the whole later tradition** — Sītā argues her way into the forest across three sargas (*Ayodhyā* 27–30) and goes; Lakṣmaṇa insists and goes (*Ayodhyā* 31). Ūrmilā neither argues nor goes, and the epic does not say she was told. The fourteen years pass on both sides of a door the text never opens. [scholarly]
- **Not a portrait of indifference** — the omission is of a piece with Vālmīki's handling of the three junior brides: Māṇḍavī and Śrutakīrti likewise vanish after Bāla 73 and reappear only as mothers of sons in the Uttara. It is the poem's economy, not a judgement on Ūrmilā — but it is the vacancy every later Rāmāyaṇa found impossible to leave alone. [scholarly]

## The Ūrmilā-nidrā — the fourteen years of sleep

- **The story** — on the first night of the exile the goddess **Nidrā (Sleep)** comes to Lakṣmaṇa, who begs to be released from sleep for the whole fourteen years so that he can guard Rāma and Sītā by night as well as by day. Nidrā consents on one condition: **someone must sleep his share**. Lakṣmaṇa names Ūrmilā, and in Ayodhyā she accepts and **sleeps continuously for fourteen years**, waking only when the exile ends — in the fullest tellings, waking in time for Rāma's coronation while Lakṣmaṇa at last sleeps.
- **Where it comes from — and where it does not** — the episode is **not in Vālmīki**, and it is **not in Tulsīdās's *Rāmcaritmānas*** either. Its identified home is the **oral and folk *Rām-kathā***, most concretely the **Telugu women's songs** — the ballad cycle known as ***Ūrmilādevi Nidra*** ("Ūrmilā Devi's sleep"), sung in Andhra households, which makes her sleep and her waking the emotional centre of the whole exile. [folk] [oral tradition] [regional]
  - *Variant (how the sleep is transferred):* in some tellings Lakṣmaṇa names his wife and Nidrā carries the burden to her; in others the transfer is automatic and Ūrmilā is never asked — the difference decides whether the sacrifice is hers or is merely done to her. [folk]
  - *Variant (the waking):* in the Telugu songs the waking is the climax — Ūrmilā, roused after fourteen years, does not recognise her husband, and must be told what has happened to the world while she slept. [regional]
- **The modern canonization — Gupta's *Sāket*** — Maithilīśaraṇ Gupta's Hindi epic ***Sāket*** (1931) rebuilds the entire *Rāmāyaṇa* around Ūrmilā's separation, giving the silent wife the interior life the Sanskrit text withholds and making her renunciation the equal of Lakṣmaṇa's. It is *Sāket*, more than any older source, that fixed Ūrmilā in the modern North Indian imagination as "the forgotten one." [late text] [scholarly]
- **The "impossible man" chain** — a widespread later tradition holds that Indrajit could be slain only by a man who had not slept, not eaten and not seen his wife's face for fourteen years — conditions Lakṣmaṇa alone met, which is why the sleep-boon is not decorative but load-bearing in those tellings. The device is **not in Vālmīki**, where Indrajit's death turns on the interruption of the Nikumbhilā rite; the full treatment sits at [Lakṣmaṇa §II](./lakshmana.md) and [Indrajit](./indrajit-meghnad.md). [folk]

## Afterlife and later traditions

- **The Jain telling** — in the Jain Rāmāyaṇa tradition Lakṣmaṇa is a *Vāsudeva* with many wives, among whom Ūrmilā appears under the name **Pṛthvīsundarī** as his principal consort — a wholly different frame from the Sanskrit epic's monogamous portrait. See [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md). [regional] [late text]
- **Maithilī memory** — in Mithilā, where the wedding is re-enacted every year at **Vivāha Pañcamī**, Ūrmilā is honoured as the second bride of the fourfold marriage and not as an afterthought; the Maithilī songs of the *vivāha* cycle name all four brides (see [Janakpur–Mithilā](../../06-sacred-geography/janakpur-mithila-sita-janmabhumi.md)). [regional]
- **The recovery tradition** — from the Telugu songs through *Sāket* to twentieth-century retellings and stage work, Ūrmilā has become the standing example of the epic's unnarrated women: the argument is not that Vālmīki wronged her but that the silence itself is worth reading, and that the folk tradition had already read it centuries before the modern retellings did. Sympathy extended to her is routinely extended onward to Māṇḍavī and Śrutakīrti. [scholarly]
