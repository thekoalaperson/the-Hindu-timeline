# Daśaratha

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Daśaratha** — the last pre-Rāma Ikṣvāku (Sūryavaṃśa) king of Ayodhyā, father of [Rāma](../ramayana/README.md), and the hinge whose boon-debt and death-by-grief drive the exile narrative. Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). His three queens are [Kausalyā](./kausalya.md), [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md), and [Sumitrā](./sumitra.md); his place in the Solar line runs through the [Sūryavaṃśa & Candravaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) tree and the [Raghuvaṃśa king-list](../../03-lineages-vamsha/raghuvamsha-kalidasa-kings.md).
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* he reigns in the Tretā Yuga of the present 28th Mahāyuga, 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara; the scholarly baseline is the Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa* (cited here by vulgate/Southern sarga throughout, with the Baroda Critical Edition locator added as "CE" where it was checked), with the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.10 supplying the terse king-list frame and Kālidāsa's *Raghuvaṃśa* (cantos 9–12) the polished mahākāvya retelling. [scholarly]

**Daśaratha** (Sanskrit *Daśaratha*, "he whose chariot [goes in] ten [directions]," i.e. a warrior whose car turns to all ten quarters; Kālidāsa's synonym **Paṅktiratha**, *paṅkti* = "ten," says the same, *Raghuvaṃśa* 9.74) is the reigning sovereign of Kosala at the *Rāmāyaṇa*'s opening — son of **Aja**, and the immediate forebear of the avatāra. Who Aja's father was depends on the text: Vālmīki's king-list makes him **Nābhāga**, Kālidāsa and the *Bhāgavata* make him **Raghu** (see §I). A righteous, long-reigning, but childless king, he is the structural pivot of the epic: his sonless grief sets the **putrakāmeṣṭi** in motion, his half-forgotten **boon-debt** to Kaikeyī forces the exile, and his **death by grief** — predetermined by an old curse — removes the one figure who might have recalled Rāma, leaving the kingdom to Bharata's regency and the forest to its fated course.

## I. Lineage and place in the Solar line

- **Last Ikṣvāku king before Rāma** - Daśaratha is the reigning *cakravartin* of the senior **Sūryavaṃśa / Ikṣvāku** branch seated at Ayodhyā; with him the pre-Rāma king-list closes and the avatāra-generation opens (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 5–6; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.10). See the [Sūryavaṃśa tree](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **Son of Aja — and whose grandson?** - all the texts agree that his father is **Aja**; they disagree about the generation above. The variants are kept apart:
  - *Vālmīki:* at Sītā's wedding Vasiṣṭha recites the Ikṣvāku line from Brahmā, and it runs **… Kakutstha → Raghu → Kalmāṣapāda → Śaṅkhaṇa → Sudarśana → Agnivarṇa → Śīghraga → Maru → Praśuśruka → Ambarīṣa → Nahuṣa → Yayāti → Nābhāga → Aja → Daśaratha** (Bāla 70.42–43; CE 1.69.26–30, *nābhāgasya babhūvāja ajād daśaratho 'bhavat*). Raghu sits many generations up, and his son is Kalmāṣapāda, not Aja. When Vasiṣṭha repeats the list to Rāma at Citrakūṭa, Nahuṣa's son is Nābhāga, and Nābhāga has two sons, **Aja and Suvrata** (Ayodhyā 110; CE 2.102.27–28).
  - *Kālidāsa, Raghuvaṃśa:* **Raghu → Aja → Daśaratha**. Aja is Raghu's son, and his love for **Indumatī** fills cantos 5–8, so Daśaratha is Raghu's grandson. See [Aja and Indumatī](../aja-indumati.md) and the [Raghuvaṃśa king-list](../../03-lineages-vamsha/raghuvamsha-kalidasa-kings.md).
  - *Bhāgavata Purāṇa:* **Khaṭvāṅga → Dīrghabāhu → Raghu → Aja → Daśaratha** (*raghus tasmāt pṛthuśravāḥ / ajas tato mahārājas tasmād daśaratho 'bhavat*, BhP 9.10.1). Here *pṛthuśravāḥ* reads naturally as an epithet of Raghu, "of wide fame". It could also be taken as an extra king between Raghu and Aja. [late text]
- **A model righteous reign** - Vālmīki opens with a long encomium: Ayodhyā prosperous and well-walled; the king learned in the Vedas, self-controlled and truthful, guarding the world "as Manu did" and the city as Indra guards Amarāvatī; master of horse and chariot; served by able ministers and the house-priest **Vasiṣṭha** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 5–7; CE 1.6.1–5).
- **The eight ministers and two priests** - his counsel rests on eight ministers. The roster differs by recension: CE 1.7.2 names Dhṛṣṭi, Jayanta, Vijaya, Siddhārtha, Arthasādhaka, Aśoka, Mantrapāla and **[Sumantra](./sumantra.md)**, while the vulgate (Bāla 7.3) has Surāṣṭra, Rāṣṭravardhana, Akopa and Dharmapāla in place of Siddhārtha, Arthasādhaka, Aśoka and Mantrapāla. Beside them stand two *ṛtvij* priests, **Vasiṣṭha** and **Vāmadeva** (Bāla 7.4; CE 1.7.3). The ministers are the ones he consults before the Aśvamedha (CE 1.8.3). Sumantra goes on to drive Rāma into exile and to bring the empty chariot home.
- **Three principal queens** - he weds three chief queens — the senior [Kausalyā](./kausalya.md), daughter of the Kosala king (*kosalendraduhitā*, CE 2.59.11), the favourite [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md) of Kekaya, and the wise [Sumitrā](./sumitra.md). When Rāma takes leave, **350 other wives** (*ardhasaptaśatāḥ*, "half of seven hundred") gather round Kausalyā (Ayodhyā 34.13; CE 2.31.10), but the narrative turns on the three.
- **A very long life without an heir** - despite the three queens Daśaratha remains sonless deep into a very long reign. He tells Viśvāmitra he is **sixty thousand years old**, and that Rāma was "begotten with difficulty" (*ṣaṣṭir varṣasahasrāṇi jātasya mama kauśika*, Bāla 20.10; CE 1.19.10). At the coronation assembly he says it again himself, having lived "many thousands of years" (Ayodhyā 2; CE 2.2.6), and the assembly answers that he is an old man "of many thousands of years" (CE 2.2.15). The numbers are formulaic epic idiom, but they belong to the critical text and are not a late addition. The long heirlessness is the one flaw in an otherwise perfect kingship (Bāla 8.1; CE 1.8.1).

## II. The foundational curse — Śravaṇa-kumāra (the seed of his death)

The decisive fact of Daśaratha's life is told only at its end, as a deathbed confession to Kausalyā, but it governs everything: his death is *fated*, and the exile merely the trigger.

- **The accidental killing** - the deed comes from his youth. "You were not yet married to me, and I was the crown prince" (*devy anūḍhā tvam abhavo yuvarājo bhavāmy aham*, Ayodhyā 63.14; CE 2.57.10), and he was then proud of his name as a **śabda-vedhī**, an archer who can hit what he only hears (Ayodhyā 63.11). Hunting by night on the bank of the **Sarayū** in the rains, he hears a water-pot being filled, takes the gurgle for an **elephant** drinking, and shoots at the sound. The arrow strikes an ascetic boy filling a water-pot for his aged, blind parents (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 63; CE 2.57.14–18). In his first lament, before the king reaches him, the boy says he grieves less for his own death than for the blind couple who now have no one to keep them (CE 2.57.23–25).
  - *The boy is unnamed in Vālmīki.* The text never gives him a name, and Kālidāsa calls him only a *muniputra* (*Raghuvaṃśa* 9.75). "**Śravaṇa / Śravaṇa-kumāra**" (and, in some tellings, **Sindhu**) is the name the later and folk tradition supplies, and it is now so universal that the anonymity of the original is generally forgotten. [folk]
- **The dying boy's answer — "I am not a brāhmaṇa"** - the mortally wounded youth reproaches the king ("what wrong had I done you?", CE 2.57.29), then asks him to go at once to his father's hermitage, along the footpath, and appease him before the old man can curse him (CE 2.57.34–35). He then **lifts the king's dread of brahmahatyā**: *"na dvijātir ahaṃ rājan… śūdrāyām asmi vaiśyena jātaḥ"* — "I am no twice-born, O king; I am born of a **śūdrā** mother by a **vaiśya** father" (Ayodhyā 63.53; CE 2.57.37). He asks Daśaratha to draw the arrow out (CE 2.57.36), and he dies as the shaft is pulled free (Ayodhyā 63.54–55). The CE's verse here (2.57.38) records only the drawing of the arrow; the death at that moment is stated when the king tells the father what happened (CE 2.58.14–16). The king then carries the filled pot to the parents (CE 2.58.2).
- **The blind parents' curse** - the old father tells the king that had he hidden the deed, his head would have burst into a hundred thousand pieces. Because it was done in ignorance, Daśaratha is spared, "or the whole Rāghava line would perish" (CE 2.58.19–21). The king leads the couple to the body. As they lament, the son appears in a divine form and rises to heaven in a celestial car (Ayodhyā 64.48–51; CE 2.58.40–42). The father then pronounces the curse: **"as I now grieve for a son, so you, O king, shall meet your end through grief for a son"** (*evaṃ tvaṃ putraśokena rājan kālaṃ kariṣyasi*, Ayodhyā 64.55; CE 2.58.46).
  - *How the parents die:* in the vulgate they lay themselves on the funeral pyre and go to heaven (Ayodhyā 64.58). The CE has no pyre verse. The father only foretells that the couple will soon follow their son to Yama's abode (CE 2.58.31–32).
  - *Variant (the curse as a boon — Kālidāsa):* the reading that the curse is also a grace, promising a childless king a son to grieve for, is **Kālidāsa's, not Vālmīki's**. In the *Raghuvaṃśa* Daśaratha answers: "Even this curse, lord, cast on one who has not yet seen a son's lotus face, comes with favour; fire that burns the ploughed field still makes it bear the seed" (paraphrasing *śāpo 'py adṛṣṭatanayānanapadmaśobhe sānugraho bhagavatā mayi pātito 'yam*, *Raghuvaṃśa* 9.80). The reading cannot work in Vālmīki's chronology, where the killing happens before his marriage.
  - *Variant (Kālidāsa's whole telling, Raghuvaṃśa 9.72–82):* in Kālidāsa the episode happens on a hunt by the reigning king, not the crown prince, and the poet tells it at once, not as a deathbed confession. The river is the **Tamasā** (9.72), not the Sarayū. The poet adds that in shooting he did "what is forbidden to a king" (*nṛpateḥ pratiṣiddham eva tat kṛtavān paṅktirathaḥ*, 9.74). Daśaratha carries the boy, arrow still in him, to his parents (9.77), and the boy dies only when they have the king draw the arrow out (9.78). The boy himself says he is the son of an ascetic who is not a brāhmaṇa (*dvijetara-tapasvi-suta*, 9.76). The father curses with water from his own tears in his hands (9.78–79). The couple then ask for fire so they can follow their son, and an additional verse (9.82a in the GRETIL text) says Daśaratha performed the cremation (9.81–82a). [scholarly]
- **The two clocks of the curse** - within the story the curse is a single human tragedy; in the providential reading it is the mechanism by which the gods guarantee the avatāra's exile will not be undone by a father's recall — Daśaratha must die before he can fetch Rāma back. The text's own statement is narrower: the dying king says the sage's words "have now come upon me" (Ayodhyā 64.61; CE 2.58.47). [scholarly]

## III. The Putrakāmeṣṭi and the four sons

- **The Aśvamedha and the son-seeking rite** - to end his childlessness Daśaratha resolves on the great **Aśvamedha** (horse-sacrifice) and consults his ministers (Bāla 8; CE 1.8.1–3). Sumantra then tells him of **Sanatkumāra**'s old prophecy that the brahmin-deer ascetic **Ṛṣyaśṛṅga** would come to Ayodhyā and bring sons to an Ikṣvāku king (Bāla 9–11). Ṛṣyaśṛṅga is summoned to officiate, and after the Aśvamedha (Bāla 12–14) he performs the **son-desiring rite** (*putrīyā iṣṭi*, popularly **Putrakāmeṣṭi**) (Bāla 15). See the dedicated [Ṛṣyaśṛṅga](../rishyashringa.md) episode.
- **Ṛṣyaśṛṅga as ṛtvij** - Ṛṣyaśṛṅga, whose own legend (the drought of Aṅga, his luring to Lomapāda's court) precedes this, is fetched from Aṅga and presides over the rite. Daśaratha goes in person to his friend Lomapāda (Romapāda) to ask for him, as the prophecy foretold (CE 1.10.4, 1.10.19–22).
  - *Vālmīki:* Śāntā is called **Lomapāda's daughter**. Sanatkumāra's prophecy pairs Daśaratha's friendship with the king of Aṅga with "a daughter named Śāntā" (*kanyā cāsya … śāntā nāma*, Bāla 11.3; CE 1.10.3; the pronoun does not settle whose daughter). The unambiguous line is Daśaratha's own request that Lomapāda let "**your daughter** Śāntā" come to Ayodhyā with her husband (*śāntā tava sutā rājan*, Bāla 11.19; CE 1.10.19). When she arrives, the palace women rejoice with affection at the sight of her (*prītyānandam upāgaman*, Bāla 11.30; CE 1.10.28). The "as if their own daughter" in the English on valmikiramayan.net is the translator's gloss, not the Sanskrit. The *Harivaṃśa* likewise calls her Lomapāda's daughter (HV 23.36).
  - *Purāṇic variant:* Śāntā is **Daśaratha's own daughter**, given to his childless friend Lomapāda to raise as a daughter. The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* says that Daśaratha, son of Aja, placed a girl (*kanyā*) named Śāntā with the childless Romapāda "in the status of a daughter" (*yasyājaputro daśarathaḥ śāntāṃ nāma kanyām anapatyasya duhitṛtve yuyoja*, ViP 4.18.17–18). The *Bhāgavata* has Daśaratha, his friend, give him "his own daughter Śāntā" (*śāntāṃ svakanyāṃ prāyacchat*, BhP 9.23.7–8). On this account Ṛṣyaśṛṅga is Daśaratha's son-in-law, and the summons becomes a family matter. Neither Purāṇa names her mother. That she was **Kausalyā's** child belongs to later and regional retellings. Full treatment: [Ṛṣyaśṛṅga](../rishyashringa.md). [dominant tradition]
- **The divine pāyasa** - from the sacrificial fire a radiant being rises bearing a golden vessel of **pāyasa** (sweet milk-rice), and he introduces himself as a "being sent by Prajāpati" (*prājāpatyaṃ naraṃ viddhi*, Bāla 16.16; CE 1.15.14). Devotional retellings make him Agni. The pāyasa is "god-made" and "makes offspring" (CE 1.15.17), and it carries the gods' answer: the descending portions of Viṣṇu, who at the devas' petition has agreed to be born to slay Rāvaṇa (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 15–16).
- **The division among the queens** - Daśaratha apportions the pāyasa: a full half to **Kausalyā** (→ **Rāma**); of the remainder, shares to **Sumitrā** and **Kaikeyī**, with Sumitrā served twice so that she bears the **twins Lakṣmaṇa & Śatrughna**, Kaikeyī bearing **Bharata**. In Vālmīki's own arithmetic Sumitrā gets half of the remainder (a quarter), Kaikeyī half of what is left (an eighth), and the final eighth goes "again to Sumitrā" (Bāla 16.27–29; CE 1.15.25–27). The exact sequence of the second and third shares varies by recension — catalogued in [Kausalyā](./kausalya.md). [disputed — the order, not the arithmetic]
  - *Variant (Kālidāsa):* the king divides the oblation (*caru*) between **Kausalyā and Kaikeyī** only. Each of them then gives Sumitrā half of her own share, so Sumitrā's twins come from both senior queens' portions (*Raghuvaṃśa* 10.55–57). [scholarly]
- **Birth of the four sons** - in due course are born **Rāma** (to Kausalyā), **Bharata** (to Kaikeyī), and the twins **Lakṣmaṇa** and **Śatrughna** (to Sumitrā) — the four into whom Viṣṇu's *aṃśas* descend, named by Vasiṣṭha after the eleventh day (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 18; CE 1.17.6–12; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.10). See [Bharata, Śatrughna and their wives](./bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md).
- *Variant (Bhāgavata compression):* the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* records the sons and the avatāra-purpose tersely within the Ikṣvāku king-list — Hari, "petitioned by the gods," becomes his son "in four parts" (*aṃśāṃśena caturdhāgāt putratvaṃ prārthitaḥ suraiḥ*, BhP 9.10.2) — omitting the sacrificial machinery that Vālmīki develops at length (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.10). [late text]

## III-a. The father's first trials — Viśvāmitra, Mithilā and Paraśurāma

- **The blank promise to Viśvāmitra** - when **[Viśvāmitra](./vishvamitra.md)** arrives at court, Daśaratha promises to do whatever he asks before he has heard the request: "I will do it entirely; you are my deity" (*kartā cāham aśeṣeṇa daivataṃ hi bhavān mama*, Bāla 18.57–58; CE 1.17.38). The sage then asks for Rāma for ten days, to guard his sacrifice from two rākṣasas (CE 1.18.17).
- **The refusal** - the king refuses. Rāma is "**not yet sixteen**" (*ūnaṣoḍaśavarṣo me rāmaḥ*, Bāla 20.2; CE 1.19.2), untrained in war, no match for rākṣasas. Daśaratha offers to go himself with a full *akṣauhiṇī* instead, says he cannot live "a moment" without Rāma, and pleads his own sixty thousand years and the hard-won son (CE 1.19.3–10).
- **Rebuked for breaking his word** - Viśvāmitra's anger is put in terms of the dynasty: "having first promised the thing, you wish to abandon your pledge — this reversal is unworthy of the Rāghavas" (paraphrasing *pūrvam arthaṃ pratiśrutya pratijñāṃ hātum icchasi / rāghavāṇām ayukto 'yaṃ kulasyāsya viparyayaḥ*, Bāla 21.2; CE 1.20.2). Vasiṣṭha tells the king that a man who says "I will do it" and then does not loses the merit of all his sacrifices and gifts (*iṣṭāpūrtavadho bhūyāt*, Bāla 21.8; CE 1.20.8), and Daśaratha lets Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa go. The scene rehearses in small the Ayodhyā crisis: a promise given before the request is known, a father's love set against his word, and his word winning. [scholarly]
- **The weddings at Mithilā** - summoned by Janaka after Rāma breaks Śiva's bow, Daśaratha travels to Mithilā. There Vasiṣṭha recites the Ikṣvāku genealogy given above (Bāla 70; CE 1.69), and all four sons are married to Sītā, Ūrmilā, Māṇḍavī and Śrutakīrti (Bāla 73). See [Sītā](./sita.md).
- **Begging Paraśurāma** - on the road home **Rāma Jāmadagnya** blocks the party. Daśaratha, "downcast, with joined palms," begs him: "you have laid aside your wrath against the kṣatriyas, you are a brāhmaṇa — grant safety to my boys" (*bālānāṃ mama putrāṇām abhayaṃ dātum arhasi*, Bāla 75.6; CE 1.74.5–6). Paraśurāma ignores him and speaks only to Rāma (CE 1.74.10). See [Paraśurāma](../parashurama.md).

## IV. The boon-debt to Kaikeyī and the Devāsura war

- **Boons earned in battle** - long before, campaigning as **Indra's ally** in a great deva–asura war, Daśaratha took Kaikeyī with him to the south, toward Daṇḍaka. The enemy was the many-wiled asura **Timidhvaja**, "known as **Śambara**," in the city of **Vaijayanta**. Unconscious in that great battle, the king was **carried off the field by Kaikeyī** (*apavāhya tvayā devi saṃgrāmān naṣṭacetanaḥ*), and "there too," wounded by weapons, he was protected by her. In gratitude he granted her **two boons, to be claimed whenever she wished** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9.11–17; CE 2.9.9–14, retrospectively narrated by Mantharā). The vulgate adds two verses the CE lacks: that the rākṣasas fell on the wounded and the sleeping by night, and that the king was cut down by the asuras' weapons (Ayodhyā 9.14–15). The text does not say how she bore him away.
  - *Later tellings:* the popular image of Kaikeyī driving his chariot, or holding a broken axle with her own hand, is not in Vālmīki. See [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md). [folk]
- **The boons left dormant** - Kaikeyī names no wish at the time, and the two boons lie unredeemed for years. Mantharā says she knows the story only because Kaikeyī once told it to her herself (Ayodhyā 9.18; CE 2.9.14), and it is she who revives the boons (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9–11). The full variant-catalogue of *how* she saved him is in [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md).
- **The decision to crown Rāma** - aged and feeling his years, Daśaratha resolves to consecrate **Rāma** as *yuvarāja*, summons the assembly and gains its joyful assent (Ayodhyā 1–2). He tells Rāma plainly why he is hurrying. He has had terrible dreams, meteors have fallen, and the astrologers say his birth-star is beset by the Sun, Mars and Rāhu — omens that usually mean a king's death or ruin (Ayodhyā 4; CE 2.4.17–19). So the consecration is fixed for the very next day, under **Puṣya** (CE 2.4.21–22), and he says outright that it should be done "while Bharata is away from the city" at Kekaya (*viproṣitaś ca bharato yāvad eva purād itaḥ*, CE 2.4.25), though he adds that Bharata is righteous and devoted to his elder brother (CE 2.4.26) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 1–6).
- **Mantharā redeems the boons** - the hunchbacked maidservant **Mantharā** poisons Kaikeyī's mind and coaches the exact demands the two dormant boons can buy: **(1) Bharata consecrated** as heir, and **(2) Rāma exiled to the Daṇḍaka forest for fourteen years** (Ayodhyā 9.20; CE 2.9.15). Kaikeyī states them to the king as "**nine and five years**" (*nava pañca ca varṣāṇi*) of ascetic life in Daṇḍaka, in bark and matted hair (Ayodhyā 11.26; CE 2.10.28) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 7–11). See [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md) and [Mantharā](./manthara.md).
- **Binding the king by oath** - finding Kaikeyī prostrate in the *krodhāgāra* (anger-chamber), the doting Daśaratha swears to grant whatever she asks — and only then does she name her price (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 10–11). The form of the oath differs by recension:
  - *Vulgate:* he names Rāma as dearest to him, then swears **by Rāma himself**, "without whom I could not live a moment" (*tena rāmeṇa kaikeyi śape te vacanakriyām*, Ayodhyā 11.6–8), and also by his own merit (*sukṛtenāpi te śape*, Ayodhyā 11.10).
  - *CE:* the oath by Rāma is absent. He names Rāma as the one man dearest to him, then swears only by his own merit (*sukṛtenāpi te śape*, CE 2.10.17–19).
  - In both, Kaikeyī then calls the thirty-three gods, sun and moon, the quarters and the household deities to witness that the king grants her boon (Ayodhyā 11.12–16; CE 2.10.21–24).
- **Daśaratha's collapse and the Raghu-vow** - the king faints, weeps, rages, and pleads through the night, offering wealth, kingdom, anything but this. He asks what Rāma has ever done to her, says he could give up Kausalyā, Sumitrā, his royal fortune or his own life but not Rāma, and touches her feet with his head (CE 2.10.33–41). Kaikeyī throws his truthfulness back at him: "you boast, great king, of being truthful and firm in your vows — why do you want to hold back my boon?" (CE 2.11.3). He is held by the boon, "like Bali in Indra's noose" (CE 2.12.8), and cannot revoke it and keep his honour (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 12–13).
  - *Attribution of the slogan:* the famous line **"the Raghus do not break their word"** (*raghukula rīti sadā cali āī, prāna jāhuṃ baru bacanu na jāī*, "this has always been the way of Raghu's house: let life go, but not one's word") is **Tulsīdās's**, not Vālmīki's (*Rāmcaritmānas*, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa, the caupāī after dohā 27). In the Mānas, Daśaratha says it himself, smiling, *before* Kaikeyī names her boons, which makes the trap tighter. See [Rāmcaritmānas](../ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md). [late text]
- **Repudiating Kaikeyī and her son** - before dawn the king tells Kaikeyī: "the hand I took in marriage before the fire, with mantras — I renounce it, and your son too, along with you" (Ayodhyā 14.14; CE 2.12.11). This curse is lifted only after his death, when he appears from heaven (§V).
- **Unable to speak the sentence** - so broken that he cannot announce the exile himself, Daśaratha lets **Kaikeyī** deliver it to Rāma's face, and Rāma accepts at once (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 16–19). When Rāma comes to take leave, the king urges him to seize power: "I am deluded by the boon I gave Kaikeyī — **restrain me and be king of Ayodhyā yourself, today**" (*ayodhyāyās tvam evādya bhava rājā nigṛhya mām*, Ayodhyā 34.26; CE 2.31.23). Rāma refuses: the king should rule a thousand years, he himself will live in the forest, and his father need commit no falsehood for him (CE 2.31.25). The king can only beg him to stay **one more night** (Ayodhyā 34.33; CE 2.31.27), and Rāma refuses that too (CE 2.31.29).
- **Army and treasury refused** - Daśaratha orders an army, treasure and townspeople to go with Rāma into the forest. Kaikeyī objects, citing King Sagara's exile of his son Asamañja, and the plan comes to nothing (Ayodhyā 36).
- **Following the chariot** - as Rāma drives out, the old king follows the chariot on foot with Kausalyā, "though worthy of a chariot," and cries "Stop!" while Rāma tells Sumantra to drive on (Ayodhyā 40.39–46; CE 2.35.29–33). When the dust has settled he falls to the ground, and when Kaikeyī tries to lift him he tells her: "Do not touch me, woman of wicked conduct. I do not want to see you; you are neither my wife nor my kin." He renounces the marriage in this world and the next, and adds: "**if Bharata is pleased** to receive this kingdom, let nothing he offers me as a son's funeral offering reach me" (Ayodhyā 42.6–9; CE 2.37.6–9). Kausalyā raises him and he is taken back to the palace (Ayodhyā 42).
- *Variant (Vaiṣṇava theology):* the *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa* and Tulsīdās make the whole entrapment a divine contrivance, so Daśaratha is the helpless instrument of Rāma's own *līlā* rather than a merely weak husband. In the *Adhyātma* the gods send Vāṇī (Sarasvatī) to obstruct the consecration, telling her to "enter Mantharā first, and then Kaikeyī" (*Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 2.44–46). In the *Mānas* the gods beg Śāradā, who unwillingly perverts the wits of "the dull-witted maid Mantharā" (*Rāmcaritmānas*, Ayodhyā 11.4–12). See [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/recensions/adhyatma-ramayana.md). [late text]

## V. Death by grief

- **The fated death arrives** - **[Sumantra](./sumantra.md)** drives Rāma to Śṛṅgaverapura and stays there a long while with **[Guha](./guha.md)** (Ayodhyā 57.1; CE 2.51.1). The vulgate adds that they first learned that Rāma had reached Bharadvāja at Prayāga and gone on to Citrakūṭa (Ayodhyā 57.2; the CE lacks this verse). He then drives the empty chariot back, reaching a joyless Ayodhyā "**in the evening of the third day**" (*tataḥ sāyāhnasamaye tṛtīye 'hani*, Ayodhyā 57.5; CE 2.51.4), read most naturally as the third day of the drive back, since the verse follows directly on his setting out. Kausalyā reproaches the king, and he folds his hands to beg her forgiveness (Ayodhyā 62.6–7; CE 2.56.4). Then, "**on the sixth night** after Rāma went into the forest, at midnight" (*sa rājā rajanīṃ ṣaṣṭhīṃ rāme pravrajite vanam / ardharātre*, Ayodhyā 63.4; CE 2.57.3), he tells Kausalyā the secret of his youth. He longs for Rāma to touch him even once today, says that what he did to Rāma was unworthy of him, that he can no longer see her, and that Yama's messengers are hurrying him (CE 2.58.48–49; Ayodhyā 64.63–66). Crying out for Rāma, he dies once midnight has passed — *gate 'rdharātre* — the curse of the blind ascetics come exactly to term (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 64.78–79; CE 2.58.56–57).
  - *Note on the "sixth night":* the figure is **the text's own** (Ayodhyā 63.4; CE 2.57.3), not a commentarial harmonisation. Both texts give Sumantra's third-day return (Ayodhyā 57.5) and the sixth-night confession. What readers have to work out is how the two fit with the days of Rāma's own journey to Citrakūṭa. The vulgate says the death took place "in the presence of Kausalyā and Sumitrā" (Ayodhyā 64.78). The CE's closing verses (2.58.56–57) name no one, and the confession is addressed to Kausalyā alone. In both, the women sleep on and learn of his death only at dawn, when Kausalyā and Sumitrā wake to the attendants' cries (Ayodhyā 65; CE 2.59.1–10).
- **The deathbed confession** - in his last hours he tells **Kausalyā** the secret of his youth — the killing of the ascetic's son (the later tradition's **Śravaṇa-kumāra**) and the death-of-grief curse. He opens with a parable: he has cut down a mango grove to water *palāśa* trees for their showy flowers, and now grieves when the fruit comes. He understands only now why he must lose Rāma and die (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 63–64; CE 2.57–58). See [Kausalyā](./kausalya.md).
- **The body kept and the sons summoned** - with the heir in exile and Bharata at Kekaya, the **ministers** lay the king's body in a **vat of oil** (*tailadroṇyām*, Ayodhyā 66.14; CE 2.60.12), and **Vasiṣṭha** sends envoys to recall **Bharata** (Ayodhyā 68). The envoys are ordered to tell Bharata **neither that Rāma is exiled nor that his father is dead** (Ayodhyā 68.8; CE 2.62.8). No cremation can proceed until a son returns (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 66–70).
- **Bharata's repudiation and the funeral** - Bharata, returning to find his father dead and the throne his by his mother's scheme, is horrified. He denounces Kaikeyī and performs the **funerary rites**: the body is lifted from the oil, carried to the pyre and cremated (Ayodhyā 76; CE 2.70.4), and the *śrāddha* gifts follow on the twelfth day (Ayodhyā 77). He then refuses the crown and goes to the forest to bring Rāma back, ruling only as regent for Rāma's sandals (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 71–83). See [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md).
- **Rāma's offering to his father** - Rāma first learns of the death at Citrakūṭa, from Bharata, and falls senseless (Ayodhyā 103). He offers water in the Mandākinī, then lays on darbha grass a cake of **iṅgudī pulp mixed with jujube** (*aiṅgudaṃ badarīmiśraṃ piṇyākaṃ darbhasaṃstare*, Ayodhyā 103.29; CE 2.95.30): a forest-dweller's food, and Rāma asks his father to eat what his sons now eat (CE 2.95.31). When the queens reach the spot, Kausalyā sees the iṅgudī cake on the southward-pointing grass and grieves that a king who had enjoyed every pleasure and ruled the four-cornered earth should eat this (Ayodhyā 104; CE 2.96.6–11).
- **The dead king's word still binds** - even after death Daśaratha's authority holds: Rāma refuses to return precisely *because* the exile was his father's (granted) word, and the dead king's truth must be kept — so the corpse, in effect, governs the whole forest narrative (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 104–112). [scholarly]
- *Variant (Kālidāsa, Raghuvaṃśa):* the four cantos are uneven. Canto 9 gives the hunt and curse at length and in lyric detail (9.72–82), with the unnamed *muniputra*. Canto 10 gives the gods' appeal and the births, canto 11 Viśvāmitra, the weddings and Paraśurāma. Then canto 12 compresses the whole crisis into about a dozen verses. Old age whispers at the king's ear, in the form of grey hair, "give the royal fortune to Rāma" (12.2). Kaikeyī spoils the consecration with the king's tears and brings up the two boons "like two snakes from a hole" (12.4–6). The king, remembering the curse his own deed had earned, counts merely giving up his body as purification (12.10). There is no deathbed confession scene (*Kālidāsa, Raghuvaṃśa* 9–12). [scholarly]
- **Reunion in heaven's chariot** - after Sītā's fire-ordeal at Laṅkā, **Śiva** shows Rāma his father standing in a celestial car: "this is King Daśaratha, your father, gone to Indra's world, saved by you, his son" (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Yuddha 119; CE 6.107.7–8). This scene is **in the critical text**. Daśaratha takes Rāma on his lap and embraces him. He says heaven and the sages' honour mean nothing to him without Rāma, that Kaikeyī's words urging the exile have stayed lodged in his heart, and that he is now freed from grief "as the sun from mist" (CE 6.107.12–15). He says Rāma has saved him "as Aṣṭāvakra saved his brāhmaṇa father" (CE 6.107.16; see [Aṣṭāvakra](./ashtavakra.md)), and that he now understands Rāma was the Supreme Person set down to slay Rāvaṇa (CE 6.107.17). Rāma asks one favour: that the curse "I abandon you, with your son" should not touch Kaikeyī and Bharata. Daśaratha agrees (*saputrāṃ tvāṃ tyajāmīti yad uktā kaikayī tvayā / sa śāpaḥ … na spṛśet*, CE 6.107.24–26). He then praises Lakṣmaṇa's service, tells Sītā not to resent her repudiation, which Rāma undertook to prove her purity, and returns to Indra's world (CE 6.107.27–36).

## VI. Character

- **The righteous king undone by love** - Vālmīki's Daśaratha is no weakling by nature but a great and just sovereign whose single fatal trait is his **uxorious devotion to Kaikeyī**; the same love that makes him grant the boons makes him unable to break them (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā). [scholarly]
- **The hinge, not the agent** - structurally he *acts* almost not at all in the crisis: he is bound by an old debt, a dynastic vow, and a curse — three forms of the past closing on him — which is precisely what makes his ruin tragic rather than culpable (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā). Vālmīki does not let him off entirely, though. In his own confession he describes himself at the time of the killing as "without control of my senses" (CE 2.57.15), and he says that what he did to Rāma was unworthy of him (CE 2.58.48). [scholarly]
- **Exemplar of satya** - the tradition holds him up as a martyr to **truth-keeping** (*satya-vrata*): he dies rather than break his word, and it is this Raghu-ideal, embodied in his death, that Rāma then carries into the forest. The ideal is already there in Vālmīki, in Viśvāmitra's rebuke (CE 1.20.2) and Kaikeyī's taunt (CE 2.11.3); Tulsīdās's *raghukula rīti* couplet gave it its proverbial form (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*; *Rāmcaritmānas*). [scholarly]

## VII. Daśaratha outside the Vālmīki tradition

- **Buddhist: the *Dasaratha Jātaka* (No. 461)** - Daśaratha is a righteous king of **Benares** with sixteen thousand wives. His chief queen bears Rāma-paṇḍita, Lakkhaṇa and a daughter, **Sītā**, and dies. The new queen bears Bharata, is granted an open boon, and later demands the kingdom for her son. The king refuses outright. Fearing she may have the elder children murdered, he **himself sends them away** to the forest. His soothsayers tell him he has twelve years left, so he tells the children to return after twelve years. There is no curse and no hunting accident. He pines for his sons and **dies in the ninth year**. At the news, Rāma teaches the others that it is not fitting to grieve, and he returns only when the full twelve years are up (*Dasaratha Jātaka*, tr. W. H. D. Rouse in Cowell (ed.), *The Jātaka* vol. IV). See [Jain and Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md). [scholarly]
- **Jain: Guṇabhadra's *Uttarapurāṇa*** - in this condensed 9th-century Digambara version, Vārāṇasī is Daśaratha's earlier capital, before Ayodhyā; Rāma is the son of queen **Subālā** and Lakṣmaṇa of **Kaikeyī**; and there is no succession dispute and no exile. This summary follows H. C. Bhayani's *Indological Studies*, a secondary account; the Sanskrit was not checked here. Catalogued in [Jain and Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md). [late text]
- **Purāṇic: Daśaratha and Śani** - a separate Purāṇic tale has the king ride his chariot into the sky of the asterisms to stop Saturn from "splitting Rohiṇī's cart" and bringing a twelve-year drought. He receives a boon and composes the *Daśaratha-kṛta Śani-stotra*. Full treatment: [Daśaratha and Śani](../dasharatha-shani-stotra-legend.md). [late text]
