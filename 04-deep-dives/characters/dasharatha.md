# Daśaratha

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Daśaratha** — the last pre-Rāma Ikṣvāku (Sūryavaṃśa) king of Ayodhyā, father of [Rāma](../ramayana/README.md), and the hinge whose boon-debt and death-by-grief drive the exile narrative. Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). His three queens are [Kausalyā](./kausalya.md), [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md), and [Sumitrā](./sumitra.md); his place in the Solar line runs through the [Sūryavaṃśa & Candravaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md) tree and the [Raghuvaṃśa king-list](../../03-lineages-vamsha/raghuvamsha-kalidasa-kings.md).
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* he reigns in the Tretā Yuga of the present 28th Mahāyuga, 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara; the scholarly baseline is the Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa* (Baroda Critical Edition), with the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.10 supplying the terse king-list frame and Kālidāsa's *Raghuvaṃśa* (cantos 9–12) the polished mahākāvya retelling. [scholarly]

**Daśaratha** (Sanskrit *Daśaratha*, "he whose chariot [goes in] ten [directions]," i.e. a warrior whose car turns to all ten quarters) is the reigning sovereign of Kosala at the *Rāmāyaṇa*'s opening — son of **Aja**, grandson of **Raghu**, and the immediate forebear of the avatāra. A righteous, long-reigning, but childless king, he is the structural pivot of the epic: his sonless grief sets the **putrakāmeṣṭi** in motion, his half-forgotten **boon-debt** to Kaikeyī forces the exile, and his **death by grief** — predetermined by an old curse — removes the one figure who might have recalled Rāma, leaving the kingdom to Bharata's regency and the forest to its fated course.

---

## I. Lineage and place in the Solar line

- **Last Ikṣvāku king before Rāma** - Daśaratha is the reigning *cakravartin* of the senior **Sūryavaṃśa / Ikṣvāku** branch seated at Ayodhyā; with him the pre-Rāma king-list closes and the avatāra-generation opens (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 5–6; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.10). See the [Sūryavaṃśa tree](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **Son of Aja, grandson of Raghu** - his father is **Aja** (whose tragic love for **Indumatī** Kālidāsa dwells on), his grandfather the eponymous **Raghu** after whom the line is the **Raghuvaṃśa**; so Daśaratha is Raghu's grandson and Rāma's father (*Kālidāsa, Raghuvaṃśa* 8–9; cf. [Raghuvaṃśa king-list](../../03-lineages-vamsha/raghuvamsha-kalidasa-kings.md)).
- **A model righteous reign** - Vālmīki opens with a long encomium: Ayodhyā prosperous and well-walled, the king learned in the Vedas, ally of Indra, master of horse and chariot, served by able ministers and the house-priest **Vasiṣṭha** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 5–7).
- **The eight ministers and two priests** - his counsel rests on a famed body of ministers and on the *purohitas* **Vasiṣṭha** and **Vāmadeva**; it is they who advise the great sacrifices and, later, witness the boon-crisis (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 7).
- **Three principal queens** - he weds three chief queens — the senior [Kausalyā](./kausalya.md) of Kosala, the favourite [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md) of Kekaya, and the wise [Sumitrā](./sumitra.md); the *Rāmāyaṇa* names 350 women of the harem besides, but the narrative turns on these three (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla–Ayodhyā).
- *Variant (long childlessness):* despite the three queens Daśaratha remains sonless deep into a very long reign — the texts give him a near-superhuman lifespan (sixty thousand years in the formulaic idiom), framing the heirlessness as the one flaw in an otherwise perfect kingship (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 8). [late text — the numbers are formulaic]

## II. The foundational curse — Śravaṇa-kumāra (the seed of his death)

The decisive fact of Daśaratha's life is told only at its end, as a deathbed confession to Kausalyā, but it governs everything: his death is *fated*, and the exile merely the trigger.

- **The accidental killing** - as a young prince, hunting by night on a riverbank and trusting his skill at **śabda-bhedī** (sound-aimed archery), Daśaratha looses an arrow at what he takes to be an elephant drinking — and strikes the ascetic boy **Śravaṇa** (Śravaṇa-kumāra / Sindhu), who was filling a water-pot for his aged, blind parents (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 63).
- **The dying boy's request** - the mortally wounded youth, rather than cursing, bids the horrified king carry the water to his blind parents and confess the deed; Daśaratha does so (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 63–64).
- **The blind parents' curse** - the boy's father, hearing of his only son's death, pronounces that **Daśaratha too shall one day die of grief for a son** (*putra-śoka*) — then the bereaved couple end their own lives; the curse is, paradoxically, also a *boon*, promising the kingless king that he will at least one day have a son to grieve for (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 64).
- **The two clocks of the curse** - within the story the curse is a single human tragedy; in the providential reading it is the mechanism by which the gods guarantee the avatāra's exile will not be undone by a father's recall — Daśaratha must die before he can fetch Rāma back (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 64). [scholarly]

## III. The Putrakāmeṣṭi and the four sons

- **The Aśvamedha and the son-seeking rite** - to end his childlessness Daśaratha undertakes the great **Aśvamedha** (horse-sacrifice) and, on counsel, the **Putrakāmeṣṭi** (son-desiring sacrifice), summoning the brahmin-deer ascetic **Ṛṣyaśṛṅga** to officiate (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 8–15). See the dedicated [Ṛṣyaśṛṅga](../rishyashringa.md) episode.
- **Ṛṣyaśṛṅga as ṛtvij** - Ṛṣyaśṛṅga, whose own legend (the drought of Aṅga, his luring to Lomapāda's court) precedes this, is fetched and married to **Śāntā** — by one tradition Daśaratha's own daughter given to Lomapāda — and presides over the rite (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 9–14). [the Śāntā-as-Daśaratha's-daughter link is regional]
- **The divine pāyasa** - from the sacrificial fire a radiant being (Prājāpatya / Agni in the devotional gloss) rises bearing a golden vessel of **pāyasa** (sweet milk-rice), charged with the gods' answer — the descending portions of Viṣṇu, who at the devas' petition has agreed to be born to slay Rāvaṇa (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 15–16).
- **The division among the queens** - Daśaratha apportions the pāyasa: a full half to **Kausalyā** (→ **Rāma**); of the remainder, shares to **Sumitrā** and **Kaikeyī**, with Sumitrā served twice so that she bears the **twins Lakṣmaṇa & Śatrughna**, Kaikeyī bearing **Bharata** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 16). The exact sequence of the second and third shares varies by recension — catalogued in [Kausalyā](./kausalya.md). [disputed — the order, not the arithmetic]
- **Birth of the four sons** - in due course are born **Rāma** (to Kausalyā), **Bharata** (to Kaikeyī), and the twins **Lakṣmaṇa** and **Śatrughna** (to Sumitrā) — the curse's promised sons, and the four into whom Viṣṇu's *aṃśas* descend (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 18; *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.10).
- *Variant (Bhāgavata compression):* the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* records the sons and the avatāra-purpose tersely within the Ikṣvāku king-list, omitting the sacrificial machinery that Vālmīki develops at length (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.10). [late text]

## IV. The boon-debt to Kaikeyī and the Devāsura war

- **Boons earned in battle** - long before, campaigning as **Indra's ally** in a great deva–asura war (the Śambara / Timidhvaja conflict), Daśaratha is gravely wounded and unconscious, and is saved by **Kaikeyī**, who drives his chariot to safety (and, in variants, holds the broken axle with her own hand); in gratitude he grants her **two boons, to be claimed whenever she wishes** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9, retrospectively narrated).
- **The boons left dormant** - Kaikeyī names no wish at the time; the two boons lie unredeemed for years — the device that fires only when Mantharā revives them (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9–11). The full variant-catalogue of *how* she saved him is in [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md).
- **The decision to crown Rāma** - aged and feeling his years (and, Vālmīki hints, sensing ill omens), Daśaratha resolves to consecrate **Rāma** as *yuvarāja*, summons the assembly and gains its joyful assent, and orders the consecration for the very next dawn — pointedly while Bharata and Śatrughna are away at Kekaya (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 1–6).
- **Mantharā redeems the boons** - the hunchbacked maidservant **Mantharā** poisons Kaikeyī's mind, and coaches the exact demands the two dormant boons can buy: **(1) Bharata consecrated** as heir, and **(2) Rāma exiled to the Daṇḍaka forest for fourteen years** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 7–11). See [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md) and [Mantharā](./manthara.md).
- **Binding the king by oath** - finding Kaikeyī prostrate in the *krodhāgāra* (anger-chamber), the doting Daśaratha swears **by Rāma himself** to grant whatever she asks — and only then does she name her price (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 10–11).
- **Daśaratha's collapse and the Raghu-vow** - the king faints, weeps, rages, and pleads through the night, offering wealth, kingdom, anything but this; but he is destroyed by the dynastic ideal that **"the Raghus do not break their word"** — he cannot revoke a granted boon and keep his honour (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 12–13).
- **Unable to speak the sentence** - so broken that he cannot announce the exile himself, Daśaratha lets **Kaikeyī** deliver it to Rāma's face; Rāma accepts at once, and the king can only cling to him and beg him to stay one more night (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 16–19, 34).
- *Variant (Vaiṣṇava theology):* the *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa* and Tulsīdās make the whole entrapment a divine contrivance (Sarasvatī deranging Mantharā's wit at the gods' bidding), so Daśaratha is the helpless instrument of Rāma's own *līlā* rather than a merely weak husband (*Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*; *Rāmcaritmānas*). [late text]

## V. Death by grief

- **The fated death arrives** - on the **sixth night** after Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa depart, Daśaratha — sleepless, broken, crying "Rāma! Rāma!" — dies of grief, the curse of the blind ascetics come exactly to term (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 64).
- **The deathbed confession** - in his last hours he tells **Kausalyā** the secret of his youth — the killing of **Śravaṇa-kumāra** and the death-of-grief curse — understanding only now why he must lose Rāma and die (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 63–64). See [Kausalyā](./kausalya.md).
- **The body kept and the sons summoned** - with the heir in exile and Bharata at Kekaya, the ministers and Vasiṣṭha preserve the king's body in oil and send envoys to recall **Bharata**; no cremation can proceed until a son returns (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 66–70).
- **Bharata's repudiation and the funeral** - Bharata, returning to find his father dead and the throne his by his mother's scheme, is horrified; he performs the **funerary rites**, denounces Kaikeyī, and refuses the crown, ruling as regent for Rāma's sandals (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 71–83). See [Kaikeyī](./kaikeyi.md).
- **The dead king's word still binds** - even after death Daśaratha's authority holds: Rāma refuses to return precisely *because* the exile was his father's (granted) word, and the dead king's truth must be kept — so the corpse, in effect, governs the whole forest narrative (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 104–112). [scholarly]
- *Variant (Kālidāsa, Raghuvaṃśa):* cantos 9–12 retell Daśaratha's hunt-and-curse, the sons' births, the boon-crisis, and the king's death in polished mahākāvya, with the hunting-accident and Śravaṇa episode given memorable lyric treatment (*Kālidāsa, Raghuvaṃśa* 9–12). [scholarly]
- *Variant (reunion in spirit):* in some recensions Daśaratha, dwelling in heaven, briefly appears to **Rāma** after Rāvaṇa's fall (Yuddha Kāṇḍa) to bless and embrace his victorious son — a consolation absent from the strictest critical text (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Yuddha Kāṇḍa, later sargas). [disputed]

## VI. Character

- **The righteous king undone by love** - Vālmīki's Daśaratha is no weakling by nature but a great and just sovereign whose single fatal trait is his **uxorious devotion to Kaikeyī**; the same love that makes him grant the boons makes him unable to break them (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā). [scholarly]
- **The hinge, not the agent** - structurally he *acts* almost not at all in the crisis: he is bound by an old debt, a dynastic vow, and a curse — three forms of the past closing on him — which is precisely what makes his ruin tragic rather than culpable (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā). [scholarly]
- **Exemplar of satya** - the tradition holds him up as a martyr to **truth-keeping** (*satya-vrata*): he dies rather than break his word, and it is this Raghu-ideal, embodied in his death, that Rāma then carries into the forest (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*; *Rāmcaritmānas*). [scholarly]
