# Vedavatī — the Ascetic Rāvaṇa Seized, and the Curse that Became Sītā

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Vedavatī** — the brahmin ascetic doing *tapas* to win Viṣṇu as husband, whom [Rāvaṇa](./ravana.md) seizes by the hair, and who cuts off the defiled hair, enters the fire, and vows to be reborn for his destruction. Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). The whole episode is a single sarga — **Uttara Kāṇḍa 17**, 31 verses in the Baroda Critical Edition, narrated by **Agastya** to Rāma after the war — and it is the textual root of the identification of [Sītā](./sita.md) as her rebirth. See the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md) and the [curse-ledger](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/tapas-boons-and-curses.md).
> Two clocks: the sarga dates her itself — *eṣā vedavatī nāma pūrvam āsīt kṛte yuge / tretāyugam anuprāpya …* — she was Vedavatī "formerly, in the **Kṛta (Satya) Yuga**", and on the coming of the **Tretā** she was born as Sītā (*Uttara* 17.31). On this reckoning Rāvaṇa's world-conquest, of which her story is one episode, reaches back across the yuga-boundary into the Kṛta age; the text does not explain this further. On the scholarly clock the Uttara Kāṇḍa is the epic's **latest major stratum**, later than the six books whose story it explains, and this backstory belongs to that stratum. [scholarly]

## Stratum — read this first

- **A single sarga of the Uttara Kāṇḍa** — in the Critical Edition Vedavatī appears nowhere in Books 1–6. Her entire story is **Uttara Kāṇḍa 17** (CE 7.17.1–31), one link in the chain of Rāvaṇa-episodes Agastya recites to Rāma once the war is over.
  - *Variant (vulgate Yuddha Kāṇḍa):* the vulgate brings her into the war book once. Back in Laṅkā after his first defeat by Rāma, Rāvaṇa lists the warnings now coming true: Anaraṇya's, then *śapto 'haṃ vedavatyā ca yadā sā dharṣitā purā / seyaṃ sītā mahābhāgā jātā janakanandinī*, "I was cursed by Vedavatī when I violated her long ago; she has been born as this Sītā, Janaka's daughter", then those of Umā, Nandīśvara, Rambhā and Varuṇa's daughter (*Yuddha* 60.8–11, vulgate, valmikiramayan.net). The Critical Edition does not have these verses. Its text runs straight from Brahmā's warning about men (CE 6.48.6–7) to the call to make ready and wake Kumbhakarṇa (CE 6.48.8–9 = vulgate 60.12–13). So this core-book mention is a vulgate addition, and it assumes the reader already knows the Uttara story. [interpolation]
- **Genuine Uttara text, and the Uttara is late** — the episode is **constituted text** of the seventh book, not an appendix or an interpolation printed outside it; but the seventh book is itself the epic's latest major layer, generally held later than Books 2–6, and the Sītā-as-Vedavatī doctrine is therefore of that layer and not of the core poem. Both halves of that sentence matter and neither cancels the other. [scholarly]
- **Which text to read it in** — valmikiramayan.net does not host the Uttara Kāṇḍa. The Critical-Edition Sanskrit is on GRETIL (the Tokunaga–Smith e-text), mirrored on sanskritdocuments.org. The vulgate English is the **Hari Prasad Shastri** translation (Wisdomlib), which also numbers this sarga 17. The Princeton translation of the Critical Edition (*Uttarakāṇḍa*, Goldman & Sutherland Goldman, 2017) is the scholarly control. Verse numbers on this page are CE unless marked; where Shastri's text has something the CE lacks, the bullet says so. [scholarly]

## Name, birth, and house

- **Vedavatī (वेदवती)** — "she of the Veda." She explains the name to Rāvaṇa herself: her father was constantly engaged in Vedic recitation, and she was born from it as a *vāṅmayī kanyā*, a girl made of speech, "known by the name Vedavatī" (*Uttara* 17.8).
- **Daughter of Kuśadhvaja, son of Bṛhaspati** — *kuśadhvajo nāma pitā brahmarṣir mama dhārmikaḥ / bṛhaspatisutaḥ śrīmān buddhyā tulyo bṛhaspateḥ*: her father is the righteous brahmarṣi Kuśadhvaja, son of **Bṛhaspati**, the gods' preceptor, and his equal in intellect (*Uttara* 17.7).
  - *Name-collision, flagged:* this Kuśadhvaja is **not** the Kuśadhvaja who is Janaka's younger brother and the father of Māṇḍavī and Śrutakīrti (Bāla 71–73). Two unrelated men of the same name stand at either end of Sītā's two biographies, and the coincidence is easy to mistake for a link. [scholarly] See [Sītā](./sita.md) and [Bharata, Śatrughna & their wives](./bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md). A **third** Kuśadhvaja, a king, is the father of the Purāṇic Vedavatī (see the Devī-Bhāgavata section below). He is neither of the other two.
- **The suitors** — gods, gandharvas, yakṣas, rākṣasas and nāgas came to her father to ask for her, and he gave her to none of them (*Uttara* 17.9–10).
- **Her mother is unnamed** — Vālmīki gives her no name, only her end: when Kuśadhvaja is killed she enters the fire embracing her husband's body. Her daughter later dies the same way. (*Uttara* 17.13)
  - *Variant (Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.16):* her mother is **Mālāvatī**, wife of King Kuśadhvaja. [late text]
- **The vow her father made for her** — Kuśadhvaja wanted **Viṣṇu, lord of the three worlds**, as his son-in-law and would give her to no one else (*Uttara* 17.11–12). It was a vow made about her before she could make it, and after his death she took it up as her own (17.14–15).

## The killing of her father

- **Śambhu the daitya king** — when he heard that she was reserved for Viṣṇu, **Śambhu**, king of the daityas and "proud of his strength", grew angry and killed **Kuśadhvaja at night as he slept** (*Uttara* 17.12). The CE Sanskrit reads *śambhur nāma* (the sanskritdocuments.org page is a mirror of the same CE e-text, not an independent witness); "Shumbhu" is the spelling in Shastri's vulgate-based English. The vulgate Sanskrit was not checked here. The text does not say that Śambhu was one of the suitors.
- **The mother's fire** — her mother, clasping her husband's body, enters the flames with it (*Uttara* 17.13).
- **The orphan's tapas** — she resolves to make "my father's wish about Nārāyaṇa" come true, and under that vow she practises great austerity, devoted to dharma and wanting Nārāyaṇa as husband (*Uttara* 17.14–16). This is where Rāvaṇa finds her: in the **Himalayan forest** (*himavadvanam*), wearing **black antelope skin** and **matted locks**, keeping the discipline of the ṛṣis (17.1–2).

## Rāvaṇa's assault and her fire

- **The encounter** — Rāvaṇa, ranging the earth, comes on her at her austerities and is overcome with desire (*Uttara* 17.1–3). He asks why she lives a life so at odds with her youth, whose daughter she is, and who her husband is (17.4–5). She first receives him as a guest with the proper rites (17.6), then tells him her story. She ends by telling him that her tapas lets her know everything in the three worlds, that she knows who he is, and that he should go (17.16–17).
- **His answer** — he climbs down from his aerial chariot, "tormented by Kandarpa's arrows" (*Uttara* 17.18). Such piety suits the old, he says, not the beauty of the three worlds in her youth. And who is this Viṣṇu? "In valour, tapas, enjoyment and strength he is not my equal" (17.19–21). The comparison he makes is between Viṣṇu and himself.
- **The hair** — she cries "No, no!" (*mā maivam*), and he lays hold of her hair with his hand (*mūrdhajeṣu … karāgreṇa parāmṛśat*, literally "touched" or "seized" with the tip of his hand, *Uttara* 17.22). In anger she **cuts off the hair with her hand** (*keśān hastena sācchinat*, 17.23).
  - *Variant (vulgate, per Shastri):* her hand is "transformed into a sword" to do it. The CE Sanskrit says only *hastena*, "with her hand".
- **Her words, and why they are not a curse** — kindling a fire (*Uttara* 17.23), she says that now that he has violated her she will not live, and will enter the fire while he watches (17.24). Because she, sinless, has been violated by him as though she had no protector, she will be born again for his death (17.25). A woman cannot kill him, she says, and if she loosed a curse on him her tapas would be used up (*śāpe tvayi mayotsṛṣṭe tapasaś ca vyayo bhavet*, 17.26). Then she prays: if she has done, given or offered anything of merit, may it make her an **ayonijā** (one not born from a womb), the virtuous daughter of a righteous man (17.27). Vālmīki's Vedavatī therefore **expressly withholds a śāpa**: she stakes her merit on a rebirth instead.
- **The fire** — she enters the blazing fire, and **a divine rain of flowers falls from the sky** (*Uttara* 17.28).
- **The rebirth** — Agastya turns to Rāma: the enemy she had already struck down by her anger has now been killed by him, relying on his superhuman valour (*Uttara* 17.29). She is born again among mortals "like a flame on the altar", in a field caught by the tip of the plough (*kṣetre halamukhagraste vedyām agniśikhopamā*, 17.30). She was Vedavatī in the Kṛta age; on the coming of the Tretā, for the death of that rākṣasa, men call her **Sītā**, because she arose from the furrow (*sītā*) (17.31). In the CE Sanskrit these verses name **neither Janaka nor Mithilā**. Shastri's vulgate-based translation does: she "was re-born in the family of the magnanimous Janaka in the race of Mithila". The furrow-birth itself is told by Janaka in the Bāla Kāṇḍa (CE 1.65.14–15; vulgate Bāla 66).
  - *The echo of ayonijā:* in the core books Janaka calls the furrow-born girl **ayonijā** (CE 1.65.15, 1.65.27), and Sītā uses the same word of herself to Anasūyā (CE 2.110.36). Neither passage mentions Vedavatī. In the Uttara the same word is what Vedavatī prays to become (17.27).

## Her place in Rāvaṇa's curse-ledger

Three separate things are routinely fused in retelling and in summary. They are three, they do different work, and the corpus keeps them apart.

- **Nalakūbara's curse over Rambhā (Uttara 26)** — if Rāvaṇa ever again forces an unwilling woman, his head will split into **seven** pieces (*mūrdhā tu saptadhā tasya śakalībhavitā*, CE 7.26.44).
- **Brahmā's curse over Puñjikasthalā (vulgate Yuddha 13.11–14)** — the same condition, but **a hundred** pieces. **Rāvaṇa states it himself**, in his own war-council, as the reason he cannot compel Sītā. The passage is vulgate only: no Puñjikasthalā episode appears in the Critical Edition's Yuddha text (in the CE she is named only in Kiṣkindhā 65.8, as Añjanā). In the vulgate Rāvaṇa also names Vedavatī himself (*Yuddha* 60.10, above). See [Puñjikasthalā & Brahmā's curse](../punjikasthala-brahma-curse.md).
- **Vedavatī's curse (Uttara 17)** — a different kind of thing altogether: **not a restraint on his hands but a sentence of death**, that she would be reborn to destroy him. Strictly, the Uttara does not make it a curse: she withholds a śāpa (17.26) and vows a rebirth instead (17.25, 27). It is Rāvaṇa, in the vulgate Yuddha, who remembers it as one (*śapto 'haṃ vedavatyā*, 60.10), and the retellings follow him. Sītā's bodily inviolability rests on the first two; her identity as his doom rests on this one. See [Rāvaṇa §V](./ravana.md) and the [tapas, boons & curses ledger](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/tapas-boons-and-curses.md).
- **Where it sits in the sequence** — in Agastya's recital the Vedavatī episode comes among Rāvaṇa's conquests: after Nandī's curse at Kailāsa (Uttara 16) and before [Anaraṇya](../../03-lineages-vamsha/anaranya-ravana-curse.md)'s dying curse (Uttara 19) and the abducted women's curse that he will die "because of a woman" (*strīkṛtenaiva vadhaṃ prāpsyati*, CE 7.24.15). The doom-roster is cumulative, and each curse closes a different route his boon had left open. (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 16–26)

## The Purāṇic Vedavatī — Devī-Bhāgavata 9.16

A second, independent life of Vedavatī, told by Nārāyaṇa to Nārada within the Lakṣmī–Tulasī cycle of the *Devī-Bhāgavata Purāṇa*'s ninth book. It shares with Vālmīki her name, a father called Kuśadhvaja, the Rāvaṇa encounter and the rebirth as Sītā, and little else. It is read here in Swami Vijñānānanda's 1921 translation (Wisdomlib), cited by his verse-groups. [late text]

- **House** — this Kuśadhvaja is a **king**, son of Rathadhvaja and grandson of Vṛṣadhvaja, the exclusively Śaiva king whom Sūrya cursed with the loss of his fortune. Kuśadhvaja's brother **Dharmadhvaja** is the father of **Tulasī**, so Vedavatī and Tulasī are cousins (DBP 9.15; 9.16.1–30). Her mother is **Mālāvatī**, and she is born "of the parts of Kamalā", a partial incarnation of Lakṣmī (9.16.1–30).
- **Name** — at birth she chants Vedic mantras from the lying-in chamber, and so is named Vedavatī. The chapter closes by saying the four Vedas dwelt in embodied form on her lips (9.16.1–30, 55–63).
- **Tapas** — she goes to **Puṣkara** and does tapas for a whole manvantara. A voice from the sky promises her Hari as husband in her next birth, and she continues her austerities in the caves of **Gandhamādana** (9.16.1–30).
- **Rāvaṇa** — he arrives as a guest, and she gives him water and fruit. When he tries to force her, she **paralyses him by the power of her tapas**. He praises her silently; pleased, she grants him merit in the next world, and then **curses him**: because he touched her body in lust, he will be destroyed with his whole family for her sake. She then **leaves her body by yogic power**, and Rāvaṇa puts the body into the Gaṅgā (9.16.1–30). This telling has no hair-cutting, no fire, no Bṛhaspati and no Śambhu, and here she does curse.
- **Rebirth** — she is born as Sītā, Janaka's daughter, wins Rāma as husband, and is a *jātismarā*, one who remembers her former life (9.16.1–30). **In this narrative Vedavatī is the real Sītā.** The shadow-Sītā is a separate being made by Agni, although the chapter's closing summary (49–53) then gives the Vedavatī–Sītā–Draupadī chain to the shadow's later form (see Variants).
- *Variant (Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, Prakṛti-khaṇḍa 14 — known here only from V. D. N. Rao's Kamakoti summary, not the Sanskrit):* the summary has the same Kuśadhvaja–Mālāvatī birth, the Vedic speech at birth, the sky-voice and the Gandhamādana tapas. In it Vedavatī curses Rāvaṇa and says she will give up her life in a fire-pit (*agni-kuṇḍa*) and cause Rāvaṇa's death in her next birth: a fire-death, unlike the Devī-Bhāgavata's yogic one. [late text]

## Variants and later developments

- *Variant (the Māyā-Sītā / chāyā-Sītā doctrine):* in these texts the real Sītā is kept safe and a **shadow-Sītā** is carried off in her place. The shadow does not always have a past life, and the texts disagree about who she is. They are kept apart here:
  - *Kūrma Purāṇa 2.33.112–140 (GRETIL numbering):* told as an example of a faithful wife's power. Sītā herself sees through Rāvaṇa's disguise, takes refuge in the household fire and praises Agni. Agni creates a *māyāmayī sītā*, an illusory Sītā, takes the real one away and keeps her with Bhavānī (Pārvatī). At the end the illusory Sītā enters the fire and is burnt, and Agni gives the real one back to Rāma. **No Vedavatī.** [late text]
  - *[Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/recensions/adhyatma-ramayana.md), Araṇya 7.1–3:* Rāma tells Sītā to leave a shadow of herself behind at the cottage and live unseen inside the fire for a year. The shadow has no name, and Vedavatī does not appear in the work (checked in an OCR text of Baij Nath's English translation, which could miss a passage). [late text]
  - *Devī-Bhāgavata 9.16.30–53:* Agni comes to Rāma disguised as a brahmin, asks for the real Sītā and leaves a **Chāyā-Sītā** he has made by yogic power. After the fire-ordeal the Chāyā asks what she should do. She is sent to Puṣkara, becomes **Svarga-Lakṣmī**, and rises from Drupada's sacrificial pit as Draupadī. **In the chapter's narrative Vedavatī is the real Sītā, not the shadow**, though its closing summary (49–53) then calls this shadow-turned-Draupadī Vedavatī as well (see the three-birth chain below). [late text]
  - *Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiṣṇava-khaṇḍa, Veṅkaṭācala-māhātmya 5.18–26 (G. V. Tagare's translation, Wisdomlib):* **here the shadow is Vedavatī.** Agni foresees the abduction and takes the real Sītā to Pātāla, where his wife **Svāhā** keeps her. Vedavatī, who had once been touched by Rāvaṇa and had given up her body in fire, was "created once again with a form similar to that of Sītā". She is the one carried off and held in Laṅkā, and she enters the fire again after his death. [regional] [late text]
  - *Brahmavaivarta, Prakṛti-khaṇḍa 14 (summary only):* the Kamakoti summary has Vedavatī, "as Chhaya Sita", do tapas at Puṣkara and be born as Draupadī. Whether the Sanskrit equates the shadow with Vedavatī as plainly as the summary does was not checked. [late text] See [Adhyātma & variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md) and [Araṇya Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md).
- *Variant (Sītā as Rāvaṇa's own daughter):* a separate and independent strand — Jain (Guṇabhadra's *Uttarapurāṇa*, Saṅghadāsa's *Vasudevahiṇḍī*), the Śākta *Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*, and the Southeast-Asian retellings — makes Sītā the exposed infant of Rāvaṇa and Mandodarī. It is not a version of the Vedavatī story and should not be blended with it. [regional] [late text] See [Mandodarī](./mandodari.md) and [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md).
- *Variant (Vedavatī reborn as Padmāvatī — Skanda Purāṇa, Veṅkaṭācala-māhātmya):* Śrīnivāsa tells his attendant Bakulamālikā why he must marry King Ākāśa's daughter. After the ordeal Agni presented Vedavatī to Rāma as the one who had endured captivity for Sītā's sake, and Sītā asked Rāma to take her as wife. Rāma promised to do so "in the twenty-eighth Kali-yuga". Until then she would stay in Brahmā's world, and afterwards she would be born of the Earth and become the adopted daughter of King Viyat (Ākāśa) (VM 5.18–32). The same māhātmya tells Padmāvatī's birth: Ākāśa finds a girl lying on a lotus and names her Padminī (VM 4.1–6), and he calls her "not born of a womb" (VM 7.40). In the Skanda recension, then, the Vedavatī link and the lotus-born, Lakṣmī-like Padmāvatī belong to **one story**. On this telling the husband Vedavatī sought by her tapas is granted to her as a reward for her captivity as the shadow-Sītā, and only two ages later, in a Kali-yuga. The recensions attached to the *Varāha* and *Bhaviṣyottara* Purāṇas were not checked here. [regional] [late text] See [Tirumala–Tirupati](../../06-sacred-geography/tirumala-tirupati-venkateshwara.md).
- *Variant (the three-birth chain — Devī-Bhāgavata 9.16.49–53):* the Purāṇa states it outright of the Svarga-Lakṣmī who rose as Draupadī: she was Vedavatī, Kuśadhvaja's daughter, in the Satya Yuga; Sītā, Janaka's daughter, in the Tretā; and **[Draupadī](./draupadi.md)**, Drupada's daughter, in the Dvāpara, and is therefore called **Trihāyaṇī**, "she of the three ages". The next passage explains Draupadī's five husbands: at Puṣkara she asked Śiva five times for a husband (9.16.55–63). The chain sits awkwardly with the chapter's own story, in which Vedavatī becomes the real Sītā and it is the shadow who becomes Draupadī; the text keeps both without comment. The Kamakoti summary of the Brahmavaivarta (Prakṛti 14) uses the same *Trihāyaṇī* label. Vālmīki has only the first link, Vedavatī in the Kṛta age (*Uttara* 17.31). [late text]
- *Commentarial reading (Araṇya 47.4):* the valmikiramayan.net translator's note on Sītā's self-introduction to the disguised Rāvaṇa reads it as a hint that she has come back, after immolating herself as Vedavatī, to end him. That is a devotional gloss; the verse does not mention Vedavatī. [disputed]
- **Not in the Mahābhārata's Rāma story** — the name Vedavatī does not occur in the BORI Critical Edition of the Āraṇyakaparvan, so the *Rāmopākhyāna* (MBh 3.258–275) carries no Vedavatī backstory.
- **Unrelated namesakes** — *Vedavatī* is also a **river** rising in the Pāripātra range in the Purāṇic river-lists (*Kūrma* 1.45.29; *Vāyu* 45.97 and *Brahmāṇḍa* 2.16.27 per the *Purāṇa Index*; see [Bhāratavarṣa & Purāṇic geography](../../06-sacred-geography/bharatavarsha-and-puranic-geography.md)), and the name of an **apsaras** (*Vāyu* 69.60 per the *Purāṇa Index*).

## Why the episode matters

- **It answers the question the core poem leaves open** — why *this* woman, and why does the abduction destroy him rather than merely provoke a war. Vālmīki's core books make Sītā's recovery the cause of Rāvaṇa's death; the Uttara makes Rāvaṇa's death the cause of Sītā's existence. The reversal is the seventh book's characteristic move. [scholarly]
- **Consent stated as the hinge** — as with Rambhā and Puñjikasthalā, the crime is the seizure of a woman who has refused, and the punishment is written into the crime. Vedavatī's version is the starkest. She refuses even the curse that was hers to give, so as not to spend her tapas (17.26), and stakes her own merit on being reborn as his death (17.27). No god intervenes. [scholarly]
- **Self-immolation as agency, not despair** — her fire is a chosen instrument. The Uttara pairs it with the vow and the staking of merit (17.24–28), and Agastya calls Rāvaṇa an enemy "already struck down by her anger" (17.29). The Devī-Bhāgavata takes the fire away (she leaves her body by yoga) and gives her an outright curse. On the wider ritual and historical frame see [satī, jauhar & the immolation rites](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/sati-jauhar-and-the-immolation-rites.md). [scholarly]
