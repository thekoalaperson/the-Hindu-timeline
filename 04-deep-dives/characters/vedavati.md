# Vedavatī — the Ascetic Rāvaṇa Seized, and the Curse that Became Sītā

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Vedavatī** — the brahmin ascetic doing *tapas* to win Viṣṇu as husband, whom [Rāvaṇa](./ravana.md) seizes by the hair, and who cuts off the defiled hair, enters the fire, and vows to be reborn for his destruction. Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). The whole episode is a single sarga — **Uttara Kāṇḍa 17**, narrated by **Agastya** to Rāma after the war — and it is the textual root of the identification of [Sītā](./sita.md) as her rebirth. See the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md) and the [curse-ledger](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/tapas-boons-and-curses.md).
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* Vedavatī's life precedes Rāma's by the length of Rāvaṇa's conquests, in the same **Tretā Yuga** of the present 28th Mahāyuga; on the scholarly clock the Uttara Kāṇḍa is the epic's **latest major stratum**, later than the six books whose story it explains — which is exactly the stratum this backstory belongs to. [scholarly]

## Stratum — read this first

- **A single sarga of the Uttara Kāṇḍa** — Vedavatī appears nowhere in the six core books. Her entire story is **Uttara Kāṇḍa 17**, one link in the chain of Rāvaṇa-episodes Agastya recites to Rāma once the war is over (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17).
- **Genuine Uttara text, and the Uttara is late** — the episode is **constituted text** of the seventh book, not an appendix or an interpolation printed outside it; but the seventh book is itself the epic's latest major layer, generally held later than Books 2–6, and the Sītā-as-Vedavatī doctrine is therefore of that layer and not of the core poem. Both halves of that sentence matter and neither cancels the other. [scholarly]
- **Which text to read it in** — valmikiramayan.net does not host the Uttara Kāṇḍa; the working English text for it in this corpus is the **Hari Prasad Shastri** translation (Wisdomlib), with the Princeton Critical-Edition *Uttarakāṇḍa* (Goldman & Sutherland Goldman, 2017) as the scholarly control. Sarga numbers here are vulgate. [scholarly]

## Name, birth, and house

- **Vedavatī (वेदवती)** — "she of the Veda," and the epic supplies the reason: she is **born of her father's speech**, brought forth by a brahmarṣi whose one occupation was the recitation of the Veda. She names herself so to Rāvaṇa: *"I, his daughter, was born of the speech of that magnanimous one whose constant pursuit is the study of the Veda; my name is Vedavatī"* (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17).
- **Daughter of Kuśadhvaja, son of Bṛhaspati** — her father is the brahmarṣi **Kuśadhvaja**, son of **Bṛhaspati**, preceptor of the gods (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17).
  - *Name-collision, flagged:* this Kuśadhvaja is **not** the Kuśadhvaja who is Janaka's younger brother and the father of Māṇḍavī and Śrutakīrti (Bāla 71–73). Two unrelated men of the same name stand at either end of Sītā's two biographies, and the coincidence is easy to mistake for a link. [scholarly] See [Sītā](./sita.md) and [Bharata, Śatrughna & their wives](./bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md).
- **Her mother is unnamed** — the sarga gives her no name, only her end: when Kuśadhvaja is killed she enters the fire with her husband's body. Her daughter's later choice repeats hers exactly. (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17)
- **The vow her father made for her** — Kuśadhvaja had determined that his daughter would be given to **Viṣṇu, Lord of the Three Worlds**, and to no other. It is a vow made about her before she is old enough to make it, and she takes it up as her own. (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17)

## The killing of her father

- **Śumbhu the daitya king** — a Daitya lord (**Śumbhu / Śambhu** in the printings) desires Vedavatī and asks for her; learning that she is promised to Viṣṇu and cannot be had, he kills **Kuśadhvaja in his sleep** in rage (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17).
- **The mother's fire** — her mother, clasping her husband's body, enters the flames with it (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17).
- **The orphan's tapas** — Vedavatī takes up her father's unfulfilled purpose and goes into the **Himalayan forest** to win Nārāyaṇa by austerity, wearing the **black antelope skin** and **matted locks** of the ascetic: *"Now I desire to fulfil his will regarding Nārāyaṇa; it is He to whom I have given my heart"* (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17).

## Rāvaṇa's assault and her fire

- **The encounter** — Rāvaṇa, ranging the earth, comes on her at her austerities and is overcome with desire. He questions her; she tells him her name, her father's, and her vow. He mocks it — that beauty like hers is wasted on tapas, that Viṣṇu is no match for her, that she should have him instead — and, refused, **seizes her by the hair** (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17).
- **The hair, and the refusal** — she **cuts off the hair his hand has touched**, and answers him: *"Soiled by your contact, O vile rākṣasa, I do not desire to live, and shall throw myself into the fire before your eyes"* (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17).
- **The curse** — *"Since you have affronted me in the forest, O wretch, I shall be reborn for your destruction."* She kindles the fire and enters it, and **a rain of flowers falls** (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17).
- **The rebirth** — Agastya closes the sarga by naming the fulfilment: she **was born again in the house of the magnanimous Janaka, in the line of Mithilā, for the destruction of that rākṣasa** — that is, as [Sītā](./sita.md), whom Janaka raises from the furrow at the plough's tip (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 17; the furrow-birth itself at *Bāla* 66).

## Her place in Rāvaṇa's curse-ledger

Three separate things are routinely fused in retelling and in summary. They are three, they do different work, and the corpus keeps them apart.

- **Nalakūbara's curse over Rambhā (Uttara 26)** — if Rāvaṇa ever again forces an unwilling woman, his head will split into **seven** pieces (*saptadhā*).
- **Brahmā's curse over Puñjikasthalā (Yuddha 13.11–15)** — the same condition, but **a hundred** pieces; and *this* is the one **Rāvaṇa names himself**, in his own voice, in his own war-council, as the reason he cannot compel Sītā.
- **Vedavatī's curse (Uttara 17)** — a different kind of thing altogether: **not a restraint on his hands but a sentence of death**, that she would be reborn to destroy him. Sītā's bodily inviolability rests on the first two; her identity as his doom rests on this one. See [Rāvaṇa §V](./ravana.md) and the [tapas, boons & curses ledger](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/tapas-boons-and-curses.md).
- **Where it sits in the sequence** — Agastya's recital places the Vedavatī episode among Rāvaṇa's conquests, alongside Nandī's curse at Kailāsa (Uttara 16) and Anaraṇya's dying curse (Uttara 19); the doom-roster is cumulative, and each curse closes a different route his boon had left open. (*Uttara Kāṇḍa* 16–26)

## Variants and later developments

- *Variant (the Māyā-Sītā / chāyā-Sītā doctrine):* several Purāṇas (**Kūrma**, **Brahmavaivarta**, **Devī-Bhāgavata**) and the [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/recensions/adhyatma-ramayana.md) hold that the real Sītā was hidden in fire before the abduction and a **shadow-Sītā** taken in her place — and in the developed form of the doctrine that shadow **is Vedavatī**, come at last to take her revenge in person, with the true Sītā restored out of the fire at the *agni-parīkṣā*. The harmonization removes the scandal of the captivity and makes Vedavatī the agent rather than merely the prophecy. [late text] See [Adhyātma & variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).
- *Variant (Sītā as Rāvaṇa's own daughter):* a separate and independent strand — Jain (Guṇabhadra's *Uttarapurāṇa*, Saṅghadāsa's *Vasudevahiṇḍī*), the Śākta *Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa*, and the Southeast-Asian retellings — makes Sītā the exposed infant of Rāvaṇa and Mandodarī. It is not a version of the Vedavatī story and should not be blended with it. [regional] [late text] See [Mandodarī](./mandodari.md) and [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md).
- *Variant (Vedavatī reborn as Padmāvatī):* the **Tirumala** temple tradition carries a strand in which the ascetic who was promised to Viṣṇu and denied him in that birth is reborn as **Padmāvatī (Alamēlu-maṅgā)** of Tiruchanūr, and marries Śrīnivāsa-Veṅkaṭeśvara at last — the vow of *Uttara* 17 fulfilled in a second life rather than avenged in one. The *Veṅkaṭācala Māhātmya*'s own account makes Padmāvatī a form of Lakṣmī born of the lotus, daughter of Ākāśarāja, and does not require the Vedavatī link. [regional] [late text] See [Tirumala–Tirupati](../../06-sacred-geography/tirumala-tirupati-venkateshwara.md).
- *Variant (the three-birth chain):* a folk and devotional strand extends the line — Vedavatī in the Satya Yuga, Sītā in the Tretā, **[Draupadī](./draupadi.md)** in the Dvāpara — threading the fire-born and the fire-entering heroines into one lineage of insulted women. [folk] [disputed]

## Why the episode matters

- **It answers the question the core poem leaves open** — why *this* woman, and why does the abduction destroy him rather than merely provoke a war. Vālmīki's core books make Sītā's recovery the cause of Rāvaṇa's death; the Uttara makes Rāvaṇa's death the cause of Sītā's existence. The reversal is the seventh book's characteristic move. [scholarly]
- **Consent stated as the hinge** — as with Rambhā and Puñjikasthalā, the crime is the seizure of a woman who has refused, and the punishment is written into the crime. Vedavatī's version is the starkest: she supplies the punishment herself, out of her own body, with no god's intervention at all. [scholarly]
- **Self-immolation as agency, not despair** — her fire is a chosen instrument; the *Rāmāyaṇa* treats it as the act that makes the curse binding. On the wider ritual and historical frame see [satī, jauhar & the immolation rites](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/sati-jauhar-and-the-immolation-rites.md). [scholarly]
