# Śūrpaṇakhā — the Catalyst of the Rāmāyaṇa

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Śūrpaṇakhā** — sister of [Rāvaṇa](./ravana.md), the rākṣasī whose rejected advances and mutilation by [Lakṣmaṇa](./lakshmana.md) at **Pañcavaṭī** ignite the whole war of the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md). Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). Her scene is the hinge of the [Araṇya Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md); her back-story (the husband **Vidyujjihva** killed by Rāvaṇa) sits in the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md); her garrison-cousin is [Khara](./khara-and-the-janasthana-command.md); her clan-context is in [Rākṣasas](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/rakshasas.md), and the ground she walks is [Daṇḍakāraṇya](../../06-sacred-geography/dandakaranya-rama-forest.md) / [Pañcavaṭī–Nāsik](../../06-sacred-geography/panchavati-nashik-godavari.md).
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* her life falls in the **[Tretā Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** of the current 28th Mahāyuga, 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara. On the academic clock the baseline is the Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa* (core books academically dated roughly mid-1st millennium BCE to early CE, with the Uttara Kāṇḍa widely held a later stratum), overlaid by Kamban's 12th-c. CE Tamil *Irāmāvatāram*, the Jain *Paümacariya* (self-dated to the 1st c. CE; modern estimates run c. 3rd–5th c. CE) and late Sanskrit retellings such as the *Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa* (c. 15th c.) and the *Garga Saṃhitā*. [scholarly]
> Numbering: Vālmīki sargas are cited in the **vulgate (Southern)** numbering of valmikiramayan.net / the Shastri translation, with the **Baroda Critical Edition (CE)** equivalent where it differs. In the Araṇya Kāṇḍa the CE runs one sarga behind the vulgate through her episode (vulgate 17 = CE 16, 18 = 17, … 30 = 29) and lacks vulgate sarga 31 entirely, so vulgate 32–35 = CE 30–33.

## Names and identity

- **Śūrpaṇakhā (शूर्पणखा)** — the name is conventionally glossed "she whose nails (*nakha*) are like winnowing-fans (*śūrpa*)" — an epithet of monstrosity; the gloss is etymological, not stated in the epic. Vālmīki introduces her as "the sister of ten-necked Rāvaṇa" (*Araṇya* 17.6 = CE 3.16.5), and she names herself "a rākṣasī called Śūrpaṇakhā, able to take any form at will (*kāmarūpiṇī*), who roams this forest alone, frightening everyone" (*Araṇya* 17.20–21 = CE 3.16.18). Her birth-verse calls her *vikṛtānanā*, "of misshapen face" (*Uttara* 9 = CE 7.9.27).
  - *Earliest mention:* Nārada's summary at the head of the epic already names her — "Śūrpaṇakhā, the shape-shifting rākṣasī living in Janasthāna, was disfigured," and at her word Khara, Triśiras and Dūṣaṇa rose against Rāma (*Bāla* 1.46–47 = CE 1.1.36–37); the table of contents lists "the dialogue with Śūrpaṇakhā and her disfigurement" (CE 1.3.11).
- **Third child of Viśravas and Kaikasī** — the birth order at **Uttara Kāṇḍa 9** is **Rāvaṇa → [Kumbhakarṇa](./kumbhakarna.md) → Śūrpaṇakhā → [Vibhīṣaṇa](./vibhishana.md)**: she is born *third*, of hideous aspect, and Vibhīṣaṇa last, "Kaikasī's youngest son", his birth alone marked by a rain of flowers and celestial drums (*Uttara* 9, Shastri tr.; the CE gives the same birth order at 7.9.25–27 but has no flower-rain). She is therefore Vibhīṣaṇa's **elder** sister, not his younger. **Kubera**, by Viśravas's first wife, is her elder half-brother (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa* 9). See [Kaikasī](./kaikasi.md) and the dynastic table in [Rākṣasas](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/rakshasas.md).
  - *Variant (Rāmopākhyāna, Mahābhārata):* the Mahābhārata's condensed Rāma-story gives Viśravas **three** rākṣasī wives, presented to him by Kubera as attendants: **Puṣpotkaṭā** bears Rāvaṇa and Kumbhakarṇa, **Mālinī** bears Vibhīṣaṇa, and **Rākā** bears **twins, Khara and Śūrpaṇakhā** (*MBh* 3.259.3–8, BORI CE). Here Khara is her full twin brother; in Vālmīki he is her **maternal cousin**, "the son of your mother's sister" (*Uttara* 24 = CE 7.24.30). The same passage calls her "fierce, a hinderer of the siddhas" (3.259.12), and has her and Khara serve and guard their brothers while they perform tapas (3.259.19) (*Mahābhārata, Vana Parva, [Rāmopākhyāna](../ramopakhyana.md)*). [disputed]
- **Candraṇakhā (Jain tradition)** — the Jain Rāmāyaṇas call her **Candraṇakhā** ("moon-nailed"), wife of the Vidyādhara lord of Pātālaṅkā and mother of **Śambūka** — a wholly re-motivated figure (see §Jain variants below) (*Vimalasūri, Paümacariya*). [scholarly]
- **Kāmavallī (Kamban)** — in the Tamil epic she makes herself beautiful by a mantra and, asked her name by Rāma, introduces herself as **Kāmavalli**, "a maiden" (*kaṉṉi*), granddaughter of Brahmā's son, younger sister of Śiva's friend (Kubera) and of the one who lifted the silver mountain (Rāvaṇa) (*Kamban, Irāmāvatāram, Āraṇya kāṇṭam, Cūrppaṇakaip paṭalam* v. 39; cf. the note in [Araṇya Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md)). [regional]

## Before Pañcavaṭī — the widow of Vidyujjihva (Uttara Kāṇḍa)

The three beats are three separate sargas, and retellings routinely compress them into one:

- **Married to Vidyujjihva (Uttara 12)** — In the round of dynastic marriages that also matches Mandodarī to Rāvaṇa, Vajrajvālā to Kumbhakarṇa and Saramā to Vibhīṣaṇa, **Rāvaṇa gives his sister to Vidyujjihva**, a lord of the **Kālakeya** dānavas — "he gave the rākṣasī Śūrpaṇakhā, his sister, to the Kālakeya, the dānava lord named Vidyujjihva" (*Uttara* 12 = CE 7.12.1–2). Her marriage is the first act of the newly consecrated king.
  - *Variant (Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa):* this late Sanskrit retelling has Rāvaṇa give her to Vidyujjihva with **the Daṇḍaka forest itself as her dowry** (*pāribarha*) — fusing the marriage and the later Daṇḍaka grant into one act (*Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa, Sāra-kāṇḍa* 13.61). [late text]
- **Widowed by her own brother (Uttara 23–24)** — Much later, after his truce with the Nivātakavacas and while searching Rasātala for Varuṇa's city, Rāvaṇa storms **Aśmanagara**, the Kālakeya stronghold. In the vulgate the narrator says that there he ran his sword through "his powerful brother-in-law Vidyujjihva, Śūrpaṇakhā's husband," before destroying four hundred daityas (*Uttara* 23, Shastri tr.); the CE line has only the capture of the city and the slaughter of the four hundred, naming no one (CE 7.23.15). Back in Laṅkā she throws herself at his feet: "I am struck down — you, the mighty one, have made me a widow"; her husband, dearer than life, was killed "by you, a brother acting like an enemy"; "surely a brother-in-law (*jāmātā*) should be protected even in battle — and you are not ashamed?" (*Uttara* 24 = CE 7.24.18–24).
  - *Variant (intent):* the question is not left open by the text; it is answered by **Rāvaṇa himself**, who pleads accident: "intent on victory, loosing arrows, I could not tell my own from others in the fighting; that is how your husband came to be killed by me" (*Uttara* 24 = CE 7.24.27). The tension is between this self-exculpation and the vulgate narrator's naming of the victim at the moment of the sword-thrust (*Uttara* 23); readers who take the killing as knowing are reading against Rāvaṇa's own word. [disputed]
- **The consolation-grant: Daṇḍaka and Khara (Uttara 24)** — Rāvaṇa promises to keep her content with honours and gifts and sends her to live beside **Khara**, "the son of your mother's sister," lord of fourteen thousand rākṣasas, with **Dūṣaṇa** as his commander. The region they go to is the forest once **cursed by Uśanas (Śukra)**, which Rāvaṇa says "will be the dwelling of rākṣasas" (cf. [Daṇḍa and the cursing of Daṇḍakāraṇya](../danda-araja-and-dandakaranya.md)). Khara rules there without a rival, and "Śūrpaṇakhā, pleased, lived in the Daṇḍaka forest" (*Uttara* 24 = CE 7.24.26–35; cf. *Araṇya* 19). This **Janasthāna** command is the very arrangement Rāma will later annihilate; see [Khara and the Janasthāna command](./khara-and-the-janasthana-command.md).
- **The name recurs in the war-book** — a **Vidyujjihva**, a māyā-adept in Rāvaṇa's service, conjures the illusory severed head of Rāma and the phantom bow shown to Sītā during the siege (*Yuddha Kāṇḍa* **31** = CE 6.22; Sītā's lament **32**; **Saramā** exposes the illusion at **33–34**). Tradition generally keeps the husband and the illusionist **distinct** — the husband is dead many sargas earlier — though the shared name invites conflation. See [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md), [Saramā](./sarama.md) and [Vibhīṣaṇa](./vibhishana.md). [disputed]
- **The revenge reading** — because Rāvaṇa killed her husband, a strand of later and modern retelling makes Śūrpaṇakhā's instigation of Rāvaṇa (below) **calculated vengeance**: knowing Rāma's prowess, she deliberately baits her brother onto Rāma's arrows to avenge Vidyujjihva. This motive is **not stated in Vālmīki**, where she acts from lust, humiliation and rage; it is an inference elaborated in folk and modern literary retellings. [folk] [disputed]

## The catalyst scene — mutilation at Pañcavaṭī (Araṇya Kāṇḍa 17–18)

- **The approach** — Śūrpaṇakhā comes upon **Rāma** "by chance" at the leaf-hut of [Pañcavaṭī](../../06-sacred-geography/panchavati-nashik-godavari.md) and is smitten. Vālmīki stages the meeting as a run of opposites — she ugly-faced, he fair; she pot-bellied, he slim-waisted; she coppery-haired and harsh-voiced, he well-tressed and gentle-spoken; she old, he young (17.9–12). Asked who she is, she names her brothers Rāvaṇa, Kumbhakarṇa and the righteous Vibhīṣaṇa, and "my brothers Khara and Dūṣaṇa, famed in battle" (17.21–23), claims she surpasses them all, and proposes herself as his wife — offering, as inducement, "to devour this misshapen human woman, together with this brother of yours" (17.24–27) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa* 17 = CE 3.16).
- **The mocking deflection** — Rāma, "with a smile," says he is married and redirects her to the supposedly wifeless Lakṣmaṇa (18.1–5); Lakṣmaṇa, smiling too, replies that she would only become a servant's wife and should be Rāma's junior wife instead, since Rāma will soon discard his "misshapen, old" human wife for her (18.8–12). She takes the irony literally (18.13), returns to Rāma, and announces she will eat Sītā before his eyes (18.14–16). Rāma himself names the game afterwards: "one should never jest (*parihāsa*) with the cruel and ignoble, Saumitri" (18.19) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa* 18 = CE 3.17).
- **The mutilation** — when she lunges at **[Sītā](./sita.md)** "like a great meteor at Rohiṇī" (18.17), Rāma checks her and orders Lakṣmaṇa: "you should disfigure (*virūpayitum*) this ugly, unchaste, wildly lustful, pot-bellied rākṣasī" (18.20). Lakṣmaṇa draws his sword and "cut off her ears and nose" (*ciccheda karṇanāsam*) before Rāma's eyes (18.21), and she flees shrieking back into the forest (18.22) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa* 18 = CE 3.17.20–22). This single stroke is the proximate cause of everything that follows — Janasthāna, the abduction, Laṅkā.
  - *Variant (Kamban, Irāmāvatāram):* the episode is rebuilt over two days. She appears first in a **mantra-made beautiful form** (Kāmavallī), tells Rāma that the approaching Sītā is a rākṣasī in disguise, and is sent away; the next dawn, with Rāma gone to his rites, she tries to carry Sītā off, and **Lakṣmaṇa acts unbidden** (*Kamban, Irāmāvatāram*; see §Kamban below). [regional]
  - *Variant (extent of the wound):* Vālmīki gives **ears and nose** only (18.21). Other tellings differ, each in its own way: the Mahābhārata has her reach Laṅkā "with nose and lips cut off" (*nikṛtta-nāsoṣṭhī*, *MBh* 3.261.44); Kamban has Lakṣmaṇa take her **nose, ears and the tips of her breasts** (*Cūrppaṇakaip paṭalam* v. 94, repeated by her at vv. 119 and 143); the *Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa* has an arrow remove her **nose, ears, lips and breasts** (*Sāra-kāṇḍa* 7.54–56; see §Later Sanskrit retellings); the *Garga Saṃhitā* keeps Vālmīki's "ears and nose" (5.11.4). The "nose" (*nāsikā*) remains the iconic core, feeding the folk etymology of **Nāsik** as the site (*Nāsik sthala-tradition*). [regional] [late text] [folk]
  - *Parallel figure:* the rākṣasī **Ayomukhī**, who later embraces Lakṣmaṇa in the Krauñca forest and claims him as husband, has her "ears, nose and breast" cut off by him with a sword (*Araṇya* 69.17), doubling the motif within the same kāṇḍa. The episode is **absent from the CE's constituted text**, which passes straight from the Krauñca forest to Kabandha (CE 3.65) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa* 69; see [Rākṣasas](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/rakshasas.md)). [interpolation]

## First instigation — Khara and the battle of Janasthāna (Araṇya 19–30)

- **Flight to Khara** — bleeding and shrieking, Śūrpaṇakhā reaches **Khara** at Janasthāna, falls before him and tells him of Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa (18.25–26). Khara demands the name of whoever dared do it (19.1–12); she describes two princes in bark-cloth and a lovely woman between them, blames "that woman" for her plight, and asks for one thing — to **drink the frothy blood of Sītā, Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa** on the battlefield (19.14–20) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa* 18–19).
- **The fourteen** — Khara first sends **fourteen rākṣasas** with her as guide, ordering them to kill the three so "this sister of mine will drink their blood" (19.21–26). Rāma kills them with fourteen arrows, and she returns to Khara shrieking again (20.1–25). This time she **taunts him**: she perceives he is no match for Rāma for all his vaunting, and if he cannot kill these "mere humans" he should leave Janasthāna with his followers; she beats her belly and threatens to give up her life before him (21.6–22) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa* 19–21).
- **Fourteen thousand destroyed** — Khara then marches with **Dūṣaṇa**, **Triśiras** and his full host of **14,000**; Rāma, alone, annihilates the army and its three commanders — emptying the Daṇḍaka garrison her brother had given her (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa* 22–30 = CE 3.21–29; see [Khara and the Janasthāna command](./khara-and-the-janasthana-command.md)).
  - *Variant (Rāmopākhyāna):* the Mahābhārata skips the wooing entirely; it says only that while Rāma lived by the Godāvarī "a great enmity arose with Khara, caused by Śūrpaṇakhā," and that Rāma killed the fourteen thousand, Khara and Dūṣaṇa (*MBh* 3.261.40–43). [disputed]

## Second instigation — goading Rāvaṇa (Araṇya 32–34)

- **An earlier messenger (vulgate only)** — in the vulgate, the first news of Janasthāna reaches Laṅkā not through her but through **Akampana**, a survivor, who tells Rāvaṇa that Rāma cannot be beaten in battle and **first proposes abducting Sītā**, whose beauty he praises; Rāvaṇa drives to **[Mārīca](./maricha.md)**, is talked out of it, and goes home (*Araṇya* 31.1–50). The **CE omits the whole sarga**, so in the critical text Śūrpaṇakhā alone carries both the news and the idea of Sītā to Laṅkā. [interpolation] [disputed]
- **To Laṅkā** — on seeing the fourteen thousand dead, Śūrpaṇakhā flies to Laṅkā and finds **Rāvaṇa** enthroned among his ministers atop his *vimāna* (commentators gloss it as the Puṣpaka) (32.1–25 = CE 3.30). Her first speech is a **lecture on kingship**: a king sunk in pleasure, without spies, unaware of a disaster in his own realm, is like a fire in a cremation ground, a worn-out garment, a crushed garland; Janasthāna is lost, Khara and Dūṣaṇa are dead, a single man did it, and Rāvaṇa has not even heard (33.1–24 = CE 3.31).
- **The poisoned bait** — when Rāvaṇa angrily asks who Rāma is, she answers with a double portrait: **Rāma's lethal prowess** — he alone destroyed fourteen thousand in a muhūrta and a half (*ardhādhikamuhūrtena*), and spared her, she guesses, only from scruple about killing a woman (34.5–12) — *and* **Sītā's incomparable beauty**, unmatched by any goddess, gandharvī, yakṣī or kinnarī (34.15–20). Then comes her own **reworked account**: "when I set out to bring her to be your wife, cruel Lakṣmaṇa disfigured me" (34.21–22a; the CE keeps only the first half, *bhāryārthe tu tavānetum udyatāham*, 3.32.18, and lacks the line naming Lakṣmaṇa) — a version of the mutilation that drops her own wooing of Rāma entirely. She ends by urging that Sītā "be abducted to be your wife" (34.25) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa* 34 = CE 3.32). Her counsel steers him toward abduction rather than open war, and plants (or, in the vulgate, rekindles) the desire that will destroy him.
  - *Variant (Rāmopākhyāna):* she arrives in Laṅkā "with nose and lips cut off," falls at Rāvaṇa's feet, and he hears her **in private**, having sent his ministers away; she tells him of Rāma's prowess and the destruction of Khara and Dūṣaṇa, and Rāvaṇa decides what to do, comforts her and sets out, reaching Mārīca in the next chapter (*MBh* 3.261.44–52; 3.262.1). The Mahābhārata text gives her no description of Sītā at this point. [disputed]
- **The chain she lights** — Rāvaṇa goes to **[Mārīca](./maricha.md)** (for the second time in the vulgate), extorts the golden-deer ruse over Mārīca's warnings, and abducts Sītā; Jaṭāyu falls; the war of the [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md) and the fall of Laṅkā follow (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Araṇya Kāṇḍa* 35 ff. = CE 3.33 ff.; see [Rāvaṇa](./ravana.md)).
- **Exit from the epic** — after the instigation scene Vālmīki gives Śūrpaṇakhā no further action of consequence and records no punishment, reconciliation or death for her — a silence the later traditions rush to fill (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*). Two later echoes in the text should be noted. [scholarly]
  - **A guard of the same name in the Aśoka grove** — among the rākṣasīs threatening Sītā, "a rākṣasī named Śūrpaṇakhā" seconds Ajāmukhī's proposal: bring wine, eat the human flesh, and dance before Nikumbhilā (*Sundara* 24.46–47 = CE 5.22.40–41). The text does not say this is Rāvaṇa's sister, and whether the same figure is meant is open. [disputed]
  - **Blamed by the widows of Laṅkā** — after the deaths of Rāvaṇa's sons and generals, the bereaved rākṣasīs ask how the old, ugly, sunken-bellied Śūrpaṇakhā dared approach a Rāma handsome as Kandarpa, and conclude that "for her sake Rāvaṇa made this great enmity," and brought Sītā to his own destruction (*Yuddha* 94.6–11 = CE 6.82.6 ff.).

## Why she matters — the hinge of the war

- **The catalyst structurally** — as [Mantharā](./manthara.md) is the human trigger of the exile, Śūrpaṇakhā is the demonic trigger of the war: two marginal women, one whispering in a palace and one shrieking in a forest, between them generate the entire plot from Ayodhyā to Laṅkā (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, structure of Kāṇḍas 2–3). The epic's own characters draw the same line: the widows of Laṅkā name her as the cause of the war (*Yuddha* 94.11). [scholarly]
- **The moral problem of the mutilation** — the disfigurement of a woman — ordered by Rāma, executed by Lakṣmaṇa, answering words and a lunge rather than accomplished violence — has been debated within and beyond the tradition; devotional readings stress her attack on Sītā and her cannibal offer, while modern and feminist scholarship (e.g., Kathleen Erndl's study of the episode in Paula Richman's *Many Rāmāyaṇas*) reads the scene as the epic's test-case on female desire and its punishment. The retellings themselves register the problem: Kamban moves the decision from Rāma to Lakṣmaṇa and has Rāma ask afterwards what her offence was (v. 120), and the Jain tellings remove the mutilation altogether (below). [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Providence through malice** — as with Mantharā, theological readings make her rage an instrument of the avatāra's purpose: without the mutilation, no abduction; without the abduction, Rāvaṇa is never brought within reach of Rāma's arrows (*devotional and commentarial readings on the Araṇya Kāṇḍa*). [late text]

## Kamban's Tamil Śūrpaṇakhā — the sympathetic portrait

- **A love-struck heroine, not a monster** — in Kamban's *Irāmāvatāram* (12th c. CE, Chola court) the episode is rendered with full courtly *kāvya* ornamentation and **psychological interiority**: Śūrpaṇakhā, meditating on Lakṣmī, works a **mantra that makes her beautiful** (v. 30) and approaches Rāma as a radiant young woman; her longing is given lyric voice through a whole night of love-sickness after he sends her away (vv. 72–89), so that her humiliation carries real pathos (*Kamban, Irāmāvatāram, Āraṇya kāṇṭam, Cūrppaṇakaip paṭalam*; verse numbers as in the Tamil Wikisource text; see [Kamban recension file](../ramayana/recensions/kamban-iramavataram.md)). [regional] [scholarly]
- **The Kāmavallī disguise** — she calls herself **Kāmavalli**, an unmarried maiden (v. 39). Rāma is uneasy from the start: if she is truly the sister of the fierce Rāvaṇa, he asks, how does she come to have this form (v. 40)? She turns the charge round, telling him the approaching Sītā is a deceitful flesh-eating rākṣasī in a false shape (v. 63), at which Rāma laughs (v. 64). The demonic reality surfaces in rage when Sītā joins him (v. 65), and Rāma drives her off — Kamban dramatizing the gap between the body desire wears and the body it hides (*Kamban, Irāmāvatāram*). [regional]
- **Lakṣmaṇa acts alone** — the next dawn, seeing Rāma away at a pool for his morning rites, she decides to seize Sītā and hide her (vv. 90–92). Lakṣmaṇa, guarding unseen nearby, cries "Stop!", and — because she is a woman — does not take up his bow: he seizes her by the hair, kicks her down, draws his sword and takes her nose, ears and nipples (vv. 93–94). Rāma returns, and when she falls before him he asks Lakṣmaṇa what her offence was (v. 120). Even mutilated she goes on pleading with Rāma to take her, offering to counter rākṣasa sorcery for him (vv. 138–141), and leaves only when Lakṣmaṇa offers to kill her, vowing to bring Khara (vv. 142–143). Before Rāvaṇa she tells the same self-serving story as in Vālmīki — that Lakṣmaṇa cut her nose as she was carrying Sītā off *for him* — and asks for Rāma for herself (*Cūrppaṇakai cūḻccip paṭalam* vv. 79–81). [regional]
- **Śambukumāran — the son Vālmīki does not give her** — a widely repeated tradition supplies a motive Vālmīki lacks: Śūrpaṇakhā has a son, killed unknowingly by Lakṣmaṇa in the forest before she ever meets Rāma, so that her approach is a bereaved mother's to her son's killers. **The son was not found in Kamban's text** in the three paṭalams that carry her story (Cūrppaṇakaip paṭalam, Karaṉ vataip paṭalam, Cūrppaṇakai cūḻccip paṭalam, Tamil Wikisource); in Kamban she presents herself as an unmarried maiden. The motif is attested elsewhere, in forms that do not agree:
  - *Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa:* her son **Sāmba** performs tapas in the Pañcavaṭī forest; Brahmā sends him a divine sword, which Sāmba does not notice; Lakṣmaṇa takes it and uses it to cut down a thicket, killing the hidden Sāmba; Rāma reassures him that he has killed "a rākṣasa, not a muni." The grieving mother then takes a lovely form, intending to kill the brothers (*Sāra-kāṇḍa* 7.41–46), and later tells Rāvaṇa that her son Sāmba has been killed too (7.75). [late text]
  - *Vettam Mani's Puranic Encyclopedia:* citing "Kamba Rāmāyaṇa," it names the son **Śambhukumāra**, makes him fall in love with Sītā and stand in the form of a tree, which Lakṣmaṇa fells while building the hut. The same entry gives her disguise as "Lalitā." These details could not be matched to the Kamban text above. [disputed]
  - *Cross-tradition note:* the same device — her son killed unknowingly in the forest, the killing rather than the mutilation as *casus belli* — is the Jain **Śambūka** of the *Paümacariya* (below), which is centuries older than the Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa. Whether the Hindu-side versions descend from the Jain one has not been established here. [scholarly]
- **Part of a larger softening** — the sympathetic Śūrpaṇakhā belongs to Kamban's general re-keying of the rākṣasa house: his Rāvaṇa too is a magnificent, tragic ruler undone by love-lust rather than a simple ogre — brother and sister are both dignified by the Tamil poet even in their ruin (*Kamban, Irāmāvatāram*; cf. [Kamban file](../ramayana/recensions/kamban-iramavataram.md)). [regional] [scholarly]

## Jain variants — Candraṇakhā and the death of Śambūka

- **Re-motivated from the root** — in **Vimalasūri's *Paümacariya*** (the earliest Jain Rāmāyaṇa) she is **Candraṇakhā**, sister of Rāvaṇa and wife of the ruler of Pātālaṅkā (the "Khara-Dūṣaṇa" figure of the Jain line), and mother of **Śambūka** (*Paümacariya*; see [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md)). [scholarly]
- **The accidental killing of her son** — her son **Śambūka** performs austerities in a **bamboo grove** to win a magical sword. Lakṣmaṇa, following a fragrance, comes upon the grove and the divine sword, named **Sūryahāsa**, lifts it, cuts the thicket to test it, and a severed head falls out — Śambūka's (*Paümacariya* 43, as summarized in K. R. Chandra's critical study). Candraṇakhā, discovering her son's body, then encounters the brothers, is momentarily smitten, is rejected — and rouses her husband and Rāvaṇa (*Paümacariya* tradition). [scholarly]
  - *Note:* Chandra sets this against Vālmīki's Uttara Kāṇḍa, where a different Śambūka, a śūdra ascetic with no tie to Śūrpaṇakhā, is beheaded by Rāma for his penance; in the Jain telling the killer is Lakṣmaṇa and the victim is Rāvaṇa's nephew (K. R. Chandra, *Paumacariya: a critical study*). [scholarly]
- **No mutilation** — in the Jain mainline the **nose-cutting is absent**: the *casus belli* is the killing of Śambūka and her grief-fed rage, not a disfigurement — the Jain authors, hostile to the cruelty of the Hindu telling, remove the act from Rāma's and Lakṣmaṇa's account (*Paümacariya* tradition; Jain rationalizing program). [scholarly]
  - *Note:* the Jain Rāmāyaṇas systematically recast rākṣasas as civilized **Vidyādharas** and redistribute the epic's violence (Lakṣmaṇa, not Rāma, kills Rāvaṇa); Candraṇakhā's story is one instance of that program — catalog, don't harmonize, against Vālmīki (see [Jain & Buddhist Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/jain-and-buddhist-ramayanas.md)). [scholarly]

## Later Sanskrit retellings

- **Bhāgavata Purāṇa** — the Purāṇa's compressed Rāma-story makes **Rāma** the grammatical agent: "he disfigured the form of the impure-minded sister of the rākṣasa," then killed her kinsmen led by Khara, Triśiras and Dūṣaṇa, fourteen thousand in all; Rāvaṇa's desire is kindled by "hearing the tale of Sītā" (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 9.10.9–10). [late text]
- **Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa** — besides the son Sāmba (above), this c. 15th-century "Vālmīki" retelling changes the mutilation itself. After the deflection to Lakṣmaṇa, who tells her she would only be a servant's servant, she rushes to seize Sītā; Rāma hands her a razor-sharp arrow as a "token" to show Lakṣmaṇa, who will then grant her wish; Lakṣmaṇa reads his brother's intent and shoots it, and the Rāma-marked arrow cuts off her nose, ears, lips and breasts and returns to Rāma's quiver (*Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa, Sāra-kāṇḍa* 7.47–56). She then goes to Khara, Triśiras and Dūṣaṇa, who send the fourteen (7.57–58). See [Adhyātma and variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md). [late text]

## Afterlives and further variants

- **Vālmīki's silence** — the Sanskrit epic gives her no ending; regional and folk traditions variously imagine her surviving the war in Laṅkā, retiring to the forest, or perishing obscurely. [folk] [open-ended]
- **The Kubjā rebirth motif** — the *Garga Saṃhitā* tells it in full: after Lakṣmaṇa cut off her ears and nose and she had reported to Rāvaṇa, Śūrpaṇakhā went to **Puṣkara** and for ten thousand years did tapas standing in the water, meditating on **Śiva**, to win Rāma as her husband; Śiva told her the boon could not bear fruit now but would at the end of the Dvāpara in Mathurā; Nārada concludes that "this very Śūrpaṇakhā" became **Kubjā**, the hunchbacked woman of Mathurā, who by Śiva's boon became dear to Kṛṣṇa (*Garga Saṃhitā, Mathurā-khaṇḍa* 5.11.2–11; cf. [Kaṃsa-vadha](../kamsa-vadha.md)). Secondary sources also attribute the identification to the *Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa* (Kṛṣṇa-janma-khaṇḍa); that text was not checked here. The identification is a late devotional overlay, not in Vālmīki. [late text] [disputed]
- **Southeast Asian retellings** — the episode travels with the epic into Southeast Asia (e.g., the Thai *Ramakien*'s version of the sister-demoness), with local renamings and re-shapings of the wooing scene (see [Southeast Asian Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/recensions/southeast-asian-ramayanas.md)). [regional]
- **Modern rehabilitations** — contemporary fiction and criticism retell the epic from her point of view — widow, mutilated woman, avenger — building on the Vidyujjihva back-story and the Kamban pathos; these are modern literary works, not scripture, and are flagged as such. [scholarly] [folk]
