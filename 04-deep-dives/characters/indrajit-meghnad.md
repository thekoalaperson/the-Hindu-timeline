# Indrajit (Meghanāda)

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Indrajit / Meghanāda** (patronymic **Rāvaṇi**), eldest son of Rāvaṇa, crown prince of Laṅkā, the rākṣasa who bound Indra and carried him to Laṅkā, master of the *Brahmāstra* and *māyā* (illusion), whose death at Lakṣmaṇa's hand breaks the back of the rākṣasa army. Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). His war and death sit inside the [Yuddha Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md); his Indra-conquest and youth are told in the [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md).
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* the Rāma narrative falls in the **Tretā Yuga** of the present 28th Mahāyuga (7th / Vaivasvata Manvantara); the *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa* (cited here by vulgate/Southern sarga, with Baroda Critical Edition numbers as "CE" where they differ) is the textual baseline, with the Mahābhārata's *Rāmopākhyāna*, the Tulsīdās *Rāmacaritamānasa*, Kṛttivāsī, Kamban, and regional Rāmāyaṇas as later or parallel strata. [scholarly]

## Name, birth, and parentage
- **Eldest son of Rāvaṇa and Mandodarī** - Mandodarī bears him in Laṅkā after the rākṣasa brothers' marriages (*Uttara* 12, Shastri; CE 7.12.26). **Atikāya** is his half-brother, Rāvaṇa's son by **Dhānyamālinī**, as Vibhīṣaṇa tells Rāma when Atikāya takes the field (CE 6.59.29). He is usually counted the senior of Rāvaṇa's sons. His uncles are Kumbhakarṇa and the defector Vibhīṣaṇa, whose guidance ultimately leads Lakṣmaṇa to him.
- **Birth-name Meghanāda ("thunder-roar")** - the newborn's first cry boomed like a rain-cloud and **stunned all Laṅkā**, so **Rāvaṇa himself** named him Meghanāda (*megha* "cloud" + *nāda* "roar"). He then grew up hidden in the women's quarters, "like fire under kindling" (*Uttara* 12, Shastri; CE 7.12.26–29).
- **Title "Indrajit" — conqueror of Indra** - **Brahmā** gives him the name when he comes to ransom Indra: "he will be famed in the world as Indrajit" (*Uttara* 30; CE 7.30.5). The Sundara and Yuddha books call him *Indrajit* and *Rāvaṇi* interchangeably.
  - *Variant (later astrological tradition):* at his birth Rāvaṇa ordered the planets fixed in exalted houses to grant the child immortality, but **Śani (Saturn)** shifted into the 12th house, fating Meghanāda to die in war; Rāvaṇa is said to have assaulted/imprisoned Śani in fury. [folk] [late text]
- **Where his powers came from (Vālmīki)** - Vālmīki names three sources. Śukra appears in them only as the *guru* who conducts the sacrifices (*gurur yajñasamṛddhaye*, CE 7.25.6), not as the giver or teacher of the weapons. (1) **Paśupati (Śiva)** gave the chariot, the *tāmasī* māyā, the quivers, the bow and the weapon at the end of the Māheśvara sacrifice, with **Śukra (Uśanas) officiating** (*Uttara* 25, Shastri; CE 7.25.6–13). At Indra's capture the poet again calls his māyā the one "given by Gopati", most likely Paśupati again (CE 7.29.23). (2) **Brahmā**, pleased by his penance, gave the **Brahmaśiras** weapon and horses that go wherever he wills (*Yuddha* 85.12; CE 6.72.12). (3) The **pre-battle fire-rite** renews these powers before each sortie (see below). The picture of Śukrācārya as his *guru* in arms belongs to later retellings. [late text]

## The conquest of Indra — how he won his name
- **War on Svarga** - When Rāvaṇa's army breaks before the gods, Meghanāda rallies the rākṣasas and charges in a fire-coloured, go-where-you-will chariot, and the gods scatter at the sight of him (*Uttara* 28, Shastri; CE 7.28.1–4). Indra sends out his son **Jayanta**. Meghanāda looses gold-decked arrows at Jayanta's charioteer **Gomukha**, the son of Mātali, and spreads a great darkness over the field. In the confusion the daitya **Pulomā**, Śacī's father and so Jayanta's grandfather, carries Jayanta off into the ocean (CE 7.28.7–18). Rāvaṇa then sends his son back and takes on Indra himself (CE 7.28.27–30).
- **Binding and abducting Indra** - Indra decides to take Rāvaṇa **alive**, since Brahmā's boon means he cannot be killed. He surrounds him, and the rākṣasas cry out that their king is taken (CE 7.29.13–21). Meghanāda then enters the divine host **invisible**. He wounds Mātali and Indra's horses, and when Indra moves to **Airāvata** he showers him with arrows. Once Indra tires, Meghanāda **binds him by māyā** and drags him back to his own lines. The gods cannot even see who is carrying their king off (CE 7.29.22–29). Still invisible, he tells his battered father that the fighting is over, and at Rāvaṇa's command he takes Indra to **Laṅkā** (CE 7.29.32–40). Compare Indra's other humiliations gathered around figures such as [Nahuṣa](../nahusha.md).
  - In the war council Indrajit himself retells this victory as a boast. He says he threw Indra down and **wrenched out Airāvata's tusks** (*Yuddha* 15.5–6). The Mahābhārata's Rāvaṇa also recalls "how you once bound Vāsava" (*Mbh* 3.272.7, BORI).
- **Brahmā's intervention and the ransom-boon** - Brahmā comes to Laṅkā with the gods, praises the son as his father's equal or better, names him Indrajit, and asks what he wants in return for Indra's release (CE 7.30.1–7). Indrajit first asks for **immortality**. Brahmā refuses, because no creature is wholly deathless (CE 7.30.8–9). Indrajit then sets his own terms. In the **Critical Edition** he says he always worships Fire with oblations before going into battle, and **if he ever fights before that japa and homa to Vibhāvasu is finished, let that be his destruction**. Others win immortality by penance, he adds, but he has won his by valour (CE 7.30.11–13). Brahmā says "So be it", Indra is freed, and the gods go home (CE 7.30.14).
  - *Recension note:* the vulgate, as Hari Prasad Shastri translates it, has an extra clause before the self-doom condition. Indrajit asks that "the chariot of Vibhāvasu harnessed to steeds" serve him and that death should not touch him while he rides it (*Uttara* 30, Shastri). The Critical Edition has no chariot clause in this sarga. A will-going chariot is already among his Māheśvara boons (CE 7.25.10).
  - **The self-doom clause is the war's hinge, and it is core Vālmīki.** The "secret of Indrajit's death" comes from words he spoke himself. In the war, Vibhīṣaṇa states the same rule in Brahmā's name: **an enemy who strikes him before he reaches Nikumbhilā and before he has fed the fire will be his death** (*Yuddha* 85.14–15; CE 6.72.13). Lakṣmaṇa does not find a hidden loophole. He collects on a condition Indrajit set for himself at the height of his triumph.
  - **Why Indra could be taken at all** - In the same sarga Brahmā tells the humiliated Indra that this is **Gautama's curse** coming due. In this telling **Brahmā had created Ahalyā** and given her to Gautama. Indra raped her, and the sage cursed him that he would **fall into an enemy's hands in battle** (CE 7.30.17–29). The Bāla-kāṇḍa's oldest scandal is the reason the Uttara's greatest rākṣasa has his name. See [Ahalyā](./ahalya.md).
  - *Variant (popular tradition):* Brahmā himself warns that **whoever destroys the sacrifice will be the one able to kill him**, a later re-voicing of the condition. Tulsīdās does not have it: the *Mānas* knows only an unnamed boon that keeps him alive under Jāmbavān's blows (Laṅkā 74.5) and Vibhīṣaṇa's warning that a completed rite would make Meghanāda hard to conquer (*Rāmacaritamānasa*, Laṅkā 75.2–3). Vālmīki's Brahmā-voiced form is the Yuddha 85.14–15 rule above. [folk]
  - *Variant (popular/devotional):* a further boon that he could be killed only by **a man who had not slept, not eaten, and not seen his wife's face for fourteen (or twelve) years** — conditions only Lakṣmaṇa, the celibate forest-ascetic, fulfilled. [folk]
- **The seven sacrifices and the origin of Nikumbhilā** - The chariot is not the whole of it. Returning from his conquests, Rāvaṇa finds his son in the **Nikumbhilā grove**, dressed in a black antelope skin with a water-pot and topknot (CE 7.25.2–4). With **Śukra (Uśanas)** officiating, Meghanāda had completed **seven great sacrifices**: *Agniṣṭoma, Aśvamedha, Bahusuvarṇaka, Rājasūya, Gomedha, Vaiṣṇava,* and the rare **Māheśvara**. At the end of the last, **Paśupati himself** granted him a **sky-going chariot that goes at will**, the **tāmasī māyā** that spreads darkness so that neither gods nor asuras can track him in battle, **two inexhaustible quivers**, an unbreakable bow and a mighty weapon. Śukra reports the tally to Rāvaṇa himself (*Uttara* 25, Shastri; CE 7.25.6–13). Rāvaṇa is **not pleased**: "this was not well done," he says, because the offerings honoured his enemies, the gods led by Indra (CE 7.25.14). The powers were taken here, so this is the only ground on which they can be denied — the fire-ground itself, and what the later strata have added to it, are held at [Nikumbhilā](../../06-sacred-geography/nikumbhila.md).

## Before the war — Hanumān and the war council
- **Binding Hanumān with the Brahmāstra** - After Hanumān kills Rāvaṇa's son **Akṣa**, Rāvaṇa sends Indrajit and reminds him that his weapons were won by propitiating Brahmā (*Sundara* 48.1–3). Indrajit rides out in a chariot **drawn by four sharp-toothed tigers** (*Sundara* 48.18). Hanumān dodges every arrow, so Indrajit concludes he cannot be killed and decides to capture him. He binds him with the **Brahmāstra** (*Sundara* 48.34–38). Hanumān honours Brahmā's weapon and lets himself be taken, because he wants an audience with Rāvaṇa (*Sundara* 48.39–45). The rākṣasas then tie him with hemp and bark ropes. The astra's hold **lapses once another binding is laid on him**, and Indrajit groans that his feat has been wasted by men who do not understand the spell (*Sundara* 48.46–50).
- **The council clash with Vibhīṣaṇa** - When Vibhīṣaṇa urges Rāvaṇa to return Sītā, Indrajit mocks him as the one coward in the family. Two men, he says, are nothing to the one who humbled Indra (*Yuddha* 15.1–7). Vibhīṣaṇa answers that he is **an immature boy** who is a son only in name and in fact his father's enemy, and that whoever let him into the council deserves death too (*Yuddha* 15.9–11). The two meet again over the Nikumbhilā fire (*Yuddha* 87).
- **The western gate** - In Rāvaṇa's dispositions Indrajit holds the **western gate** of Laṅkā (*Yuddha* 37.11). He sallies from it with the māyā-Sītā (*Yuddha* 81.3). Vālmīki places the man at the west gate but never locates the Nikumbhilā grove itself; its western siting is a later stratum (see below).

## Powers and arsenal
- **Master of māyā (illusion)** - conjures phantoms, fights unseen, vanishes and reappears, and veils the sky in smoke-like darkness and mist (*Yuddha* 80.25). His hallmark is shooting **from the sky while invisible**, chariot and all (*Yuddha* 73.27–29; 80.22). The *tāmasī* māyā of the Māheśvara boons is exactly this darkness-making power (CE 7.25.10–11).
- **Invisibility in battle** - his single most feared gift. He boasts that when he fights invisible even Indra cannot see or approach him (*Yuddha* 45.11). Only **Vibhīṣaṇa**, using his own occult sight, makes out his nephew hidden nearby (*Yuddha* 46.9–10).
- **His weapons in Vālmīki** - the **Brahmāstra / Brahmaśiras** (*Sundara* 48.36; *Yuddha* 73.27; 85.12); **serpent-arrows** (*Yuddha* 44.36; 50.49); in the last duel the **Raudra**, **Āgneya** and an **Āsura** weapon, and an arrow **given by Yama** (*Yuddha* 90.47, 90.56–60). The **Vaiṣṇavāstra** and **Pāśupatāstra** in his hands, and the name **Nāgapāśa**, come from later retellings. [late text]
- **The Brahmāstra against the vānaras** - in his second sortie the Brahmāstra strikes down **sixty-seven crores** (670 million) of vānaras in the last watch of the day. **The figure is Vālmīki's own** (*Yuddha* 74.12). These monkeys are "struck down", not finally slain: Hanumān's herb-mountain revives them (*Yuddha* 74).
- **Sacrifice at Nikumbhilā** - his invulnerability hinges on a pre-battle yajña at the **Nikumbhilā** sanctuary (*caitya*) inside Laṅkā — the same ground where the seven sacrifices of *Uttara* 25 were performed — where he pours oblations of flesh and blood to the *bhūtas* before a banyan "terrible to look at, like a black cloud." Vālmīki fixes the altar by name and by use and gives it **no quarter of the city and no presiding deity** (*Yuddha* Kāṇḍa 82.24–26, 87.2–4). Canonical home: [Nikumbhilā](../../06-sacred-geography/nikumbhila.md).
  - **The field rite** - before his Brahmāstra sortie and again before his third, Vālmīki describes the rite exactly. Weapons stand in for the *śara* grass and Vibhītaka wood is the fuel, with red cloths and an **iron ladle**. Indrajit seizes a **live black goat** by the throat and offers it, and the smokeless fire rises with its flames turning to the right, a sign of victory (*Yuddha* 73.21–26; 80.5–8).
  - *Recension note:* the Critical Edition sends him to **Nikumbhilā** for the Brahmāstra sortie (CE 6.60.18). The vulgate's sarga 73 simply has him reach the battlefield and sacrifice there.
  - *Variant (commentarial / southern / tantric):* the altar is given a goddess of its own — **Nikumbhilā-devī**, identified with **Bhadrakālī** and, most widely in the south, with **Pratyaṅgirā** — and the rite read as her invocation rather than a fire-oblation; the siting of the grove in Laṅkā's **western quarter** belongs to the same stratum. [late text] [regional]

## First engagement — the Nāgapāśa
- **Striking unseen** - in the first night battle **Aṅgada** kills Indrajit's horses and charioteer, so that Indrajit has to abandon his chariot, and the gods applaud (*Yuddha* 44.28–33). Furious at the defeat, Indrajit vanishes and fights on invisible (*Yuddha* 44.34–35).
- **Binding Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa with serpent-arrows** - he looses **serpent-like arrows** into both princes and wraps them in a "**binding of arrows**" (*śarabandha*) (*Yuddha* 44.36–39). The ten vānara chiefs sent to find him cannot see him in the darkness (*Yuddha* 45.1–6). Both brothers fall, with "not a finger's breadth" of their bodies left unpierced (*Yuddha* 45.16–22). Indrajit takes them for dead and returns to Laṅkā in triumph (*Yuddha* 46). Rāvaṇa then has Sītā flown over the field in the Puṣpaka to see them (*Yuddha* 47). **Vālmīki's episode never calls the weapon "Nāgapāśa"**: the Critical Edition text on GRETIL has no occurrence of the word, and the vulgate Sanskrit of these sargas calls the binding a *śarabandha* (*Yuddha* 44–46). Garuḍa later explains that the arrows were **Kadrū's serpent-sons, turned into arrows by Indrajit's māyā** (*Yuddha* 50.49). The name *nāgapāsa* is Tulsīdās's (*Rāmacaritamānasa*, Laṅkā 73.7). [late text]
- **Rescue by Garuḍa** - Suṣeṇa first proposes sending monkeys to the Milk Ocean for the herbs *sañjīvakaraṇī* and *viśalyā*, which grow on the mountains **Candra and Droṇa** (*Yuddha* 50.26–31). Before that can happen **Garuḍa** arrives **unsummoned**. The serpents flee at his coming, and his touch heals the princes (*Yuddha* 50.36–39). He calls himself Rāma's dear friend but **refuses to explain** the friendship: "you will know it when the war is won" (*Yuddha* 50.46, 50.57). See [Garuḍa](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/garuda.md).
  - *Variant:* the rescue's framing varies. In Vālmīki Garuḍa comes on his own. In the *Rāmacaritamānasa* the sage **Nārada sends** him, and he eats the māyā-serpents (Laṅkā 74.5 and dohā 74 ka). In the Mahābhārata's constituted text Garuḍa does not appear at all; he is found only in star-passages, in one of which Rāma calls him to mind at the Wind's word (*Mbh* 3.273.4 *1288–*1289, BORI). The constant is that the serpent-binding dissolves.

## Second engagement — the Brahmāstra that fells both brothers

Retellings routinely fuse Indrajit's second sortie with **Rāvaṇa's** felling of Lakṣmaṇa much later in the war, and so credit Indrajit with a śakti-wound he never inflicts. In Vālmīki the two are separate battles, separate weapons, and separate herb-flights.

- **The weapon is the Brahmāstra, and it takes both brothers** - Indrajit performs his oblation, goes **invisible into the sky**, and looses the **Brahmāstra**. Rāma recognises the weapon as Brahmā's, calls the Self-born inconceivable, and tells Lakṣmaṇa they must **bear it rather than resist** (*Yuddha* 73.70). Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa fall together with sixty-seven crores of vānaras, and Indrajit returns to Laṅkā proclaiming them dead (*Yuddha* 73–74; CE 6.60–61, the 67 crores at CE 6.61.12). **He never strikes Lakṣmaṇa with a śakti anywhere in Vālmīki.**
- **The first herb-flight** - **Jāmbavān** sends **Hanumān** across the ocean to the Himālaya for **four** herbs — *mṛta-sañjīvanī, viśalya-karaṇī, suvarṇa-karaṇī, sandhānī* — growing on a blazing all-herb mountain in the Himālaya that Vālmīki places **between Mount Ṛṣabha and Kailāsa and never names** (*Yuddha* Kāṇḍa 74.29–33); unable to pick them out, he carries the whole mountain, and the scent of the herbs revives Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa and the host (*Yuddha* Kāṇḍa 74). The name **"Droṇagiri" for this Himālayan mountain is not Vālmīki's**. The only mountain called Droṇa in the Critical Edition text is one in the Milk Ocean that Suṣeṇa names in the earlier serpent-arrow crisis (*Yuddha* 50.31), and the plan to go there is dropped. Later tradition grafts the name onto this flight in almost every retelling. [folk]
- **The other wounding is Rāvaṇa's, not his** - The spear that drops Lakṣmaṇa bathed in blood is thrown by **Rāvaṇa**. It is an eight-belled, never-failing *śakti* "made by **Maya** through his magic, a slayer of foes" (*śaktim aṣṭaghaṇṭāṃ … mayena māyāvihitām amoghāṃ śatrughātinīm*, *Yuddha* 100.30–31; CE 6.88.30). It is not Indra's Vāsavī Śakti and not Indrajit's. (The "Maya-made" missile at *Yuddha* 100.2 is a different weapon, aimed at Rāma.) The **second** herb-flight follows: **Suṣeṇa** the vānara physician sends Hanumān, this time to **Mount Mahodaya/Oṣadhi**, and Lakṣmaṇa is revived (*Yuddha* Kāṇḍa 101). See [Lakṣmaṇa](./lakshmana.md).
  - *Variant (Rāmacaritamānasa and popular tradition):* in Tulsīdās, **Meghanāda** hurls the **"Vīraghātinī" (hero-slaying) śakti** into Lakṣmaṇa's chest during their duel, and a hundred crore warriors as strong as Meghanāda cannot lift the fallen Śeṣa (*Rāmacaritamānasa*, Laṅkā 54.4 and dohā 54). The epithet echoes Vālmīki's *śatrughātinī* for Rāvaṇa's spear. This is the conflation described above, and it is the version most retellings and stage traditions carry, often with the blow struck from behind. [late text] [folk]

## The māyā-Sītā — the illusion of slaying Sītā
- **The third sortie** - after Makarākṣa's death Rāvaṇa sends his son out again. Indrajit renews his rite and fights invisible from the sky, veiling the quarters in darkness (*Yuddha* 80.1–25). Lakṣmaṇa offers to loose the Brahmāstra and wipe out the whole rākṣasa race. Rāma forbids it: one should not kill the whole race for one rākṣasa, nor strike those who are not fighting, are hiding, beg for refuge or are fleeing (*Yuddha* 80.37–41). Indrajit reads Rāma's intent and withdraws into the city (*Yuddha* 81.1).
- **The phantom Sītā** - he comes out of the **western gate** with an illusory Sītā (*māyāmayī sītā*) on his chariot: a single braid, a worn garment, a face thin from fasting (*Yuddha* 81.3–9). Hanumān, who saw the real Sītā only recently, takes her for real. Indrajit drags her by the hair and, when Hanumān protests that women may not be killed, answers that whatever torments the enemy must be done (*Yuddha* 81.14–28). Then, with his sword, he **cuts her through diagonally, along the line of the sacred thread** (*yajñopavīta-mārgeṇa*), and she falls (*Yuddha* 81.29–30). It is a slash through the body, not a beheading.
- **Hanumān deceived, Rāma collapses** - Hanumān calls off the fight and reports the killing; Rāma faints (*Yuddha* 82.20–23; 83.10). Lakṣmaṇa's grief bursts out in a speech arguing that virtue cannot protect the virtuous and is useless (*Yuddha* 83.14–22). Meanwhile Indrajit goes to **Nikumbhilā** (*Yuddha* 82.24).
- **Vibhīṣaṇa exposes the trick** - **Vibhīṣaṇa** assures Rāma that Rāvaṇa would never allow Sītā to be killed. The slain "Sītā" was pure illusion, staged to hold the vānaras off while Indrajit performs the rite at **Nikumbhilā**. Once that rite is complete, not even the gods could face him (*Yuddha* 84.14–23). This is the hinge: the fake murder is itself cover for the yajña.

## The Nikumbhilā yajña and his death
- **The race to the sacrifice** - Vibhīṣaṇa cites Brahmā's ordinance (*Yuddha* 85.14–15) and urges that Indrajit be attacked **before the rite completes**. Rāma sends **Lakṣmaṇa**, guided by Vibhīṣaṇa and with Aṅgada, Hanumān and a vānara force (*Yuddha* 85.16–35). Lakṣmaṇa takes his stand "in conformity with Brahmā's ordinance" (*Yuddha* 85.34).
- **The rite left unfinished** - Lakṣmaṇa's arrows fall on the rākṣasa army drawn up at the grove (*Yuddha* 86.1–6). Hearing his troops routed, **Indrajit himself breaks off the sacrifice before it is complete** (*Yuddha* 86.14). He mounts his chariot, already yoked and waiting, and turns first on Hanumān (*Yuddha* 86.15–35). Vibhīṣaṇa leads Lakṣmaṇa to the black-cloud **banyan** where Indrajit offers to the *bhūtas* before every battle and tells him to strike before Indrajit reaches it (*Yuddha* 87.2–6). Nobody wrecks the fire. The condition is broken because Indrajit fights with the oblation unfinished. In the **Critical Edition** too, Vibhīṣaṇa's description of Indrajit is "not having reached Nikumbhilā, not having fed the fire" (CE 6.72.13).
- **Uncle and nephew** - Seeing Vibhīṣaṇa beside Lakṣmaṇa, Indrajit turns on him. You were born and raised here, he says, my father's own brother, and you serve our enemy. Kinship counts for nothing with you, and an enemy is always an enemy (*Yuddha* 87.10–16). Vibhīṣaṇa replies that he left Rāvaṇa for his vices: theft of others' wealth, assault on others' wives, and distrust of friends. He tells Indrajit, "you cannot reach the banyan any longer" (*Yuddha* 87.18–30). In Vālmīki this exchange states the "treachery" theme long before Dutt (see below).
- **The final duel** - Lakṣmaṇa fights from **Hanumān's back** (*Yuddha* 88.5). The two shatter each other's armour (*Yuddha* 88.56–59). Lakṣmaṇa kills the charioteer with a *bhalla* arrow, and Indrajit drives and shoots at once (*Yuddha* 89.40–43). Four vānaras, **Pramāthin, Rabhasa, Śarabha and Gandhamādana**, crush his horses, and he fights on foot (*Yuddha* 89.48–54). He then slips back into **Laṅkā for a fresh chariot** and returns, to the attackers' surprise (*Yuddha* 90.6–13). His new charioteer is beheaded, and he hurls a javelin at Vibhīṣaṇa (*Yuddha* 90.28, 90.43). Then come the matched weapons. Indrajit's **arrow given by Yama** meets Lakṣmaṇa's arrow **given by Kubera in a dream**, and both shatter (*Yuddha* 90.47–54). Lakṣmaṇa's **Vāruṇa** is broken by Indrajit's **Raudra**. Indrajit's **Āgneya** is stopped by Lakṣmaṇa's **Saura**, and Indrajit's **Āsura** weapon is checked by Lakṣmaṇa's **Māheśvara**, while gods and sages led by Indra stand guard over Lakṣmaṇa (*Yuddha* 90.56–65). Rāma later says it took **three days and nights** to bring Indrajit down (*Yuddha* 91.16).
  - *Variant (popular devotional and screen retellings):* his supreme weapons, the Brahmāstra, Vaiṣṇavāstra and Pāśupatāstra, refuse to harm Lakṣmaṇa, **circling him in salute** and returning, revealing Lakṣmaṇa's divine nature. This is not in Vālmīki, where the weapons are traded and countered (*Yuddha* 90.47–62), nor in Tulsīdās's account of the death (Laṅkā 76). [folk]
- **Slain by Lakṣmaṇa** - Lakṣmaṇa fits the **Aindrāstra**, the weapon with which Indra once beat the dānavas, and makes an act of truth: "**If Rāma son of Daśaratha is righteous and true to his word, and unrivalled in manhood, then slay this son of Rāvaṇa**" (*Yuddha* 90.69–72; CE 6.78.30–32). The arrow cuts off Indrajit's head, helmet and blazing earrings, and the head lies on the ground shining like gold (*Yuddha* 90.73–76; CE 6.78.33–34). The weapon of Indra kills the conqueror of Indra. The vānaras and Vibhīṣaṇa roar as the gods did when Vṛtra fell, and gods, sages, gandharvas and apsarases celebrate in the sky (*Yuddha* 90.77–97). The name **Añjalikāstra**, sometimes given for this arrow in popular retellings, is not Vālmīki's name for it. In Vālmīki *añjalika* is only a common type of arrow, and Indrajit himself shoots añjalikas at Rāma in the first engagement (*Yuddha* 45.23; CE 6.35.22). The best-known *añjalika* is the arrow that beheads Karṇa in the Mahābhārata (*Mbh* 8.67.33, BORI). Tulsīdās names no weapon for the death-arrow (Laṅkā 76.8). [folk]
  - *Variant (devotional):* Lakṣmaṇa alone could kill him because Lakṣmaṇa is the incarnation of **Śeṣa-nāga** and had kept fourteen years of waking, fasting, celibate service in the forest, satisfying the boon's conditions. [folk]
  - *Variant (Rāmacaritamānasa):* Meghanāda performs his "impure" sacrifice in a **mountain cave**, offering **blood and live buffaloes**. The monkeys wreck it and drag him out by the hair. He fights with a trident, now visible and now vanishing in many guises, until Lakṣmaṇa's arrow strikes him **in the chest** (no beheading). Dying, he drops all deceit and cries "**Where is Rāma's younger brother? Where is Rāma?**", and Aṅgada and Hanumān exclaim "blessed, blessed is your mother". Hanumān lays the body at Laṅkā's gate (Laṅkā 75.1–77.1). [late text]
- **Aftermath in Laṅkā** - Rāvaṇa's grief turns first on Sītā. His son, he says, showed a mere illusion of her death, and he will make it real. He rushes sword in hand toward the Aśoka grove (*Yuddha* 92.36–40) until the good minister **Supārśva** dissuades him (*Yuddha* 92.62 ff.). Rāvaṇa then sends out his remaining army and, after its destruction, takes the field himself for the last time (*Yuddha* 93 onward). Suṣeṇa heals Lakṣmaṇa's wounds with a herb given through the nose (*Yuddha* 91.23).

## The Mahābhārata's Rāmopākhyāna
- In Mārkaṇḍeya's retelling to Yudhiṣṭhira (*Mbh* 3.272–273, BORI Critical Edition), Indrajit's war is compressed into two scenes, with no māyā-Sītā and no Nikumbhilā by name. [scholarly]
  - **Aṅgada's tree** - Aṅgada's thrown *śāla* trunk smashes Indrajit's chariot, horses and charioteer. Indrajit vanishes by māyā and fills Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa with boon-given arrows until they drop "like the sun and moon from the sky" and lie caged in a **net of arrows** (3.272.14–26; 3.273.1–2).
  - **The revival** - **Vibhīṣaṇa** wakes them with the ***prajñāstra***, and **Sugrīva** draws out the arrows with the herb ***viśalyā*** (3.273.5–6). A guhyaka then brings **water from Kubera**. Bathing their eyes with it lets Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa and the vānara chiefs **see invisible beings** (3.273.8–14). This is the epic's own answer to his invisibility.
  - **The death** - Indrajit returns, and Lakṣmaṇa, following Vibhīṣaṇa's advice, attacks him "**before he had performed his daily rite**" (*akṛtāhnikam*, 3.273.16–17), the Mahābhārata's form of the self-doom condition. With three arrows Lakṣmaṇa cuts off **his bow-arm, his other arm, and his head** (3.273.21–24). The horses bring the empty chariot back to Laṅkā. Rāvaṇa rushes at Sītā with a sword and is stopped by the counsellor **Avindhya** (3.273.25–30).
  - *Textual note:* lines in which "Paulastya" fells Lakṣmaṇa with a śakti and Hanumān fetches herbs (*1290), and Garuḍa's rescue (*1288–*1289), are star-passages the BORI editors left out of the constituted text. [interpolation]

## Wife and the Sulocanā tradition
- **No wife named in Vālmīki** - the *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa* does not name a consort for Indrajit at all. [scholarly]
- **Sulocanā — and where she actually comes from** - in the vernacular tradition his wife is **Sulocanā**, daughter of the serpent-king **Śeṣa-nāga** (hence the irony of her husband's killer being Lakṣmaṇa-as-Śeṣa): she claims his body from Rāma's camp, and burns with it. She is **not in the *Rāmcaritmānas*** either — Tulsīdās does not tell her story — and the attribution of the cycle to "Rāmcaritmānas-era texts" is loose; her home is the **Kṛttivāsī-adjacent Bengali** tradition and the **Marathi** and **Rāmlīlā** performance repertoires. The satī cycle, the writing hand, and the Pramīlā identification of Dutt's *Meghnādbadh Kābya* are set out in full at **[Sulocanā](./sulochana.md)**. [folk] [regional]

## Regional and modern variants
- *Variant (Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇa, Bengali):* the source of the **Mahīrāvaṇa / Ahirāvaṇa** Pātāla episode and an enlarged Sulocanā cycle — material absent from Vālmīki; alluded to (not narrated) in Tulsīdās's devotional hymns. [folk] [late text]
- *Variant (Meghnād Badh Kāvya, Michael Madhusudan Dutt, 1861):* a celebrated Bengali epic poem that **recasts Meghanāda as the tragic hero** killed by treachery (the un-warriorlike interruption of his worship), inverting the moral frame of the war. Dutt's charge against Vibhīṣaṇa amplifies the reproach Vālmīki already gives Indrajit at Nikumbhilā (*Yuddha* 87.10–16). [late text] [scholarly]
- *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa & other sectarian retellings):* foreground the device that Rāma/Lakṣmaṇa are divine and that the rākṣasas' weapons are powerless against them — see [Adhyātma & variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).
- **Open roster of son(s)/lineage** - genealogical/folk sources occasionally extend his line, but no canonical offspring is fixed; flagged as open-ended. [folk]

## Why he matters
- **The unbeaten warrior** - he is reckoned among the supreme warriors of Hindu epic. He is the son whom even Brahmā rates his father's equal or better (CE 7.30.3), the captor of Indra, and the single greatest threat Rāma's army faced. Vālmīki has him twice lay both princes low (*Yuddha* 45; 73). He is undone not by superior force but by his uncle's knowledge and by a rite left unfinished, under a condition he had set for himself (CE 7.30.12; *Yuddha* 85.14–15; 86.14).
- **The māyā-Sītā as narrative mirror** - his illusion of killing Sītā prefigures and inverts the war's central question of the "real" vs. "shadow" Sītā (the *Chāyā-Sītā* / *Māyā-Sītā* motif developed in the Adhyātma and Kūrma traditions). See [Adhyātma & variant Rāmāyaṇas](../ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md).
