# Dhṛtarāṣṭra — the Blind King of Hāstinapura

> Where this sits: a **Dvāpara-era life** at the Dvāpara–Kali junction of the current (28th) Mahāyuga — the sightless Kuru monarch whose indulgence of his hundred sons lets the [Kurukṣetra war](../mahabharata/README.md) happen. Up: [Epic Characters](README.md) · Parent epic: [Mahābhārata](../mahabharata/README.md). Back to the [master index](../../README.md).

**Dhṛtarāṣṭra** ("he who holds the kingdom") is the blind eldest son of the Kuru house — begotten by **[Vyāsa](../vyasa.md)** on Queen **[Ambikā](ambika-ambalika.md)** by *niyoga*, passed over for the throne because of his blindness, husband of **[Gāndhārī](gandhari.md)**, and father of the **hundred Kauravas** led by **[Duryodhana](duryodhana.md)**. He is the epic's great study in weakness of will: a king who *knows* dharma (he hears [Vidura](vidura.md)'s counsel all night and praises it), *sees* the catastrophe coming (he laments it verse after verse to [Sañjaya](sanjaya.md)), and yet at every hinge — the dice game, the second dice game, the refusal of peace — assents to his son. The whole *Mahābhārata* is framed as an answer to his question: the first verse of the **[Bhagavad-Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md)** is Dhṛtarāṣṭra asking Sañjaya what his sons and the Pāṇḍavas did on the field of dharma.

---

## I. Niyoga birth — the closed eyes of Ambikā (Mbh Ādi Parva)

- **The dying line of Śāntanu** — **Vicitravīrya**, husband of [Ambikā and Ambālikā](ambika-ambalika.md), dies childless of consumption; the Kuru succession fails, and the queen-mother **[Satyavatī](satyavati.md)** must find a way to continue the dynasty (Mbh Ādi Parva).
- **Bhīṣma refuses; Vyāsa is summoned** — Satyavatī first asks **[Bhīṣma](bhishma.md)** to beget heirs on the widows; bound by his vow of celibacy he refuses, and Satyavatī reveals her pre-marital son, the sage **[Vyāsa](../vyasa.md)**, calling him to perform **niyoga** (levirate begetting) on the two queens (Mbh Ādi Parva).
- **Ambikā shuts her eyes — the blind conception** — confronted with Vyāsa's grim ascetic appearance (matted hair, fierce eyes, smell of austerity), **Ambikā closes her eyes** throughout the union; Vyāsa pronounces that because of this her son will be **born blind** — and so Dhṛtarāṣṭra comes into the world sightless (Mbh Ādi Parva). His brothers by the same rite are the pale **Pāṇḍu** (from Ambālikā, who blanched) and the wise, flawless **[Vidura](vidura.md)** (from Ambikā's śūdra maid, who alone did not flinch) — see the full episode at [Ambikā & Ambālikā](ambika-ambalika.md).
- **A Gandharva's portion** — the Ādi Parva's roll of incarnations (Aṃśāvataraṇa) identifies the earthly Dhṛtarāṣṭra with a **Gandharva king of the same name** born among men (Mbh Ādi Parva, Aṃśāvataraṇa) — an identity that returns at his afterlife (see §VIII).

## II. Denied the throne — the eldest who could not be king (Mbh Ādi Parva)

- **Blindness bars the succession** — though the **eldest**, Dhṛtarāṣṭra is set aside from the throne of Hāstinapura on the ground that a blind man cannot rule and protect the kingdom; the younger **Pāṇḍu** is consecrated king instead, with Bhīṣma as guardian of the house (Mbh Ādi Parva). This original deprivation is the wound the epic keeps reopening — his sons inherit the grievance.
- **Marriage to Gāndhārī** — Bhīṣma arranges his marriage to **[Gāndhārī](gandhari.md)**, princess of Gandhāra, who on learning of his blindness **blindfolds herself for life**; her brother **[Śakuni](shakuni.md)** comes to live at the Kuru court (Mbh Ādi Parva).
- **Regent by default** — when Pāṇḍu, under Kindama's curse, renounces the throne for the forest (and later dies there), Dhṛtarāṣṭra takes up the rule of Hāstinapura — a king in fact but never quite in right, holding the realm in trust for heirs whose identity is precisely the coming quarrel (Mbh Ādi Parva).

## III. The hundred sons (Mbh Ādi Parva)

- **Vyāsa's boon and the flesh-ball** — Gāndhārī, granted the boon of **a hundred sons** by Vyāsa, carries her pregnancy two years and miscarries a ball of flesh; Vyāsa divides it into a hundred and one portions incubated in **ghee-jars**, yielding the hundred Kauravas and one daughter, **Duḥśalā** (Mbh Ādi Parva ~107). Full treatment at [Gāndhārī](gandhari.md).
- **Duryodhana's evil omens — the first fatal indulgence** — at [Duryodhana](duryodhana.md)'s birth jackals howl and asses bray; **Vidura** and the brahmins counsel the king to **abandon this one son** to save the family and the world; Dhṛtarāṣṭra, "out of affection for his son," cannot do it (Mbh Ādi Parva ~108). The pattern of his whole reign is set here.
- **Yuyutsu, the son of the vaiśya woman** — during Gāndhārī's long pregnancy Dhṛtarāṣṭra begets a son, **[Yuyutsu](yuyutsu.md)**, on a vaiśya serving-woman — the one son of his who will cross to the Pāṇḍava side and survive the war (Mbh Ādi Parva).
- **The righteous Kaurava** — among the hundred, **[Vikarṇa](vikarna.md)** alone will later speak for Draupadī in the gambling hall (Mbh Sabhā Parva).
- **The partition of the kingdom** — as the cousins' rivalry hardens (the lac-house plot at Vāraṇāvata, contrived by Duryodhana with the old king's uneasy connivance, having failed), Dhṛtarāṣṭra splits the realm: the Pāṇḍavas receive the wilderness of **Khāṇḍavaprastha**, which they raise into **Indraprastha** (Mbh Ādi Parva).
  - *Variant (the lac house):* the depth of Dhṛtarāṣṭra's own complicity at Vāraṇāvata is left ambiguous — the counsel of the minister **Kaṇika** urges ruthlessness on him, but the execution is Duryodhana's and Purocana's; tellings differ on how much the father knew and willed. [disputed]

## IV. The dice-game assents (Mbh Sabhā Parva)

- **The fatal permission** — Duryodhana, sick with envy after Yudhiṣṭhira's Rājasūya, demands a dice match with **Śakuni** as thrower; **Vidura** warns Dhṛtarāṣṭra plainly that the game will destroy the family; the king wavers, blames **fate (daiva)**, and **orders the hall built and the invitation sent** (Mbh Sabhā Parva). The epic marks this as *his* act — the assent only he could give.
- **Silence through the disrobing** — through the rigged game, the staking of kingdom, brothers, self, and **[Draupadī](draupadi.md)**, and her dragging into the hall, the old king sits silent while **[Vikarṇa](vikarna.md)** and Vidura protest (Mbh Sabhā Parva).
- **The boons that undo the game** — when evil omens (a jackal's howl in his agnihotra hall) terrify him, Dhṛtarāṣṭra grants **Draupadī boons**, freeing Yudhiṣṭhira and his brothers and restoring their arms and kingdom (Mbh Sabhā Parva).
- **The second assent — the exile throw** — almost immediately, at Duryodhana's pleading that the humiliated Pāṇḍavas will return for revenge, Dhṛtarāṣṭra **permits the second, single-throw game** with the thirteen-year exile stake — overriding Gāndhārī's and Vidura's protests, again invoking fate (Mbh Sabhā Parva). The war's clock starts here.
- **The sleepless night and Vidura-nīti** — years later, with Sañjaya returned from the peace embassy, the king cannot sleep and summons Vidura for counsel; the all-night discourse on statecraft and self-mastery is the **[Vidura-nīti](../vidura-niti.md)** (Mbh Udyoga Parva ~33–40). He praises every word — and follows none; when Vidura's advice touches giving the Pāṇḍavas their due, he answers that his heart is fastened to his son.

## V. Hearing the war — Sañjaya's divine eye and the viśvarūpa (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva)

- **Vyāsa's offer of sight refused** — on the eve of battle, Vyāsa offers Dhṛtarāṣṭra the **divine eye** to watch the war; the king refuses to see the slaughter of his kin, and Vyāsa confers the *divya-cakṣus* on **[Sañjaya](sanjaya.md)** instead, making the charioteer-minister the war's omniscient narrator to his blind master (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva).
- **"Dharma-kṣetre kuru-kṣetre…" — the framing question** — the **[Bhagavad-Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md)** opens with Dhṛtarāṣṭra's single verse, asking Sañjaya what his sons and Pāṇḍu's sons did when assembled on the field of dharma (Gītā 1.1; Mbh Bhīṣma Parva). The entire Gītā is Sañjaya's report **to Dhṛtarāṣṭra**.
- **The viśvarūpa at second hand** — when Kṛṣṇa reveals the **cosmic form** to Arjuna, Sañjaya beholds it by his divine eye and describes it to the blind king — Dhṛtarāṣṭra thus "hears" the theophany he cannot see (Gītā 11; Mbh Bhīṣma Parva); Sañjaya closes the Gītā telling him that wherever Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna are, there are fortune and victory (Gītā 18.78) — a verdict against the king's own side.
- **The long lament** — each parva of the war opens with Sañjaya announcing a fallen commander (Bhīṣma, Droṇa, Karṇa, Śalya, Duryodhana) and Dhṛtarāṣṭra collapsing into questions — "what did my people do?" — the refrain that structures Books VI–IX (Mbh Bhīṣma–Śalya Parvas).

## VI. The iron Bhīma — the embrace that crushed a statue (Mbh Strī Parva)

- **The grief-wrath of the father** — after the eighteenth day, the bereaved king meets the victorious Pāṇḍavas; he asks to **embrace [Bhīma](bhima.md)**, the slayer of all hundred of his sons (Mbh Strī Parva).
- **Kṛṣṇa's substitution** — reading the strength of the old man's rage, **Kṛṣṇa** pushes forward the **iron statue of Bhīma** (the effigy on which Duryodhana had practiced his mace-blows); Dhṛtarāṣṭra crushes it to pieces in his arms, vomits blood, and then, grief spent, weeps that his anger is gone and accepts the real Bhīma and the Pāṇḍavas (Mbh Strī Parva ~11–12). The episode is the epic's emblem of his blind strength — the text says he had the might of a hundred thousand elephants, and nothing to aim it at.
- **On the field with Gāndhārī** — he then goes out with [Gāndhārī](gandhari.md), [Kuntī](kunti.md) and the women to the corpse-field for the laments and rites of the [Strī Parva](../mahabharata/11-stri-parva.md) (Mbh XI).

## VII. Fifteen years under Yudhiṣṭhira, forest retirement, death by fire (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva)

- **The honored dependent** — for about **fifteen years** after the war, **[Yudhiṣṭhira](yudhishthira.md)** keeps the old king in royal honor, commanding that Dhṛtarāṣṭra be obeyed as before; but **Bhīma's taunts** — boasts within the old man's hearing about the arms that slew his sons — poison the arrangement (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva).
- **The going-forth (vānaprastha)** — with Vyāsa's sanction and against Yudhiṣṭhira's tearful protest, Dhṛtarāṣṭra takes leave, performs the śrāddha rites and gives gifts for the war-dead, and retires to a **Gaṅgā-side forest hermitage** with Gāndhārī, **Kuntī**, **[Vidura](vidura.md)** and **[Sañjaya](sanjaya.md)**, living as a matted-haired ascetic (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva; see [15 — Āśramavāsika Parva](../mahabharata/15-ashramavasika-parva.md)).
- **Vidura's departure** — when the Pāṇḍavas visit, the emaciated Vidura, in his final austerity, **enters Yudhiṣṭhira body and spirit** (both being Dharma's), dying standing against a tree (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva).
- **The dead raised for a night** — at the mothers' grief, **Vyāsa** calls the slain warriors up from the **Gaṅgā** for one night of reunion before they return to the waters (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva).
- **Death in the forest fire** — some two to three years into the retreat, a **forest conflagration** sweeps toward the hermitage; the aged king — enfeebled by fasts, unable and unwilling to flee — sits facing east in yogic composure with Gāndhārī and Kuntī, and the three are **consumed in the flames**; Sañjaya escapes at the king's own bidding and departs for the Himālaya; **Nārada** brings the news to Yudhiṣṭhira (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva ~37–39).
  - *Variant (the fire's origin):* the blaze is variously the untended **agnihotra fire** of the ascetics' own rites spreading through the dry forest, or an ordinary wildfire; Nārada consoles Yudhiṣṭhira that a king burned by his own sacred fire has not died an unclean death. [catalog both] (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva)

## VIII. Afterlife and frame (Mbh Svargārohaṇa Parva; character notes)

- **Return to the Gandharva throne** — in Yudhiṣṭhira's tour of the hereafter, Dhṛtarāṣṭra is seen restored to glory as a **king among the Gandharvas**, the station from which the Aṃśāvataraṇa said he came (Mbh Svargārohaṇa Parva).
- **Blindness as the epic's master-metaphor** — tradition and commentary alike read his sightlessness doubly: the physical blindness that cost him the throne, and the **moral blindness** (*putra-sneha*, doting on his son) that cost the world; his refrain "my son is wrong, but what can I do?" makes him the counter-image of a dharmic king. [scholarly]
- **Cross-links** — his mother [Ambikā](ambika-ambalika.md); wife [Gāndhārī](gandhari.md); brother-counselor [Vidura](vidura.md) and the [Vidura-nīti](../vidura-niti.md); narrator [Sañjaya](sanjaya.md); sons [Duryodhana](duryodhana.md), [Duḥśāsana](dushasana.md), [Vikarṇa](vikarna.md), [Yuyutsu](yuyutsu.md); begetter [Vyāsa](../vyasa.md); the war he heard at [Bhīṣma Parva](../mahabharata/06-bhishma-parva.md) and the retreat at [Āśramavāsika Parva](../mahabharata/15-ashramavasika-parva.md).

## Two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic:** Dhṛtarāṣṭra's life spans the end of **Dvāpara Yuga** in the current (28th) Mahāyuga; by the traditional count the Kurukṣetra war falls ~3138 BCE and he dies in the forest fire roughly eighteen years later — still before **Kali Yuga's onset (3102 BCE)** at Kṛṣṇa's departure.
- **Academic / historical:** the *Mahābhārata* in which he figures was composed and redacted over centuries (c. 400 BCE–400 CE); historians treat Dhṛtarāṣṭra as an epic-legendary figure of the Kuru saga rather than a datable monarch, while noting a *Dhṛtarāṣṭra Vaicitravīrya* named in the Kāṭhaka Saṃhitā of the Yajurveda as a possible old-Kuru memory behind the epic character. [scholarly] [disputed]
