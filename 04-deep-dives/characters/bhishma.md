# Bhīṣma

> Where this sits: a **Dvāpara-era life of extraordinary length**, spanning four generations of the Kuru house — from the reign of [Śāntanu](../mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md) to the [Kurukṣetra War](../mahabharata/06-bhishma-parva.md) and the dharma-teachings that follow it. Bhīṣma belongs to the [Mahābhārata deep-dives](../mahabharata/) and to the [28th Mahāyuga, Dvāpara Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md), at the very junction with the [Kali Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md).
> Up: [characters](./README.md) · [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md) · His war-book: [06 — Bhīṣma Parva](../mahabharata/06-bhishma-parva.md) · His teaching-books: [12 — Śānti Parva](../mahabharata/12-shanti-parva.md), [13 — Anuśāsana Parva](../mahabharata/13-anushasana-parva.md)

**Bhīṣma** (Sanskrit *Bhīṣma*, "the terrible / the one of the dreadful vow"), born **Devavrata** ("vowed to the gods"), is the **grand-uncle, regent, patriarch, and conscience** of the [Mahābhārata](../mahabharata/). He is the eighth son of the river-goddess **Gaṅgā** and King **Śāntanu** of the lunar (Candravaṃśa) line; the incarnation of the Vasu **Dyaus/Prabhāsa**; the taker of the **terrible vow** of lifelong celibacy and throne-renunciation; the holder of **icchā-mṛtyu** (death only at his own will); the abductor of the Kāśi princesses whose act dooms him through **Ambā/Śikhaṇḍin**; the **first commander-in-chief** of the Kaurava host, falling on the *śaraśayyā* ("bed of arrows") on the tenth day; and, dying, the **mouthpiece of the epic's longest discourse on dharma**. His epithets — **Gāṅgeya / Gaṅgāputra** (son of Gaṅgā), **Śāṃtanava** (son of Śāntanu), **Devavrata**, **Pitāmaha** ("grandfather"), **Tālaketu** (palm-banner) — track the stages of that life.

---

## I. Divine origin — the Vasu and the curse (Mbh Ādi Parva ~91–100)

- **One of the eight Vasus** - Bhīṣma is the mortal incarnation of a **Vasu**, the elemental attendant-deities of the sky. He is the chief offender among the eight, and so bears the longest sentence on earth (Mbh Ādi Parva ~99).
  - *Variant (naming the Vasu):* the offending Vasu is named **Dyaus** (the Sky) in most tellings; he is also called **Prabhāsa** in the Mahābhārata's own account. The two names are used interchangeably for the same Vasu who becomes Bhīṣma.
- **The theft of Nandinī** - The Vasus, wandering, come upon the hermitage of sage **Vasiṣṭha**; at the urging of Dyaus's wife (who covets the wish-cow for a mortal friend), the Vasus steal **Nandinī**, daughter of the cow **Kāmadhenu / Surabhi** (Mbh Ādi Parva ~99). [The instigating wife is a recurring motive — the act is for *her*, not for the Vasus.]
- **Vasiṣṭha's curse** - Knowing by his tapas who took the cow, Vasiṣṭha curses all eight Vasus to be **born as mortals**. Pleaded with, he relents: **seven will be freed within a year of birth**, but **Dyaus**, the actual thief, must **live a full long human life** — childless, celibate, and renowned (Mbh Ādi Parva ~99). This curse is the seed of every later sorrow.
- **The Vasus seek Gaṅgā as mother** - To minimize the suffering, the Vasus ask the river-goddess **Gaṅgā** to bear them and release them at once. She agrees (Mbh Ādi Parva ~99).

Cross-links: on the Vasus as a class see [02 — deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/); on the lunar dynasty see [03 — lineages-vaṃśa](../../03-lineages-vamsha/).

## II. Birth: Gaṅgā and Śāntanu (Mbh Ādi Parva ~96–100)

- **Śāntanu meets Gaṅgā** - King **Śāntanu** of Hastināpura (a Kuru, descendant of Bharata) falls in love with a radiant woman on the riverbank; she consents to marry **only on the condition that he never question any deed of hers**, on pain of her departure (Mbh Ādi Parva ~97).
  - *Variant (a prior betrothal):* some retellings recall that Śāntanu's ancestor **Mahābhiṣa** was cursed to be reborn as Śāntanu, and Gaṅgā took mortal form to fulfill the Vasus' request and an old attraction — framing the marriage as predestined on both sides.
- **The seven drowned sons** - Gaṅgā bears seven sons in turn and **drowns each newborn in her own waters**, freeing the seven lesser Vasus from the curse; Śāntanu, bound by his promise, stays silent and grieving (Mbh Ādi Parva ~98–99).
- **The eighth son spared** - When the **eighth** child is born, Śāntanu can bear it no longer and **stops her**; Gaṅgā, her vow broken, reveals her identity and the Vasus' story, and **departs, taking the infant** to raise (Mbh Ādi Parva ~99–100). This eighth child is **Devavrata** — the Vasu Dyaus, who must live out the full human term.
- **Celestial fosterage and education** - Gaṅgā rears Devavrata in heavenly realms; he is schooled by the greatest teachers — **Bṛhaspati** and **Śukra** in statecraft (*daṇḍanīti*), **Vasiṣṭha** (and **Cyavana**) in the Vedas, **Paraśurāma** in arms, and **Indra/the gods** in celestial weapons (Mbh Ādi Parva ~100). He is thus a master of both *śāstra* and *astra* before he ever sits a throne.
- **Restored to his father** - Years later Gaṅgā returns the grown prince to Śāntanu, who recognizes his lost son and names him crown prince (yuvarāja) (Mbh Ādi Parva ~100).

## III. The terrible vow (Bhīṣma-pratijñā) and the boon of icchā-mṛtyu (Mbh Ādi Parva ~100)

- **Śāntanu's second love — Satyavatī** - Śāntanu falls for **Satyavatī** (Kālī / Matsyagandhā / Yojanagandhā), the foster-daughter of the chief of fishermen, **Dāśarāja**. The father will give her **only if her son inherits the throne** — impossible while Devavrata is heir (Mbh Ādi Parva ~100). Śāntanu, unwilling to disinherit his son, pines in silence.
- **The renunciation of the throne** - Learning his father's grief, Devavrata goes to Dāśarāja and **renounces the throne** so that **Satyavatī's son shall rule** (Mbh Ādi Parva ~100).
- **The terrible vow of celibacy** - Dāśarāja objects that Devavrata's own future sons could still contest the crown. To remove all doubt, Devavrata takes the **dreadful oath of lifelong celibacy (brahmacarya)** — he will **never marry, never father children, never rule** — so that Satyavatī's line is forever secure (Mbh Ādi Parva ~100).
- **The name "Bhīṣma"** - At this **terrible (*bhīṣma*)** vow the gods rain flowers and proclaim him **Bhīṣma** — "he of the dreadful vow." The name supplants Devavrata for the rest of the epic (Mbh Ādi Parva ~100).
- **The boon of icchā-mṛtyu** - Moved by the magnitude of the sacrifice, his father grants the boon that **Bhīṣma shall die only when he himself wills it** — *icchā-mṛtyu*, death-at-will; no force shall take his life against his choosing (Mbh Ādi Parva ~100).
  - *Variant (who grants it):* the Mahābhārata most directly attributes the boon to **Śāntanu's** pleased blessing; many retellings broaden the grantor to **the gods** who showered flowers, since a mortal father granting deathlessness reads as divine assent ratifying the paternal word. The substance — Bhīṣma cannot be killed until he consents — is constant.

This single vow is the **hinge of the whole epic**: it deprives the Kuru throne of Bhīṣma's own heirs, forces the levirate that produces Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Pāṇḍu, and binds Bhīṣma by *loyalty to the throne* to the wrong side of the war.

## IV. Regent and grand-patriarch of Hastināpura (Mbh Ādi Parva ~101–110)

- **The death of Vicitravīrya's line begins** - Śāntanu and Satyavatī have two sons, **Citrāṅgada** (killed by a gandharva of the same name) and **Vicitravīrya**. Bhīṣma, true to his vow, **rules as regent** rather than king through these minorities (Mbh Ādi Parva ~101–102).
- **The Kāśi abduction (see §V)** - Bhīṣma wins brides for Vicitravīrya by force at the Kāśi svayaṃvara — the act that will return to destroy him.
- **The niyoga that founds both houses** - When Vicitravīrya dies childless, Satyavatī first asks **Bhīṣma himself** to take the widows or the throne; he **refuses, citing the vow** — twice immovable. Satyavatī then summons her pre-marital son, the sage **Vyāsa**, who by **niyoga** (levirate) fathers **Dhṛtarāṣṭra** (on Ambikā), **Pāṇḍu** (on Ambālikā), and **Vidura** (on a maid) (Mbh Ādi Parva ~104–106). Every Kaurava and Pāṇḍava thus descends from a chain set in motion by Bhīṣma's celibacy.
- **Guardian of three generations** - Bhīṣma raises and arranges the marriages of Dhṛtarāṣṭra (to **Gāndhārī**), Pāṇḍu (to **Kuntī** and **Mādrī**), and Vidura; then fosters the **Kauravas and Pāṇḍavas** alike as **Pitāmaha** ("grandfather"), the supreme elder and moral authority of the court (Mbh Ādi Parva ~109–110). He is present, and largely silent, through the dice-game crisis — a silence the epic does not let him forget.

Cross-links: the founding niyoga is detailed in [01 — Ādi Parva](../mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md); the dice-game in [02 — Sabhā Parva](../mahabharata/02-sabha-parva.md).

## V. Ambā, Ambikā, Ambālikā — and the seed of his fall (Mbh Ādi Parva ~102; Udyoga Parva ~170–193)

- **The abduction at Kāśi** - To secure wives for **Vicitravīrya**, Bhīṣma storms the **svayaṃvara of the three princesses of Kāśi** — **Ambā, Ambikā, Ambālikā** — and carries all three off by force, defeating the assembled kings (Mbh Ādi Parva ~102). He fights and overcomes **Śālva**, king of Saubha, in the pursuit.
- **Ambā's plea and rejection** - **Ambā**, the eldest, reveals she had already **chosen Śālva** in her heart. Bhīṣma, honoring dharma, **sends her back to Śālva** — but Śālva, humiliated at having been beaten before her, **refuses her**. Vicitravīrya too will not wed a woman pledged to another. Ambā is left without husband or home (Mbh Udyoga Parva ~173–178).
- **Ambā blames Bhīṣma** - With nowhere to turn, Ambā holds **Bhīṣma** responsible for ruining her life and demands he either marry her or fight to make amends; the vow forbids both (Mbh Udyoga Parva ~178).
- **Paraśurāma vs. Bhīṣma** - Ambā appeals to **Paraśurāma**, Bhīṣma's own martial guru, who orders Bhīṣma to take her. Bhīṣma refuses to break his vow even for his teacher; the two fight a colossal **multi-day duel at Kurukṣetra** that ends **inconclusively** when the gods and **Nārada** intervene (Mbh Udyoga Parva ~179–186).
  - *Variant (duration):* the duel is most often given as **twenty-three days**, with intervention on the **twenty-fourth**; other recensions abbreviate the count. The outcome — a stalemate neither can win — is constant.
- **Ambā's tapas and Śiva's boon** - Spurned by all, Ambā performs ferocious austerities. **Śiva** appears and promises she shall be **reborn as the cause of Bhīṣma's death**, and shall **remember her past life** (Mbh Udyoga Parva ~187–188). She immolates herself to hasten the rebirth.
- **Rebirth as Śikhaṇḍin** - Ambā is reborn as **Śikhaṇḍin(ī)**, child of King **Drupada** of Pāñcāla (Mbh Udyoga Parva ~188–193).
  - *Variant (sex-transformation):* born female and raised as a son, Śikhaṇḍin obtains **manhood by exchanging sex with a yakṣa named Sthūṇa(karṇa)**, who lends his maleness (temporarily or permanently depending on the telling) so the curse can find its target. Bhīṣma, knowing Śikhaṇḍin was "born a woman," vows **never to fight him** — the precise crack through which his death enters.
  - *Variant [folk]:* regional and theatrical retellings vary whether Śikhaṇḍin is fully male in war, a transgender warrior, or merely "once a woman"; the load-bearing fact is only that **Bhīṣma will not raise weapons against** the reborn Ambā.

Cross-links: Śikhaṇḍin's birth and the yakṣa exchange are told within [05 — Udyoga Parva](../mahabharata/05-udyoga-parva.md).

## VI. Commander of the Kaurava host — the ten days (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~6–119)

- **Appointed senāpati** - At the war's opening Duryodhana names **Bhīṣma supreme commander (senāpati)** of the eleven-akṣauhiṇī Kaurava army; Bhīṣma accepts on conditions, chiefly that **he will not kill the Pāṇḍavas** (whom he loves) and will fight ferociously but not to their personal destruction (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~16).
- **The Karṇa condition** - Bhīṣma declares he **will not fight while Karṇa is in the field** (a long enmity over Karṇa's *sūta* status and rivalry), so **[Karṇa](../karna.md) sits out the first ten days** — a vow that costs Duryodhana his deadliest archer for the whole of Bhīṣma's command (Mbh Udyoga/Bhīṣma Parva).
- **Days of carnage** - For ten days Bhīṣma is **invincible**, slaying thousands daily and holding the Pāṇḍava host at bay; the epic gives him tens of thousands of foot-soldiers and a thousand chariot-warriors per day (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva). [The exact tallies are a rhetorical roster, not a fixed count.]
- **Kṛṣṇa's broken vow** - Enraged at Bhīṣma's slaughter on the **ninth day**, **Kṛṣṇa** abandons his vow not to take up arms and rushes at Bhīṣma with a **chariot-wheel** (or whip-handle) as a weapon; Bhīṣma joyfully welcomes death at the Lord's hand, and **Arjuna restrains Kṛṣṇa** (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~59). The moment proves Bhīṣma cannot be beaten by ordinary means while he wills to live.
- **The Pāṇḍavas ask Bhīṣma how to kill him** - Stalemated, the Pāṇḍavas come **at night to Bhīṣma himself** and ask the secret of his death; bound by truth, **Bhīṣma reveals it**: he will lay down his weapons before **Śikhaṇḍin**, "one born a woman," and can then be struck (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~119). The patriarch effectively **consents to his own fall**.

Cross-links: the full battle-narrative is [06 — Bhīṣma Parva](../mahabharata/06-bhishma-parva.md); the night-counsel and Karṇa's sidelining sit at the Udyoga/Bhīṣma seam.

## VII. The fall on the bed of arrows (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~119–120)

- **Śikhaṇḍin to the fore** - On the **tenth day**, **Arjuna** advances with **Śikhaṇḍin** before his chariot. Seeing Ambā-reborn, **Bhīṣma lowers his weapons**, refusing to fight one "born a woman" (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~119).
- **Arjuna's volley** - Shielded behind Śikhaṇḍin, **Arjuna pierces Bhīṣma's whole body** with arrows until the old warrior topples — but is held **off the ground**, suspended on the very shafts that struck him: the **śaraśayyā**, the "bed of arrows" (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~119–120).
- **The pillow of arrows** - When Bhīṣma's head hangs unsupported and he asks for a pillow, the kings offer soft cushions; he refuses them as unfit for a warrior's bed. **Arjuna** drives **three arrows into the earth** beneath his head to prop it — the true soldier's pillow Bhīṣma accepts (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~120).
- **The boon takes hold** - Because of **icchā-mṛtyu**, Bhīṣma **does not die**: he chooses to **wait on the bed of arrows** for the auspicious **uttarāyaṇa** (northward course of the sun), refusing to depart while the sun runs its southern course (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~120). He thus lies dying — but living — through the remaining eight days of the war.
- **The water of Arjuna's arrow** - Thirsty, Bhīṣma refuses ordinary water; **Arjuna shoots a shaft into the earth and a jet of pure (Gaṅgā / pātāla) water springs up** to his lips — a final tribute from grandson to grandsire (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva ~120). [folk emphasis varies on whether the water is Gaṅgā herself rising for her son.]

## VIII. The teachings from the bed of arrows (Mbh Śānti & Anuśāsana Parvas)

- **The long wait as a teaching couch** - After the war, the grieving, guilt-ridden **Yudhiṣṭhira** comes to the dying Bhīṣma; at **Kṛṣṇa's** bidding, Bhīṣma — though pierced and dying — delivers the **single longest body of dharma-instruction in the epic** (Mbh Śānti Parva onward).
- **Śānti Parva — three great sections** - Bhīṣma expounds **(1) Rāja-dharma** (the duties of a king, statecraft, danger and emergency-ethics *āpad-dharma*), **(2) the dharma of liberation and renunciation (mokṣa-dharma)**, with extensive Sāṃkhya-Yoga and devotional teaching (Mbh [12 — Śānti Parva](../mahabharata/12-shanti-parva.md)). It is the epic's encyclopedic political-philosophical core.
- **Anuśāsana Parva — instruction continued** - The teaching flows on into dāna (charity), gift-merit, the duties of varṇa and āśrama, the power of brāhmaṇas, women's dharma, vows, and pilgrimage, given through parables and ancient legends (Mbh [13 — Anuśāsana Parva](../mahabharata/13-anushasana-parva.md)).
- **The Viṣṇu-sahasranāma** - Within this discourse Bhīṣma recites to Yudhiṣṭhira the **Viṣṇu Sahasranāma**, the "Thousand Names of Viṣṇu," in answer to "Who is the one supreme refuge?" — one of the most recited Vaiṣṇava texts, embedded in the Anuśāsana Parva (Mbh Anuśāsana Parva ~135 in the vulgate; placement varies by recension).
- **Why Bhīṣma may teach** - The frame makes the point that a man who has spent his life upholding dharma, even while bound to the losing side, earns the right to **transmit dharma at his death** — Bhīṣma the silent witness of Draupadī's humiliation becomes Bhīṣma the great teacher of righteousness.

## IX. Death at uttarāyaṇa (Mbh Anuśāsana Parva ~167–168)

- **Waiting for the northward sun** - Having held his life by **icchā-mṛtyu** through the war and the long teaching, Bhīṣma at last departs when the sun turns north (**uttarāyaṇa**), the gateway of the gods that leads the soul to light and release (Mbh Anuśāsana Parva ~167).
- **Conscious release of the breaths** - Surrounded by the Pāṇḍavas, Vyāsa, Kṛṣṇa and the sages, Bhīṣma **withdraws his life-breaths (prāṇas) in stages by yogic will**, the arrows falling from his body, and his spirit **rising to the heavens** amid celestial portents (Mbh Anuśāsana Parva ~167–168).
- **The date — Bhīṣma Aṣṭamī** - Tradition fixes his death on **Māgha śukla aṣṭamī** (the eighth lunar day of the bright fortnight of Māgha, Jan–Feb), observed as **Bhīṣma Aṣṭamī**; the preceding **Bhīṣma Ekādaśī** and the death-day carry **tarpaṇa / Ekoddiṣṭa-śrāddha** offered for the sonless patriarch by all who honor him (Mbh tradition; observed pan-India).
  - *Variant (number of days on the bed):* counting from his fall on the **tenth day** of an eighteen-day war to uttarāyaṇa, traditions reckon his time on the arrow-bed as **fifty-eight nights** (a widely cited number, e.g., 58 nights) — a figure derived from later harmonization of the lunar calendar rather than stated as such in the core text [disputed].
- **Why the chosen day matters** - Dying in uttarāyaṇa, by his own will, fully conscious, having discharged his teaching — Bhīṣma achieves the **ideal warrior's death**: not slain against his will, but releasing himself in the auspicious season toward the path of the gods. The boon of icchā-mṛtyu, given for his vow, finds its perfect use at the very end.

## X. Character, meaning, and open questions

- **The tragedy of the binding vow** - Bhīṣma is the epic's great study in a **vow that outlives its purpose**: his celibacy secures one succession but unravels the dynasty, and his **loyalty to "the throne of Hastināpura"** (not to dharma or to persons) chains him to Duryodhana's unjust war. His greatness and his guilt are the same fidelity.
- **The silence in the sabhā** - The Mahābhārata never fully exonerates Bhīṣma's **silence at Draupadī's disrobing**; his own teachings on the limits of obedience to an unjust master read, in retrospect, as a meditation on that failure.
- **Two clocks** - *Scriptural*: Bhīṣma's life sits at the **close of Dvāpara Yuga**, the war marking the threshold of [Kali Yuga (3102 BCE)](../../00-time-and-cosmology/). *Academic [scholarly]*: the Bhīṣma material is layered across the long redaction of the Mahābhārata (roughly **4th c. BCE – 4th c. CE**), with the **Śānti/Anuśāsana didactic books and the Viṣṇu-sahasranāma widely held to be later strata** [late text] grafted onto the older battle-narrative.
- **Open-ended rosters** - The exact daily kill-tallies of his ten days, the precise day-count of the Paraśurāma duel, and the number of nights on the bed of arrows are **traditional/rhetorical figures** that vary by recension — flagged here rather than fixed.
