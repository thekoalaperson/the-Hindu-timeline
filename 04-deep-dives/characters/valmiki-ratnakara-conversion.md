# Vālmīki and the Robber's Conversion — Ratnākara, the Marā-Marā Mantra, and the Anthill

> The conversion legend of the **ādi-kavi** ("first poet"): how a highway robber — popularly named **Ratnākara** — whose own family refused to share his sins was turned by a chance meeting with sages into the seer who composed the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md). Sits in **[Tretā-yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** of the current [28th Mahāyuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md), *before* the composition of the epic — though the legend itself is recorded only in **late, post-epic texts** [late text].
> Up: [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Master index: [../../README.md](../../README.md) · The poet's own frame-story: [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md) · His epic afterlife: [Uttara Rāmāyaṇa / Lava-Kuśa](../uttara-ramayana-lava-kusha.md).

**Vālmīki** (traditionally derived from *valmīka*, "anthill") is the *ādi-kavi*, author of the *Rāmāyaṇa* and — in his own poem — a contemporary of [Rāma](../ramayana/README.md) who shelters the banished [Sītā](./sita.md) and teaches the epic to her sons. Around this austere figure the later tradition wrapped one of Hinduism's most beloved redemption stories: before he was a sage he was a **brigand of the forest roads**; his **wife and children refused to share the burden of his sins**; too impure to pronounce the name *Rāma*, he was set to repeating it **backwards as *marā*** ("dead"); and he sat in japa so long that an **anthill (valmīka) swallowed him**, from which he emerged reborn as Vālmīki. The story is a jewel of *nāma-māhātmya* (glory-of-the-Name) theology; it is also **absent from Vālmīki's own Rāmāyaṇa** and attested only in medieval texts. This file catalogs the versions — which disagree with each other far more than popular retellings admit — keeping the two clocks distinct.

## I. Vālmīki in his own Rāmāyaṇa — no robber anywhere (the base text)

- **Prācetasa, son of Pracetas** — In his own epic Vālmīki identifies himself by the patronymic **Prācetasa** ("son of Pracetas"); tradition therefore counts him a brāhmaṇa of high descent. Nothing in the poem hints at a criminal past. (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa)
- **Nārada plants the seed** — The epic opens with Vālmīki asking **[Nārada](../narada.md)**: *who in this world is the perfectly virtuous man?* Nārada answers "Rāma of the Ikṣvākus" and recites the compressed *saṃkṣipta-Rāmāyaṇa* — the germ of the whole poem. (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa 1) → full treatment in [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md) and [Nārada](../narada.md).
- **The krauñca bird and the first śloka** — At the **Tamasā river** Vālmīki sees a hunter shoot one of a pair of mating **krauñca** (curlew) birds; his grief (*śoka*) bursts out as metrical verse (*śloka*) — the birth of the anuṣṭubh meter and of poetry itself. (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa 2) → overview in [smṛti — itihāsa & epics](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md).
- **Brahmā's commission** — Brahmā appears, reveals that the verse arose by his will, and commands Vālmīki to compose Rāma's whole life in the new meter, granting him yogic insight into every event. (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa 2)
- **Guardian of Sītā, teacher of Lava-Kuśa** — In the epilogue Vālmīki shelters the banished [Sītā](./sita.md) in his āśrama, raises her twins **Lava and Kuśa**, teaches them the completed poem, has them sing it at Rāma's Aśvamedha, and **stakes his own tapas on an oath of Sītā's purity**. (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa) → [Uttara Kāṇḍa summary](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md) · [Uttara Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive](../uttara-ramayana-lava-kusha.md).
- **The oath that contradicts the legend** — In that scene Vālmīki declares (vulgate Uttara Kāṇḍa, sarga 96; numbering varies by recension) that he is **Pracetas' tenth son**, that he **does not remember ever uttering a falsehood**, and that his thousands of years of tapas have been **without sin in thought, word, or deed**. The epic's own self-portrait thus leaves **no room for a robber past** — a point urged against the conversion legend by traditional defenders of the sage and text-critical scholars alike. (Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa 96) [scholarly]

## II. Conversion legend, version 1 — the hunter and the brāhmaṇa Śaṅkha (Skanda Purāṇa) [late text]

The **Skanda Purāṇa's** telling sits inside the **Vaiśākha-māsa-māhātmya** of the [Vaiṣṇava-khaṇḍa](../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana-khanda-map.md), chapters 17–21 — framed as **Nārada's teaching to King Ambarīṣa** (cf. [Ambarīṣa](../ambarisha-durvasa.md)), the inner tale told by the brāhmaṇa **Śrutadeva** to a king. Being a month-māhātmya, its engine is **Vaiśākha merit**, not the inverted mantra.

- **The robbery on the road** — A fierce **hunter-robber** waylays the brāhmaṇa **Śaṅkha** as he travels in the burning month of Vaiśākha, stripping him of earrings, umbrella, rosary, water-pot, and **sandals**. (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiṣṇava-khaṇḍa, Vaiśākhamāsa-māhātmya 17)
- **The sandals returned — the first stirring** — Seeing the barefoot brāhmaṇa hopping in agony on the scorched ground, the robber is **moved to pity and gives the sandals back** — a small act of Vaiśākha-season kindness that becomes the hinge of his redemption. Śaṅkha, pleased, expounds the greatness of Vaiśākha. (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiśākhamāsa-māhātmya 17)
- **The lion and the elephant** — As Śaṅkha speaks, a **lion and an elephant** who overhear him are instantly freed from their forms: they are **Dantila and Kohala**, sons of the sage Mataṅga, cursed for scorning their father's teaching and released by hearing this very discourse. Witnessing the miracle, the robber **throws down his weapons** and begs the sage for guidance. (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiśākhamāsa-māhātmya 17)
- **The previous birth revealed** — Śaṅkha discloses the hunter's earlier life: he was **Stamba**, a brāhmaṇa of Śākala who abandoned the Veda for a courtesan; his chaste wife **Kāntimatī** served him faithfully and, when the sage **Devala** visited, offered him a sweet drink **in the month of Vaiśākha** — merit performed with her husband's leave. Dying with his mind on the courtesan, Stamba was reborn a hunter, yet the residue of that shared Vaiśākha merit steered him to this meeting. (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiśākhamāsa-māhātmya 18)
- **The two-syllabled Name — given forwards, not backwards** — After further instruction (the wind-god's curse, the Bhāgavata dharmas, chs. 19–20), Śaṅkha imparts to the hunter **the two-syllabled name "Rāma,"** "more splendid than and superior to the Devas," telling him to **repeat it continuously and keep the Vaiśākha rites till death**. In this version there is **no marā-marā inversion**: the robber receives the Name directly. (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiśākhamāsa-māhātmya 21)
- **A pious death and rebirth as Vālmīki** — The converted hunter plants trees, builds a water-booth, feeds travelers, and repeats *Rāma* day and night until death. He is then **reborn as the son of the sage Kṛṇu**, an ascetic so long motionless in penance that **an anthill covered his body**, whence he was called **Valmīka**; the child born in that hermitage (of Kṛṇu's seed, received by a celestial dancer) becomes famed as **Vālmīki**, "who made the story of Rāma popular in the world by means of his charming verses." Note the inversion of the familiar image: here the **anthill belongs to the father**, "Vālmīki" is a patronymic, and the robber's transformation runs **through an actual death and rebirth**, not through japa inside the mound. (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiśākhamāsa-māhātmya 21)
- **Chapter frame** — The same chapter carries the parallel redemption of the serpent **Rocana**, a proud brāhmaṇa of Prayāga cursed for disrupting a Vaiśākha discourse and freed by hearing Śaṅkha's tale — in this text the saving power is **Vaiśākha and the heard word**, with the Rāma-nāma as its crown. (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiśākhamāsa-māhātmya 21)

## III. Conversion legend, version 2 — the first-person confession (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa 2.6) [late text]

The version behind the popular telling — family refusal, marā-marā, the anthill over the sinner himself — is given in the **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** (Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa, sarga 6), where **Vālmīki narrates his own past to Rāma** when the exiles reach his āśrama and ask where they should dwell (the answer: **Citrakūṭa** — cf. [Chitrakoot](../../06-sacred-geography/chitrakoot-kamadgiri.md)). → recension profile: [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/recensions/adhyatma-ramayana.md).

- **A brāhmaṇa fallen among Kirātas** — Vālmīki confesses that though **born a brāhmaṇa**, he grew up in the company of **Kirātas** (forest hunters), took a **śūdra wife**, fathered children, and lived by **robbing and binding travelers** on the forest roads, killing without scruple. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa 6)
- **The seven sages waylaid** — One day he fell upon the **seven sages (Saptarṣi)** in the forest. Unafraid, they asked him *why* he sinned; he answered: to feed his family. They posed the fatal question: *will your wife and children, who eat what you bring, also **share your sins**? Go home and ask them.* (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa 6)
- **The family's refusal** — Trusting the sages to stay (they waited, bound by their word), the robber asked each member of his household. All answered alike: **the sin is the doer's alone** — it was his duty to feed them, by whatever means he chose; they would share his earnings, **not his guilt**. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa 6)
- **Collapse and surrender** — Shattered by the discovery that he had damned himself alone for people who would not carry one grain of it, he ran back, fell at the sages' feet, and begged for rescue. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa 6)
- **The inverted mantra** — Judging him too sin-laden to receive (or pronounce) the Name directly, the sages set him to repeating its syllables **in reverse: "marā, marā"** — which, said continuously, becomes *rāma rāma* on the tongue. The theological point is the *nāma-māhātmya* claim itself: **the Name purifies even uttered backwards, even without understanding**. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa 6)
- **The anthill and the second birth** — He sat in japa, motionless, for so long (the text reckons **thousands of years**) that an **anthill (valmīka) rose over him**. Returning at last, the sages called him out of the mound and named him: *because you were reborn from the valmīka, you shall be **Vālmīki***. The robber's emergence is a **rebirth without death** — a *dvitīya-janma* through the Name. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa 6)
- **The moral drawn for Rāma** — Vālmīki closes: such is the power of your Name, Rāma, that it made *me* a sage — how much greater the presence of the Named. The confession is thus staged as **testimony inside the bhakti frame**, spoken to the very God whose name saved him. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā-kāṇḍa 6)

## IV. The name "Ratnākara" and other variants — cataloged, not merged

- **"Ratnākara" is not in the Sanskrit versions** — Neither the Skanda's telling nor the Adhyātma's confession names the robber **Ratnākara** ("mine of jewels"): in the Adhyātma he narrates namelessly; in the Skanda he is simply "the hunter" (previously Stamba). The name belongs to **later vernacular and popular tradition** — the Bengali stream around the [Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ](../ramayana/recensions/krittivasi-ramayan.md), stage retellings, modern comics and cinema — and its earliest textual source is unclear. [folk] [regional] [disputed]
- *Variant (popular/folk telling):* the sage the robber waylays is **Nārada alone**, whose vīṇā-song softens him — a conflation of the Adhyātma's Saptarṣi episode with Nārada's canonical role as Vālmīki's prompter in Bāla Kāṇḍa 1. In this telling it is Nārada who poses the family question and gives the marā mantra. [folk] [oral tradition]
- *Variant (Skanda Purāṇa):* as in §II — **no family scene, no inverted mantra**; conversion by Vaiśākha merit and the direct Rāma-nāma; the anthill attaches to the sage-father Valmīka/Kṛṇu, and the robber becomes Vālmīki only through death and rebirth. (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiśākhamāsa-māhātmya 17–21) [late text]
- *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa):* as in §III — family refusal, marā-marā, the anthill over the sinner himself, no death. (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa 2.6) [late text]
- **Tulsīdās seals the popular form** — The [Rāmcaritmānas](../ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) (Bāl-kāṇḍ, praise of the Name) canonizes the inverted-mantra motif for North India: *ulaṭā nāmu japata jagu jānā, bālamīki bhaye brahma samānā* — "the world knows that by repeating the Name reversed, Vālmīki became the equal of Brahman." Rāma also meets Vālmīki en route to Citrakūṭ in the Mānas' Ayodhyā-kāṇḍ, but Tulsī narrates no robbery — only this allusion. [late text]
- **The pun in *marā*** — Tradition savors the paradox that *marā* means "dead" (or evokes *māra*, killing): the killer repeats "death" until it turns into God's name — death itself, reversed, is Rāma. This gloss is homiletic, found across the commentarial and kathā traditions rather than fixed in one scripture. [oral tradition]

## V. Two clocks — placement and scholarship

- **Scriptural clock** — Within the tradition, all versions place the conversion in **[Tretā-yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)**, before Rāma's exile (Adhyātma: the reborn sage already keeps his āśrama when the exiles arrive) or at least before the epic's composition (Skanda: the reborn Vālmīki "makes Rāma's story popular"). The thousands of years of japa fit comfortably in yuga-scale time.
- **Academic clock** [scholarly] — The **Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa's** core is dated roughly **7th–4th c. BCE** (Bāla and Uttara kāṇḍas later; see [smṛti — itihāsa & epics](../../90-literature-corpus/smriti-itihasa-epics.md)). The **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** is generally placed c. **14th–15th c. CE**; the [Skanda Purāṇa's](../../90-literature-corpus/puranas/skanda-purana.md) māhātmya strata, including the Vaiśākha-māhātmya, are **medieval accretions** in a text that grew from roughly the 6th–8th c. into the 2nd millennium CE. On this clock the robber-conversion is a **bhakti-era legend some two millennia younger than the poet it explains** — a *nāma-māhātmya* teaching-story retrofitted as biography. [scholarly]
- **Not in the early record** — No early source — the epic itself, the Mahābhārata's mentions of Vālmīki, or the early Purāṇic notices — knows the robber story; the Uttara Kāṇḍa's "I never sinned" oath (§I) positively contradicts it. [scholarly]
- **A living controversy** — Communities that venerate the sage as **Bhagavān Vālmīki** — notably the **Valmiki/Balmiki community** of North India, for whom he is the central religious figure — **reject the robber narrative as a late slander**; public retellings of it have drawn organized protest and legal complaints in contemporary India. Responsible retellings flag the legend's late, contested status. [regional] [scholarly] [disputed]

## VI. The ādi-kavi after the conversion — pointers to canonical homes

- **The first poet** — Post-conversion (however reached), Vālmīki's career is the frame of the epic itself: Nārada's question, the krauñca-śloka, Brahmā's commission → [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md); the sheltering of Sītā, the twins' recital, the oath → [Uttara Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md) and the [Uttara Rāmāyaṇa deep-dive](../uttara-ramayana-lava-kusha.md).
- **Attributed works** — Tradition also assigns Vālmīki the **[Yoga-vāsiṣṭha](../yoga-vasishtha-tales-catalog.md)** (as narrator-compiler) and the **Adbhuta Rāmāyaṇa**; the texts themselves are much later. [late text] [scholarly]
- **Vālmīki Jayantī** — The sage's birth is celebrated on the **full moon of Āśvina** as **Vālmīki Jayantī / Pargat Divas**, the principal festival of the Valmiki community; the conversion legend is retold — or pointedly not retold — on this day. [folk] [regional]
