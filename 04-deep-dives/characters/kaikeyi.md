# Kaikeyī

> Where this sits: dedicated deep-dive on **Kaikeyī** — second queen of Daśaratha, princess of Kekaya, mother of Bharata, and the woman whose two boons drive the entire engine of the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md). Up to the [Epic Characters index](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). Her decisive act fills the [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/2-ayodhya-kanda.md) (vulgate sargas 7–19); the exile she forces shapes the life of [Sītā](./sita.md) and the vow of [Lakṣmaṇa](./lakshmana.md). Companion pages: [Mantharā](./manthara.md) · [Daśaratha](./dasharatha.md) · [Kausalyā](./kausalya.md) · [Sumitrā](./sumitra.md) · [Bharata and Śatrughna](./bharata-shatrughna-and-their-wives.md).
> Two clocks: as *itihāsa* she belongs to the Tretā Yuga of the present 28th Mahāyuga, 7th (Vaivasvata) Manvantara. The scholarly baseline is the Vālmīki *Rāmāyaṇa*. It is cited here by **vulgate (Southern) sarga.verse**, with the **Baroda Critical Edition (CE)** number added where the two differ or where the CE lacks a passage. The Mahābhārata's *Rāmopākhyāna*, the Buddhist *Dasaratha Jātaka*, the Jain Rāma story, the *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*, Tulsīdās's *Rāmcaritmānas* and the Bengali *Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ* give other or later layers; the last three are devotional or regional works of the 14th–16th centuries. [scholarly]

**Kaikeyī** (Sanskrit *Kaikeyī*, "she of the Kekaya country") is the daughter of the king of Kekaya, called **Aśvapati**, and the best-loved of Daśaratha's three principal queens. She is the mother of **Bharata**. Before the crisis she is a beloved, generous, even valiant queen. Her single catastrophic intervention, claiming two long-deferred boons to crown Bharata and banish Rāma, makes her the proximate cause of the whole forest narrative. It also makes her one of Sanskrit literature's most contested figures: a villainess to popular imagination, blameless in a larger design to the sage Bharadvāja in Vālmīki, and an instrument of the gods' purpose to the later theologians.

## I. Lineage and the marriage-condition

- **Princess of Kekaya** - Her father's house is in **Rājagṛha / Girivraja**, the Kekaya capital, which Ayodhyā's messengers reach by way of the Bāhlīka country and the Vipāśā (Beas) in the north-west (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 68.6, 68.18–21; CE 2.61.6, 2.62.13, 2.64.1). The vulgate names the city Girivraja (68.21); the CE calls it Rājagṛha. Her father appears under the name or title **Aśvapati**, "lord of horses" (Ayodhyā 70.21; CE 2.64.19), and Bharata taunts her that she cannot be Aśvapati's daughter (74.9; CE 2.68.9). Her brother **Yudhājit**, Bharata's maternal uncle, arrives at Mithilā during the four weddings, wanting to see his sister's son (Bāla 73.1–6; CE 1.72.1–3). In the Uttara Kāṇḍa he is "Yudhājit the Kekaya king". He sends his guru Gārgya to Rāma about the Gandharva country, Rāma sends Bharata, and Yudhājit marches with Bharata against the Gandharvas (CE 7.90.1–9, 7.91.1–3).
- *Variant (vulgate only) — her mother and the speech of birds:* in the minister **Sumantra**'s rebuke, Kaikeyī's father had a boon that let him understand the cries of all creatures. When he laughed at the sound of a creature (a *jṛmbha*) by his bed, his queen demanded to know why, though telling her would cost him his life, so he **repudiated her**. Sumantra's moral is that Kaikeyī "takes after her mother", since "honey does not ooze from a neem tree" (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 35.16–28). The tale, like the whole of Sumantra's speech in vulgate sarga 35, is **absent from the Critical Edition**. [interpolation] It is the textual root of the popular statement that her mother was exiled.
- **The rājya-śulka — the kingdom as bride-price** - When Daśaratha married Kaikeyī, he promised her father **the kingdom as an unsurpassed bride-price** (*rājyaśulkam anuttamam*). **Rāma himself** tells Bharata so at Citrakūṭa: *purā bhrātaḥ pitā naḥ sa mātaraṃ te samudvahan / mātāmahe samāśrauṣīd rājyaśulkam anuttamam*. In the next verses he adds the boon from the deva–asura war, as a second obligation their father was discharging (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 107.3–7; CE 2.99.3–7). This is the only place in the Critical Edition where the *rājyaśulka* is mentioned; in the CE neither Daśaratha nor Kaikeyī invokes it. Still, it is the linchpin of every sympathetic reading of Kaikeyī: her demand collects on a promise made before she bore a son.
- **Mother of Bharata** - Daśaratha divides the sacrificial *pāyasa*. Kausalyā receives half, Sumitrā a quarter and Kaikeyī an eighth, and the last eighth goes to Sumitrā again (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Bāla 16.27–29). Kaikeyī bears **Bharata** (Bāla 18.13). Sumitrā's younger son **Śatrughna** is as close to Bharata as Lakṣmaṇa is to Rāma (Bāla 18.30–33). See [Lakṣmaṇa](./lakshmana.md) for the pairing.
- **The favourite queen** - Daśaratha tells her that no one on earth is dearer to him than she is, except Rāma (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 11.5). Her apartments are where he goes first with good news, and he is lost when he does not find her there (Ayodhyā 10.9–21). Mantharā counts on exactly this: "the king would walk into fire for you" (Ayodhyā 9.24–26). This intimacy is what makes his entrapment possible.
- **Before the crisis** - Daśaratha says that until that night she had never done anything unreasonable or hurtful to him (Ayodhyā 12.20). Bharata recalls that Rāma treated her exactly as he treated his own mother, and that Kausalyā behaved towards her like a sister (Ayodhyā 73.9–10). Mantharā, for her part, reminds her that she once slighted Kausalyā out of pride as the favoured wife (Ayodhyā 8.37; CE 2.8.26).

## II. The origin of the two boons — the Śambara war

The boons are not arbitrary: they are an old debt, earned when Kaikeyī saved Daśaratha's life in battle. The texts disagree about the battle, the rescue and even the number of boons. The versions are catalogued below; the same spread is surveyed inline in the [Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa](../ramayana/2-ayodhya-kanda.md).

- **The base frame (Vālmīki)** - Mantharā tells the story back to Kaikeyī. Daśaratha took Kaikeyī with him when he went with other royal sages to help Indra in a deva–asura war. The war was fought in the south, towards the Daṇḍaka forest, at the city of **Vaijayanta**, the seat of **Timidhvaja**, the great asura "known as **Śambara**", master of a hundred illusions (*śatamāya*), whom the gods could not defeat. Daśaratha was struck senseless and wounded by weapons. Kaikeyī **carried him, unconscious, away from the battlefield**, and there too, when he was wounded by weapons, she protected him: *apavāhya tvayā devi saṃgrāmān naṣṭacetanaḥ / tatrāpi vikṣataḥ śastraiḥ patis te rakṣitas tvayā*. Pleased, he gave her **two boons**, and she said she would claim them when she wished (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9.11–17; CE 2.9.9–14). The vulgate also has the rākṣasas slaughtering wounded and sleeping men by night, and Daśaratha "hacked to pieces" (9.14–15). The CE lacks both verses.
  - *Note — one rescue or two?* *tatrāpi* ("there too") can be read as a second rescue, and some readers take the two boons as matching two rescues. The text never draws that link. Kaikeyī's own account speaks of one act of saving him (Ayodhyā 11.18–19, 18.32). Rāma's account in the CE has the king first give "a boon" (*varam*, singular, CE 2.99.4), which she then claims as **two** (*dvau varau*, CE 2.99.5).
- *Variant — the chariot linchpin (popular retelling):* in the battle a **chariot wheel is about to come off**, and Kaikeyī **thrusts her thumb into the hole of the bolt** to hold it, which wins her the two boons. This version is recorded in Vettam Mani's *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia* (p. 364, as cited by Wikipedia), and no earlier text for it was found. It has no basis in Vālmīki, who does not put Kaikeyī at the reins. [folk][late text][unverified]
- *Variant — Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ (Bengali), as reported:* the two boons are earned **separately**. The **first** comes in the Śambara war, when Kaikeyī heals the king's wounds with a mantra. The **second** comes later, when one of his nails festers and, told by the royal physician that the pus must be sucked out, she **sucks the pus from the infected nail** herself. The only source found is a popular online summary that cites no edition or chapter; the Bengali text has not been checked. [regional][unverified]
- *Variant — Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna (BORI CE):* there is no war backstory and only **one** wish. Kaikeyī goes to Daśaratha adorned and smiling, not in a sulking-chamber, and reminds him of "the one wish (*kāmam ekam*) you once granted me". She asks that **Bharata receive the consecration prepared for Rāma and that Rāma go to the forest**. Daśaratha cannot speak a word, and Rāma leaves of his own accord so that his father's word holds true (*Mahābhārata* 3.261.19–27). The fourteen-year term (*nava pañca ca varṣāṇi*) occurs only in a starred passage the CE relegates (3.261.25 \*1246).
- *Variant — Dasaratha Jātaka (Buddhist, Jātaka no. 461):* the queen, who is not named, is the king's second queen-consort, raised after the first queen's death. The king offers her a boon at **the birth of her son Bharata**, and she claims it for his kingship when the boy is seven. **The king refuses** her outright. Fearing she will have his elder children killed, he sends them away, to a neighbouring kingdom or the forest, to return after his death. [Buddhist]
- *Variant — Jain (Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra 7.4, tr. H. M. Johnson):* Kaikeyī is the daughter of **King Śubhamati of Kautukamaṅgala**. She chooses Daśaratha at her **svayaṃvara**, the rejected suitors attack, and **she drives his chariot** while he defeats them. Pleased with her charioteering, he grants **one** boon, which she leaves "on deposit". [Jain]
- **The boons left "on credit"** - In Vālmīki and in the Jain telling, Kaikeyī names no wish when the boon is given. In Vālmīki it lies dormant until Mantharā revives it, a device the epic plants long before firing it (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9.17, 9.28).

## III. Mantharā and the poisoning of the mind (Ayodhyā 7–9)

- **The hunchbacked maidservant** - **[Mantharā](./manthara.md)** is a hunchbacked (*kubjā*) servant of Kaikeyī's natal family (*jñātidāsī*) who has lived with her since her birth. From the palace roof she sees Ayodhyā decked out, learns from a nurse (*dhātrī*) of the consecration fixed for the next day, and hurries to her mistress (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 7.1–13; CE 2.7.1–6). The text does not call her part of a dowry.
- **Kaikeyī's first, generous reaction** - Crucially, Kaikeyī's **first response is joy**. She gives the news-bearer **a fine ornament** and declares *rāme vā bharate vāhaṃ viśeṣaṃ nopalakṣaye*: she sees no difference between Rāma and Bharata (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 7.32–35; CE 2.7.27–30). Mantharā flings the ornament away. Kaikeyī then **defends Rāma**: he is righteous and the eldest, he will protect his brothers like a father, Bharata's turn will come after Rāma's hundred-year reign, and Rāma serves her even more than he serves Kausalyā (Ayodhyā 8.1, 8.13–19; CE 2.8.10). The villainy is not native to her: it is **manufactured by Mantharā**. [a key point in any sympathetic reading]
- **The argument of fear** - Mantharā works on her step by step. Once Rāma is crowned his line will hold the throne and Bharata's name will drop out of the royal house (Ayodhyā 8.22–25). **Bharata, kept away at Kekaya, will be exiled or killed** (8.27–28). Kaikeyī will serve Kausalyā as a maid (8.10–11), and Kausalyā will take her revenge for Kaikeyī's old arrogance (8.37). Fear for her son and for her own standing, not malice toward Rāma, is the lever.
- **The plan: invoke the old boons — knowledge Kaikeyī herself supplied** - Kaikeyī, now won over, resolves on her own to send Rāma to the forest that very day and have Bharata consecrated **as yuvarāja**, and asks how it can be done (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9.2–3; *yauvarājyena bharataṃ*, CE 2.9.2–3). Mantharā reminds her of the Śambara-war boons and **coaches the exact demands**: Bharata's consecration, and Rāma's banishment for **fourteen years** (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9.20, 9.30; CE 2.9.15). Mantharā says plainly that she was not there and knows nothing of that war: *anabhijñā hy ahaṃ devi tvayaiva kathitā purā*, "it was you yourself who told me this long ago" (Ayodhyā 9.18; CE 2.9.14). The vulgate adds a verse in which she has kept the story in her mind out of affection and urges Kaikeyī to stop the consecration by force (9.19). The CE lacks the whole verse. Fourteen years, she reasons, will let Bharata put down roots (9.21, 9.31, 9.34; CE 2.9.25).
- **The "anger chamber" (krodhāgāra)** - Mantharā tells her to go into the **krodhāgāra** (anger-chamber), lie on the bare floor in soiled clothes, refuse all offers of jewels, remind him of the war boons, and name her wish only once he has committed himself (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9.22–29). Kaikeyī praises Mantharā's cleverness and promises to have her hump adorned with gold (9.38–52). She swears she will die rather than see Rāma crowned, strips off her ornaments and lies on the floor (9.58–66, 10.5–8). The vulgate narrator adds that, swayed by the hunchback's words, she went astray "like a young girl" (*kiśorīvotpathaṃ gatā*, 9.37), a verse not in the CE.
- *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna) — the gods' plan, already in the epic:* before the avatāra's birth, Brahmā orders the **gandharvī Dundubhī** to be born on earth as the hunchbacked **Mantharā** "to accomplish the gods' purpose", and he instructs her in what to do (*gandharvīṃ dundubhīṃ nāma … mantharā mānuṣe loke kubjā samabhavat*, *Mahābhārata* 3.260.9–10, 3.260.14; BORI CE). This is the earliest attested form of the "divine instrument" reading, though here it is aimed at Mantharā rather than Kaikeyī. [scholarly]
- *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa / Vaiṣṇava theology):* while Kausalyā is praying to Durgā for the consecration, the gods send the goddess **Vāṇī (Sarasvatī)** to Ayodhyā on Brahmā's word to obstruct it. Her instruction is to **enter Mantharā first and then Kaikeyī** (*mantharāṃ praviśasvādau kaikeyīṃ ca tataḥ param*, *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 2.43–46). This makes both women **divine instruments** rather than free agents. Kaikeyī's first gift to Mantharā is here a jewelled golden anklet (2.54–55). [late text]
- *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas):* the gods implore **Śāradā (Sarasvatī)**, and she turns the dull-witted Mantharā's mind (*gaī girā mati pheri*, Tulsīdās, *Rāmcaritmānas*, Ayodhyā 11–12). The whole episode unfolds as Rāma's own will: Kaikeyī's guilt is dissolved theologically even while the drama condemns it. [late text]

## IV. The two demands and Daśaratha's ruin (Ayodhyā 10–13)

- **Binding the king to his word** - Finding Kaikeyī on the floor, the besotted Daśaratha offers to free the condemned, punish anyone, or give her the wealth of all his lands (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 10.28–38). She asks him to promise first. He swears **by Rāma himself** (11.6–8), and she calls the gods, the heavenly bodies and the spirits to witness the oath (11.13–16). Only then does she name her price.
- **The demands spoken** - She recalls that she saved him in the deva–asura war (11.18–19). She then claims the boons: **Bharata to be consecrated with the preparations already made for Rāma** (11.24), and, "the second boon", **Rāma to live in the Daṇḍaka forest for fourteen years as an ascetic in bark, deerskin and matted hair** (11.25–26) (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 11).
- **Daśaratha's collapse** - The king faints, rages and pleads through the night. He calls her a poisonous serpent he took into his house (12.9), asks what Rāma has ever done to her (12.7), folds his hands and touches her feet (12.36), and begs the night not to end (13.17). Kaikeyī is **immovable**. She threatens to drink poison if Rāma is crowned (12.47) and taunts him with his boast of always keeping his word (13.3). She cites the exemplars of truth: **Śaibya**, who gave his own flesh to the hawk, **Alarka**, who gave away his eyes, and the **ocean**, which keeps its shore (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 12–14, exempla at 14.3–6; Śaibya at CE 2.12.4).
- **The Truth-of-the-Raghus** - Daśaratha is undone not by force but by his own plighted word: pressed by her, he cannot loosen the noose (*pāśa*), any more than Bali could loosen the one Indra put on him (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 14.11; CE 2.12.8). Tulsīdās gives the ideal its proverbial form, but puts it in **Daśaratha's own mouth** as he swears to grant her wish before she has named it: *raghukula rīti sadā cali āī / prāna jāhuṁ baru bacanu na jāī*, "the Raghu custom has always been this: let life go, but never the word" (*Rāmcaritmānas*, Ayodhyā 28.2). [late text]
- **The king repudiates her and her son** - With what control he has left, Daśaratha tells her: *yas te mantrakṛtaḥ pāṇir agnau pāpe mayā dhṛtaḥ / taṃ tyajāmi svajaṃ caiva tava putraṃ saha tvayā*, "the hand of yours I took over the fire with mantras, I renounce, and with you the son I begot" (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 14.14; CE 2.12.11). This is the "curse" that Rāma will ask him to lift (§V).
  - *Vulgate only:* if Bharata welcomes Rāma's exile, he is not to perform his father's funeral rites (12.93). Neither Kaikeyī nor her son may offer the funeral water; Rāma will (14.16–17). These lines were not found in the CE. [interpolation]
- **Delivering the sentence herself** - When the broken king cannot speak, it is **Kaikeyī who tells Rāma** of the boons and the exile. "This king gave me a boon and now regrets it like a common man," she says (18.22–23). She retells the war and the two boons and orders him to Daṇḍaka "today" for fourteen years (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 18.32–37; CE 2.16.22). Rāma accepts at once and without anger (19.2–11). She presses him to leave immediately, saying his father will neither bathe nor eat until he is gone (19.12–18).
- **Before the court** - When Daśaratha orders an army and treasure to follow Rāma, Kaikeyī objects that Bharata will not take a kingdom drained "like wine with its spirit drunk off". She then cites the Ikṣvākus' own precedent: **Sagara banished his eldest son Asamañja, so Rāma too should go**. The king can only say "Shame!" (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 36.12–17; CE 2.32.10–13). The old minister **Siddhārtha** answers that Asamañja drowned children in the Sarayū, and asks what Rāma has done (36.18–30). In the vulgate, **Sumantra** has already warned her that kin and brahmins will desert her and that great infamy will follow her (35.12–13, 35.33), but she was not shaken and the colour of her face did not change (35.37). That whole speech of Sumantra's is absent from the CE. [interpolation] See [Sumantra](./sumantra.md).
- **The bark garments** - Kaikeyī **brings the bark garments herself** and hands them to Rāma before the crowd (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 37.6–7; CE 2.33.6–7). Sītā cannot work out how to wear the bark, and Rāma ties it over her silk.
  - **Vasiṣṭha's rebuke (vulgate):** **Vasiṣṭha** stops Sītā and rounds on Kaikeyī as *kulapāṃsanī*, "disgrace of the house". Sītā should not go to the forest at all, he says, and if she goes with Rāma the city will follow; Bharata and Śatrughna will join him in bark, and even beasts and trees will turn towards him. The boon covered Rāma alone, so Sītā should at least go adorned (Ayodhyā 37.21–36). This speech is **absent from the CE**. There the crowd cries "Shame on Daśaratha!", and it is **Daśaratha** who tells Kaikeyī that Sītā should not go in bark, asking whether Rāma's exile is not enough for her (CE 2.33.13–15 = vulgate 38.1–3). [interpolation] In both texts Daśaratha then has fine clothes and jewels brought for her from the treasury (vulgate 39.14–18; CE 2.34.15–17). See [Sītā](./sita.md).

## V. Aftermath, remorse, and rehabilitation

- **Daśaratha's death and Kaikeyī's blame** - The king dies of grief **at midnight on the sixth night after Rāma left** (*rajanīṃ ṣaṣṭhīm … ardharātre*, *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 63.4; CE 2.57.3), after Sumantra has brought the empty chariot home (Ayodhyā 57). In the vulgate his last cry names her: *hā nṛśaṃse mamāmitre kaikeyi kulapāṃsani*, "ah, cruel one, my enemy, Kaikeyī, disgrace of the house" (Ayodhyā 64.76–79). **The CE has no such address.** Its dying king calls only on Rāma (*hā rāghava mahābāho*, CE 2.58.56–57). Kaikeyī is held responsible for the king's death as much as for the exile. See [Daśaratha](./dasharatha.md) for the curse behind it. The sixth night is stated in the text itself, in both the vulgate and the CE.
- **Her last speech** - Kaikeyī receives Bharata joyfully, takes him on her lap, and tells him "as though it were good news" that his father is dead (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 72.1–14). Pressed, she makes a revealing admission: Rāma had taken no brahmin's wealth, harmed no one, and never looked at another man's wife. He was not exiled for any fault. She had simply asked for the kingdom for Bharata and for Rāma's banishment, and "all this I did for your sake" (72.47–54; CE 2.66.40–44). This is her **last direct speech found in the Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa**, and no later one was found.
- **Bharata's repudiation** - **Bharata** is **horrified**. He calls her cruel, a murderer of her husband, "my mother in appearance only", tells her to leave the kingdom, and swears he will bring Rāma back and serve him (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 73–74, esp. 73.25–27, 74.2–8). When **Śatrughna** drags Mantharā across the floor and turns on Kaikeyī, she, terrified, **takes refuge with Bharata**. Bharata stops him: women are not to be killed, and Rāma would never forgive them (78.16–24; CE 2.72.19). Afterwards Kaikeyī quietly consoles the terrified Mantharā (78.26; CE 2.72.25). Bharata refuses the throne. At Citrakūṭa he receives Rāma's **sandals (pādukā)**, and he rules as regent from **Nandigrāma** (Ayodhyā 112–115). The scheme fails utterly: the son she schemed for rejects the crown.
- **The journey to Citrakūṭa** - Kaikeyī rides out with Sumitrā and Kausalyā in Bharata's train (Ayodhyā 83.6). At **Bharadvāja**'s hermitage she clasps the sage's feet "**with shame**" (*savyapatrapā*), her desire unfulfilled and "reviled by all the world" (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 92.16; CE 2.86.16). This is the only such gesture of shame found in the text. Bharata introduces her as *aiśvaryakāmām … anāryām āryarūpiṇīm*, greedy for power and ignoble in noble guise, the root of the whole disaster (92.25–26; CE 2.86.25). Bharadvāja replies: *na doṣeṇāvagantavyā kaikeyī bharata tvayā / rāmapravrājanaṃ hy etat sukhodarkaṃ bhaviṣyati*, "do not hold Kaikeyī at fault; this exile will end in happiness" (92.29; CE 2.86.28). The vulgate goes further than the CE: the exile will benefit "gods, dānavas and sages" (92.30). So the "divine purpose" reading has a foothold **inside Vālmīki**, though it is explicit only in the vulgate verse.
- **Rāma's absolutions** - Rāma never blames her in Vālmīki, and he repeatedly forbids others to:
  - Among his first questions to Bharata is whether "noble Queen Kaikeyī is happy" (*āryā ca devī nandati kaikayī*, Ayodhyā 100.10; CE 2.94.6).
  - On Bharata's arrival he takes the feet of **all** his mothers (Ayodhyā 104.18). Nothing singles Kaikeyī out.
  - He calls her demand *proper*: *yuktam uktaṃ ca kaikeyyā pitrā me sukṛtaṃ kṛtam*, "what Kaikeyī said was fitting, and what my father did was rightly done" (Ayodhyā 111.29; CE 2.103.29).
  - Whether she acted from desire or greed, he tells Bharata to put it out of his mind and treat her as his mother (*vartitavyaṃ ca mātṛvat*, Ayodhyā 112.19; CE 2.104.19).
  - At parting he binds Bharata by an oath, his own and Sītā's, to **protect Kaikeyī and not be angry with her** (*mātaraṃ rakṣa kaikeyīm*, Ayodhyā 112.27–28, vulgate only).
  - In the forest, when Lakṣmaṇa says it is hard to see how a woman with such a husband and such a son could be so cruel, Rāma cuts him off: *na te 'mbā madhyamā tāta garhitavyā kathaṃ cana*, "you must never speak ill of our middle mother" (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Araṇya 16.35–37; CE 3.15.33–35).
- **The curse lifted (Yuddha Kāṇḍa)** - After Sītā's fire-ordeal, Daśaratha appears from heaven and says Kaikeyī's words urging the exile are still lodged in his heart (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Yuddha 119.15; CE 6.107.14). Rāma asks him: *kuru prasādaṃ dharmajña kaikeyyā bharatasya ca*. "You said, 'I renounce you together with your son'; let that terrible curse not touch Kaikeyī and her son." Daśaratha agrees (Yuddha 119.25–27; CE 6.107.24–26). This lifts the repudiation of Ayodhyā 14.14 (§IV).
- **Return and death** - On his return Rāma salutes "the illustrious Kaikeyī" (*kaikeyīṃ ca yaśasvinīm*) among his mothers at Nandigrāma, and again in his father's palace (*Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Yuddha 127.49, 128.44; CE 6.115.39, 6.116.39). Long into his reign, after Kausalyā's death, "Sumitrā followed, and **the illustrious Kaikeyī** too, having done much dharma, took her place in heaven" (*anviyāya sumitrāpi kaikeyī ca yaśasvinī / dharmaṃ kṛtvā bahuvidhaṃ tridive paryavasthitā*, *Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa*, Uttara Kāṇḍa, CE 7.89.11–12). [late text — the Uttara Kāṇḍa is generally held to be later than Books 2–6]
- **Vālmīki gives her no repentance scene** - This is the sharpest divergence between the text and popular memory. After Ayodhyā 72 no further speech of hers was found. She travels to Citrakūṭa and is present when the brothers meet, but the epic gives her no speech of contrition, no plea for pardon and no change of heart. The nearest it comes is the single word *savyapatrapā* at Bharadvāja's feet (92.16). The remembered scene of a weeping, repentant Kaikeyī comes from later texts.
- *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa) — the private confession:* as the party prepares to leave Citrakūṭa, Kaikeyī comes to Rāma alone, in tears and with folded hands. She confesses that her mind was deluded by māyā, She hails him as Viṣṇu and says that it was he, about to do the gods' work, who impelled her (9.57–59). Rāma smiles and agrees: *mayaiva preritā vāṇī tava vaktrād vinirgatā / devakāryārthasiddhyartham atra doṣaḥ kutas tava*, "it was I who sent that speech out of your mouth, for the gods' purpose; where is your fault?" He promises her liberation through devotion, and she goes home in joy (*Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa*, Ayodhyā 9.55–69). [late text]
- *Variant (Rāmcaritmānas):* at Citrakūṭa, seeing Sītā and the two brothers so guileless, the "crooked queen" is consumed with regret. She begs the earth to open and Yama to take her, but neither will (*kuṭila rāni pachitāni aghāī … avani jamahi jācati kaikeī*, Tulsīdās, *Rāmcaritmānas*, Ayodhyā 252.3). On the return to Ayodhyā she shrinks inwardly as she embraces Rāma. Lakṣmaṇa embraces her again and again but cannot shake off his resentment (Uttara 6 ka–kha). Rāma, knowing she is ashamed, **goes to her palace first** and comforts her before entering his own (*prabhu jānī kaikaī lajānī / prathama tāsu gṛha gae bhavānī*, Uttara, caupāī after dohā 10 kha). This is the version nearly every reader carries. [late text]
- *Variant (Jain, Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra 7.4):* Daśaratha wants to renounce the world and become a monk. Kaikeyī claims her deposited boon, asking only that **Bharata be given the kingdom**; she does not ask for Rāma's exile. Rāma chooses the forest himself, so that Bharata will accept the throne. When Bharata still refuses, Kaikeyī calls herself wicked for acting without reflection and goes to the forest with him. There she backs Bharata's plea that Rāma return, takes the fault on herself and asks pardon. Rāma keeps his father's promise and goes on. [Jain]
- *Variant (Mahābhārata, Rāmopākhyāna):* when Kaikeyī offers Bharata the kingdom "free of thorns", he rebukes her for killing her husband and ruining the family out of greed for wealth. "Be content, mother," he says, and weeps (*sakāmā bhava me mātar*, *Mahābhārata* 3.261.30–33). She then travels to Citrakūṭa with the other queens (3.261.35–37). The Rāmopākhyāna gives her no repentance either.
- **The reckoning later softened** - Once Kaikeyī is cast as an instrument of the gods (the seed is in Bharadvāja's words at 92.29–30, and the full form in the Adhyātma and Tulsīdās), the tradition lets her fade from blame after the reunion. The exile she caused is reread as the **necessary precondition for Rāvaṇa's death**: without the forest there is no abduction, no war, and no fulfilment of the avatāra's purpose. [scholarly]

## VI. Character and the contested verdict

- **The popular villain** - In folk performance, proverb, and pan-Indian idiom Kaikeyī is the archetype of the **wicked stepmother / scheming queen**, paired with Mantharā as the corrupting whisperer; her name is a byword for jealousy and broken households. [folk]
- **The voices inside Vālmīki** - The epic's own verdict is divided by speaker. **Daśaratha** (12–14, 64.77 vulgate), **Bharata** (73–74, 92.26), **Kausalyā** (75.11–13), **Vasiṣṭha** (37.22, vulgate) and **Lakṣmaṇa** (Araṇya 16.35) condemn her. **Bharadvāja** (92.29) and **Rāma** (111.29, 112.19, Araṇya 16.37, Yuddha 119.25–26) refuse to blame her. Even Kaikeyī's own last speech clears Rāma of any fault (72.48).
- **The valiant queen** - Against this, Vālmīki's own portrait is of a **brave, generous, and dearly-loved** woman who saved her husband in war and loved Rāma — undone by a single moment of coached fear for her son, which makes her a tragic rather than a simply evil figure. [scholarly]
- **The instrument of dharma** - The theological reading goes furthest. Its seed is in Vālmīki (Bharadvāja, 92.29–30) and in the Mahābhārata's Dundubhī-Mantharā (3.260.9–10). In the Adhyātma and Tulsīdās, Kaikeyī becomes the **unwilling hinge of the divine plan**: Sarasvatī sways her at the gods' request, and Rāma tells her that he spoke through her mouth (*Adhyātma* Ayodhyā 9.63). The whole crisis becomes Rāma's own *līlā*. [late text]
- **Modern re-readings** - Contemporary and feminist retellings recover her **legitimate claim**: the *rājyaśulka*, her father's marriage-condition that her son should rule (Ayodhyā 107.3). They also stress her victimhood, at Mantharā's hands and under the absolute vow, and reframe her as a woman trapped by a system rather than a villain by nature. [scholarly]
