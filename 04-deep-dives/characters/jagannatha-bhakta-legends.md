# Bhaktas of Jagannātha — Sālabega, Dāsiā Baurī, Bandhu Mohanty

> Detail node under [`04-deep-dives/characters/`](README.md) — the classic **Odia devotee-miracle triad** of **[Jagannāth Purī](../../06-sacred-geography/jagannath-puri-purushottama-kshetra.md)**: the Muslim poet **Sālabega**, for whom the Ratha Yātrā chariot halts at his tomb; **Dāsiā Baurī**, whose coconut the Lord takes in his own carved hand; and **Bandhu Mohanty**, fed at night with mahāprasāda by the Lord's own command. These are the Purī counterparts of [Śabarī](shabari.md) and [Guha](guha.md) — proofs that Jagannātha's grace overleaps the temple wall of caste and creed.
> Sits in the historical **Kali-Yuga** (Yuga began 3102 BCE); the miracle-legends are Odia bhakta-caritra / [oral tradition], their datable cores **[scholarly] c. 16th–17th c. CE** — the two-clocks method, cross-ref [`recorded-history.md`](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/recorded-history.md). Up: [`04-deep-dives`](../README.md) · Master index: [`../../README.md`](../../README.md).

**Jagannātha of Purī** ("Lord of the Universe") is worshipped in an abstract wooden (**dāru-brahma**) form whose great social teaching is that **mahāprasāda dissolves all caste** — offered food, once touched by the Lord, may be shared by anyone from the same vessel (see [`anna-naivedya-and-prasada.md`](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/anna-naivedya-and-prasada.md)). Yet the medieval temple itself barred **Muslims (*yavana*)** and **"untouchable" (Bauri, Caṇḍāla) castes** from the sanctum. Out of exactly this contradiction Odisha's bhakti tradition generated its most beloved *bhakta-caritra* (devotee-lives): stories in which, when a devotee is shut out at the door, **the deity himself comes out to the devotee** — halting his chariot, stretching out his hand, reopening his kitchen at night. The three below form the standard triad recited in Odia devotional culture, transmitted through **bhajan, palm-leaf hagiography, and Mādaḷā Pāñji temple-lore** rather than the Sanskrit canon. [regional] [oral tradition]

## I. Sālabega — the Muslim poet whose chariot waits (c. 1607–1675 CE)

- **Who he was** — **Sālabega** (Sālabeg), the great Odia Jagannātha **bhajan-poet**, son of a **Muslim father** — a Mughal *subedār* named **Lālbeg** (a commander in the Mughalbandi tract of coastal Odisha) — and a **Hindu Brāhmaṇ widowed mother**. Because of his paternal descent he was reckoned a *yavana* and, by the rule of the age, **could never enter the Jagannātha temple**. He became instead the archetype of the devotee who loves the Lord **from outside the wall**. [scholarly] [regional]
  - *Dating:* traditionally placed in the **early–mid 17th c.**, roughly **c. 1607/1608 – c. 1675 CE**, a contemporary of the later Odia bhakti milieu after the [Pañcasakhā](../../90-literature-corpus/odia-literature-sarala-and-panchasakha.md) and Caitanya's Purī period. Exact dates are hagiographic, not documentary. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The wound and the vow** — the standard life-legend: Sālabega, a soldier, was **gravely wounded in battle** and lay dying of a festering injury no physician could heal. His Brāhmaṇ mother taught him to **call on Kṛṣṇa / Jagannātha**; he vowed his life to the Lord, was healed, and renounced arms to become a wandering singer of Jagannātha. [oral tradition] [folk]
- **The songs** — Sālabega composed **Odia and Braja-miśra bhajans** of intense longing, of which **"Āhe Nīla Śaila"** ("O Blue Mountain" — addressing Jagannātha as the dark hill of **Nīlācala/Nīlagiri**, begging the Lord to be the **raft across the ocean of existence**) is the most famous, sung across Odisha to this day. His refrain signs himself the **"yavana Sālabega,"** openly owning the birth that shut him out — devotional defiance parallel to [Ravidās](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/ravidas.md) naming his own caste. [regional]
- **The chariot that halted — the central miracle** — the legend for which he is remembered: Sālabega, travelling (in the common telling, returning from **Vṛndāvana**), fell ill and feared he **could not reach Purī in time for the Ratha Yātrā** to see the Lord ride out. He prayed to Jagannātha to **wait for him**. When the great chariot **Nandighoṣa** reached the spot on the **Baḍa Daṇḍa (Grand Road)** where Sālabega lay, it **stopped and could not be moved** — by the massed pullers, by elephants, by any force — **until the poet arrived** and had his darśana; only then did the car roll on. [oral tradition] [folk]
- **The samādhi and the living rite** — Sālabega was buried on the **Baḍa Daṇḍa** itself, and his **samādhi (tomb)** stands there still. In continuing observance, the **Jagannātha chariot pauses at Sālabega's samādhi during every Ratha Yātrā** — an annual, public re-enactment of the miracle, and the one point on the sacred route marked by a **Muslim-born devotee's grave**. [regional]
  - *Note:* Sālabega's is a **tomb / samādhi**, not a temple image; he is venerated as a *bhakta-poet*, and his memorial's place on the processional road is treated as proof that Jagannātha's love ignores the *yavana* barrier the temple maintained. [regional] [scholarly]

## II. Dāsiā Baurī — the coconut the Lord took in his own hand

- **Who he was** — **Dāsiā Baurī** (Dāsiā of the **Baurī** caste, an "untouchable"/Dalit community of Odisha), a poor devotee traditionally placed at **Baligaon / a village near Purī**, and associated in the *bhakta-caritra* with the later Pañcasakhā-era bhakti milieu (some accounts link him to **Ananta Dāsa** of the five friends). As a Baurī he was **forbidden entry to the temple** and could not offer to Jagannātha with his own hand. [regional] [oral tradition]
  - *Dating:* usually set in the **16th c. CE**, but wholly hagiographic. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The offering sent by proxy** — Dāsiā's devotion was total; barred from the sanctum, he would send his offerings **through pilgrims and Brāhmaṇs** going up to the temple, trusting them to place his gift before the Lord. The famous episode turns on a **coconut** he gives to a temple-goer (in many tellings a **Brāhmaṇ pilgrim**) to offer on his behalf. [oral tradition]
- **The mocking question** — the pilgrim, contemptuous of an untouchable's devotion, asks derisively whether **Jagannātha will take the coconut in his own hand** — a taunt at the very idea that the Lord would receive an offering routed from a Baurī. Dāsiā, in simple faith, says **yes, the Lord will take it himself**. [folk]
- **The hand of the deity — the central miracle** — when the pilgrim reaches the sanctum and, half in mockery, holds out Dāsiā's coconut, the **wooden Jagannātha image stretches out its arm and takes the coconut into its own hand** — accepting the untouchable's gift directly, before the temple's eyes, when it would not so receive the offering of the high-caste bearer. The miracle vindicates Dāsiā and rebukes the caste-pride of the intermediary. [oral tradition] [folk]
  - *Variant (the recognition):* in some retellings the astonished priests/king seek out Dāsiā, and a Pañcasakhā sant (Ananta Dāsa) affirms him as a true bhakta whom the Lord favours over ritual status. [regional] [disputed]
- **Meaning** — Dāsiā Baurī is Purī's canonical proof, alongside the mahāprasāda rule, that **Jagannātha owns no caste-line**: the "polluting" hand is exactly the one the Lord reaches for — the Purī echo of [Śabarī](shabari.md)'s half-tasted berries and [Guha](guha.md)'s welcome. [scholarly] [regional]

## III. Bandhu Mohanty — fed at night with mahāprasāda

- **Who he was** — **Bandhu Mohanty** (Bandhu Mahānti), a **poor devotee** of Jagannātha from a village in the Purī country, remembered for his single, life-defining longing: to **offer bhoga to the Lord and partake of mahāprasāda**, which his poverty long denied him. [regional] [oral tradition]
- **The saved offering, arrived too late** — the legend: Bandhu Mohanty scrimped and **saved for a long time** to gather the little money needed for an offering, then walked to Purī to present his bhoga. He **reached the temple after the day's rituals had closed** — the deities had been put to rest for the night (the **paḥuḍa / night retirement** performed, the doors shut) — so that he **could not make his offering** and sat outside the closed temple **weeping**, his lifetime's hope undone. [oral tradition] [folk]
- **The night command — the central miracle** — that night Jagannātha, moved by the poor man's grief, **intervened**: in the common telling the Lord appears in a **dream to the temple servitor (or the king / superintendent)**, commanding that the temple be **reopened after hours** and that **Bandhu Mohanty be fed with mahāprasāda** — the sleeping Lord's own kitchen unstopped in the night for one shut-out devotee. Bandhu Mohanty is fed the **great grace-food** he had despaired of, by the Lord's direct order. [oral tradition] [folk]
  - *Variant:* some tellings have the offering **accepted directly** by the Lord that night rather than mediated by a dream-command, and stress that the strict temporal rule of temple-service was **suspended for a bhakta** — grace overriding ritual clock. [regional] [disputed]
- **Meaning** — where Sālabega's barrier is **creed** and Dāsiā's is **caste**, Bandhu Mohanty's is **poverty and the impersonal machinery of temple time**; the miracle asserts that **love, not wealth or the ritual timetable, commands the Lord** — a Purī gloss on the mahāprasāda theology that makes offered food the vehicle of liberation (**Kaivalya**). [regional] [scholarly]

## IV. The pattern, and the two clocks

- **One structure, three barriers** — all three legends run the same shape: a devotee is **excluded** (as *yavana*, as untouchable, as too poor / too late), loves anyway, and **the deity comes out to meet him** — halting a chariot, extending a hand, reopening a kitchen. Together they are Purī's standard sermon that **Jagannātha's grace is casteless, creedless, and cannot be timetabled**, dramatizing the same egalitarian logic as the temple's own mahāprasāda rule. [scholarly] [regional]
- **Transmission** — the triad lives in **bhajan (Sālabega's own songs), palm-leaf *bhakta-caritra*, kīrtan, and Mādaḷā Pāñji temple-chronicle lore**, not in the Sanskrit Purāṇas; they are **hagiographic / devotional history**, not text-critical record. [oral tradition] [folk]
- **Scriptural clock** — as *bhakta* legends they hang from the timeless māhātmya frame of Jagannātha's abode-status in **Kali-Yuga**; the miracles are undatable "grace-events." [oral tradition]
- **Academic clock** — the human cores are **early-modern**: **[scholarly] Sālabega c. 1607–1675 CE**; **Dāsiā Baurī and Bandhu Mohanty c. 16th c. CE**, in and after the age of the [Pañcasakhā](../../90-literature-corpus/odia-literature-sarala-and-panchasakha.md) and **[Caitanya](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/nimbarka-vallabha-chaitanya.md)** at Purī. The **living rites** (the Ratha Yātrā halt at Sālabega's samādhi) are ongoing historical practice — the mythic and the documented running on separate clocks, never collapsed. [scholarly] [disputed]
