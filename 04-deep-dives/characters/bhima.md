# Bhīma

> Where this sits: a **Dvāpara-era life**, second of the five [Pāṇḍava](../mahabharata/) brothers, spanning the whole [Mahābhārata](../mahabharata/) from a strength-marked infancy to the Kurukṣetra War and the final mountain-march. Bhīma belongs to the [Mahābhārata deep-dives](../mahabharata/) and to the [28th Mahāyuga, Dvāpara Yuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md), at the Dvāpara–[Kali](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) junction.
> Up: [04-deep-dives](../README.md) · Parent epic: [Mahābhārata](../mahabharata/) · Master index: [the-Hindu-timeline](../../README.md)

**Bhīma** (Sanskrit *Bhīma*, "the terrible / the formidable") is the second-born [Pāṇḍava](../mahabharata/), the **son of [Kuntī](../mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md) by Vāyu**, the wind-god, and therefore the **half-brother of [Hanumān](../ramayana/)** (also a son of Vāyu). He is the epic's incarnation of **raw physical power, appetite, and ferocious loyalty**: the strongest of the brothers, the family's mace-and-wrestling champion, the devourer of demons, and the avenger who personally fulfils the **two great oaths of the dice-hall** — to drink **Duḥśāsana's** blood and to shatter **Duryodhana's** thigh. His many names — **Vṛkodara** ("wolf-belly," for his hunger and strength), **Bhīmasena, Vāyuputra/Mārutātmaja** ("son of the wind"), **Jarāsandha-jit** ("conqueror of Jarāsandha"), **Ballava** (his cook-disguise at Virāṭa) — each mark a facet of that force.

---

## I. Birth and the wind-god's strength (Mbh Ādi Parva)

- **Son of Vāyu via the Durvāsa-mantra** - Unable to bear children herself because of [Pāṇḍu's](../mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md) curse, **Kuntī** uses the **boon-mantra of sage Durvāsa** to summon the **wind-god Vāyu**, who begets **Bhīma** in her — the second of the Pāṇḍavas, born after **Yudhiṣṭhira** (Dharma) and before **Arjuna** (Indra) (Mbh Ādi Parva ~123).
- **Born terrible** - The voice from heaven names him *Bhīma*; he inherits from Vāyu **superhuman strength, speed, and endurance** that make him from birth the mightiest of the brothers (Mbh Ādi Parva).
- **The shattered rock** - As an infant, slipping from Kuntī's lap on a Himālayan slope, the baby Bhīma **falls onto a rock and smashes it to powder while remaining unhurt** — the first omen of his strength (Mbh Ādi Parva ~123).
  - *Variant:* some retellings place the fall when a startled Kuntī, fleeing a tiger, drops him; the splitting of the stone is constant.
- **Half-brother of Hanumān** - Because both are **sons of Vāyu**, Bhīma and **[Hanumān](../ramayana/)** are spiritual brothers — a kinship made explicit later in the Saugandhika episode (see §V).

## II. Childhood, the poisoning, and the Nāga-nectar (Mbh Ādi Parva ~127–128)

- **Boyhood rivalry with the Kauravas** - The young Bhīma routinely **out-wrestles and out-eats the hundred sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra**, dunking them in rivers, shaking them from trees, and humiliating them in play — seeding **Duryodhana's** lifelong hatred (Mbh Ādi Parva ~127).
- **Poisoned and thrown in the Gaṅgā** - **Duryodhana**, with **Śakuni's** counsel, feeds Bhīma **poisoned food (kālakūṭa)** at a water-sports outing at **Pramāṇakoṭi**, binds the unconscious boy, and **casts him into the Gaṅgā** to drown (Mbh Ādi Parva ~128).
- **The Nāga realm and the strength-nectar** - Bhīma sinks to the **realm of the Nāgas (Nāgaloka/Rasātala)**, where the serpents' **venom neutralizes the poison**; the Nāga king **Vāsuki** (a kinsman through Kuntī's line) welcomes him and gives him **eight jars of *rasa* (divine nectar)**, **each conferring the strength of a thousand elephants** (Mbh Ādi Parva ~128).
  - *Variant:* tellings differ on the count and the host — commonly **eight draughts = the strength of ten thousand elephants** in sum; some say Bhīma drank for eight days, one cup per day.
- **Return after eight days** - Bhīma climbs back to the surface on the eighth day, his power now **magnified beyond any mortal**, and tells Kuntī and Yudhiṣṭhira of the plot — which they keep secret to avoid open war (Mbh Ādi Parva ~128).
- **The lacquer-house (Jatugṛha/Lākṣāgṛha)** - When the Kauravas trap the Pāṇḍavas in the **flammable house at Vāraṇāvata**, it is **Bhīma who carries Kuntī and his four brothers out of the inferno** on his shoulders and through the escape tunnel (Mbh Ādi Parva ~145–147).

## III. The forest demons: Hiḍimba, Bakāsura, Kirmīra, Jaṭāsura (Mbh Ādi & Vana Parvas)

- **Hiḍimba slain; marriage to Hiḍimbā** - Fleeing the lacquer-house through the forest, the Pāṇḍavas rest while Bhīma guards them; the cannibal **rākṣasa Hiḍimba** sends his sister **Hiḍimbā** to lure them, but she falls in love with Bhīma. Hiḍimba attacks; **Bhīma kills him in a furious wrestling match** (Mbh Ādi Parva ~154–155).
- **Ghaṭotkaca born** - With Kuntī's and Yudhiṣṭhira's consent, Bhīma weds **Hiḍimbā** for a season; their son **[Ghaṭotkaca](../mahabharata/07-drona-parva.md)** is born and grows instantly to manhood by rākṣasa nature, pledging to come whenever his father thinks of him (Mbh Ādi Parva ~155). Ghaṭotkaca later dies absorbing **Karṇa's** infallible **Śakti** spear in the [Droṇa Parva](../mahabharata/07-drona-parva.md) — the night-battle.
- **Bakāsura of Ekacakrā** - Living disguised as Brāhmaṇas in **Ekacakrā**, the Pāṇḍavas learn a man-eating rākṣasa, **Baka**, exacts a cartload of food and a human victim from the town in turn. Bhīma takes the family's turn, **eats the food himself on the way**, and when Baka attacks, **breaks his back across his knee**, freeing the town (Mbh Ādi Parva ~159–160).
- **Kirmīra of the Kāmyaka forest** - Hiḍimba's brother (or kinsman) **Kirmīra** ambushes the exiled Pāṇḍavas; Bhīma **uproots a tree and beats him, then strangles/dashes him to death**, avenging Baka and Hiḍimba (Mbh Vana Parva ~11).
- **Jaṭāsura** - The rākṣasa **Jaṭāsura**, posing as a Brāhmaṇa, abducts Yudhiṣṭhira, Draupadī, and the twins; Bhīma returns and **kills him in single combat** (Mbh Vana Parva ~157).
- **Maṇimān and the yakṣas** - In the Saugandhika quest (§V) Bhīma also slays the yakṣa **Maṇimān** and his guards at **Kubera's** lake (Mbh Vana Parva ~155).

## IV. Jarāsandha — the wrestling-duel and the "split body" (Mbh Sabhā Parva ~20–24)

- **The tyrant of Magadha** - **Jarāsandha**, king of Magadha and father-in-law of the slain **Kaṃsa**, has imprisoned **86 (var. 100) kings** for a mass human-sacrifice to Śiva (Rudra) and is the chief obstacle to Yudhiṣṭhira's **Rājasūya** (Mbh Sabhā Parva ~13–14).
- **The brāhmaṇa disguise** - **Kṛṣṇa, Bhīma, and Arjuna** enter Magadha **disguised as snātaka Brāhmaṇas**, reveal themselves, and **challenge Jarāsandha to single combat**; he picks **Bhīma**, the match of his own strength (Mbh Sabhā Parva ~21–22).
- **The marathon wrestling-match** - The two grapple **without food or rest for thirteen (var. fourteen / twenty-seven) days**; Jarāsandha cannot be killed because his body, born in two halves and joined by the rākṣasī **Jarā**, **rejoins each time Bhīma tears him apart** (Mbh Sabhā Parva ~23–24).
  - *Variant:* the duration is most often given as **thirteen** or **fourteen** days; folk retellings stretch it.
- **Kṛṣṇa's signal — the split leaf** - Exhausted, Bhīma sees Kṛṣṇa **tear a leaf (or blade of grass / a twig) lengthwise and fling the two halves in opposite directions**. Taking the hint, Bhīma **rends Jarāsandha from the crotch upward and hurls the two halves apart** so they cannot rejoin — killing him (Mbh Sabhā Parva ~24).
  - *Variant:* some tellings have Bhīma place his foot on one thigh and pull the other leg, splitting him head-down rather than crotch-up.
- **Result** - The **captive kings are freed**, **Sahadeva** (Jarāsandha's son) is enthroned as a vassal, and the path is cleared for the **Rājasūya** ([02 — Sabhā Parva](../mahabharata/02-sabha-parva.md)).

## V. Exile: the lotus-quest and the meeting with Hanumān (Mbh Vana Parva ~146–157)

- **The Saugandhika flower** - In the Himālayan exile a fragrant **Saugandhika (golden lotus)** is wind-borne to **Draupadī**, who longs for more; Bhīma sets off **northeast to fetch them**, smashing through forests (Mbh Vana Parva ~146).
- **Hanumān blocks the path** - In a plantain-grove (**Kadalīvana**) Bhīma finds an old monkey's **tail across the trail** and arrogantly orders it moved; unable to **lift the tail even with all his strength**, the humbled Bhīma realizes this is **[Hanumān](../ramayana/), his half-brother** (both sons of Vāyu) (Mbh Vana Parva ~147–149).
- **The blessing** - Hanumān shows Bhīma a glimpse of his cosmic **Tretā-yuga Setu-leaping form**, blesses him with added strength, and **vows to roar from Arjuna's chariot-banner (kapidhvaja)** in the coming war — directing him on to **Kubera's lake** (Mbh Vana Parva ~149–150).
- **Kubera's lake** - Bhīma fights and kills the yakṣa guards (**Maṇimān** among them), gathers the lotuses, and is granted them by **Kubera**, who recalls an old curse now lifted (Mbh Vana Parva ~152–155).

## VI. The dice-game and the great vows (Mbh Sabhā Parva ~58–72)

- **The rigged game** - In Hastināpura, **Śakuni** dices on Duryodhana's behalf and **Yudhiṣṭhira loses kingdom, brothers, himself, and finally Draupadī**; she is **dragged into the assembly by the hair** by **Duḥśāsana** and an attempt is made to **disrobe her** ([02 — Sabhā Parva](../mahabharata/02-sabha-parva.md)).
- **Duryodhana's insult — the bared thigh** - **Duryodhana**, mocking Draupadī, **slaps/bares his left thigh** and invites her to sit on it — a sexual taunt that ignites Bhīma's most famous oath (Mbh Sabhā Parva ~63).
- **Vow #1 — Duḥśāsana's blood** - Bhīma swears that **he will tear open Duḥśāsana's chest and drink his blood** for laying hands on Draupadī (Mbh Sabhā Parva ~68).
- **Vow #2 — Duryodhana's thigh** - Bhīma swears that **he will smash Duryodhana's thigh** with his mace in battle for the insult of baring it (Mbh Sabhā Parva ~68).
  - *Variant:* he is sometimes given a **third vow — to break the arm(s) of Duḥśāsana** (the arm that seized Draupadī's hair), and a vow to **kill all the Dhārtarāṣṭras**.
- **Held back by Yudhiṣṭhira** - Bhīma, ready to **burn Yudhiṣṭhira's gambling hands** in his rage, is restrained by Arjuna; the brothers go to **thirteen years' exile** carrying these oaths (Mbh Sabhā Parva).

## VII. The Kīcaka episode (Mbh Virāṭa Parva ~14–24)

- **Disguised as the cook Ballava** - In the **thirteenth (concealment) year** at King **Virāṭa's** court of Matsya, Bhīma serves as the royal cook **Ballava (Vallabha)**, also winning wrestling-bouts in the arena (Mbh Virāṭa Parva ~2, ~12).
- **Kīcaka's assault on Draupadī** - **Kīcaka**, Virāṭa's brother-in-law and army-chief, lusts after Draupadī (disguised as the maid **Sairandhrī**); he **molests and kicks her** in open court, and she secretly begs Bhīma's help (Mbh Virāṭa Parva ~15–16).
- **The night ambush** - Bhīma has Draupadī **lure Kīcaka to a dark dancing-hall**; lying in her place, Bhīma **seizes Kīcaka and pounds his limbs, head, and joints into a shapeless ball of flesh ("a tortoise / a ball")**, killing him (Mbh Virāṭa Parva ~21).
- **The Upakīcakas** - Kīcaka's **105 kinsmen (Upakīcakas)**, trying to **burn Draupadī on Kīcaka's pyre**, are all **killed by Bhīma**, who uproots a tree and falls on them (Mbh Virāṭa Parva ~22–23).

## VIII. The Kurukṣetra War — slaying the hundred Kauravas (Mbh Bhīṣma–Śalya Parvas)

- **The mace-and-club champion** - Trained by **Balarāma** (with Duryodhana) and a master of the **gadā (mace)**, Bhīma is the Pāṇḍavas' shock-troop, also a fierce archer and elephant-fighter; he is reckoned to have the **strength of many thousands of elephants** (Mbh Bhīṣma Parva onward).
- **He kills all 100 sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra** - Across the eighteen days Bhīma **personally slays every one of the hundred Kaurava brothers** — Vikarṇa, Citrasena, Vivṃśati, Duḥsaha, and the rest — leaving Duryodhana last (Mbh Droṇa/Śalya Parvas; the count of 100 is the epic's own tally).
  - *Variant:* a few brothers are killed by other Pāṇḍavas in some recensions, but the **mainline tradition credits Bhīma with all hundred**, fulfilling his dice-hall vow.
- **The death of Duḥśāsana — the blood-oath fulfilled** - On the **17th day** Bhīma **breaks Duḥśāsana's chariot, tears off his arm, rips open his chest, and drinks (or laps) his warm blood** — then smears it toward Draupadī's unbound hair, fulfilling Vow #1 (Mbh Karṇa Parva ~83; [08 — Karṇa Parva](../mahabharata/08-karna-parva.md)).
  - *Variant [folk]:* a widespread tradition holds that **Draupadī then washes/ties her hair with Duḥśāsana's blood**, the oath she made at the dice-hall; this scene is dramatized in folk-theatre and TV but is **not in the critical text** [late text].
  - *Variant:* some say Bhīma only **tasted/smeared** the blood rather than truly drinking it, to keep his vow's letter without true cannibalism.
- **Other Kaurava-side kills** - Bhīma also slays **Bāhlīka, Bhagadatta's allies**, many of **Śakuni's** Gāndhāra kin, and crushes vast infantry and elephant divisions; he and Ghaṭotkaca anchor the Pāṇḍava night-fighting (Mbh Droṇa Parva).

---

## IX. The mace-duel with Duryodhana and the thigh-blow (Mbh Śalya Parva ~31–60)

- **Duryodhana's last stand** - After the army is annihilated, **Duryodhana hides in Lake Dvaipāyana** (stupefying the waters); taunted out by the Pāṇḍavas, he agrees to a **single mace-duel** and chooses **Bhīma** (Mbh Śalya Parva ~28–32).
- **Balarāma presides** - **Balarāma**, guru to both fighters, arrives to witness the bout at **Samantapañcaka/Kurukṣetra** (Mbh Śalya Parva ~54).
- **Duryodhana the better technician** - In fair mace-craft **Duryodhana is the superior fighter** and steadily wears Bhīma down, nearly felling him (Mbh Śalya Parva ~57–58).
- **Kṛṣṇa's reminder — the thigh-slap** - Seeing Bhīma flag, **Kṛṣṇa slaps his own thigh** to remind Bhīma of his dice-hall vow; Bhīma understands the cue (Mbh Śalya Parva ~58).
- **The illegal thigh-blow** - Bhīma feints, then **smashes his mace into Duryodhana's thighs**, shattering both and felling him — a **strike below the navel, forbidden in mace-duelling rules** — fulfilling Vow #2 (Mbh Śalya Parva ~58).
- **The kick and Balarāma's fury** - Bhīma **kicks/places his foot on Duryodhana's crowned head**; **Balarāma**, enraged at the foul blow, **raises his plough to attack Bhīma** but is restrained by Kṛṣṇa, who justifies the blow as karmic payment for the bared thigh (Mbh Śalya Parva ~59–60).
  - *Variant [scholarly]:* the foul-blow is precisely the epic's **dharma-paradox** — Kṛṣṇa argues *adharma* served *dharma* (Duryodhana could not be beaten fairly); commentators treat it as one of the war's "five foul deeds."
- **Aftermath** - The dying Duryodhana's wrath at the unfair blow fuels **Aśvatthāman's** night-massacre of the Pāṇḍava camp ([10 — Sauptika Parva](../mahabharata/10-sauptika-parva.md)).

## X. Later life, the great departure, and themes

- **King's mace-bearer and crown prince's strength** - In the peacetime reign, Bhīma is **Yudhiṣṭhira's right arm**; he confronts the blind **Dhṛtarāṣṭra** with harsh words over the slain Kauravas, and later Dhṛtarāṣṭra's revenge-attempt (a crushing iron statue) is foiled by Kṛṣṇa (Mbh Śānti / Āśramavāsika Parvas).
- **The great departure (Mahāprasthāna)** - On the final Himālayan march toward heaven, **Bhīma is the fourth (var. last of the brothers) to fall**; Yudhiṣṭhira explains his flaw was **gluttony / pride in his strength and over-eating** ([17 — Mahāprasthānika Parva](../mahabharata/17-mahaprasthanika-parva.md)).
  - *Variant:* the order of the falls is fixed in the text — **Draupadī, Sahadeva, Nakula, Arjuna, Bhīma**, then Yudhiṣṭhira alone with the dog.
- **Wives and sons** - **Hiḍimbā** (son **Ghaṭotkaca**); **Draupadī** (shared, son **Sutasoma**); **Balandharā** of Kāśī (son **Sarvaga/Sarvagata**) (Mbh Ādi/Vana references).
- **Themes** - Bhīma embodies **bala (might), bhakṣaṇa (appetite), and pratijñā (the binding vow)**: the brother who **does** what the others deliberate, the instrument of vengeance whose terrible deeds (thigh-blow, blood-drinking) are the epic's hardest tests of when force becomes dharma.
- **Afterlife in tradition** - Folk worship of **Bhīma/Vṛkodara** as a strongman-deity persists in tribal and Himālayan cults; he is the patron of wrestlers and the comic-and-fearsome favorite of folk-theatre [folk].
