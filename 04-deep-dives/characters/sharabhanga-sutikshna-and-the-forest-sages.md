# Śarabhaṅga, Sutīkṣṇa and the Forest Sages

> The **bench of ascetics who receive Rāma in the Daṇḍaka** and, between them, structure the whole first half of the [Araṇya-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md): **Śarabhaṅga**, who keeps Indra's chariot waiting rather than die before seeing Rāma and then walks into his own fire; the **ṛṣi delegation** that lays the forest's bones at his feet and extracts the vow the rest of the book pays for; **Sutīkṣṇa**, who has likewise refused heaven and offers Rāma the worlds he won by austerity; **Dharmabhṛta**, who explains the singing lake; and **Agastya's brother**, the unnamed sage one yojana short of the arsenal. Each hands Rāma on to the next — a relay of hermitages that ends with a bow, a quiver and the road to Pañcavaṭī.

The Daṇḍaka sages are usually summarised in a line — "Rāma visits some hermitages" — and the line loses the architecture. Vālmīki builds the opening third of the [Araṇya-kāṇḍa](../ramayana/3-aranya-kanda.md) as a **chain of hand-offs**: the dying [Virādha](./viradha.md) names Śarabhaṅga; Śarabhaṅga names Sutīkṣṇa and the river to follow; Sutīkṣṇa names the road to Agastya; Agastya's brother stands one yojana short of Agastya; and Agastya names Pañcavaṭī, where the abduction will happen. Not one of these sages needs anything from Rāma — two of them have already been offered heaven and turned it down to wait for him, and both make him a present of their accumulated worlds. What the forest wants from him is stated once, collectively, by a delegation carrying **the bones of eaten ascetics** (Araṇya 6), and Rāma's answer to it is the vow that makes [Khara's](./khara-and-the-janasthana-command.md) war and everything after it inevitable. The bench is not scenery. It is the mechanism by which a prince in exile becomes the forest's armed protector.

## I. The shape of the relay (Rām. Araṇya-kāṇḍa 1–14)

- **Entry into Daṇḍaka (Araṇya 1)** — the trio come first upon a **ring of hermitages** — darbha grass and bark cloth, the three ritual fires, oblation vessels, lotus ponds and fruit trees, "resounding with Brahmā's own recitation." The ascetics bless Rāma on sight, feed him with roots and fruit, and put the request to him at once: **be our protector** (Rām. Araṇya 1).
- **Virādha hands him on (Araṇya 4)** — the buried rākṣasa, released into his true form as the gandharva **Tumburu**, directs the brothers to **Śarabhaṅga's** hermitage before ascending. This is plain vulgate text, not a recension-variant (Rām. Araṇya 4). → [Virādha](./viradha.md).
- **The chain thereafter** — Śarabhaṅga → Sutīkṣṇa (5) → the ṛṣi delegation and the vow (6) → Sutīkṣṇa's hermitage and the night there (7–8) → **[Sītā's](./sita.md) counsel against the vow** on the road (9) and Rāma's reply that the promise outranks his life (10) → ten years among the Daṇḍaka hermitages, the **Pañcāpsaras lake**, and **Agastya's brother** (11) → **[Agastya](../agastya.md)** and the arsenal (12) → **Pañcavaṭī** named (13) → **[Jaṭāyu](./jatayu-and-sampati.md)** met on arrival (14).

## II. Śarabhaṅga — the sage who kept Indra waiting (Rām. Araṇya-kāṇḍa 5)

- **Indra in the sky above the hermitage** — Rāma arrives to find a chariot standing in the air, blazing "like the sun and like fire," **yoked with green horses** (*haritair vājibhir yuktam*) and attended by the host of the gods: **Indra** has come in person to carry Śarabhaṅga to **Brahmaloka**, the reward of his austerity. Rāma sees it from a distance and asks what it is (Rām. Araṇya 5).
- **Why he would not go** — Indra departs without him, because the sage refuses to leave: *"knowing you were near, tiger among men, I do not go to Brahmā's world without having seen my beloved guest with these eyes"* (Rām. Araṇya 5). A sage postpones heaven for a *darśana*; that is the beat.
- **Why Indra will not speak to him** — the god leaves before Rāma arrives, and says why: Rāma must be let go to his vow first, and *"after the purpose of his incarnation is accomplished, then he is fit to see me"* (Rām. Araṇya 5). The commentators read this as the epic protecting its own premise — open traffic between Rāma and the gods would advertise that Rāvaṇa faces Viṣṇu and not a man, and Rāvaṇa's boon exempts him only from gods. The scene is one of the few places the text handles the avatāra's incognito explicitly. [scholarly]
- **The gift of the worlds** — Śarabhaṅga offers Rāma outright **all the imperishable worlds he has won by penance** — the Brahmā-worlds and the heavens — to take as his own (Rām. Araṇya 5).
  - *On Rāma's answer:* the reply is famously two-edged and the commentators split on it. Read plainly, Rāma **declines** — he says he will win those worlds himself, and asks instead only to be shown **a place in this forest to live**. Read theologically (the Vaiṣṇava commentators), he accepts what is already his and asks the sage for the one thing a god-in-exile lacks, a dwelling. The corpus records both readings; the *narrative* consequence is the same either way — the sage answers with a direction, not a hermitage. [scholarly]
- **The fire-departure** — Śarabhaṅga then heaps a pyre, pours the oblation, and **enters the flame himself**. The fire consumes "his hair and body-hair, his aged skin, his bones, his flesh and his blood," and out of it he rises **as a youth, radiant like fire** — passing upward through the worlds of the sages and the gods to **Brahmā's own seat**, where Brahmā welcomes him (Rām. Araṇya 5). This is not a cremation: it is a **yogic self-offering**, the body given as the last oblation of a life of sacrifice.
- **The route he leaves behind** — his last act is wayfinding. He names **Sutīkṣṇa** — "a great and resplendent sage lives in this forest" — and tells Rāma to **follow the Mandākinī against its current**, the river that carries flower-ferries, until he reaches the hermitage (Rām. Araṇya 5).
  - *Note (naming):* Vālmīki gives no back-story for Śarabhaṅga — no parentage, no earlier deed, no curse. He exists in the epic for exactly one sarga and is defined wholly by what he gives up in it. Retellings that supply him a biography are supplying it. [scholarly]

## III. The delegation, the bones, and the vow (Rām. Araṇya-kāṇḍa 6)

The single most consequential scene of the bench, and the one the summaries drop.

- **Who comes** — with Śarabhaṅga gone, the ascetics of the whole quarter converge on Rāma in a body. Vālmīki names them by **order of austerity**, a taxonomy found almost nowhere else in the epic: **vaikhānasas** (born of Prajāpati's nails) and **vālakhilyas** (of his hair), **saṃprakṣālas** (of his feet-washing), **marīcipas** who drink the rays of sun and moon, **aśmakuṭṭas** who pound their grain — and themselves — with stones, **patrāhāras** who eat only leaves, **dantolūkhalinas** who use their teeth for a mortar, **unmajjakas** who stand neck-deep in water, **gātraśayyas** who make a bed of their own limbs, **anavakāśikas** who allow themselves no rest, **salilāhāras** and **vāyubhakṣas** who live on water and on air, **ākāśanilayas** who meditate aloft, and **sthaṇḍilaśāyins** who sleep on bare ground (Rām. Araṇya 6.2–4).
- **What they show him** — not a petition but an exhibit. They lead Rāma to see **the bodies of contemplative sages killed in various ways by the rākṣasas** of the forest, strewn along the Pampā, the Mandākinī and about Citrakūṭa, and tell him the toll is continuous (Rām. Araṇya 6.16). The forest's grievance is presented as evidence.
- **The argument** — they make it in the language of kingship, not of charity: a king takes a **sixth of the realm's merit** and owes protection in exchange; the exile is still the king's son, and the ascetics are still subjects (Rām. Araṇya 6).
- **Rāma's vow** — he says he needs no petitioning — *"I am at your behest"* — that he entered the forest precisely to **destroy the enemies of the sages**, and that they shall see his valour and his brother's in doing it (Rām. Araṇya 6.25). It is the promise that the [Śūrpaṇakhā](./shurpanakha.md) episode, the fourteen thousand of Janasthāna, and ultimately the abduction all issue from.
  - *The counter-argument, kept in the corpus:* [Sītā](./sita.md) puts the case against this vow three sargas later — that bearing arms in a forest of ascetics is itself a subtle vice, and that hostility unprovoked is one of the three sins born of desire (Araṇya 9). Rāma's answer, that **a promise given to sages outranks Lakṣmaṇa, Sītā and his own life**, is the keystone of his forest conduct (Araṇya 10). The two speeches belong to this scene and should be read with it.

## IV. Sutīkṣṇa — the second refusal of heaven (Rām. Araṇya-kāṇḍa 7–8, 11)

- **The reception** — Rāma follows the Mandākinī upstream to **Sutīkṣṇa**, seated in matted hair and bark, who greets him *"Welcome, Rāma, best of the Raghus"* and offers the hermitage itself as a residence: it has roots and fruits always (Rām. Araṇya 7).
- **The worlds again** — Sutīkṣṇa repeats Śarabhaṅga's gesture and states its motive outright: **Indra had come to him too**, telling him he had conquered all the worlds by his merit — and he sent the god away. *"I am waiting for you alone, O illustrious one, without casting off this body and ascending"* — and he makes over to Rāma the heavens his austerity has bought (Rām. Araṇya 7.9–11). Two sages in three sargas hold their own liberation in escrow against Rāma's arrival; the epic is establishing that the forest has been expecting him.
- **The herds, and why they do not stay** — the sage warns that **great herds of animals** come to the hermitage and disturb it. Rāma offers to clear them with his arrows; then, watching Sutīkṣṇa's tenderness toward the same beasts, he judges the place wrong for a long stay and does not (Rām. Araṇya 7). The scene is a small, exact study of the tension Sītā will name at Araṇya 9 — the archer inside the sanctuary.
- **The leave-taking** — after the night there they ask permission to go; Sutīkṣṇa blesses them, tells them to see the woods and waters of Daṇḍaka and **to come back to this hermitage afterwards**, and **Sītā herself hands the brothers their quivers, bows and swords** as they leave (Rām. Araṇya 8) — a detail worth keeping beside her ahiṃsā speech one sarga later.
- **The return, and the road to Agastya** — after the compressed **ten years** of moving among the Daṇḍaka hermitages, the family comes back to Sutīkṣṇa exactly as told, and Rāma asks the way to **[Agastya](../agastya.md)**. Sutīkṣṇa gives the directions southward and sends them on (Rām. Araṇya 11).
  - *Note (the "disciple of Agastya" gloss):* Sutīkṣṇa is widely described, in retellings and in summaries alike, as **Agastya's disciple**. Vālmīki does not say so: in the sargas where Sutīkṣṇa appears (7–8, 11) Agastya is named only as "the great sage," with no relation of teacher, brother or pupil stated, and Rāma has to ask where he lives. The discipleship is traditional harmonisation, not text. [scholarly]

## V. Dharmabhṛta and the singing lake (Rām. Araṇya-kāṇḍa 11)

- **The ascetic who explains the marvel** — on the road south the party passes a lake from which **song, instruments and the jingle of women's ornaments** rise with nobody visible on the banks. It is the ascetic **Dharmabhṛta** ("dharma-bearer"), one of the munis travelling with them, who names the wonder: the sage **Māṇḍakarṇi** stood in this water ten thousand years living on air, the alarmed gods sent **five apsaras**, he took all five to wife and built a **mansion hidden beneath the lake**, and the music is theirs. Hence the pool's name, **Pañcāpsaras** (Rām. Araṇya 11).
- **Why the narrator's name matters** — Vālmīki assigns the telling to a **named** sage, not to "a hermit"; abridgements that fold the explanation into Sutīkṣṇa's or Agastya's mouth lose the epic's own attribution. [scholarly] → the legend's canonical home: [Māṇḍakarṇi and the Pañcāpsaras lake](../mandakarni-and-panchapsaras-lake.md).

## VI. Agastya's brother — the sage Vālmīki leaves unnamed (Rām. Araṇya-kāṇḍa 11)

- **The stage before the last** — **four yojanas** from Sutīkṣṇa's hermitage stands the hermitage of **Agastya's brother**, and **one yojana** beyond that Agastya's own. The relay's final two stops are measured in the text (Rām. Araṇya 11).
- **No name in the text** — Vālmīki calls him only *Agastya's brother*, never anything else. The commentarial tradition supplies **Sudarśana**, and the standard translations note the name precisely in order to record that **the poem never uses it**. The corpus keeps him unnamed with the gloss attached, rather than promoting a commentator's name into the text. [scholarly]
- **What his hermitage is** — a grove of flowering and fruiting trees with the sacred fires burning, frequented by gods, gandharvas, siddhas and great sages; the text says plainly that **liars, the cruel, the deceitful and the sinful cannot live there**, and that those who worship there with devotion are granted celestial states. The brothers and Sītā spend the night and go on at dawn (Rām. Araṇya 11).
- **The story told on this leg** — it is here, approaching the two hermitages, that **Rāma himself** narrates to Lakṣmaṇa the tale of **Ilvala and Vātāpi** — the ram-shaped demon served to brahmin guests and called out of their bellies, until Agastya digested him past recall and burnt Ilvala with a glance. That the Rāmāyaṇa carries its own version of the tale, in the hero's own voice, rather than only the Mahābhārata's, is the detail most retellings drop (Rām. Araṇya 11). → [Agastya §3, §5](../agastya.md).
  - *Note:* the deed is **Agastya's**, not the brother's. The brother's hermitage is the setting of the telling, not its subject — a conflation easy to make and worth refusing.

## VII. Why the bench matters

- **It converts an exile into a campaign** — Rāma enters Daṇḍaka as a banished prince keeping a fourteen-year term; he leaves the hermitage chain as a sworn protector under arms, carrying **Viṣṇu's bow, Brahmā's arrow, Indra's two inexhaustible quivers and a gold-sheathed sword** (Araṇya 12). The sages are the ones who ask for it, and the ones who supply the weapons. → [Daṇḍakāraṇya](../../06-sacred-geography/dandakaranya-rama-forest.md).
- **It is a relay, and the relay is the plot** — every stop names the next, and the last one names **Pañcavaṭī**. The route to the abduction is laid out for Rāma by holy men, each of whom means him nothing but good. [scholarly]
- **Two men refuse heaven for him before a single demon is killed** — Śarabhaṅga and Sutīkṣṇa both hold off their own ascent to see him and both hand him their accumulated worlds. Long before the vānaras or Vibhīṣaṇa, the epic has established that **what Rāma is owed, he is offered unasked** — and that he does not take it. [scholarly]
- **The taxonomy of austerity (Araṇya 6.2–4)** is, incidentally, one of the fullest lists of ascetic orders anywhere in the epics, and the one-line summaries erase it entirely.
