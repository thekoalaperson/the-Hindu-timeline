# Gāndhārī — the Blindfolded Queen of Hāstinapura

> Where this sits: a **Dvāpara-era life** at the Dvāpara–Kali junction — the Kuru queen whose hundred sons fall at [Kurukṣetra](../mahabharata/README.md) and whose curse sets the clock to the Yādava ruin. Up: [Epic Characters](README.md) · Parent epic: [Mahābhārata](../mahabharata/README.md). Back to the [master index](../../README.md).

**Gāndhārī** (Sanskrit *Gāndhārī*, "she of Gandhāra," her personal name often given as a princess of the Gandhāra realm, modern Kandahar/north-west Pakistan) is the daughter of King **Subala** of Gandhāra, sister of **[Śakuni](../mahabharata/02-sabha-parva.md)**, wife of the blind Kuru king **Dhṛtarāṣṭra**, and mother of the **hundred [Kauravas](../mahabharata/01-adi-parva.md)** led by **[Duryodhana](duryodhana.md)**. She is one of the great moral figures of the epic: a wife so faithful that she binds her own eyes for life so as not to outshine her sightless husband, a mother whose love for her sons never quite overrides her sense of justice, and finally the grieving matriarch whose curse upon **Kṛṣṇa** in the [Strī Parva](../mahabharata/11-stri-parva.md) seals the death of the Yādava clan in the [Mausala Parva](../mahabharata/16-mausala-parva.md). The text treats her as a *pativratā* of formidable austerity (*tapas*), her accumulated power literally able to scorch and to curse.

---

## I. Marriage and the self-blindfolding (Mbh Ādi Parva)

- **Princess of Gandhāra** — daughter of **Subala**, king of Gandhāra, and sister of **Śakuni**; given in marriage to **Dhṛtarāṣṭra**, the blind elder Kuru prince, in an alliance arranged by **Bhīṣma** for the Kuru house (Mbh Ādi Parva). 
- **The vow of the blindfold** — on learning her husband is congenitally blind, Gāndhārī **binds a folded cloth over her own eyes** and vows to share his darkness for life, "not wishing to enjoy what her lord could not" — the act of *pativratā* devotion that defines her (Mbh Ādi Parva).
  - *Variant (motive, devotional gloss):* the self-blinding is read less as renunciation than as an austerity that **stores up tapas-power**, the same store she later expends in her divine sight and her curse. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Śakuni's grievance, late/folk):* some retellings hold that **Śakuni** resented the marriage of his sister to a blind king and bore a lifelong grudge against the Kurus, framing his later dice-intrigue as revenge — a motivation elaborated in regional and televised tellings rather than fixed in the critical text. [folk] [late text]
  - *Variant (the boon of a faithful wife):* in some traditions her devotion earns her the boon (or the latent power) by which her gaze, when at last unveiled even partially, can affect what it falls upon — anticipating the scorching of Yudhiṣṭhira's nails. [folk]

## II. The boon of a hundred sons and the flesh-ball birth (Mbh Ādi Parva ~107–108)

- **Vyāsa's boon of a hundred sons** — Gāndhārī tends the sage **[Vyāsa](../vyasa.md)** during his stay and, pleased, he grants her the boon of **a hundred sons (śata-putra)** equal to her husband in strength (Mbh Ādi Parva). This is the boon on which the whole Kaurava line depends.
- **The two-year pregnancy and the blow** — Gāndhārī conceives but carries the embryo **two full years** without delivery; hearing that **Kuntī** has already borne **Yudhiṣṭhira**, she strikes her own womb in jealous grief and miscarries a hard **ball of flesh (māṃsa-peśī)**, lifeless (Mbh Ādi Parva ~107).
- **Vyāsa's incubation in jars** — summoned, Vyāsa forbids her to discard the mass; he sprinkles it with water, has it **divided into a hundred and one thumb-sized pieces**, and seals each in a **pot of ghee (ghṛta-kumbha)** for incubation, so fulfilling the boon (Mbh Ādi Parva ~107). See [Duryodhana](duryodhana.md) for the births that follow.
  - *Variant:* the **101st piece**, requested by Gāndhārī for a daughter, becomes **Duḥśalā**, the only Kaurava sister.
- **Duryodhana's ill-omened birth** — when the first jar opens to yield **Duryodhana**, jackals howl and fires blaze in evil directions; Vidura and the sages urge the king to abandon this one son to save the line, but Gāndhārī and Dhṛtarāṣṭra, out of love, **rear him** — the epic's first fatal indulgence (Mbh Ādi Parva ~108).

## III. The restraining mother — counsel and rebuke (Mbh Sabhā / Udyoga Parvas)

- **At Draupadī's outrage** — during the dice-hall disrobing, Gāndhārī (with Vidura) protests the treatment of Draupadī and, with dire omens, helps frighten the blind king into **granting Draupadī the boons** that free the Pāṇḍavas (Mbh Sabhā Parva).
- **"Where there is dharma, there is victory"** — on the eve of war, summoned to counsel her son, Gāndhārī refuses to bless Duryodhana with victory, telling him plainly **yato dharmas tato jayaḥ** ("where there is righteousness, there is victory") — she will pray for the right, not for him (Mbh Udyoga Parva). Her warnings, like Bhīṣma's and Vidura's, fail against his resolve.
- **The one moment of a mother's power [folk/late text]** — a widely-told tradition holds that, before the duel with Bhīma, Gāndhārī summons Duryodhana to her, intending to **unveil her eyes** and make his entire body adamantine with her tapas-charged gaze; Kṛṣṇa intercepts him and shames him into wearing a loincloth, so that his **thighs remain unhardened** — the spot Bhīma later shatters. Absent from the critical text; a popular bridge-narrative for the thigh-weakness. [folk] [late text]

## IV. The Strī-Parva threnody — the divine eye over Kurukṣetra (Mbh Strī Parva)

- **The procession to the field** — after the eighteen days, Gāndhārī sets out with Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Kuntī, Draupadī and the Kaurava and Pāṇḍava women to the corpse-strewn plain for the rites (Mbh XI). See the full book at [Strī Parva](../mahabharata/11-stri-parva.md).
- **The scorched nails of Yudhiṣṭhira** — as Yudhiṣṭhira approaches, Gāndhārī's grief-wrath nearly breaks loose; through the gap below her blindfold her glance falls on the king and **scorches his fingernails (or toenails)**; Vyāsa and Kṛṣṇa restrain her, and she relents (Mbh XI).
  - *Variant:* fingernails vs. toenails differs by translation. [folk]
- **The divine eye (divya-cakṣus)** — by the power of her lifelong austerity (and, in many tellings, a **boon of vision from Vyāsa**), the still-blindfolded queen surveys the **whole field of Kurukṣetra from afar** and describes the carnage corpse by corpse to Kṛṣṇa — the structural heart of the parva and one of the epic's set-piece laments (Mbh XI.16ff).
- **Over Duryodhana's body** — she sees her eldest son slain, his thighs shattered by Bhīma's mace, and faints; she moves across the field naming the slain — **Karṇa, Abhimanyu, Droṇa, Bhīṣma, Jayadratha** (mourned doubly beside her daughter Duḥśalā), **Śalya, Śakuni** her brother, and the wandering, half-mad widows of both armies (Mbh XI.16–25).

## V. The curse on Kṛṣṇa — setting the 36-year clock (Mbh Strī Parva ~25)

- **The accusation** — Gāndhārī charges **Kṛṣṇa** with having had the power and knowledge to halt the slaughter yet permitting kinsmen — Kuru and Pāṇḍava both — to annihilate one another (Mbh XI.25).
- **The curse [pivotal]** — in her grief she curses that **in the thirty-sixth year** Kṛṣṇa will watch his own **[Yādava](../mahabharata/16-mausala-parva.md) (Vṛṣṇi–Andhaka)** kin destroy one another exactly as the Kurus did, that the Yādava women will wail as the Bhārata widows now wail, and that Kṛṣṇa himself will **die alone and ingloriously in the wilderness** (Mbh XI.25). This is the clock that runs out in the [Mausala Parva](../mahabharata/16-mausala-parva.md).
- **Kṛṣṇa's acceptance** — Kṛṣṇa accepts with a faint smile, saying she has only confirmed what he himself willed — that none could destroy the Vṛṣṇis but themselves — and gently reminds her that she too, by not restraining Duryodhana, shares the burden of the war (Mbh XI.25).
  - *Variant (converging cause):* the Yādava ruin is also pinned on the **sages' curse** at Pindāraka (Viśvāmitra, Kaṇva, Nārada) on Sāmba's iron club; Gāndhārī's curse and the sages' curse are presented as **converging causes**, both fulfilled in the Mausala. [catalog both] (Mbh XVI)
  - *Variant (theological reading):* later Vaiṣṇava commentary frames the curse not as a binding on the avatāra but as Kṛṣṇa's own **līlā** — the divine choosing the manner of his withdrawal at the dawn of Kali. [scholarly]

## VI. The forest retreat and death by wildfire (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva ~37–39)

- **Withdrawal to the forest (vānaprastha)** — about fifteen years after the war, Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Gāndhārī, **Kuntī**, **Vidura** and **Sañjaya** renounce the kingdom and retire to a forest hermitage on the Gaṅgā for ascetic life (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva).
- **Vyāsa raises the dead for a night** — at the grieving mothers' wish, **Vyāsa** calls up the slain warriors from the **Gaṅgā** for a single night so Gāndhārī and Kuntī may behold their fallen sons once more before they return to the water (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva).
- **Death in the wildfire** — two years on, a **forest conflagration (dāvāgni)** sweeps the hermitage; the aged, blind Dhṛtarāṣṭra, **Gāndhārī**, and **Kuntī** — refusing to flee, fixed in yoga (some tellings) or simply overtaken — are consumed together in the flames. **Sañjaya** survives and brings the news; **Nārada** reports their deaths to Yudhiṣṭhira (Mbh Āśramavāsika Parva ~37–39).
  - *Variant (the fire's origin):* the blaze is variously their own **sacred agnihotra fire** left burning when they had moved on (Dhṛtarāṣṭra's own ritual fire), or an ordinary **forest wildfire** — the critical text leaves the queen unflinching either way. [catalog both]

## VII. Character and themes (epic & scholarly)

- **The ideal pativratā turned tragic** — Gāndhārī embodies wifely fidelity carried to its severest limit; the same blindfold that signals devotion also figures her culpable refusal to "see" her sons' wrongdoing until the field is full of their corpses.
- **Tapas as power** — her stored austerity is shown as a concrete force: it scorches nails, grants panoramic vision, and binds the most powerful curse in the epic upon an avatāra. [scholarly]
- **The shared blame** — Kṛṣṇa's gentle counter (that her own indulgence of Duryodhana helped cause the war) makes her one of the epic's morally weighted figures, neither wholly victim nor wholly innocent.
- **Cross-links** — her son [Duryodhana](duryodhana.md); the boon-giver and consoler [Vyāsa](../vyasa.md); her lament in the [Strī Parva](../mahabharata/11-stri-parva.md); the fulfilment of her curse in the [Mausala Parva](../mahabharata/16-mausala-parva.md); her brother [Śakuni](../mahabharata/02-sabha-parva.md).

## Two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic:** Gāndhārī's life spans the **Dvāpara–Kali junction** of the current (28th) Mahāyuga; her curse explicitly meters out the **36 years** from Kurukṣetra (war traditionally ~3138 BCE) to Kṛṣṇa's departure and the **onset of Kali Yuga (3102 BCE)**.
- **Academic / historical:** as with the rest of the epic's cast, "Gāndhārī" belongs to a poem redacted over centuries (c. 400 BCE–400 CE); her Gandhāra origin is sometimes read as a memory of north-western marriage-alliances woven into the Kuru saga rather than a datable biography. [scholarly]
