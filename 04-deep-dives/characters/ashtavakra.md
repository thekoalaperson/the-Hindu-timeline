# Aṣṭāvakra

> Where this sits: a dedicated deep-dive on **Aṣṭāvakra** (Aṣṭāvakra, "eight-bent") — the boy-sage of Videha whose body was twisted in eight places by a womb-curse, who at twelve (or ten) shattered the unbeaten debater **Bandin/Vandin** at King **Janaka's** sacrifice and so freed his drowned father, and to whom tradition ascribes the radical Advaita text the **Aṣṭāvakra Gītā**.
> Up to [Epic Characters](./README.md) · [Deep Dives](../README.md) · master [index](../../README.md). Cosmic address: deep [Tretā/Dvāpara antiquity](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md) of the [28th Mahāyuga](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md); narrated *within* the [Mahābhārata](../mahabharata/README.md) as an older tale. Two clocks: scripture sets him in the age of the great ṛṣis; academic dating of the *Aṣṭāvakra Gītā* is post-Bhagavad-Gītā and is given separately below. [scholarly]
> Companion to his lineage-kin [Vishvāmitra–Vasiṣṭha](../vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) milieu of ṛṣi rivalries, the [lesser-Gītās catalog](../lesser-gitas-catalog.md), and the framing exile-tale-tellers of the [Mahābhārata Vana Parva](../mahabharata/README.md).

**Aṣṭāvakra** is the great paradox of the tradition: the body that is a catalog of defects housing the mind that has no defect at all. Cursed in the womb for correcting his father's faulty Vedic recitation, he is born bent at eight joints; orphaned in effect when that same father is drowned by an unbeatable court-debater; and then, a child, he walks into the court of the philosopher-king **Janaka**, is mocked for his crooked frame, turns the mockery into a teaching on the difference between the warped vessel and the straight Self within, out-argues the deathless **Bandin**, frees the drowned brāhmaṇas including his father, and is finally made straight by bathing in a sacred river. The frame is told by the ṛṣi **Lomaśa** to the exiled Pāṇḍavas on pilgrimage. (Vyāsa, *Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva, chs. 132–134; Ganguli tr.)

## Names & Identity
- **Aṣṭāvakra (अष्टावक्र)** - "eight-bent / crooked in eight places" (*aṣṭan* = eight + *vakra* = bent/crooked), naming the eight angular deformities of his limbs, neck, chest and feet. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII)
- **The boy-ṛṣi of Videha** - born and raised at the hermitage of his maternal grandfather in/near Videha (Mithilā; tradition locates it at Janakpur in present Nepal). (Mbh, Vana P.)
- **Author-by-ascription of the *Aṣṭāvakra Gītā*** - the Advaita dialogue is attributed to him as teacher of King Janaka, though its composition is far later than the epic milieu (see Dating). [scholarly]
  - *Variant (entirely separate Aṣṭāvakra):* a late/folk strand makes "Aṣṭāvakra" not the womb-cursed boy at all but a sage **Devala** twisted into eight bends by the apsarā **Rambhā's** curse after he spurned her; this is a doublet etiology for the same name, not the Vana-Parva hero. [folk] [late text]

## Lineage — A House of Vedic Sages
- **Father: Kahoḍa (Kahola)** - a learned but proud brāhmaṇa, devoted disciple of Uddālaka; in some tellings titled **Vājaśravas**. He marries his teacher's daughter Sujātā. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII)
  - *Variant (father's name):* given as **Kahoḍa** in the Mahābhārata; spelled **Kahola** in many retellings; the by-name **Vājaśravas** appears in devotional versions. [folk]
- **Mother: Sujātā** - daughter of the great ṛṣi **Uddālaka Āruṇi**; she carries Aṣṭāvakra and is the one who hears him cry out from the womb. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII)
- **Maternal grandfather: Uddālaka Āruṇi (Aruṇi)** - the celebrated Upaniṣadic teacher of the *Chāndogya* ("*tat tvam asi*"); his āśrama is where Aṣṭāvakra grows up believing Uddālaka to be his father. (Mbh, Vana P.; cf. Upaniṣadic Uddālaka.)
- **Cousin/uncle-figure: Śvetaketu** - son of Uddālaka and therefore Aṣṭāvakra's maternal uncle, though near him in age; he accompanies Aṣṭāvakra to Janaka's court. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII–CXXXIV)
  - *Note:* the family is a knot of famous names — Uddālaka and Śvetaketu are themselves Upaniṣadic figures — so Aṣṭāvakra is born into the very household that fixed Vedānta's foundational sayings. [scholarly]

## The Womb-Curse — Eight Bends
- **The fetus corrects the father** - while Sujātā is pregnant, Kahoḍa recites the Vedas nightly; from the womb the unborn child detects errors of intonation/recitation and, in some tellings, says the Vedas may be learned in many nights but his father still stumbles in their meaning. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII)
- **The curse of the eight crooks** - the father, his learned pride wounded at being corrected by a fetus, curses the child: *because you mocked me from the womb, you shall be born crooked in eight parts.* So the boy is born bent at eight joints — hence **Aṣṭāvakra**. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII)
  - *Variant (the precise eight bends):* retellings enumerate the eight as the two feet, two knees, two hands (or arms), the chest, and the head/neck — but the epic gives no fixed list; "eight" is the constant, the inventory is folk elaboration. [folk]
  - *Variant (who curses):* the canonical Vana-Parva version is the father Kahoḍa cursing in anger; the **Rambhā–Devala** doublet (above) is a wholly different etiology for the name. [folk]

## The Father's Death — Bandin and the Drowning
- **Kahoḍa goes to Janaka's sacrifice** - poor and needing wealth, Kahoḍa attends the great sacrifice of **King Janaka of Videha**, where learned brāhmaṇas debate for prizes. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII)
- **Bandin/Vandin, the unbeaten debater** - the king's court hosts **Bandin** (also **Vandin**), a sūta/learned debater who challenges all comers; the defeated are **cast into water and drowned**. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII)
  - *Variant (name):* **Bandin** and **Vandin** are the two common spellings of the same figure. [folk]
- **Kahoḍa defeated and drowned** - Kahoḍa loses the disputation and is drowned, per the rule. The household conceals this from the boy, raising Aṣṭāvakra to believe Uddālaka is his father. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII)
- **The secret revealed** - a quarrel (in some tellings Śvetaketu rebukes the boy for sitting in "his father's" lap) exposes the truth: Uddālaka is his grandfather, Kahoḍa his father, and Kahoḍa lies drowned by Bandin. Aṣṭāvakra resolves to go to Janaka's court and avenge/free his father. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXII)
- **Age at the journey** - the epic stresses he is a small child, **twelve years old** in the standard count; some retellings make him ten. (Mbh, Vana P.; variant retellings.)
  - *Variant (age):* twelve (canonical) vs. ten (popular). [folk]

## At Janaka's Court — The Door and the Test of the Crooked Body
- **Barred at the gate** - the gatekeeper/warder refuses the boy entry to the sacrifice reserved for old, learned brāhmaṇas and kings; Aṣṭāvakra retorts that **age is not in grey hair but in knowledge**, and that a man is "old" by learning, not years. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXIII)
- **Janaka's own riddles** - the king tests the boy with riddling questions (on the one, the many, the dream-state, etc.), which Aṣṭāvakra answers, winning admission. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXIII)
- **Mocked for his crookedness** - the assembled brāhmaṇas laugh at his eight bends; Aṣṭāvakra laughs back and rebukes them: leather is judged by the cobblers, not the hide — *the Self is not crooked though the body be crooked*; they are "skin-deep" knowers mistaking the bent vessel for the straight dweller within. This rebuke is the philosophical kernel of the tale. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXIII)
  - *Variant (the cobbler image):* commonly paraphrased as "you are like leather-workers who see only the surface of the skin" — the verse is the moral fulcrum, widely quoted in retellings. [folk]

## The Great Debate — Counting One to Thirteen
- **The contest form** - Aṣṭāvakra and Bandin debate by **alternately composing extempore verses keyed to ascending numbers, one through twelve**, each capping the prior — a virtuoso display of cosmological/Vedic enumeration (the one Self, the two Aśvins, the three fires, the four Vedas/varṇas, the five senses, the six seasons, the seven sages, the eight... up through twelve). (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXIV)
- **The break at thirteen** - Bandin manages only the **first half of the verse on thirteen** and falters; Aṣṭāvakra **completes the second half**, decisively winning the disputation. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXIV)
- **Bandin revealed as Varuṇa's son** - defeated, Bandin discloses he is the **son of Varuṇa** (god of waters); the drowned brāhmaṇas were not killed but **sent down to Varuṇa's twelve-year sacrifice** beneath the waters. The term being now complete, they all rise alive. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXIV)
  - *Variant (purpose of drownings):* the "drowning" is reframed as a divine summons to Varuṇa's underwater yajña, not murder — softening the tale; some folk versions keep it as straightforward execution that the victory undoes. [folk]
- **Kahoḍa restored** - Aṣṭāvakra's father returns alive from the waters, and Bandin departs into the sea. The boy has freed his father by intellect, not arms. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXIV)

## The Straightening — The Sacred River
- **The cure** - the restored father Kahoḍa, pleased, bids Aṣṭāvakra **bathe in the river Samaṅgā** (some texts the **Madhuvilā**); emerging, his **eight bends are made straight** and his body whole. (Mbh, Vana P. CXXXIV)
  - *Variant (which river):* the Mahābhārata names **Samaṅgā**; variant retellings give **Madhuvilā** or simply "a holy river." [folk]
  - *Note:* the straightening is the narrative seal — the crooked vessel, having served to teach that the Self is uncrooked, is finally made to match the inner perfection. [scholarly]

## Later Life — The Marriage Test [Anuśāsana Parva]
- **Wooing Suprabhā** - in a separate episode (Mbh, Anuśāsana Parva), the grown Aṣṭāvakra seeks to marry **Suprabhā**, daughter of the ṛṣi **Vadānya**. (Mbh, Anuśāsana P.)
- **Vadānya's condition** - the father sends him north past Kailāsa to a realm of women to prove his self-mastery and steadfastness before granting the marriage. (Mbh, Anuśāsana P.)
- **The northern temptation passed** - he meets seven (or many) alluring women; the eldest/queen — named **Uttarā** in some tellings — tries to seduce him; he refuses, faithful to his vow. She reveals herself as a disguised guardian-queen testing him and blesses him; he returns and weds Suprabhā. (Mbh, Anuśāsana P.)
  - *Variant (the laughing celestials):* a folk strand has heavenly women (apsarāses) **laughing at his eight bends**, for which they are cursed/punished; this motif is sometimes fused into the marriage-test journey. [folk]

## The Aṣṭāvakra Gītā (Aṣṭāvakra Saṃhitā)
- **What it is** - a Sanskrit dialogue, **~298 verses in 20 chapters**, between the sage Aṣṭāvakra (teacher) and King **Janaka** (disciple) — a pure, uncompromising exposition of **Advaita Vedānta** / non-dual self-realization. (Aṣṭāvakra Gītā; tradition.)
  - *Variant (count):* commonly cited as ~298 ślokas across 20 chapters; minor manuscript counts vary slightly. [scholarly]
- **Core teaching** - the **Self (Ātman) is identical with Brahman**: formless, unattached, the unmoving **witness (sākṣin)**, ever-free; bondage and liberation are themselves notions of the mind, and the world is unreal (or irrelevant) to the realized. It rejects graded practice — **liberation is recognized, not achieved**. (Aṣṭāvakra Gītā.)
- **The dialogic arc** - Janaka, already a king-sage, asks how knowledge, freedom and detachment arise; Aṣṭāvakra answers, and uniquely Janaka then *voices his own awakening* (e.g. the famous "wonder" / *aho* verses), so the text dramatizes a disciple realizing the Self mid-dialogue. (Aṣṭāvakra Gītā, esp. ch. 2.)
- **Tone** - among the most radical and austere of the Gītā-genre: no ritual, no devotional theism, no gradualism — often grouped with the boldest jñāna-mārga texts. (Tradition; cf. [lesser-Gītās catalog](../lesser-gitas-catalog.md).) [scholarly]
- **Authorship & dating (the second clock)** - traditionally ascribed to Aṣṭāvakra of the epic, but scholars treat the ascription as honorific. R. K. Mukerjee placed it **after the Bhagavad-Gītā but before the common era**; broader scholarly estimates range **c. 5th c. BCE – 8th c. CE (often medieval, ~7th–14th c. CE)**, and the text is acknowledged as **effectively undatable**. The epic Aṣṭāvakra and the Gītā's author need not be one historical person. [scholarly] [late text]
  - *Variant (dating):* "pre-common-era, post-Gītā" (Mukerjee) vs. "medieval Advaita composition" (others); flag as genuinely open. [disputed]

## Significance & Cross-Links
- **The body/Self distinction** - Aṣṭāvakra is the tradition's living parable that **wisdom is independent of the body**; the deformity exists to be transcended in argument and then erased in water. (Mbh, Vana P.)
- **Janaka the philosopher-king** - the tale binds Aṣṭāvakra to **Janaka of Videha**, the ideal *rājarṣi*, who recurs across the Upaniṣads and is father of Sītā in the [Rāmāyaṇa](../ramayana/README.md). (Mbh; Upaniṣads; Rāmāyaṇa.)
- **Place in the Gītā corpus** - one of the "lesser Gītās"; compare the [Uddhava Gītā](../uddhava-gita.md), [Anu-Gītā](../anugita.md), and others in the [lesser-Gītās catalog](../lesser-gitas-catalog.md); contrast its bare non-dualism with the bhakti-jñāna synthesis of the [Bhagavad-Gītā](../bhagavad-gita.md).
- **Open rosters [flag]** - the enumerated "eight bends," the precise verse-by-verse content of the 1–13 counting debate, and the exact chapter divisions/verse total of the Gītā all vary by edition; treated above as variants rather than fixed canon. [folk] [scholarly]
