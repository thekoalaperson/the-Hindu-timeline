# Pūcalār (Pusalar) — the temple built in the mind

> A **Kali-Yuga documented-history** Tamil Śaiva legend: a penniless brāhmaṇa of **Tiruniṉṟavūr (Tirunindravur)**, unable to raise the money for a stone temple, builds Śiva a **complete temple entirely in his mind** — every brick, beam and rite imagined in order — and on the day fixed for its mental consecration Śiva warns the Pallava king **Rājasiṃha** in a dream to *postpone* the grand Kāñcī **[Kailāsanāthar](../06-sacred-geography/kanchipuram-city-of-temples.md)** dedication, for the Lord must first attend Pūcalār's. The corpus's foremost *mānasa-pūjā* (mental-worship) parable.
> ⬆ Parent index: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Master index: [README](../README.md) · Roster: [The 63 Nāyaṉārs](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/alvars-and-nayanars.md) · Text map: [Periya Purāṇam — legend map](../90-literature-corpus/periya-puranam-63-nayanmar-legend-map.md)

**Pūcalār Nāyaṉār** (Tamil *Pūcalār*, anglicized **Pusalar / Poosalar**) is one of the **63 Nāyaṉārs** — the canonical Tamil Śaiva poet-saints and servant-devotees — numbered **58th in the standard enumeration** and grouped by Cēkkiḻār among the *saints of quiet, interior service*. His *Puranam* in the **Periya Purāṇam** tells no battle, no self-mutilation, no gruesome test: only a poor brahmin who longs to build Śiva a temple, cannot afford the stone, and so **raises the whole edifice in his own mind** — collecting materials, laying the foundation, erecting the *vimāna* and *gopura*, installing the *liṅga*, and fixing a day for the *kumbhābhiṣeka* — all as an unbroken act of imaginative devotion. Its climax is the tale's celebrated reversal: **Śiva ranks the mind-temple above the king's masterpiece in dressed stone**, telling **Rājasiṃha** (**Narasiṃhavarman II Pallava**), builder of the great Kāñcī temple, to hold back his own consecration because the Lord is already engaged to attend Pūcalār's on the same date.

---

## Where it sits — the two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — As devotional *līlā* the episode belongs to the timeless mode in which **Śiva accepts and honours the interior worship of his devotees**, appearing in dream to redirect a king — a recurring Nāyaṉār motif with no fixed yuga-date. The hagiography places all sixty-three saints within **Kali Yuga**, the present degenerate age (began **3102 BCE** on the scriptural clock), when *bhakti* — here in its most bodiless form, pure *mānasa-pūjā* — is upheld as the surest path to grace.
- **Academic / historical frame** — The story is anchored to a **datable monument and king**. **Rājasiṃha = Narasiṃhavarman II Pallava** reigned **[scholarly] c. 690–728 CE**; his **Kailāsanāthar (Rājasiṃheśvara) temple at Kāñcī** — the oldest standing structural temple in the city and a foundation-stone of the Dravidian style — is dated **c. 685–705 CE**. Pūcalār is therefore placed **[scholarly] c. late 7th – early 8th century CE**, and the corpus files him under **Kali Yuga — Documented History**. The saint is already named in **Cuntarar's (Sundarar's) Tiruttoṇṭattokai** (7th–8th c. CE) and given full legend by **Cēkkiḻār** in the **Periya Purāṇam** (c. 1135 CE). The two clocks are kept apart: the *king and his temple* are documented history; the *dream and the mind-temple* are hagiography layered over that history. [scholarly]

## The saint and his town

- **A poor brāhmaṇa of Tiruniṉṟavūr** — Pūcalār is born in **Tiruniṉṟavūr (Tirunindravur / Thiruninravur)**, a Śiva town on the road between Kāñci (Kanchipuram) and the coast, in the Toṇṭai country of the Pallavas. He is learned in the Vedas and Āgamas and wholly devoted to Śiva, but **without wealth** — the one thing a temple-builder needs. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)
- **The longing to build a temple** — Seeing that no fitting shrine housed the Lord in his town, Pūcalār conceives the desire to **build Śiva a temple** himself. He begins gathering the means — but the cost of stone, timber, labour and image is far beyond a poor man, and no patron comes forward. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)
- **The resolve to build within** — Rather than abandon the wish, Pūcalār resolves to raise the temple where **no wealth is needed and nothing can be lacking: in his own mind**. He purifies himself and undertakes the construction as a sustained, disciplined act of mental worship — the standing Śaiva practice of *mānasa-pūjā* (worship performed entirely in the imagination) carried to its furthest reach: not a rite offered to a mental image, but **an entire temple architecturally built, stone by imagined stone**. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)

---

## The building of the mind-temple

- **The full Āgamic sequence, imagined in order** — Pūcalār does not merely *picture* a finished shrine; he **executes every stage of temple construction in his mind, in the prescribed sequence**, as the Āgamas lay it down. He selects the site, tests and consecrates the ground, gathers stone and timber, lays the **foundation**, raises the walls, erects the sanctum and the **vimāna** (tower over the sanctum), sets up the **gopura**, mounts the finial, and installs the **liṅga** — omitting no ritual step. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam) [the tale's distinctive emphasis: the *order* of the work, not a single vision]
- **Years of interior labour** — The mental construction takes him a long time — the narrative dwells on his patient, unhurried care, each element completed and only then the next begun, exactly as a real builder proceeds. His whole life becomes the building of this temple. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)
- **Fixing the day of consecration** — When the imagined temple is complete to the last detail, Pūcalār determines, as any temple-founder must, an **auspicious day for the *kumbhābhiṣeka* / consecration (*prati­ṣṭhā*)** — the ceremonial "awakening" that establishes the Lord in the shrine. He fixes the date and readies himself, in mind, for the great rite. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)

## The king, the stone temple, and the dream

- **Rājasiṃha's great temple at Kāñci** — In the same years the Pallava emperor **Rājasiṃha (Narasiṃhavarman II)**, seated at **Kāñci**, has built at vast expense a magnificent stone temple to Śiva — in tradition identified with the **[Kailāsanāthar / Rājasiṃheśvara temple](../06-sacred-geography/kanchipuram-city-of-temples.md)**, the earliest Pallava structural Śiva temple. He too has chosen an auspicious day for its **consecration** — and it falls on the **very same day** Pūcalār has set for his mind-temple. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)
- **Śiva's dream-command to postpone** — On the eve of the ceremony **Śiva appears to the king in a dream** and tells him He **cannot attend the royal consecration on that day**, for He is engaged to be present at the consecration of the temple **His devotee Pūcalār has built at Tiruniṉṟavūr** — and the king must therefore **postpone his own** to another date. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam) [the reversal: the Lord prefers the poor man's mental shrine over the emperor's stone one]
- **The king's search** — Astonished — and, in the telling, a little wounded that any temple could outrank his own — Rājasiṃha rides to **Tiruniṉṟavūr** to see this rival shrine and its wealthy builder. But he finds **no temple at all**: only the poor brahmin **Pūcalār**, and the townsfolk who know of no construction. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)
- **"The temple is in his mind"** — Questioned, Pūcalār explains that having no means to build in stone he **built the temple within his heart**, brick upon imagined brick, and had fixed that day for its consecration. The king understands that the shrine Śiva chose to attend is the one **raised in a devotee's mind** — and bows to him, recognizing that **interior devotion outweighs the grandest outward work**. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)

---

## The consecration and the ascent

- **The mental *kumbhābhiṣeka*** — On the appointed day Pūcalār performs, in his mind, the full **consecration** of his temple, and **Śiva, true to His dream-word, is present** at it — the mind-temple is thereby made as real and as inhabited by the Lord as any built of stone. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)
- **Attaining Śiva's feet** — Having completed his life's single work, Pūcalār at the end is taken by Śiva to His own world — the saint **reaches the feet of the Lord (Śivaloka / Kailāsa)**, the standard consummation of a Nāyaṉār life. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)
- **The king's temple, dedicated after** — Rājasiṃha, chastened, returns and holds his own consecration on the later date the Lord had allowed — the royal Kāñci temple standing to this day, while the greater temple of the story leaves no stone behind. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam) [the deliberate irony: the surviving monument is the *lesser* of the two]

## Reading the tale — *mānasa-pūjā* and the primacy of the interior

- **The doctrine of mental worship** — Śaiva (and pan-Hindu) practice recognizes **worship offered wholly in the mind (*mānasa-pūjā*)** as a valid and often higher mode than external ritual, since it needs no materials and admits no impurity — one "offers" imagined flowers, lamps and food perfect beyond any real substance. Pūcalār's story is the tradition's **charter-parable for this teaching**, pushing it past the offering of *pūjā* to the construction of the *temple itself*. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam; Śaiva Āgama practice) [scholarly]
- **Wealth is no bar to devotion** — The point Cēkkiḻār draws is explicitly social and theological: the **poorest devotee**, owning nothing, can build the Lord a dwelling that an **emperor's treasury cannot equal** — grace answers the intensity of the heart, not the scale of the endowment. (Periya Purāṇam, Pūcalār Purāṇam)
- **The pairing with Vāyilār** — Cēkkiḻār places Pūcalār beside **Vāyilār Nāyaṉār** of Mylapore, whose worship is likewise **entirely interior** — a "temple of the mind" furnished with *thought-lamps* and imagined offerings. The two are the corpus's twin exemplars of bodiless, mater-less *bhakti*, and are read together. (Periya Purāṇam, Vāyilār Purāṇam) [scholarly]
- **Contrast with the "obedience beyond ethics" tales** — Pūcalār's is the gentlest register of the Nāyaṉār canon — no killing, no wound, no violence — the exact opposite pole from the terrible obedience-tests of **[Ciṟuttoṇṭar](siruthondar-nayanar.md)** (the child-curry) or **[Caṇḍeśa](characters/chandesvara-chandikeshvara.md)** (the boy who strikes his father's leg for Śiva). The canon deliberately holds both extremes: devotion proved by unbearable sacrifice, and devotion proved by pure inwardness. (Periya Purāṇam) [scholarly]

## Historical notes and disambiguation

- **Which king, which temple** — Tradition names the king simply as the Pallava sovereign who built the **great stone temple at Kāñci**; art-historically this is **Rājasiṃha / Narasiṃhavarman II** and his **Kailāsanāthar (Rājasiṃheśvara) temple**, c. 685–705 CE. [scholarly] Some retellings leave the king unnamed as "the Pallava," and the identification of *the* Kāñci temple with the Kailāsanāthar specifically is a reasonable inference from date and grandeur rather than a name given in Cēkkiḻār's verse. [disputed]
- **Do not conflate the two Narasiṃhavarmans** — The king of *this* legend is **Narasiṃhavarman II Rājasiṃha** (r. c. 690–728 CE, the temple-builder), **not** his great-grandfather **Narasiṃhavarman I Māmalla** (the Vātāpi conqueror, c. 630–668 CE) who figures behind the **[Ciṟuttoṇṭar / Paranjōti](siruthondar-nayanar.md)** legend. Two different Pallava Narasiṃhavarmans stand behind two different Nāyaṉār stories. [scholarly]
- **Tiruniṉṟavūr today** — The town survives as **Thiruninravur**, a suburb north-west of Chennai on the Chennai–Arakkonam line; its Śiva temple (Bhaktavatsaleswarar / the Lord "who cherishes His devotees") is locally associated with the Pūcalār legend. [regional]
