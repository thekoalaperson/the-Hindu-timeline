# Nala & Damayatī — the Dice, the Curse, and Reunion

> Where this sits: a **Dvāpara-era tale-within-a-tale**, narrated to Yudhiṣṭhira during the Pāṇḍavas' forest exile. It belongs to the [Mahābhārata deep-dives](./../04-deep-dives/) and to the [Mahābhārata corpus](./mahabharata/) inside the [28th Mahāyuga, Dvāpara Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md). Its villain is **Kali** — the same age-demon whose epoch we now inhabit ([Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)).
> Up: [04-deep-dives](./../04-deep-dives/) · Parent epic: [Mahābhārata](./mahabharata/)

The **Nalopākhyāna** ("Episode of Nala") is a self-contained sub-epic embedded in the **Āraṇyaka (Vana) Parva** of the Mahābhārata. In the BORI Critical Edition it fills **chapters 3.50–78** (29 chapters, about 900 numbered verses in the constituted text; the vulgate is longer), with the frame in 3.49 and 3.78. In Ganguli's translation, which follows the vulgate, the same material is **sections LIII–LXXIX**, with the frame in LII. Franz Bopp printed it with a Latin translation in 1819 (*Nalus*), called the first Sanskrit "reader" to appear in the West. Bopp's *Nalus* "remained one of the standard first texts for Sanskrit students for more than a half-century", the story recurs in nineteenth-century Sanskrit readers, and today the excerpt is most often met in Lanman's *Sanskrit Reader* (1884) [scholarly: Tull 2015].

**How to read the locators.** "CE 3.x.y" is the BORI Critical Edition (chapter.verse) as given on GRETIL. "G n" is the Ganguli section number. The two do not run in parallel throughout:

| CE chapters | Ganguli sections |

A starred passage (`*nnnn`) or an Appendix I passage (`App. I no. n`) is text the critical editors found in only part of the manuscript tradition and left out of the constituted text (starred passages are printed below the line, longer ones in Appendix I). Much of this material is in the vulgate and therefore in Ganguli, but not all of it. Where the vulgate differs from the constituted text, the page says so.

## Frame: why the story is told

- **Yudhiṣṭhira's despair** - While Arjuna is away in Indra's world gaining weapons, the sage **Bṛhadaśva** visits the Pāṇḍavas in the Kāmyaka forest. Yudhiṣṭhira complains that he lost his wealth and kingdom to cheats skilled at dice, though he himself did not know the game, and that his wife was dragged into the assembly; he asks whether any king was ever more unfortunate (CE 3.49.29–34; G 52).
- **Bṛhadaśva's answer** - The sage says he will tell of a king more wretched still: **Nala**, son of Vīrasena of Niṣadha, who was beaten at dice by Puṣkara through trickery and lived in the forest with only his wife — no horse, no chariot, no brother, no kinsman — whereas Yudhiṣṭhira is surrounded by heroic brothers and Brahmins (CE 3.49.35–43; G 52).
- **The close of the frame** - Only after the tale does Bṛhadaśva teach Yudhiṣṭhira the **akṣa-hṛdaya** ("heart of the dice"), so that he need not fear being challenged again; then he leaves to bathe at Aśvaśiras (Ganguli: "Hayasirsha") (CE 3.78.14–17; G 79). He also gives a *phala-śruti*: the story is called *kali-nāśana* ("destroyer of Kali"), and those who tell or hear it are not visited by misfortune (CE 3.78.10–13). The tale is thus both consolation and a mirror of the main epic's dice-disaster.

## The principals

- **Nala** - King of **Niṣadha**, son of **Vīrasena**; handsome, expert in horses (*aśvakovida*), truthful, devoted to Brahmins, and — significantly — **fond of dice** (*akṣapriya*) (CE 3.50.1–4; G 53). In the epic itself he is a peerless charioteer and a master cook (CE 3.64.2–3, 3.68.6). The cookery manual *Pākadarpaṇa* is ascribed to him [late attribution; date uncertain].
- **Damayantī** - Princess of **Vidarbha**, daughter of King **Bhīma** (capital Kuṇḍina). Bhīma was childless until the Brahmin seer **Damana** granted him and his queen a jewel of a daughter and three sons: **Dama, Dānta and Damana** (CE 3.50.5–9; G 53). She is matchless in beauty among gods, yakṣas and humans (CE 3.50.10–13). She has a **congenital lotus-like mole (*piplu*) between her brows**, which later betrays her identity (CE 3.66.5–10; G 69).
- **Children** - A daughter **Indrasenā** and a son **Indrasena** (named at CE 3.57.21; G 60). The verse narrating their birth is a starred passage after CE 3.54.37 (*0229), present in Ganguli (G 57).
- **Puṣkara** - Nala's brother (*bhrātā*), whom Kali uses against him (CE 3.56.4–7; G 59).

## Act I — Love by reputation, and the golden swan

- **Falling in love unseen** - People praise Nala in Damayantī's presence and Damayantī in Nala's, again and again, until love grows in each without their having met (CE 3.50.15–16; G 53).
- **The haṃsa (swan/goose) messenger** - Pining, Nala sits alone in a grove near the women's quarters. He sees **haṃsas with golden wings** and catches one. The bird (*antarikṣaga*, "sky-goer") speaks: "Do not kill me; I will do what you love — I will speak of you to Damayantī so that she will think of no other man." Released, the flock flies to Vidarbha. Damayantī and her friends each chase a bird; the one she chases speaks in human voice, praising Nala as equal to the Aśvins in beauty. She asks it to speak of her to Nala in the same way, and it does (CE 3.50.17–31; G 53).
  - *Variant (later poetic retellings, e.g. Śrīharṣa's* **Naiṣadhīya-carita**, *12th c.):* the swan episode is hugely expanded into elaborate love-messenger *kāvya*. The poem, in 22 cantos, carries the story only through the wedding and the couple's first days together; it never reaches the dice or the exile [late text].

## Act II — The svayaṃvara and the four gods

- **Bhīma proclaims the svayaṃvara** - Damayantī's friends tell her father she is lovesick; seeing her come of age, Bhīma summons the kings to her self-choice (CE 3.51.1–10; G 54).
- **Why the gods come** - The seers **Nārada and Parvata** visit Indra, who asks why the warrior-kings no longer arrive in heaven as his guests. Nārada explains that they are all going to Damayantī's svayaṃvara. The Lokapālas, with Agni, hear this and decide to go too (CE 3.51.11–24; G 54).
- **The gods intercept Nala** - On the way, the gods see Nala, beautiful as Kāma himself, and ask him to be their messenger. He promises before asking who they are. Then he learns they are **Indra, Agni, Varuṇa and Yama**, and that he is to urge Damayantī to choose one of them. He protests that he cannot plead against his own interest, but they hold him to his promise. By their power he enters her guarded palace unseen (CE 3.51.26–3.52.24; G 54–55).
- **Damayantī refuses the gods** - She offers herself and all she has to Nala, and says she will take poison, fire, water or the rope if he rejects her. When he warns her that a mortal who displeases the gods dies, she proposes a way out: all of them, gods and Nala, should come to the svayaṃvara, and she will choose Nala in the gods' presence, so that no blame falls on him. Nala reports this to the gods truthfully (CE 3.53.1–21; G 56).
- **The five identical Nalas** - In the assembly hall she sees **five men of identical form** and cannot tell which is Nala (CE 3.54.10–12; G 57).
- **The recognition by divine signs** - She recalls the marks of gods that she had heard of from her elders, bows to the gods, and makes an act of truth: as she chose Nala on hearing the haṃsas, and has never strayed from him in word or thought, let the gods show him to her and resume their own forms. Moved, the gods show their marks. **They do not sweat, their eyes do not blink, their garlands are fresh and free of dust, and they stand without touching the ground.** Nala **has a shadow, a wilting garland, dust and sweat, stands on the earth and blinks.** (The gods' lack of a shadow is implied by the contrast, not stated.) She chooses him, takes the hem of his garment and lays the garland on his shoulder (CE 3.54.13–26; G 57).
- **The gods' boons** - Pleased, the Lokapālas give Nala **eight boons**. **Indra** gives visible sight of himself at sacrifices and an excellent path hereafter. **Agni** gives his own presence wherever Nala wishes, and worlds as bright as himself. **Yama** gives flavour in food (*anna-rasa*) and firmness in dharma. **Varuṇa** gives water wherever Nala wishes and a garland of excellent fragrance. The text adds that "all gave a *mithuna* (pair)"; Ganguli takes this to mean that each god gave a couple of boons (CE 3.54.28–32; G 57). The kings depart, and Nala rules justly and performs the **horse sacrifice** and many other rites (CE 3.54.33–38).

## Act III — Kali's grudge and the long wait

- **Kali and Dvāpara arrive too late** - Returning, the gods meet **Kali** with **Dvāpara**. Kali says he means to choose Damayantī himself. Indra laughs that the svayaṃvara is over and that she chose Nala in the gods' presence. Kali, furious, says she deserves heavy punishment for choosing a man among gods. The gods answer that they consented to her choice; whoever would curse a king of Nala's virtues curses himself and sinks into hell. After they leave, Kali tells Dvāpara he cannot contain his anger. He will dwell in Nala and drive him from his kingdom and from Damayantī, and he asks Dvāpara to **enter the dice** and help him (CE 3.55.1–13; G 58).
- **The twelve-year vigil** - Kali lives in Niṣadha for a long time, always looking for an opening (*antara*), and finds it **in the twelfth year** (CE 3.56.1–2; G 59).

## Act IV — Possession, the rigged dice, and exile

- **The ritual loophole** - After urinating, Nala sips water and sits for the twilight prayer (*sandhyā*) **without washing his feet**; through that lapse Kali **enters him** (CE 3.56.3; G 59).
- **Kali incites Puṣkara** - The possessed Nala's brother **Puṣkara** is told by Kali, "Play with Nala; with my help you will win Niṣadha" (CE 3.56.4–5; G 59).
- **The dice-demons** - Dvāpara's entry into the dice is the plan agreed in CE 3.55.13. At the game itself the readings divide:
  - *CE 3.56.6–7:* **Kali** becomes the **vṛṣa** ("bull") "among the cattle" and goes with Puṣkara, who keeps calling Nala to play "with the *vṛṣa*". A starred passage (*0234) makes the bull Puṣkara's stake, since he had no wealth to wager.
  - *Variant (vulgate, G 59):* **Dvāpara** approaches Puṣkara "becoming the principal die called Vrisha".
- **The ruinous game** - Unable to refuse a challenge while Damayantī watches, Nala loses gold, chariots and teams, and garments. The citizens and ministers gather at the gate; Damayantī begs him to see them, but the possessed king does not answer, and they go away ashamed. The game goes on **for many months** (CE 3.56.8–18; G 59).
- **Saving the children** - Through her nurse **Bṛhatsenā**, Damayantī summons the ministers again, and then the charioteer **Vārṣṇeya**. With the ministers' approval he yokes Nala's favourite horses, takes Indrasenā and Indrasena to her kin in **Kuṇḍina**, leaves the horses and chariot there, and then wanders to **Ayodhyā**, where he takes service as **Ṛtuparṇa's charioteer** (CE 3.57.4–23; G 60).
- **Stripped and exiled** - Having won everything, Puṣkara laughs and asks what stake is left: "only Damayantī — let her be the wager, if you like." Nala, heart breaking, says nothing. He takes off all his ornaments and walks out **in a single garment**, and Damayantī, also in one garment, follows him. Puṣkara proclaims death for anyone who treats Nala well, so they stay **three nights outside the city** on water alone (CE 3.58.1–10; G 61).
- **The birds steal the last cloth** - Starving, Nala sees **birds with golden-looking wings** and throws his lower garment over them to catch them for food. They fly off with it and say, "We are **the dice**, fool; we came to take your garment, for we were not pleased that you left with any clothing" (CE 3.58.11–18; G 61).
- **The roads to the south** - Nala repeatedly points out the roads: past Avantī and the Ṛkṣavant mountain, the Vindhya, the Payoṣṇī river, the way to Vidarbha, the way to Kosala, and the Deccan (*dakṣiṇāpatha*) beyond. Damayantī realises he means to leave her and begs that they go to her father together. He refuses to return there ruined (CE 3.58.19–3.59.2; G 61–62).

## Act V — The abandonment

- **Nala deserts Damayantī** - The two share **her one garment** and reach a rest-house (*sabhā*), where they sleep on the bare ground. Unable to sleep, Nala reasons that without him she might go back to her kin and find some happiness. Wandering through the rest-house, he finds a **naked sword**, cuts away **half of her garment** and puts it on, and runs. Grief brings him back again and again — "like a swing" — and each time Kali drags him away, until he leaves her asleep in the empty forest (CE 3.59.3–25; G 62).

## Act VI — Damayantī alone in the forest

- **Lament and the python** - Waking alone, Damayantī calls to him, reminds him of the promises he made before the Lokapālas, and **curses the being through whose curse Nala suffers**: may it suffer more than he does. Wandering, she is seized by a **huge, hungry python (*ajagara*)** (CE 3.60.1–22; G 63).
- **The hunter** - A **hunter (*mṛgavyādha*)** kills the snake by slashing its mouth, washes her and feeds her, then hears her story and is overcome by lust. Damayantī **curses him**: "As I think of no man but Naiṣadha even in my mind, so let this wretch fall dead." He falls lifeless like a tree burned by fire (CE 3.60.25–38; G 63).
- **Lament to the forest** - She wanders a forest full of beasts, unafraid. She addresses a **tiger**, asking him to tell her of Nala or else devour her; then a **mountain**, to whom she gives her full lineage. Going north for three days and nights, she reaches a **hermitage** of ascetics like Vasiṣṭha, Bhṛgu and Atri, who foretell that she will soon see Nala restored and ruling — and then vanish, hermitage, river and all, so that she wonders whether it was a dream. She circles an **aśoka tree**, begging it to live up to its name ("sorrowless") (CE 3.61.1–107; G 64).
- **The merchant caravan** - She joins a great **caravan** bound for the land of **Subāhu, king of Cedi**. Its people take her for a goddess of the forest, a yakṣī or a rākṣasī and beg her protection; its leader **Śuci** invokes the yakṣa-king **Maṇibhadra** (CE 3.61.110–3.62.1; G 64–65). At midnight, beside a lotus lake, a **herd of wild elephants** coming to drink tramples the sleeping camp (CE 3.62.2–10; G 65).
  - *CE 3.62.11–17 (constituted text):* among the survivors, Damayantī blames her own fate and the anger of the Lokapālas she had rejected, and she goes on with surviving Vedic Brahmins.
  - *Variant (App. I no. 11, inserted after 3.62.10, with similar text in nos. 10 and 12; in the vulgate, G 65):* the survivors blame "that mad-looking woman", a rākṣasī or yakṣī, and plan to stone her, so she flees into the forest.
- **Refuge in Cedi** - At dusk she enters Subāhu's city, dishevelled and half-clad, with village boys trailing her out of curiosity. From her palace the **queen-mother** sees her and takes her in. Damayantī calls herself a **sairandhrī** (chamber-attendant) and sets three conditions: she will not eat leavings, will not wash anyone's feet, and will not speak with other men; any man who solicits her is to be punished, and she may meet Brahmins in order to search for her husband. The queen-mother agrees and gives her to her daughter **Sunandā** as a companion (CE 3.62.18–43; G 65).
- **Recognized by the mole** - Bhīma sends Brahmins everywhere, offering a thousand cows and a village to whoever brings the couple back (CE 3.65.1–5; G 68). The Brahmin **Sudeva**, a friend of her brother, sees her at a *puṇyāhavācana* rite and recognises her by her bearing and features (CE 3.65.6–28). At the queen-mother's request he explains who she is and names the **lotus-like mole between her brows**, hidden by grime; Sunandā washes it clear. The queen-mother then reveals that she is Damayantī's **maternal aunt**: both were daughters of **Sudāman, king of Daśārṇa**, one given to Bhīma and the other to **Vīrabāhu**. Damayantī asks to go home, and the queen-mother sends her to Vidarbha with an escort (CE 3.66.1–22; G 69).

## Act VII — Nala, Kārkoṭaka, and the disguise of Bāhuka

- **Rescuing the burning nāga** - Having left Damayantī, Nala hears a voice from a forest fire crying "Run here, Nala!" The nāga-king **Karkoṭaka**, lying coiled, says he deceived a sinless *brahmarṣi* and was cursed by him to be unable to move a step. He offers friendship and good counsel, shrinks to the size of a thumb, and is carried out of the fire (CE 3.63.1–9; G 66).
  - *Variant (vulgate, G 66):* the cursing sage is named **Nārada**, and the curse is spelled out: Karkoṭaka must stay like an immobile thing until Nala carries him away. The CE main text names no sage, and the curse terms are a starred passage (*0300).
- **The protective bite** - Karkoṭaka tells Nala to walk counting his steps, and **bites him at the tenth step**. Nala's form vanishes into a **deformed one** — later described as **ugly and short-armed** (*virūpo hrasvabāhukaḥ*). The nāga explains why. The disguise keeps people from recognising him, and the one through whom Nala suffers will **live in torment inside him, wrapped in the venom**. Nala himself will have no fear of fanged creatures, enemies or Brahmins, will feel no pain from poison, and will always win in battle (CE 3.63.11–18, 3.68.6, 3.72.1; G 66).
- **The divine garments** - Karkoṭaka gives him a **pair of divine garments**: whenever Nala wants his own form back, he should remember the nāga and put one on (CE 3.63.22–23; G 66).
- **Instructions** - "Go today to **Ayodhyā**, to **Ṛtuparṇa** of the Ikṣvāku line, calling yourself the charioteer **Bāhuka**. He knows the art of dice and will give you the *akṣa-hṛdaya* in exchange for the *aśva-hṛdaya* (heart of horses). When you know the dice you will regain your wife, your children and your kingdom" (CE 3.63.19–21; G 66).
  - *Note:* a starred verse here (*0301) says that reciting the names of Karkoṭaka, Damayantī, Nala and Ṛtuparṇa destroys Kali; it is not in the constituted text. Ganguli has no such verse at this point, but his closing section says the same of this history (G 79).
- **Bāhuka at Ṛtuparṇa's court** - On the **tenth day** Nala enters Ṛtuparṇa's city and offers his services: no one on earth drives horses as he does; he can be consulted on difficult matters; he knows cookery better than anyone; and he will attempt any craft. Ṛtuparṇa, who loves speed above all, makes him master of horses at a salary of "a hundred hundreds", with **Vārṣṇeya and Jīvala** to attend him (CE 3.64.1–8; G 67). Each evening Nala recites one verse: "Where does she lie, hungry, thirsty and weary, remembering that fool — and whom does she wait on now?" When Jīvala asks whom he grieves for, Nala tells the story of "a foolish man" who lost his wife (CE 3.64.9–19; G 67).

## Act VIII — The second-svayaṃvara ruse

- **Damayantī's stratagem** - Back home, Damayantī tells her mother she will die unless Nala is found. She sends Brahmins through every kingdom to repeat in public: "Where have you gone, gambler (*kitava*), after cutting off half my garment and leaving your loving wife asleep in the forest?…" They are to report anyone who answers (CE 3.67.1–22; G 69). After a long time the Brahmin **Parṇāda** returns from Ayodhyā. Ṛtuparṇa's court stayed silent, but in private the king's charioteer **Bāhuka**, "ugly and short-armed, skilled at fast driving and a fine cook", wept and answered. Noble women, he said, guard themselves even in misfortune and do not grow angry with a husband who was beside himself, whose garment the birds stole while he sought food (CE 3.68.1–12; G 70).
- **The false announcement** - Damayantī keeps the plan from her father and, in her mother's presence, sends **Sudeva** to tell Ṛtuparṇa alone that, since Nala's fate is unknown, she will hold **a second svayaṃvara tomorrow at sunrise** (CE 3.68.13–24; G 70). Later she explains why: no one but Nala could drive **a hundred yojanas in one day** (CE 3.75.4–5; G 76).
- **The race across the land** - Ṛtuparṇa asks Bāhuka to get him to Vidarbha in one day. Bāhuka is stricken — could Damayantī really do this? — but promises, resolved to learn the truth. He picks **lean Sindhu horses** that the king scorns as weak; when the king mounts, they fall to their knees, but Nala soothes them, and they fly as if through the sky. On the chariot, **Vārṣṇeya** wonders whether Bāhuka is Mātali, Indra's charioteer, or **Śālihotra**, master of horse-lore, or Nala himself in another body (CE 3.69.1–34; G 71). The king's upper garment falls; he asks Bāhuka to hold the horses so that Vārṣṇeya can fetch it, but Nala answers that it is already a yojana behind (CE 3.70.1–5; G 72).

## Act IX — The exchange of secret knowledges

- **The Vibhītaka tree** - Passing a fruiting **vibhītaka** tree, Ṛtuparṇa shows off his skill with numbers. The fallen leaves and fruit exceed those on the tree by one hundred and one, and its two branches carry **five koṭis (fifty million) of leaves and 2,095 fruits**. Bāhuka insists on checking. He jumps down, **cuts down the tree** and counts, and finds the numbers exact (CE 3.70.6–21; G 72).
- **Trade of the two "hearts"** - Ṛtuparṇa says he knows the **akṣa-hṛdaya** and is expert in numbers. Bāhuka asks for that knowledge and offers the **aśva-hṛdaya** in return. Ṛtuparṇa gives the dice-lore at once, but says the horse-lore should **stay with Bāhuka as a deposit (*nikṣepa*)** (CE 3.70.22–26; G 72). Nala hands it over only later, at Kuṇḍina (see Act X).
- **Kali expelled** - As soon as Nala knows the dice, **Kali comes out of his body, vomiting Karkoṭaka's venom**; the fire of Damayantī's curse also leaves him. Nala, angry, wants to curse him. Kali, trembling, begs him to restrain his anger and promises "supreme fame": those who glorify Nala will never have fear born of Kali. Nala restrains himself, and Kali, unseen by anyone else, **enters the vibhītaka tree**, which has been inauspicious (*apraśasta*) ever since (CE 3.70.27–36; G 72). Nala drives on, freed of Kali but **still in the deformed shape** (CE 3.70.37–39).
  - *Note:* this is the demon **Kali (Kaliyuga's personification)** — the same malign power whose age we now inhabit ([Kali Yuga README](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)); the tale dramatizes Kali's defeat by dharma, virtue, and self-mastery.

## Act X — Recognition and reunion

- **No second svayaṃvara found** - Ṛtuparṇa reaches Kuṇḍina at dusk. The thunder of the chariot makes Nala's old horses rejoice, and the palace peacocks and elephants cry out as if at the coming of rain; Damayantī recognises Nala's driving in the sound. Ṛtuparṇa sees no rival kings and no preparations, so he tactfully says he has only come to pay his respects. Bhīma, who knows nothing of the plan, suspects there is more behind a journey of over a hundred yojanas (CE 3.71.1–27; G 73).
- **Keśinī's tests** - Damayantī sends her messenger **Keśinī** to question the "deformed, short-armed" charioteer and to repeat Parṇāda's verse to him. He gives the same answer and weeps (CE 3.72.1–30; G 74). Sent again, Keśinī reports uncanny signs. At a low passage he never stoops: it rises for him, and a narrow way opens wide. A jar he looks at fills with water. A handful of grass he waves bursts into flame. He touches fire without being burned, water flows for him at will, and flowers crushed in his hands become fresher and more fragrant (CE 3.73.1–17; G 75).
- **The taste of his cooking** - Damayantī has Keśinī bring her some meat Bāhuka has cooked. Tasting it, she knows the hand of Nala, whose cooking she had often eaten, and cries out (CE 3.73.20–22; G 75).
- **The children's proof** - She then sends Indrasenā and her brother with Keśinī. Bāhuka runs to them, takes them on his lap and weeps aloud. He then tells Keśinī they look very like his own two children, and asks her not to keep coming, lest people suspect wrong (CE 3.73.23–28; G 75).
- **The confrontation** - With her parents' leave, Damayantī has Bāhuka brought to her quarters. She is wearing ochre robes, her hair is matted, and she is grimy. She asks him whether he ever knew a righteous man who left his sleeping, blameless wife in the forest — who but Nala? Nala answers that Kali did it, not he. **Her curse in the forest had burned Kali inside him** "as if in fire", and Kali has now left him. Then he asks how a woman could choose a second husband (CE 3.74.1–24; G 76).
- **The vindication** - Damayantī explains the ruse and calls the Wind, the Sun and the Moon to take her life if she has sinned. **Vāyu speaks from the sky**: she has done no wrong, and the gods have been witnesses and guardians of her virtue **for three years**. Flowers rain down and divine drums sound (CE 3.75.1–15; G 76).
- **The true form restored** - Only then does Nala, his doubt gone, put on the **nāga's dustless garment**, remember Karkoṭaka and **regain his own form**. Damayantī embraces him, and he embraces the children. Her mother tells Bhīma, and the couple spend the night talking over their wanderings: they are reunited **in the fourth year** (CE 3.75.16–27; G 76).
- **Ṛtuparṇa and the deposit** - Next morning Bhīma receives Nala like a son, and the city celebrates. Ṛtuparṇa learns that Bāhuka was Nala, and the two kings ask each other's pardon. Nala calls him a friend and kinsman, and now hands over the **aśva-hṛdaya** he held in trust. Ṛtuparṇa returns home with another charioteer (CE 3.76.1–18; G 77).

## Act XI — Recovery of the kingdom

- **Return to Niṣadha** - After a month at Kuṇḍina, Nala sets out with a small force: one chariot, sixteen elephants, fifty horses and six hundred foot-soldiers. He challenges **Puṣkara** to play again: Nala stakes Damayantī and the wealth he has gained, Puṣkara stakes the kingdom, and both stake their lives on **a single throw**. If Puṣkara prefers, they can settle it by **single combat** instead (CE 3.77.1–10; G 78).
- **The kingdom won back** - Puṣkara, sure of winning, gloats that the winnings will bring Damayantī to wait on him. Nala, angry, wants to cut off his head with his sword, but holds back, and wins everything with a single throw. He tells Puṣkara he may never look at Damayantī, but that the earlier defeat was Kali's work and not Puṣkara's, and so he **spares his life**. He embraces him as a brother and sends him home honoured and wealthy (CE 3.77.11–29; G 78). Nala brings Damayantī home with a great army, her father sending her off with honour, and Nala rules again and performs many sacrifices. Bṛhadaśva tells Yudhiṣṭhira that he too, with his kin, will soon shine in the same way, and goes on to teach him the *akṣa-hṛdaya* (CE 3.78.1–17; G 79).

## Themes & cross-links

- **Mirror of the main epic** - Nala's dice-ruin, exile, and recovery deliberately parallel Yudhiṣṭhira's, offering hope that the [Mahābhārata's](./mahabharata/) own catastrophe can be reversed. Bṛhadaśva draws the comparison himself: Nala suffered alone, while Yudhiṣṭhira has his brothers, Kṛṣṇā (Draupadī) and the Brahmins with him (CE 3.78.6–9).
- **Kali as moral allegory** - The demon **Kali** can possess Nala only through a single ritual lapse and is driven out by knowledge and virtue — a dharma-lesson about the [Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) we live in. The tale presents itself as *kali-nāśana* (CE 3.78.10), and Kali promises that those who praise Nala will not fear him (CE 3.70.33).
- **Truth (satya) as power** - Damayantī's chastity and Nala's truthfulness repeatedly bend reality: her act of truth makes the gods reveal themselves, her word kills the hunter, her curse torments Kali, and her oath draws Vāyu's testimony (CE 3.54.17–20, 3.60.37, 3.74.17–18, 3.75.7–11).
- **Afterlife of the tale** - Reworked across centuries: Śrīharṣa's *Naiṣadhīya-carita* (Sanskrit *mahākāvya*, 12th c.) [late text]; vernacular versions in Telugu, Tamil, Hindi, Bengali, and Kannada; a staple of Kathakali, Yakshagana, and folk theatre [folk tradition].
