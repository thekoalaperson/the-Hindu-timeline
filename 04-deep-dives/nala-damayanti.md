# Nala & Damayatī — the Dice, the Curse, and Reunion

> Where this sits: a **Dvāpara-era tale-within-a-tale**, narrated to Yudhiṣṭhira during the Pāṇḍavas' forest exile. It belongs to the [Mahābhārata deep-dives](./../04-deep-dives/) and to the [Mahābhārata corpus](./mahabharata/) inside the [28th Mahāyuga, Dvāpara Yuga](./../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md). Its villain is **Kali** — the same age-demon whose epoch we now inhabit ([Kali Yuga](./../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)).
> Up: [04-deep-dives](./../04-deep-dives/) · Parent epic: [Mahābhārata](./mahabharata/)

The **Nalopākhyāna** ("Episode of Nala") is a self-contained sub-epic embedded in the **Vana Parva (Āraṇyaka / Book of the Forest), sections 50–78 (some recensions 52–79)** of the Mahābhārata — 26–28 chapters, ~1,000+ verses. It is one of the oldest standalone narratives preserved in the epic and is so admired as a model of pure Sanskrit *kāvya* that for centuries it was the first literary text taught to Sanskrit students in Europe.

---

## Frame: why the story is told

- **Yudhiṣṭhira's despair** - In exile after losing everything in a rigged dice match to the Kauravas, Yudhiṣṭhira asks the sage **Bṛhadaśva** whether any king ever suffered a calamity greater than his own (Mbh Vana Parva 50).
- **Bṛhadaśva's answer** - The sage replies that **Nala**, king of Niṣadha, endured worse — dice-loss, exile, separation from his wife — yet recovered everything; and he teaches Yudhiṣṭhira the **akṣa-hṛdaya** ("heart/secret of the dice") so he need never lose at gambling again (Mbh Vana Parva 50, 78). The tale is thus both consolation and a thematic mirror of the main epic's central dice-disaster.

---

## The principals

- **Nala** - King of **Niṣadha**, son of **Vīrasena**; paragon of dharma, peerless **charioteer** and **cook** (the cookbook *Pākadarpaṇa* / *Pākaśāstra* is traditionally ascribed to him) [folk/late attribution] (Mbh Vana Parva 50; Wikipedia "Nala").
- **Damayantī** - Princess of **Vidarbha**, daughter of King **Bhīma** (of Kuṇḍina); famed for unmatched beauty, marked by a **lotus-shaped mole/birthmark between her brows** that later betrays her identity (Mbh Vana Parva 50, 65).
- **Children** - A son **Indrasena** and a daughter **Indrasenā** (Mbh Vana Parva 59).

---

## Act I — Love by reputation, and the golden swan

- **Falling in love unseen** - Nala and Damayantī each hear the other endlessly praised and fall in love before ever meeting (Mbh Vana Parva 50).
- **The haṃsa (swan/goose) messenger** - Nala catches golden **haṃsas** in his garden; one pleads for release and in return flies to Vidarbha to extol Nala to Damayantī, kindling her longing — the divine matchmaker that links the two courts (Mbh Vana Parva 51–52).
  - *Variant (later poetic retellings, e.g. Śrīharṣa's* **Naiṣadhīya-carita**, *12th c.):* the swan episode is hugely expanded into elaborate love-messenger *kāvya*; the bird is a single golden **haṃsa** of supernatural eloquence [late text].

## Act II — The svayaṃvara and the four gods

- **Bhīma proclaims the svayaṃvara** - Pining Damayantī's father announces a self-choice assembly; kings converge on Kuṇḍina (Mbh Vana Parva 53).
- **The gods intercept Nala** - On the road, the **four Lokapālas — Indra, Agni, Varuṇa, and Yama** — descend, also seeking Damayantī's hand. Bound by his truthfulness, Nala is compelled to serve as **their messenger** and plead the gods' suit to the very woman he loves (Mbh Vana Parva 53–54).
- **Damayantī refuses the gods** - She insists she will choose Nala alone, vowing it before the gods (Mbh Vana Parva 54).
- **The five identical Nalas** - At the svayaṃvara the four gods each assume **Nala's exact form**, presenting Damayantī with five indistinguishable suitors (Mbh Vana Parva 54).
- **The recognition by divine signs** - Damayantī prays on the strength of her truthfulness; the gods reveal themselves through marks of divinity that mortals lack: **they cast no shadow, do not sweat, do not blink, their flower garlands stay unwithered and dustless, and their feet do not touch the ground** — while Nala alone shows shadow, sweat, blinking, wilting garland, dusty feet. She garlands the true Nala (Mbh Vana Parva 54).
- **The gods' boons** - Pleased, the four gods grant Nala gifts: **Indra** — presence at sacrifices and a sure path to heaven; **Agni** — fire on demand and radiant lustre; **Varuṇa** — water wherever he wills; **Yama** — mastery of dharma and supreme skill in cooking. Each also grants twin boons; the couple marries and Damayantī bears Indrasena and Indrasenā (Mbh Vana Parva 56).

## Act III — Kali's grudge and the long wait

- **Kali and Dvāpara arrive too late** - The age-demons **Kali** and **Dvāpara** journey to the svayaṃvara; meeting the departing gods, they learn Damayantī has already chosen Nala. Enraged that a mortal won a bride the gods coveted, **Kali vows to destroy Nala** (Mbh Vana Parva 56).
- **The twelve-year vigil** - Kali cannot strike at once: Nala is so flawless in dharma that the demon finds **no opening (chidra)** and must lurk for **twelve years**, waiting for a lapse (Mbh Vana Parva 56).

## Act IV — Possession, the rigged dice, and exile

- **The ritual loophole** - At last Nala, having urinated, performs his evening prayers (sandhyā) with **feet unwashed/improperly purified**. This ritual impurity gives Kali his opening, and the demon **enters Nala's body** (Mbh Vana Parva 56).
- **Dvāpara enters the dice** - Kali's partner **Dvāpara possesses the dice (akṣa)** themselves, ensuring Nala will lose (Mbh Vana Parva 56).
- **Kali incites Puṣkara** - Possessed-Nala's brother **Puṣkara** is goaded by Kali to challenge Nala to *dyūta* (dice) (Mbh Vana Parva 56).
- **The ruinous game** - Maddened, Nala gambles away gold, chariots, garments, and finally his **entire kingdom** to Puṣkara over many months; counsel and Damayantī's pleas fail (Mbh Vana Parva 57).
- **Saving the children** - Foreseeing disaster, Damayantī sends her son and daughter with the charioteer **Vārṣṇeya** to her father Bhīma in Kuṇḍina for safety (Mbh Vana Parva 57).
- **Stripped and exiled** - Puṣkara wins all and decrees none may aid the brother. Nala and Damayantī leave the city with **only the single garment each wears**, wandering the wilderness (Mbh Vana Parva 58).
- **The birds steal the last cloth** - Starving Nala tries to net golden birds for food; they fly off with his only garment — revealing themselves as **the dice in bird-form**, taunting that they would not leave him even a covering. Husband and wife are left to **share Damayantī's single cloth** (Mbh Vana Parva 58).

## Act V — The abandonment

- **Nala deserts Damayantī** - Tormented by Kali within and convinced she will fare better returning to her own people, Nala **cuts away half of Damayantī's single garment** with an abandoned sword while she sleeps, and steals away into the night, returning twice in anguish before finally fleeing (Mbh Vana Parva 59).

## Act VI — Damayantī alone in the forest

- **Lament and the python** - Waking abandoned, Damayantī wanders lamenting; a **giant python/serpent** seizes her (Mbh Vana Parva 60–63).
- **The hunter** - A **hunter (vyādha)** kills the serpent and frees her, then lusts after her; she **curses him dead** by the power of her chastity (Mbh Vana Parva 63).
- **The merchant caravan** - She joins a trading **caravan**, which is later destroyed in a night stampede of wild elephants near a lake; survivors blame the strange woman and she wanders on (Mbh Vana Parva 64).
- **Refuge in Chedi** - She reaches the city of **Subāhu, king of Chedi**, disheveled and taken for a madwoman; the queen-mother takes her in as a **sairandhrī (attendant)** named **Bṛhannalā/"Sairandhrī,"** on condition she neither eats leavings nor speaks to men (Mbh Vana Parva 65).
- **Recognized by the mole** - The Brahmin **Sudeva**, sent by her father, finds her and recognizes the **lotus-mark between her brows** beneath the grime; the Chedi queen-mother turns out to be Damayantī's **maternal aunt** (both daughters of King Sudāman of Daśārṇa). Subāhu sends her home to Vidarbha in honor (Mbh Vana Parva 65–66).
  - *Variant (regional/folk retellings):* the recognition turns more on Damayantī's faultless conduct and royal bearing than on the birthmark alone [folk tradition].

## Act VII — Nala, Kārkoṭaka, and the disguise of Bāhuka

- **Rescuing the burning nāga** - Nala hears a voice from a forest fire: the serpent-king **Kārkoṭaka**, cursed by the sage Nārada to stay immobile until Nala saves him. Nala carries him from the flames (Mbh Vana Parva 66).
- **The protective bite** - Kārkoṭaka **bites Nala**, whose venom does not kill but (a) **torments Kali within him**, beginning the demon's expulsion, and (b) **transforms Nala into a short, ugly man (a dwarf/hunchback)**, hiding his identity (Mbh Vana Parva 66).
- **The divine garments** - Kārkoṭaka gives Nala **two celestial garments (vastra)** that, when worn, will **restore his true form** whenever he wishes (Mbh Vana Parva 66).
- **Instructions** - The nāga directs Nala to **Ayodhyā** to serve King **Ṛtuparṇa** under the alias **Bāhuka**, as charioteer and cook — for Ṛtuparṇa holds the **akṣa-hṛdaya** (secret of dice), which Nala must obtain to undo his ruin (Mbh Vana Parva 66).
- **Bāhuka at Ṛtuparṇa's court** - The disguised Nala becomes a superlative charioteer and cook (his Agni-/Yama-given gifts still work — he summons fire and water effortlessly), serving alongside the grooms Jīvala and Vārṣṇeya (Mbh Vana Parva 67).

## Act VIII — The second-svayaṃvara ruse

- **Damayantī's stratagem** - Back home, Damayantī sends Brahmins everywhere reciting a cryptic verse reproaching a husband who cut his sleeping wife's garment — listening for the reply only Nala could give. The Brahmin **Parṇāda** reports a strange dwarf charioteer of Ṛtuparṇa who answered with grief, defending the deserter (Mbh Vana Parva 68–70).
- **The false announcement** - Suspecting Bāhuka is Nala, Damayantī proclaims a **second svayaṃvara to be held tomorrow at dawn in Kuṇḍina** — an impossible distance — knowing only Nala's superhuman charioteering could cover it in time (Mbh Vana Parva 70).
- **The race across the land** - Ṛtuparṇa, eager to compete, has **Bāhuka drive him 100+ yojanas in a single day**; the chariot flies so fast Ṛtuparṇa's own outer garment blows away and cannot be retrieved (Mbh Vana Parva 71).

## Act IX — The exchange of secret knowledges

- **The Vibhītaka tree** - To prove his arithmetic, Ṛtuparṇa **counts the leaves and fruit of a Vibhītaka (bibhitaki) tree at a glance**; astonished Bāhuka verifies it by felling a branch and finds him exact (Mbh Vana Parva 72).
- **Trade of the two "hearts"** - Marveling, Bāhuka begs Ṛtuparṇa to teach him the **akṣa-hṛdaya** (secret of dice / numbers); in return Nala gives Ṛtuparṇa the **aśva-hṛdaya** (secret of horses/charioteering) (Mbh Vana Parva 72).
- **Kali expelled** - The instant the dice-secret enters Nala, **Kali is forced out of his body**, vomited forth; the demon, terrified, **begs forgiveness**. Nala curses him but spares his life; Kali takes refuge in the **Vibhītaka tree** (which is thereafter shunned). Nala is freed at last (Mbh Vana Parva 72).
  - *Note:* this is the demon **Kali (Kaliyuga's personification)** — the same malign power whose age we now inhabit ([Kali Yuga README](./../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md)); the tale dramatizes Kali's defeat by dharma, virtue, and self-mastery.

## Act X — Recognition and reunion

- **No second svayaṃvara found** - Arriving in Kuṇḍina, Ṛtuparṇa finds no suitors and no svayaṃvara, and tactfully says nothing (Mbh Vana Parva 73).
- **Kesinī's tests** - Damayantī sends her maid **Kesinī** to observe Bāhuka. She reports uncanny signs: doorways shrink/rise to admit him, vessels fill with water at his glance, fire kindles untended, grass and flowers he handles bloom fresh, and meat he cooks needs no water (his divine boons) (Mbh Vana Parva 74).
- **The children's proof** - Damayantī has her **son and daughter brought before Bāhuka**; on seeing them he weeps and embraces them, betraying a father's heart (Mbh Vana Parva 75).
- **The confrontation** - Summoned, Bāhuka and Damayantī exchange the reproachful verses; he explains his desertion as Kali's doing. To dispel her last doubt about his altered body, Nala **dons Kārkoṭaka's divine garments and resumes his true radiant form** (Mbh Vana Parva 75–76).
- **The vindication** - When Damayantī fears blame for summoning him under a false svayaṃvara, the **Wind-god (Vāyu) speaks from the sky** affirming her unbroken chastity; flowers rain down. They are reunited (Mbh Vana Parva 76).

## Act XI — Recovery of the kingdom

- **Return to Niṣadha** - Armed now with the dice-secret, Nala returns and challenges **Puṣkara** to a rematch, staking everything (and his very life) on a single throw (Mbh Vana Parva 77).
- **The kingdom won back** - Nala wins all back at dice; magnanimously he **spares and even honors Puṣkara** rather than killing him. He is restored to his throne with Damayantī, and Bṛhadaśva closes the tale, having taught Yudhiṣṭhira the *akṣa-hṛdaya* (Mbh Vana Parva 78).

## Themes & cross-links

- **Mirror of the main epic** - Nala's dice-ruin, exile, and recovery deliberately parallel Yudhiṣṭhira's, offering hope that the [Mahābhārata's](./mahabharata/) own catastrophe can be reversed.
- **Kali as moral allegory** - The demon **Kali** can possess Nala only through a single ritual lapse and is driven out by knowledge and virtue — a dharma-lesson about the [Kali Yuga](./../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md) we live in.
- **Truth (satya) as power** - Damayantī's chastity and Nala's truthfulness repeatedly bend reality (cursing the hunter, the gods' self-revelation, Vāyu's testimony).
- **Afterlife of the tale** - Reworked across centuries: Śrīharṣa's *Naiṣadhīya-carita* (Sanskrit *mahākāvya*, 12th c.) [late text]; vernacular versions in Telugu, Tamil, Hindi, Bengali, and Kannada; a staple of Kathakali, Yakshagana, and folk theatre [folk tradition].
