# Siruthondar — the child-curry test of Bhairava

> A **Kali-Yuga documented-history** Tamil Śaiva legend: Śiva, disguised as a **Bhairava (Kāpālika) ascetic**, tests the war-hero-turned-devotee **Ciṟuttoṇṭar** by demanding a curry cooked from his only son **Cīrāḷaṉ** — the most extreme obedience-tale in the Nāyaṉār canon. Its hero is historically the Pallava general **Paranjōti**, who sacked Vātāpi in **642 CE** [scholarly].
> ⬆ Parent index: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Master index: [README](../README.md) · Roster: [The 63 Nāyaṉārs](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/alvars-and-nayanars.md) · Text map: [Periya Purāṇam — legend map](../90-literature-corpus/periya-puranam-63-nayanmar-legend-map.md)

**Ciṟuttoṇṭar** ("the little/humble servant," Tamil *ciṟu* "small" + *toṇṭar* "devotee") is the **36th of the 63 Nāyaṉārs** — the canonical Tamil Śaiva poet-saints and servant-devotees. His *Puranam* in **Cēkkiḻār's Periya Purāṇam** (c. 1135 CE) tells the corpus's single most disturbing test of *bhakti*: the god **Śiva**, come as a wandering **Bhairava mendicant**, demands to be fed a curry made from the flesh of a **five-year-old only son**, killed and cooked by the boy's own parents, who must serve it **without a trace of grief**. The devotee and his wife comply to the letter — and the child is restored alive. The tale is simultaneously a **historical memory**: Ciṟuttoṇṭar is identified within tradition itself with **Paranjōti**, the Pallava commander who led Narasiṃhavarman I's army to burn the Cāḷukya capital **Vātāpi (Bādāmi)**, then renounced war for Śiva.

---

## Where it sits — the two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — As a devotional *līlā*, the episode belongs to the timeless mode in which **Śiva walks the earth in disguise to test his devotees** (the Bhairava/Kāpālika begging-form), a recurring Śaiva motif that has no fixed yuga-date. The Nāyaṉār hagiography places all such saints within **Kali Yuga**, the present degenerate age (began **3102 BCE** on the scriptural clock), when *bhakti* is upheld as the surest path to grace.
- **Academic / historical frame** — Ciṟuttoṇṭar/Paranjōti is a **genuine 7th-century CE figure**. The Vātāpi campaign of **Narasiṃhavarman I Māmalla (Pallava)** against **Pulakeśin II (Cāḷukya)** is dated **[scholarly] c. 642 CE**. The saint is named already in **Cuntarar's (Sundarar's) Tiruttoṇṭattokai** (7th–8th c. CE) and given his full legend by **Cēkkiḻār** (c. 1135 CE). So: the *man* is documented history; the *miracle* is hagiography layered over him **[scholarly]**. The two are kept distinct — the corpus files this under **Kali Yuga — Documented History**.

## The historical Paranjōti — general of Vātāpi

- **The Pallava commander** — Before his sainthood the hero was **Paranjōti**, a general (*senāpati*) in the army of the Pallava king **Narasiṃhavarman I "Māmalla"** of Kāñci. He led the campaign that avenged the earlier Cāḷukya sack of Kāñci and **destroyed the Cāḷukya capital Vātāpi (modern Bādāmi, Karnataka)**, defeating and (by Pallava record) killing **Pulakeśin II**. [scholarly] c. 642 CE. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam; Pallava epigraphy)
- **The renunciation** — Sated with victory and its slaughter, Paranjōti **laid down his arms**, returned south to his native town **Tirucceṅkāṭṭaṅkuḍi** (Tiruchengattankudi, Nāgapaṭṭinam district), and gave himself wholly to the service of Śiva — earning the humble name **Ciṟuttoṇṭar**, "the little servant." (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)
- **His vow of hospitality** — At Tirucceṅkāṭṭaṅkuḍi (whose Śiva is **Uttarapatīśvara / Gaṇapatīśvara**) he took a standing vow: **never to eat without first feeding a devotee of Śiva (a Māheśvara / aḍiyār)**. His household — wife **Tiruveṇkāṭṭu Nangai** (Veṇkāṭṭu Nangaiyār), son **Cīrāḷaṉ** (Seeralan), and the loyal servant-woman — lived to keep that vow. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)
  - *Disambiguation [scholarly]:* This **Paranjōti = Ciṟuttoṇṭar** (7th-c. general-saint) is **not** to be confused with **Paranjōti Munivar**, the c. **17th-century** author of the canonical *Tiruviḷaiyāḍal Purāṇam* of Madurai — a different person a millennium later. See [Śiva myth-cycle](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md).

## The Bhairava's arrival and the terrible demand

- **The starving guest** — One day, when no aḍiyār had come and the family waited hungry, a fierce **Bhairava ascetic** — matted-haired, ash-smeared, a **Kāpālika** bearing skull-bowl and trident — arrived. Overjoyed to have a Śiva-devotee to feed at last, Ciṟuttoṇṭar and his wife welcomed him and begged him to accept a meal. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)
- **"I eat only one food"** — The ascetic warned that his food-vow was one no householder could keep: he ate **only the flesh of a human child** — and not any child, but a **male, five years old, an only son, unblemished and of good family**, and it must be **killed by the father while the mother holds it, and cooked by the mother**, with **the parents smiling, willing, and without sorrow**. He named the impossible conditions precisely to turn the devotee away. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam) [the deliberately unbearable test-terms]
- **The one child who fits** — Ciṟuttoṇṭar answers that he **has exactly such a son** — **Cīrāḷaṉ**, five years old, his only child — and offers him at once. The ascetic, feigning doubt that the parents could really do it, presses; the couple insist with joy. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)

## The cooking of Cīrāḷaṉ

- **Fetching the boy from school** — Cīrāḷaṉ is away at the **teacher's house / school**. His **mother Veṇkāṭṭu Nangai** goes to bring him; the boy comes home gladly, not knowing why. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)
- **The killing, done by the parents' own hands** — Per the ascetic's terms, **the father holds and the mother restrains**, and the child is slain and its body prepared. The Periya Purāṇam dwells on the parents' **unwavering serenity** — the test is not the deed alone but their **freedom from grief** while doing it. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam) [the central horror of the tale, told as supreme obedience]
- **The head set aside** — As the mother cooks the flesh into a **curry**, the couple, unsure whether the head is wanted, **set the head apart**. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)
- **"Cook the head too"** — When the meal is served, the Bhairava asks whether **any part has been withheld**. Told only the head remains, he orders **that too be cooked and brought** — nothing of the child may be spared. The couple obey without demur. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)

## "Call your son to the table" — the reversal

- **The demand for a companion at the meal** — Seated before the curry, the ascetic refuses to eat alone: he insists the **host's own son** join the meal, for he will not eat **unless a child of the house shares his plate**. The parents — who have just cooked that very son — do not flinch; they take it as one more term of hospitality. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)
- **The mother calls "Cīrāḷaṉ!"** — At the ascetic's command, **Veṇkāṭṭu Nangai goes to the door and calls her son by name** — "Cīrāḷaṉ, come to eat!" — exactly as she would on any other day. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)
- **The child runs in alive** — At the call **Cīrāḷaṉ comes running home, whole and living**, as though he had merely been out playing. When the parents turn back to the guest, the **Bhairava and the curry have both vanished**. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam) [the miracle: the sacrificed son restored the instant of the call]
- **Śiva's revelation and the boon** — **Śiva then appears in his true form, with Umā, on the bull Nandi**, declaring the test passed. He takes Ciṟuttoṇṭar, his wife, his son Cīrāḷaṉ, and even his devoted servant to his own world — the household **attains Kailāsa / Śivaloka** together. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)

## Sundarar's roll-call and the canonization

- **Named in the Tiruttoṇṭattokai** — Ciṟuttoṇṭar is honoured in **Cuntarar's (Sundarar's) Tiruttoṇṭattokai** ("the list of the holy servants," Tēvāram Book 7), the 11-verse hymn whose refrain *"aḍiyēṉ"* ("I am servant to…") names the great devotees group by group. He appears in **verse 6** of the Tiruttoṇṭattokai — Sundarar bowing to *"the servant of the one who fed the Bhairava."* (Cuntarar, Tiruttoṇṭattokai v. 6)
- **Expanded to full legend** — The bare name-list was expanded by **Nampiyāṇṭār Nampi** (the *Tiruttoṇṭar Tiruvantāti*, c. late 10th–early 11th c.) and finally versified as full hagiography by **Cēkkiḻār** in the **Periya Purāṇam** (c. 1135 CE), where the **Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam** gives the story above. (Periya Purāṇam; Tiruttoṇṭattokai) See [Periya Purāṇam legend-map](../90-literature-corpus/periya-puranam-63-nayanmar-legend-map.md).
- **The child-saint Cīrāḷaṉ** — In later Tamil devotion **Cīrāḷaṉ himself** is venerated as a child-martyr of *bhakti*; the family is remembered as a group at **Tirucceṅkāṭṭaṅkuḍi**, whose temple preserves shrines and the festival of the *Ciṟuttoṇṭar* household. [regional]

## Reading the tale — obedience, Bhairava, and the ethics of the test

- **The extreme of *toṇḍu* (service)** — The Nāyaṉār corpus prizes **unconditional service to Śiva and his devotees** over conventional morality; Ciṟuttoṇṭar's story is its outer limit — the willingness to give **even one's only child, cheerfully**, because the guest is Śiva's devotee. It is deliberately paired in the tradition with other "obedience beyond ethics" Nāyaṉār tales (see below). [scholarly]
  - *The debate [disputed]:* This is the **most contested episode of Tamil Śaivism** — modern readers and even pre-modern commentators find the child-killing morally intolerable, and the tale is variously read as (a) a literal charter of absolute surrender, (b) an allegory in which the "child" is the ego offered up, or (c) a test whose whole point is that Śiva **never lets the deed stand** — the son is always restored, so no real killing occurs. The corpus records the disagreement without resolving it. [disputed]
- **Why a Bhairava?** — Śiva chooses the **Bhairava / Kāpālika** guise precisely because its transgressive, cremation-ground associations (skull-bowl, flesh-eating) make the demand *plausible* from such a figure — the terrible form that tests whether devotion holds even when the god appears at his most fearsome. Compare the origins of the Bhairava form in [Vaṭuka-Bhairava](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vatuka-bhairava-origin.md) and the martial Bhairava of [Khaṇḍobā / Mārtaṇḍa-Bhairava](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md).
- **The restored child as the tale's key** — Every telling ends the same way: **the son runs in alive at the call**. The reversal is not incidental garnish but the theological pivot — the test is of *the parents' will*, not of the child's life, which Śiva holds in his own keeping. This aligns the story with the broader Śaiva pattern of tests that **look like destruction but are grace**. (Periya Purāṇam, Ciṟuttoṇṭa Purāṇam)
