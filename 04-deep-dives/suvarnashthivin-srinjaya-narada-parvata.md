# Suvarṇaṣṭhīvin — Nārada, Parvata and Sṛñjaya's gold-spitting son

> Where this sits: a *Mahābhārata* consolation-inset of the **Śānti Parva** (Rājadharma), in which **Bhīṣma**, dying on his bed of arrows, tells **Yudhiṣṭhira** the old tale of **King Sṛñjaya** — his boon-son **Suvarṇaṣṭhīvin** ("the boy who voids gold"), the boy's murder by robbers, and his revival by **Nārada** — the tale that carries, as its climax, the roll of the [Sixteen Kings](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md).
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Frame: [Śānti Parva](mahabharata/12-shanti-parva.md) · Twin tale: [Jantu — Somaka's hundred sons](jantu-somaka.md)

This is the **full narrative frame** of the [Ṣoḍaśarājakīya](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md) in its Śānti-Parva recension — the version in which the sage **Nārada** (with his nephew **Parvata**) recites the deaths of sixteen mighty emperors of old to console **King Sṛñjaya** over a dead son. The corpus has carried only the **one roster-bullet** of this frame in the [Sixteen Kings](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md) file (and a naming crosswalk in [Jantu](jantu-somaka.md)); none of the surrounding plot — the two sages' vow, Nārada's love for **Sukumārī**, the reciprocal curses that gave Nārada a monkey's face, the golden boon-child and his slaughter — has been told before. This file is that plot's canonical home.

---

## A note on names — reading the tale's proper nouns

- **Suvarṇaṣṭhīvin** — the boon-son's name is a compound: *suvarṇa* ("gold") + *ṣṭhīvin* (from *ṣṭhīv-*, "to spit / eject"), i.e. **"one who spits (voids) gold."** By the boon, everything his body eliminated — spittle, phlegm, sweat, urine, ordure — turned to gold; the name records the miracle and, in the same breath, seals his doom. Ganguli anglicises the name **Suvarṇaṣṭhīvin / Swarnashthivin**; the Śānti-Parva frame in [Sixteen Kings](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md) spells it **Svarṇaṣṭhīvin**. [scripture]
- **Sṛñjaya** — the grieving father, a Pāñcāla king of the **Lunar line (Candravaṃśa)**; "Sṛñjaya" is also the clan-name of a Pāñcāla branch, so the tale's Sṛñjaya is at once a person and an eponym. **Not** to be confused with [Somaka](jantu-somaka.md), the twin-tale Pāñcāla king who *sacrifices* his only son for a hundred — the two upākhyānas are structural mirrors (a king, an only son, a loss, a restoration) but distinct stories in distinct parvans. [scholarly]
- **Nārada** — the sky-roaming Devarṣi (see [Nārada](narada.md)); here narrator of the Sixteen Kings **and** the wonder-worker who first grants the boon and finally raises the dead boy. This is one of the rare tales in which Nārada is not the meddler but the **consoler and restorer**.
- **Parvata** — Nārada's companion in the frame, described as his **nephew** (sister's son, *bhāgineya*); the two are inseparable wandering ascetics bound by a mutual vow of full disclosure. Not the mountain-personification of the same name, nor a place. [scholarly]
- **Sukumārī** — Sṛñjaya's daughter, whose beauty triggers the curse-exchange; the corpus returns zero prior mentions of her — this file is her first appearance. [scripture]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — Sṛñjaya reigns in the **deep dynastic past of the current 28th Mahāyuga**, in the Pāñcāla (Lunar-line) antiquity; the tale is *narrated* by Bhīṣma in the **Dvāpara→Kali junction**, in the days after the Kurukṣetra war, before Kali Yuga's onset (traditionally 3102 BCE). Nārada and Parvata, being deathless ṛṣis, move freely across the yugas in which the story is set and told. [scripture]
- **Academic / historical frame** — the episode belongs to the **Rājadharma-parvan of the Śānti Parva**, part of the great post-war didactic stratum of the epic. Text-critical dating of the Śānti Parva's instructional matter: **[scholarly] c. 200 BCE – 400 CE**, appreciably later than the epic's martial core; the constituted text is the BORI/Poona Critical Edition (Śāntiparvan, 1949–54). The tale is a **narrative charter for the vanity of grief and the mortality even of gold-rich kings**, not datable history. [scholarly]

---

## The frame — Bhīṣma consoles Yudhiṣṭhira

- **The occasion** — In the Rājadharma teaching, **Yudhiṣṭhira**, sick with grief for the slain of Kurukṣetra, is instructed by **Bhīṣma**, who "in this connection" recites the ancient dialogue between **Nārada and Sṛñjaya** as a specific antidote to a father's mourning (*Mahābhārata*, Śānti Parva ~29–31; Ganguli Sections XXIX–XXXI). [scripture]
- **A story within a story** — Bhīṣma is the outer narrator; **Nārada** is the inner narrator of the Sixteen-Kings recitation; **Sṛñjaya** the bereaved listener within. The Sixteen Kings thus reach Yudhiṣṭhira across *two* frames of consolation — grief answered by the memory of grief answered. [scholarly]
- **Parallel telling** — the *same* roll of sixteen emperors is recited by **Vyāsa** to console Yudhiṣṭhira after Abhimanyu's death in the **Droṇa Parva** (~55–72); the Śānti-Parva Sṛñjaya frame and the Droṇa-Parva Abhimanyu frame are the two great settings of the [Ṣoḍaśarājakīya](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md). [scripture]

## The two sages' vow

- **Nārada and Parvata** — the Devarṣi Nārada and his nephew Parvata wander the worlds together, bound by a **compact of total transparency**: whatever thought arose in the mind of either, and whatever either wished to eat or do, must be disclosed to the other — **nothing concealed** (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXX). [scripture]
- **The lodging with Sṛñjaya** — the two sages come to **King Sṛñjaya**, who receives them with every honour; they dwell in his house, and his daughter **Sukumārī** waits upon them (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXX). [scripture]

---

## The boon of a gold-voiding son

- **Sṛñjaya asks a boon** — pleased with the king's devotion, the sages offer him a boon. Sṛñjaya asks for **a son** of surpassing energy, forbearance and fame — and, specifically, **a son from whose body would issue gold**: whose every excretion should be gold, so that his line might never want (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXX). [scripture]
- **Nārada grants it — with a warning** — Nārada bestows the son, to be named **Suvarṇaṣṭhīvin** because he would **void gold**; but he foretells the price: **the very gold will make the boy short-lived**, for men will covet it and slay him. Sṛñjaya, out of a father's longing, accepts the gift even at that cost (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXX–XXXI). [scripture]
  - *Reading:* the boon is a **trap dressed as a gift** — the miracle that enriches the child is the same fact that dooms him; the tale is built on this irony, and Nārada's foreknowledge makes the later revival a completion of the boon rather than a reversal of it. [scholarly]
- **The child born** — in time the son is born, and it is as promised: whatever the boy's body eliminates — phlegm, urine, sweat, ordure — becomes **gold**, and the palace prospers past reckoning (*Śānti Parva*). [scripture]

## Sukumārī and the reciprocal curses — Nārada's monkey face

*This sub-tale is the frame's most vivid episode: an aside on why the messenger-sage bore, for a time, the face of an ape.*

- **Nārada smitten** — dwelling in the king's house, **Nārada beholds Sukumārī** and is seized with love for her. Sṛñjaya, to gratify his guest, **bestows Sukumārī on Nārada** in marriage (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXX). [scripture]
- **The concealment** — because he desired the girl, Nārada **hid the fact from Parvata**, breaking their vow of full disclosure — fearing, the text implies, that his nephew might also covet her (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXX). [scripture]
- **Parvata's curse** — discovering the breach, the wronged Parvata curses Nārada: *"Because you concealed from me the thing you should have told, your face — beautiful as it is — shall become the face of a **monkey (ape)**."* (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXX). [scripture]
- **Nārada's counter-curse** — stung, Nārada retorts with a curse of his own: that Parvata, for all his penances, **shall not be able to ascend to heaven** (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXX). [scripture]
- **Sukumārī's fidelity** — Nārada weds Sukumārī nonetheless, now **ape-faced**; yet she, seeing her husband's disfigurement, **does not forsake him**, cleaving to him faithfully and refusing to look on any other — the loyalty that ultimately dissolves the curses (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXXI). [scripture]
- **The reconciliation** — Parvata, wandering, later meets Nārada again; moved by his uncle's plight and Sukumārī's devotion, and pained that each has cursed the other, he **releases Nārada from the ape-face**, restoring his beauty; Nārada in turn **frees Parvata to reach heaven**. Sukumārī, beholding her husband suddenly transformed to loveliness, at first fears he is a stranger, until Nārada reassures her, and the two sages part in love (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXXI). [scripture]
  - *Variant (relationship & motive):* recensions and retellings agree Parvata is Nārada's **nephew** and that the curse is for a **broken vow of concealment**; some retellings soften Nārada's motive (fear of rivalry) or leave Sukumārī's bestowal implicit rather than stated. The **ape-face curse and its mutual dissolution are stable** across the tradition. [scholarly] [disputed]

## The murder — robbers, gold, and an empty corpse

- **The rumour of gold** — word spreads that the prince's very body yields gold; **robbers** conclude the boy must be **full of gold within**, a living treasure-house to be plundered (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXXI). [scripture]
- **The killing** — the robbers **seize and slay Suvarṇaṣṭhīvin**, then cut and search his body for the hoarded gold — and **find nothing**: the gold came *through* the living boy, not stored in his dead flesh. They gain nothing; the corpse is left, and the murderers themselves perish (in some tellings falling out over the non-existent spoil) (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXXI). [scripture]
- **The double loss** — Sṛñjaya thus loses **both son and gold at a stroke**, exactly as Nārada's warning had foretold: the boon consumed itself (*Śānti Parva*). [scholarly]

## The grief, the Sixteen Kings, and the revival

- **Sṛñjaya's grief** — the king is prostrated with sorrow for his slain son; it is to answer *this* grief that **Nārada** appears and speaks (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXXI). [scripture]
- **The roll of the Sixteen** — Nārada recites the deaths of sixteen supreme emperors of antiquity — **Marutta, Suhotra, Śibi, Bhagīratha, Rāma, Māndhātṛ, Yayāti, Ambarīṣa, Śaśabindu, Gaya, Rantideva, Bharata, Pṛthu, Dilīpa, Sagara** and their peers — each a sacrificer, conqueror and giver beyond measure, and each dead; the cumulative argument is that **death spares none**, so grief for one boy is vain (*Śānti Parva*; full roster and recensional variants in [Sixteen Kings](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md)). [scripture]
  - *The Śānti-Parva refrain* — after each king Nārada presses the point that this emperor was **far greater** than Sṛñjaya's son — a performer of Rājasūyas and horse-sacrifices, a giver of oceans of gold — and that **no one said to him "arise and live"**; why then should Sṛñjaya mourn a son who did no such deeds? The rhetoric is *a fortiori*, driving toward resignation. [scripture]
- **The offer and the revival** — moved by the king's love (and, in the logic of the boon, completing his own gift), **Nārada offers to restore the boy** and asks which boon Sṛñjaya will have; the king begs for his son's life. **Nārada revives Suvarṇaṣṭhīvin**, who lives out a full span of years — the tale closing not on the mortality-lesson but on grace beyond it (*Śānti Parva*, Ganguli XXXI). [scripture]
  - *Variant (function of the revival):* the Sixteen-Kings recitation is formally a **teaching that the dead do not return** — yet this frame ends with a **resurrection**, a tension the tradition preserves rather than resolves: the lesson consoles, and then the sage's grace exceeds the lesson. The [Droṇa-Parva parallel](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md) (Vyāsa to Yudhiṣṭhira) has **no revival** — Abhimanyu stays dead — sharpening the contrast between the two frames. [scholarly]

## Reading the tale

- **The gold that kills** — Suvarṇaṣṭhīvin is a parable of **wealth as a mark for violence**: the body that mints gold is the body men will butcher, and the treasure that seemed a dynasty's security is the reason it is destroyed. The boon and the doom are one fact. [scholarly]
- **The twin of Jantu** — the tale is the near-perfect mirror of [Jantu](jantu-somaka.md): both are Pāñcāla kings (Sṛñjaya / Somaka) who lose an only, cherished son and regain him by a sage's or a rite's power; where Jantu's father *sacrifices* his son and pays in hell, Sṛñjaya's son is *stolen* by fate and *given back* by grace. The corpus keeps them as a deliberate pair. [scholarly]
- **Nārada inverted** — elsewhere the [kalaha-priya](narada.md) sage sows the quarrels that drive the bhakti-tales; here he is the **consoler who raises the dead**, and even his own humiliation (the ape-face) is folded into a lesson on fidelity and forgiveness. [scholarly]
- **Consolation that overflows itself** — the Ṣoḍaśarājakīya is designed to teach that mourning cannot recall the dead; this frame lets the teaching stand and then **transcends it with a miracle**, a shape the tradition uses deliberately — the argument disciplines the grief, the grace answers the love. [scholarly]
