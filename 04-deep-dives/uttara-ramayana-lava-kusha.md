# The Uttara Rāmāyaṇa — Rāma-rājya, Sītā's Banishment, Lava-Kuśa & Śambūka

> Cosmic address: [Vaivasvata Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md) → 28th Mahāyuga → **[Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md)** → the closing chapter and aftermath of the Rāma-avatāra cycle. > Companion files (event-by-event narrative): **[Uttara Kāṇḍa kāṇḍa-summary](ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md)** · [Yuddha Kāṇḍa (the first agni-parīkṣā)](ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md) · [Rāmāyaṇa overview & recensions](ramayana/README.md)

This file is **not** the kāṇḍa-summary (that is [here](ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md)). It treats the *uttara* ("latter") material as a single **literary and ethical problem-cluster** — the aftermath cycle that turns the triumphant Rāma into the most morally interrogated figure in the tradition. Each beat (the washerman, the second ordeal-demand, the banishment, the twins, the Aśvamedha and the capture of the horse, Śambūka, Sītā's return to the Earth, Rāma's *jala-samādhi*) is followed across the four great post-Vālmīki re-handlings — **Kālidāsa's *Raghuvaṃśa*** (cantos 14–15), the **Padma Purāṇa** (Pātāla-khaṇḍa), **Bhavabhūti's *Uttararāmacarita*** (a 7-act drama), and the **folk / regional Rāmāyaṇas** (the Lava-Kuśa cycle, Kamban, Tulsīdās) — together with the [disputed] authenticity debate that hangs over all of it.

> **The two clocks.** *Scripture-internal:* all of this is Tretā Yuga, in the closing centuries of the 11,000-year Rāma-rājya. *Academic:* the Uttara Kāṇḍa is dated by most philologists later than the epic's 5–6-book core; Kālidāsa is c. 4th–5th c. CE, the Padma Purāṇa's Pātāla-khaṇḍa is medieval, and Bhavabhūti is c. early 8th c. CE — so the "after-story" we read is itself a thousand-year conversation *about* a story. [scholarly]

---

## I. The problem stated: why is there an *uttara* at all?

- **The structural seam** - Vālmīki's Yuddha Kāṇḍa already ends in catharsis — Rāvaṇa dead, the [first agni-parīkṣā](ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md) passed, coronation, *phala-śruti*. The Uttara Kāṇḍa re-opens a closed narrative; many readers feel the epic "ends twice." This doubled ending is the formal sign of the aftermath as a distinct compositional layer (Vālmīki *Uttara* 37 ff.). [scholarly]
- **The ethical core** - The aftermath exists to ask one question Vālmīki's heroic books cannot: what does a king who *is* dharma do when **public dharma (rāja-dharma) and private dharma (love, justice to Sītā) collide irreconcilably**? The answer the text gives — Rāma sacrifices his own happiness and his innocent wife to the demands of reputation — is precisely what makes the section a perennial controversy rather than a coda.
- **The "two-clock" tension internal to the text** - Rāma is simultaneously the perfect human king (who *must* heed *loka-apavāda*, popular censure) and Viṣṇu incarnate (who *knows* Sītā's purity absolutely). The aftermath is the drama of a god acting out the constraints of the man he chose to be (Vālmīki *Uttara* 45). 
- **The interpolation thesis as framing device** - Because Śambūka and the banishment seem to contradict the Rāma of Books 2–6, the modern question "is the Uttara Kāṇḍa authentic?" *is itself* part of how the tradition processes the ethical problem — excision and allegory are both ways of saving Rāma's character. See [§IX](#ix-the-disputed-authenticity-debate). [disputed]

## II. Rāma-rājya — the ideal reign as backdrop

- **The proverbial perfect kingdom** - Timely rains, no premature death, no disease, no theft, no widow's grief; the dharma-bull standing on **three legs** (the Tretā quota — cf. [yugas](../00-time-and-cosmology/yugas-and-mahayuga.md)). "Rāma-rājya" becomes the standing Indian metaphor for just governance (Vālmīki *Uttara* 42).
- **The reign's length** - Traditionally **eleven thousand years** (*ekādaśa sahasrāṇi*) — a Tretā-scale lifespan that the human-drama of the banishment sits inside (Vālmīki *Uttara* 42; echoed at the close).
- **The irony the aftermath exploits** - The very perfection of the reign is what makes the slander unbearable: in a kingdom where the king's word is law and example, a single washerman's grumble about the *queen's* honour becomes a flaw in the cosmic order Rāma embodies. The utopia is the pressure that detonates the tragedy.
  - *Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa 14):* the reign is painted as Rāma ruling his brothers "with equal love," holding the triad of *dharma-artha-kāma* in balance — the calm before canto 15's grief (Raghu. 14).

## III. The washerman (dhobī) and the second ordeal-demand

- **The slander (loka-apavāda)** - Rāma's secret agents (*cāra*) report night-talk in the city: citizens murmur that the king has taken back a wife who lived a year in **another man's house** (Laṅkā), and that now they must tolerate the like in their own wives (Vālmīki *Uttara* 43–44).
- **The washerman scene** - The proverbial spark: a **washerman (rajaka)**, throwing out his own wife who had stayed a night in another's house, declares "I am not Rāma, to take back a woman who dwelt in a stranger's home." The private quarrel becomes the public verdict the king cannot ignore (Vālmīki *Uttara* 45).
  - *Folk afterlife:* in many regional and oral tellings the washerman is later cursed or punished, and his slander is read as the residue of a past-life grudge — a karmic mechanism that absolves Rāma of arbitrariness. [folk]
- **No second fire-ordeal is actually held in Vālmīki** - A crucial and much-confused point: in Vālmīki, Rāma does **not** demand a second *agni-parīkṣā* in the city; the slander leads straight to **banishment**. The "second ordeal" is a separate later event — the **final public oath** before Sītā returns to the Earth ([§VII](#vii-the-second-public-ordeal-and-sitas-return-to-the-earth)) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 45–47, 96).
  - *Variant (Kālidāsa, Raghuvaṃśa 15):* Kālidāsa **conflates** the pressure into a humiliating renewed demand — the public effectively forces a "second fire-ordeal" expectation, sharpening the injustice; the people then drop their eyes in shame as the pregnant queen is led away. [late text]
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās, Rāmcaritmānas):* the whole banishment is **omitted**; Tulsī's devotional retelling ends in the harmony of the reign, declining to narrate Sītā's abandonment at all. [folk / devotional]
- **The collision made explicit** - Rāma to his brothers: he would abandon Lakṣmaṇa, or life itself, rather than let *apavāda* touch the Ikṣvāku throne — the rāja-dharma absolutism that the rest of the cycle tests to destruction (Vālmīki *Uttara* 45).

## IV. The banishment to Vālmīki's āśrama

- **Lakṣmaṇa as unwilling instrument** - Rāma orders **Lakṣmaṇa** to take the **pregnant** Sītā to the forest under the pretext of fulfilling her wish to visit the hermitages on the Gaṅgā, and to leave her there without warning. Lakṣmaṇa, weeping, obeys (Vālmīki *Uttara* 45–47).
- **The abandonment on the Gaṅgā** - Across the Gaṅgā near the **Tamasā**, Lakṣmaṇa reveals the king's command and departs; Sītā's lament — that she is cast off pregnant and blameless, yet she sends Rāma blessings and bids the kings of her line keep dharma — is one of the cycle's emotional peaks (Vālmīki *Uttara* 47–48).
- **Vālmīki's shelter** - The sage **Vālmīki**, knowing her by his ascetic insight, takes Sītā into his āśrama and entrusts her to the women ascetics (Vālmīki *Uttara* 49).
- **Treatments of the banishment:**
  - *Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa 14–15):* Sītā has just told Rāma she longs to see the forest hermitages again — and it is *into* that very wish that the cruel order is folded; the dramatic irony (her happiness used as the vehicle of her exile) is Kālidāsa's signature stroke. The poet stresses Rāma's own ruin: he keeps the kingdom but loses the meaning of it. (Raghu. 14–15)
  - *Bhavabhūti (Uttararāmacarita, Acts I–III):* the play opens with Rāma and Sītā viewing a **Citra-śālā (picture-gallery)** of their own past — a poignant device that lets the audience relive the love about to be destroyed; the banishment-order arrives mid-tenderness. In **Act III** the river-goddesses **Tamasā and Muralā** converse, and Sītā returns as an **invisible, shadow-like presence (chāyā)** who watches the grief-maddened Rāma wander the Daṇḍaka — she suffers his suffering, unseen. Bhavabhūti makes the banishment the engine of *karuṇa-rasa* (the flavour of pathos / compassion), of which he is the acknowledged master ("*eko rasaḥ karuṇa eva*"). [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Kamban, Irāmāvatāram, Tamil):* the entire Uttara material — banishment included — is **absent**; Kamban ends at the coronation. [folk / regional]
  - *Variant (Adhyātma / Ānanda Rāmāyaṇa & folk):* softened by the **Māyā-Sītā / chāyā-Sītā** doctrine (the real Sītā was never truly defiled, only a shadow was abducted), which blunts the scandal's premise — see the [Vedavatī / Māyā-Sītā cross-link](ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md). [late text]
  - *Variant (modern reinterpretations):* C. N. Sreekantan Nair's Malayalam **Kāñcana Sītā** and a large 20th-c. feminist re-reading center Sītā's perspective and indict Rāma's choice. [scholarly / modern]

## V. The birth and education of Lava and Kuśa

- **Birth in the hermitage** - Sītā bears **twin sons**; Vālmīki performs their birth-rites and names them. The traditional folk-etymology: the boys were purified after birth with blades of **kuśa-grass** and **lava** (a tuft / cutting), giving their names (Vālmīki *Uttara* 66; the *kuśa/lava* etymology elaborated by Kālidāsa, Raghu. 15).
- **Reared as ṛṣi-kumāras** - They grow up in the āśrama as Veda-learning ascetic-princes, ignorant of their royal birth and of who their father is — the dramatic-irony engine of the recognition scene to come.
- **Vālmīki teaches them the Rāmāyaṇa** - Vālmīki, the **ādi-kavi** ("first poet"), composes the **24,000-śloka Rāmāyaṇa** and trains the twins to chant it perfectly to musical measure (*gāna*) in the seven *svaras* — so the epic's first reciters are the hero's own unrecognised sons singing his life back to him (Vālmīki *Uttara* 71, 93). This is the tradition's foundational image of itself: poetry born of grief (*śoka → śloka*), transmitted by the wronged son.
- **The "two boys, one face" motif** - Their identical resemblance to Rāma is the visual proof that drives the recognition; exploited heavily in drama and folk theatre.
  - *Bhavabhūti:* Act IV–VII bring Lava and Kuśa to the fore; Lava, not knowing his father, clashes with the royal party — the near-fatal **father-son combat** is staged as tragic irony before recognition. [scholarly]

## VI. The Aśvamedha and the capture of the horse

- **Why an Aśvamedha** - Lacking an acknowledged consort at court (he will not remarry), Rāma performs the great **horse-sacrifice** of imperial sovereignty using a **golden image of Sītā** (*hema-Sītā / kāñcana-Sītā*) in the wife's ritual seat — a standing emblem of his refusal to replace her (Vālmīki *Uttara* 91).
- **The twins chant the epic at the sacrifice** - Lava and Kuśa, brought by Vālmīki to the sacrificial assembly, recite the Rāmāyaṇa before Rāma and the kings; the matter of the song and the boys' faces reveal them as his sons. In Vālmīki this **recognition by recitation** is the climax — gentle, not martial (Vālmīki *Uttara* 93–94).
- **The capture of the horse — the folk/Purāṇic amplification** - Vālmīki keeps the recognition non-violent; the popular imagination did not. A vast tradition has the boys **seize the wandering sacrificial horse**, read out the challenge-plate, and **defeat in succession Śatrughna, Lakṣmaṇa, Bharata and finally Hanumān** (who lets himself be bound, delighting in the boys' valour), before Rāma himself comes and the truth emerges.
  - *Padma Purāṇa (Pātāla-khaṇḍa):* gives the **fullest battle-narrative** — Agastya counsels the Aśvamedha; the horse is loosed on Vaiśākha Pūrṇimā and wanders; it is seized near the āśrama; Kuśa and Lava rout Rāma's army; allied kings (e.g. **Vīramaṇi** and his son) and the brothers are overcome; the recognition follows the war, not a recital. This is the principal scriptural home of the "Lava-Kuśa war." [late text]
  - *Variant (folk / Lava-Kuśa cycle, stage & cinema):* the single most popular form of the whole after-story in South Asia — the boys binding Hanumān, defeating the uncles, and only then being recognised — is the backbone of the blockbuster **Lava Kusa** films and innumerable Harikathā / Yakṣagāna performances. Only lightly present in Vālmīki; massively elaborated in performance. [folk]
  - *Note:* the martial version subtly **rehabilitates Sītā's line** — her sons prove unbeatable, vindicating her honour through their valour rather than through any further ordeal.

## VII. The second public ordeal and Sītā's return to the Earth

- **The summons** - After recognition, Rāma sends for Sītā, intending reunion — but still asks her to **publicly swear her purity once more** before the assembled people, to lay the slander to rest (Vālmīki *Uttara* 96). *This* — not any event before the banishment — is the actual "second ordeal" of the cycle.
- **Bhūmi receives her daughter** - Sītā, having endured enough, makes her oath into a final act of self-vindication and refusal: "If I have never in thought turned to any but Rāma, may my mother **Mādhavī (Bhūmi-Devī, the Earth)** receive me." A jewelled throne rises from the ground borne by nāgas; the Earth-goddess embraces her, and Sītā **descends into the earth forever** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 97).
- **The ring closed** - Sītā was **earth-born** (found in Janaka's furrow — see [Bāla backstory](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md)); her return *to* the Earth completes that arc and is her unanswerable verdict: she proves her truth and simultaneously **refuses to remain** with the husband who twice made her prove it. Rāma's grief and threat to plough up the Earth are calmed by Brahmā's promise of reunion in heaven (Vālmīki *Uttara* 97).
- **Treatments of the ending — the great divergence:**
  - *Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa 15):* follows Vālmīki — Sītā is swallowed by the Earth after the recognition; Rāma is left to rule on in sorrow before his own departure. [tragic ending]
  - *Bhavabhūti (Uttararāmacarita, Act VII):* **breaks with the tragedy** — the play stages a happy reunion. Sītā's chastity is witnessed by **Gaṅgā and Pṛthvī**, and **Arundhatī** formally restores her to Rāma; the family returns to Ayodhyā. Bhavabhūti deliberately overturns the canonical *viraha* (separation) into *milana* (reunion), refusing to leave his audience in the karuṇa he had so masterfully built. This is the most famous "rewrite of the ending" in Sanskrit literature. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (folk / Lava-Kuśa cinema):* most popular versions also end in **reunion** — Sītā restored, the family whole — because audiences would not accept her final loss; the Earth-swallowing is softened or replaced. [folk]
  - *Variant (Māyā-Sītā traditions):* since the abducted Sītā was a shadow, the "real" Sītā emerges/returns from fire and is reunited — the doctrine engineered partly to permit a non-tragic close. [late text]

## VIII. The Śambūka episode — the dharma-controversy

- **The dead brāhmaṇa boy** - A brāhmaṇa's young son dies prematurely; the grieving father lays the death at the king's door — proof of some *adharma* in the realm, since no untimely death should occur under a righteous king (Vālmīki *Uttara* 73–74).
- **Nārada's diagnosis** - The sage **Nārada** explains the text's varṇa-yuga logic: in Tretā, tapas is licensed to the upper varṇas; somewhere a **śūdra is performing austerities** out of his ordained order, and this transgression is the cosmic cause of the boy's death (Vālmīki *Uttara* 74).
- **Rāma slays Śambūka** - Touring the realm on the **Puṣpaka**, Rāma finds the ascetic **Śambūka** hanging head-down in penance; learning he is a śūdra seeking **heaven in his mortal body**, Rāma **beheads him**. The brāhmaṇa boy instantly revives; the gods rain flowers and praise the king (Vālmīki *Uttara* 76).
- **The controversy — among the most contested passages in Hindu literature [disputed]:**
  - *Anti-caste critique:* read as the scriptural enforcement of varṇa-exclusion from spiritual practice — **B. R. Ambedkar** (in *Riddles in Hinduism*) and the **Self-Respect / Dravidian** movement (e.g. **Tripuraneni Ramaswamy's** *Śambuka Vadha*) treat Śambūka as the wronged victim and Rāma as the agent of caste violence. The episode is widely cited in Dalit literature. [scholarly]
  - *Allegorical / devotional defence:* defenders argue Śambūka attains **mokṣa by Rāma's own hand** (death by the avatāra = liberation), that the act is the cosmos self-correcting in the Tretā framework, or that the passage is a **later interpolation** alien to Rāma's character. Tagore and Gandhi treated it as interpolation. [folk / devotional]
  - *Omission in regional Rāmāyaṇas:* **Kamban** and **Tulsīdās** drop the episode entirely; many medieval and modern retellings either omit or soften it — itself evidence of long discomfort with the beat. [folk]
  - *As authenticity-evidence:* for philologists the Śambūka and banishment episodes are **Exhibit A** for the Uttara Kāṇḍa's late, interpolated character (see [§IX](#ix-the-disputed-authenticity-debate)). [scholarly]
  - *Kālidāsa & Padma Purāṇa:* both **retain** the Śambūka killing (Raghu. 15; Purāṇic recensions), showing it was firmly in the received aftermath by the classical and medieval periods — the omissions are the *later* reformist move, not the original. [late text]

## IX. Rāma's departure — the *jala-samādhi* / Mahāprasthāna

- **Kāla comes for Viṣṇu** - **Time (Kāla)**, disguised as an ascetic, requests a sealed private audience to tell Rāma his earthly work is done and Viṣṇu must return; the meeting may not be interrupted on pain of death (Vālmīki *Uttara* 103–104).
- **Durvāsā and the sacrifice of Lakṣmaṇa** - The irascible **Durvāsā** demands instant entry, threatening to curse the realm. To spare the kingdom, **Lakṣmaṇa** breaks the sealed meeting and so incurs death; Rāma, bound by his own word, "abandons" him — Lakṣmaṇa walks into the **Sarayū** and is taken up as his true form, **Śeṣa / Ananta** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 105–106). The cycle's logic of "rāja-dharma over love" claims even the most beloved brother.
- **Succession** - Rāma enthrones the twins — **Kuśa at Kuśāvatī** (and the Ikṣvāku line continues through him; cf. [Sūryavaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)) and **Lava at Śrāvastī** — and settles his brothers' sons over many realms (Vālmīki *Uttara* 107–108).
- **The Great Departure (jala-samādhi)** - Robed in white, holding kuśa-grass, reciting the Veda, Rāma walks out of Ayodhyā to the **Sarayū**, followed by his brothers, the citizens, and the vānaras and ṛkṣas; Brahmā and the gods receive him as he enters the water and **resumes his form as Viṣṇu**, ascending to **Vaikuṇṭha** with all who followed — the collective liberation that closes the avatāra (Vālmīki *Uttara* 109–110).
  - *Variant (Vaiṣṇava devotional):* Rāma reabsorbs the deva-portions that composed the vānaras and ascends four-armed; **Hanumān** is granted **cirañjīvitva** to remain on earth as long as the Rāma-kathā is told — see [The Chiranjīvis](the-chiranjivis.md). [late text]
  - *Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa 15):* Rāma divides the kingdom among brothers and sons and ascends to heaven — the dynastic continuity (the *Raghu-vaṃśa* itself) is Kālidāsa's real subject, so the departure is a transition, not a void. (Raghu. 15)
- **The phala-śruti** - The book closes with the merits of hearing the Rāmāyaṇa — a formal integral ending that the authenticity debate must reckon with (Vālmīki *Uttara* 110).

## IX-b. The [disputed] authenticity debate {#ix-the-disputed-authenticity-debate}

- **The interpolation thesis** - Mainstream modern philology holds the **Uttara Kāṇḍa (with the Bāla Kāṇḍa)** to be a **later addition** to a once five- or six-book core: it is thin in the oldest manuscript strata, its frame ("the sages ask Rāma about Rāvaṇa") is appendix-like, and Śambūka + the banishment seem to **contradict the dharma and character** of the Rāma of Books 2–6. [scholarly]
- **The traditional / counter-view** - The **Baroda Critical Edition did not excise** the Uttara Kāṇḍa; classical commentators, the entire devotional tradition, and Kālidāsa (4th–5th c.) **already knew and retold** the after-story — so it is at minimum very old and was canonical for the whole classical period. Astronomical-dating advocates (e.g. **Nilesh Oak**) argue affirmatively for authenticity. [disputed]
- **Why the debate is itself part of the tradition** - As noted in [§I](#i-the-problem-stated-why-is-there-an-uttara-at-all), excision (Kamban, Tulsī), allegory (mokṣa-by-Rāma's-hand), the Māyā-Sītā doctrine, and Bhavabhūti's happy-ending rewrite are **all strategies for resolving the same ethical scandal**. The aftermath cycle is less a fixed text than a millennium-long argument about whether the perfect king did the right thing. This file records the variants and flags the controversy rather than resolving it.
