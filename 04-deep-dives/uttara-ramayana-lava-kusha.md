# The Uttara Rāmāyaṇa — Rāma-rājya, Sītā's Banishment, Lava-Kuśa & Śambūka

This file is **not** the kāṇḍa-summary (that is [here](ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md)). It treats the *uttara* ("latter") material as a single **literary and ethical problem-cluster** — the aftermath cycle that turns the triumphant Rāma into the most morally interrogated figure in the tradition. Each beat (the slander and its famous washerman, the second ordeal-demand, the banishment, the twins, the Aśvamedha and the capture of the horse, Śambūka, Sītā's return to the Earth, Rāma's *jala-samādhi*) is followed across the four great post-Vālmīki re-handlings — **Kālidāsa's *Raghuvaṃśa*** (cantos 14–15), the **Padma Purāṇa** (Pātāla-khaṇḍa), **Bhavabhūti's *Uttararāmacarita*** (a 7-act drama), and the **folk / regional Rāmāyaṇas** (the Lava-Kuśa cycle, Kamban, Tulsīdās) — with the **Bhāgavata Purāṇa** (9.11) and the **Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa** added where they tell a beat differently, together with the [disputed] authenticity debate that hangs over all of it.

> **The two clocks.** *Scripture-internal:* all of this is Tretā Yuga, in the closing centuries of the Rāma-rājya, which the text itself measures as both ten and eleven thousand years (see §II). *Academic:* the Uttara Kāṇḍa is dated by most philologists later than the epic's 5–6-book core; Kālidāsa is c. 4th–5th c. CE, the Padma Purāṇa's Pātāla-khaṇḍa is medieval, and Bhavabhūti is c. early 8th c. CE — so the "after-story" we read is itself a thousand-year conversation *about* a story. [scholarly]

> **How the verse references work.** "Vālmīki *Uttara* n" is the **vulgate** sarga number, the numbering of H. P. Shastri's translation linked under Sources. Where the line was checked against the Sanskrit, the **Baroda Critical Edition** reference follows as "CE 7.sarga.verse" (GRETIL text). The CE Uttara has 100 sargas to the vulgate's 111 plus inserted sargas, so its numbers run lower: vulgate 42–49 ≈ CE 41–48, vulgate 66–99 ≈ CE 58–89, vulgate 103–110 ≈ CE 93–100. Vulgate 98 and 111 have no CE counterpart.

## I. The problem stated: why is there an *uttara* at all?

- **The structural seam** - Vālmīki's Yuddha Kāṇḍa already ends in catharsis — Rāvaṇa dead, the [first agni-parīkṣā](ramayana/6-yuddha-kanda.md) passed, coronation, and a summary of the ideal reign; in the vulgate it also closes on a full *phala-śruti* (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 128.107–125). The Uttara Kāṇḍa re-opens a closed narrative with a new frame — the sages arrive to congratulate Rāma (CE 7.1.1) — and many readers feel the epic "ends twice." The CE's Yuddha stops at the reign summary without that phala-śruti (CE 6.116.90), but the seam remains: Nārada's summary of the poem in Bāla 1 also ends with the coronation, the long reign and a one-line forecast that Rāma "will go to Brahmaloka", with no word of the banishment (CE 1.1.76–77). [scholarly]
- **The ethical core** - The aftermath exists to ask one question Vālmīki's heroic books cannot: what does a king who *is* dharma do when **public dharma (rāja-dharma) and private dharma (love, justice to Sītā) collide irreconcilably**? The answer the text gives — Rāma sacrifices his own happiness and his innocent wife to the demands of reputation — is precisely what makes the section a perennial controversy rather than a coda.
- **The "two-clock" tension internal to the text** - Rāma is simultaneously the perfect human king (who *must* heed *loka-apavāda*, popular censure) and Viṣṇu incarnate (who *knows* Sītā's purity absolutely). Vālmīki has him state that knowledge in plain human terms to his brothers: Agni, Vāyu, the Sun and the Moon declared her sinless in Laṅkā, and "my inner self knows Sītā to be pure" (*antarātmā ca me vetti sītāṃ śuddhām*) — and still the censure decides (Vālmīki *Uttara* 45; CE 7.44.6–13). The aftermath is the drama of a god acting out the constraints of the man he chose to be.
- **The interpolation thesis as framing device** - Because Śambūka and the banishment seem to contradict the Rāma of Books 2–6, the modern question "is the Uttara Kāṇḍa authentic?" *is itself* part of how the tradition processes the ethical problem — excision and allegory are both ways of saving Rāma's character. See [§IX](#ix-the-disputed-authenticity-debate). [disputed]

## II. Rāma-rājya — the ideal reign as backdrop

- **The proverbial perfect kingdom** - No widow's lament, no fear of wild beasts or disease, no thieves, no old man performing a child's funeral rites, trees always in flower and fruit, rain in its season, every varṇa content in its own work (Vālmīki *Yuddha* 128.99–105; CE 6.116.84–89). "Rāma-rājya" becomes the standing Indian metaphor for just governance. The Uttara opens the reign on Rāma and Sītā's private happiness in the Aśoka grove instead (Vālmīki *Uttara* 42; CE 7.41), but the utopia returns inside the Uttara itself after Sītā has gone into the earth — rain in season, no untimely death, no disease, no adharma (Vālmīki *Uttara* 99; CE 7.89.9–10).
  - *The yuga frame:* the Uttara voices the Tretā doctrine through Nārada — in Tretā, adharma set down its first foot on the earth (Vālmīki *Uttara* 74; CE 7.65.15); hence dharma "on three legs" (cf. [yugas](../00-time-and-cosmology/yugas-and-mahayuga.md)).
- **The reign's length** - The text gives two figures. The Yuddha's reign summary says **ten thousand years** (*daśa varṣa-sahasrāṇi*: Vālmīki *Yuddha* 128.96, 128.106; CE 6.116.82, 6.116.90). Nārada's forecast in the Bāla and Kāla's message in the Uttara say **eleven thousand** — *daśa varṣa-sahasrāṇi daśa varṣa-śatāni ca*, "ten thousand years and ten hundred years" (CE 1.1.76; Vālmīki *Uttara* 104; CE 7.94.12, where it is the span Rāma fixed for himself among men). Either way it is a Tretā-scale lifespan that the human drama of the banishment sits inside.
- **The irony the aftermath exploits** - The very perfection of the reign is what makes the slander unbearable: in a kingdom where the king's word is law and example, street-corner muttering about the *queen's* honour becomes a flaw in the cosmic order Rāma embodies. The utopia is the pressure that detonates the tragedy.
  - *Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa 14):* the reign is painted as Rāma treating *dharma-artha-kāma* and his younger brothers with the same even hand, and the old hardships of the Daṇḍaka, remembered in the painted palace rooms, turning into pleasures — the calm before the slander (Raghu. 14.21–25).

## III. The slander and the second ordeal-demand

- **Bhadra's report (loka-apavāda)** - Sītā, pregnant, has just asked to spend a night at the hermitages on the Gaṅgā, and Rāma has promised she will go next day (Vālmīki *Uttara* 42; CE 7.41.22–26). He then asks **Bhadra**, one of a circle of ten jesting companions, what the city says of him. Bhadra first offers praise; pressed, he reports the talk "in squares, markets, streets, woods and parks": the king took back a wife whom Rāvaṇa had held on his lap and kept in Laṅkā, and "we too will have to bear it in our wives, for as the king does, the subjects follow." The others confirm it (Vālmīki *Uttara* 43; CE 7.42.2–22). In Vālmīki the slander has a named reporter and no single author: it is **general city talk**, and no washerman appears in the sarga.
- **The washerman scene** - The proverbial spark of the *received* story, and one of the clearest cases in the whole cycle of a later scene remembered as Vālmīki's. Its attestations are post-Vālmīki and differ from one another: [late text]
  - *Bhāgavata Purāṇa (9.11.8–10):* Rāma himself, walking the city at night unrecognised, overhears an unnamed man (*kasyacit*) spurning his wife, who had gone to another's house: "I will not keep you; an uxorious Rāma may keep Sītā, not I." Afraid of the many-mouthed world, Rāma abandons her and she goes to Vālmīki's āśrama. The speaker is not called a washerman.
  - *Padma Purāṇa (Pātāla-khaṇḍa 55–56):* spies are sent to sound out the king's fame; the sixth overhears a **washerman (rajaka)** driving out his wife for staying in another man's house and refusing his mother's plea — he is not King Rāma, to keep a wife who lived in another's house. Told of it, Rāma lays it before **Bharata** and faints; Bharata pleads that Sītā is pure and pregnant, and Rāma still insists on the banishment.
  - *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa (Uttara 4):* the reporter is the courtier **Vijaya**, and the report follows Vālmīki's Bhadra speech closely; there is no washerman.
  - *Why it stuck:* the dhobī gives the slander a face, a scene and a line of dialogue where Vālmīki gives only a report — the reason stage, Rāmlīlā and cinema have preferred it, and the reason it is almost universally miscited.
  - *The karmic back-story is Purāṇic, not only folk:* Padma Pātāla 57 makes the washerman, **Krodhana**, the rebirth of a male parrot. As a girl in Janaka's garden Sītā caught a pair of parrots who were singing of her future marriage to Rāma and would not free the pregnant female; dying, the female cursed her to be separated from her husband while pregnant, and the male drowned himself in the Gaṅgā vowing to be born in Rāma's city to bring it about. The device moves the cause from Rāma's choice to Sītā's own past act. [late text]
- **No second fire-ordeal is actually held in Vālmīki** - A crucial and much-confused point: in Vālmīki, Rāma does **not** demand a second *agni-parīkṣā* in the city; the slander leads straight to **banishment**. The "second ordeal" is a separate later event — the **final public oath** before Sītā returns to the Earth ([§VII](#vii-the-second-public-ordeal-and-sitas-return-to-the-earth)) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 44–45, 95–97; CE 7.43–44, 7.86–88).
  - *Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa 14):* keeps the same order — a spy (*apasarpa*) named Bhadra reports, Rāma resolves privately, Lakṣmaṇa is ordered — with no renewed ordeal (Raghu. 14.31–45). He sharpens the injustice through **Sītā's own message**: tell the king that he saw her purified in the fire before his eyes and still left her on hearsay — is that worthy of his line? She then blames her own past-life sins instead, vows tapas after the birth so that in a later birth too he may be her husband, without separation, and asks only to be protected as he would protect any ascetic in his realm (Raghu. 14.61–67). The people's lowered eyes belong to the final oath in canto 15, not to the banishment (Raghu. 15.78). [late text]
  - *Variant (Tulsīdās, Rāmcaritmānas):* the whole banishment is **omitted**; Tulsī's devotional retelling ends in the harmony of the reign, declining to narrate Sītā's abandonment at all. [folk / devotional]
- **The collision made explicit** - Rāma to his brothers: out of fear of censure he would give up his own life, or them, "how much more Janaka's daughter" (*apy ahaṃ jīvitaṃ jahyāṃ yuṣmān vā … kiṃ punar janakātmajām*); and he binds them by oath not to plead for her (Vālmīki *Uttara* 45; CE 7.44.13, 7.44.18–19). This is the rāja-dharma absolutism that the rest of the cycle tests to destruction.

## IV. The banishment to Vālmīki's āśrama

- **Lakṣmaṇa as unwilling instrument** - Rāma orders **Lakṣmaṇa**, with Sumantra driving, to leave the **pregnant** Sītā beyond the Gaṅgā near Vālmīki's āśrama on the **Tamasā**, using her own earlier wish to see the Gaṅgā hermitages as the cover, and to come straight back (Vālmīki *Uttara* 45; CE 7.44.15–21). Lakṣmaṇa obeys in misery; they spend a night by the Gomatī, and on the road Sītā notices ill omens and prays for the family's welfare (Vālmīki *Uttara* 46; CE 7.45.11–17).
- **The abandonment on the Gaṅgā** - Lakṣmaṇa ferries her across, falls to the ground before her weeping, and tells her the king has abandoned her, blameless, out of fear of the citizens' talk; Vālmīki, he adds, was a close friend of Daśaratha (Vālmīki *Uttara* 47; CE 7.46.3–18). Sītā asks what sin of a former birth she is paying for; she would drown herself in the Gaṅgā now but for the royal line she carries; and she sends her greetings to the queens and a message to Rāma — treat the citizens as you would your brothers, for that is the highest dharma and the highest fame (Vālmīki *Uttara* 48; CE 7.47.3–12). It is one of the cycle's emotional peaks.
- **Vālmīki's shelter** - The hermitage boys hear her weeping and fetch **Vālmīki**, who already knows her innocence by ascetic insight and entrusts her to the women ascetics (*tāpasī*) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 49; CE 7.48.1–20).
- **Treatments of the banishment:**
  - *Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa 14):* Kālidāsa keeps Vālmīki's device of Sītā's longing (*dohada*) for the forest hermitages, folded into the order (Raghu. 14.26–28, 14.45). His own strokes: Rāma tells his brothers he knows she is sinless, but public censure is stronger, as people take the earth's shadow on the pure moon for a stain (14.40); the Earth, uncertain, does not yet open for her when she collapses (14.55); her message to Rāma (14.61–67); and Vālmīki, the poet whose grief at the hunter's arrow first became verse, admits his own anger with Rāma (14.70–73). Rāma rules on, takes no other wife, and performs his sacrifices with her image (*pratikṛti*) as companion (14.84–87). The poet stresses Rāma's own ruin: he keeps the kingdom but loses the meaning of it.
  - *Bhavabhūti (Uttararāmacarita, Acts I–III):* the play opens with Rāma and the pregnant Sītā viewing a **Citra-śālā (picture-gallery)** of their own past; the paintings stir her wish to see the Gaṅgā again, and while she sleeps on Rāma's arm the old spy **Durmukha** whispers the slander. Rāma, knowing her innocent, resolves to send her away under cover of the wish (Act I). Twelve years pass before Act II (Śambūka, §VIII); on the same day, **Act III** ("The Shadow") brings the river-goddesses **Tamasā and Muralā**, and Sītā, made invisible by the Gaṅgā's favour, watches the grief-maddened Rāma at Pañcavaṭī in Janasthāna, revives him by her touch, and by the act's end forgives him — she suffers his suffering, unseen. Bhavabhūti makes the banishment the engine of *karuṇa-rasa* (the flavour of pathos / compassion), of which he is the acknowledged master ("*eko rasaḥ karuṇa eva*", Act III, v. 47; v. 48 in Belvalkar's numbering). [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Kamban, Irāmāvatāram, Tamil):* the entire Uttara material — banishment included — is **absent**; Kamban ends at the coronation. Tamil tradition attributes a separate Tamil *Uttara Kāṇṭam* to the 12th-c. poet **Oṭṭakkūttar**. [folk / regional]
  - *Variant (Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa):* the banishment becomes a **pre-arranged līlā**. Sītā tells Rāma in private that the gods want her to go ahead to Vaikuṇṭha; Rāma answers that he will use a public scandal as the pretext, leave her in the forest like an ordinary man afraid of the world, that she will bear two sons at Vālmīki's āśrama, then take an oath and go into the earth, and that he will follow (Adhyātma *Uttara* 4.35–44). This is separate from the **Māyā-Sītā / chāyā-Sītā** doctrine, which the same text uses earlier — the real Sītā waits inside the fire and only an illusory Sītā is abducted (Adhyātma *Araṇya* 7.1–4) — and which removes the scandal's premise; see the [Vedavatī / Māyā-Sītā cross-link](ramayana/7-uttara-kanda.md). [late text]
  - *Variant (modern reinterpretations):* C. N. Sreekantan Nair's Malayalam **Kāñcana Sītā** and a large 20th-c. feminist re-reading center Sītā's perspective and indict Rāma's choice. [scholarly / modern]

## V. The birth and education of Lava and Kuśa

- **Birth in the hermitage** - Sītā bears **twin sons** on the very night **Śatrughna**, marching against the demon Lavaṇa, lodges at Vālmīki's hut — a rainy night of Śrāvaṇa; he hears the news at midnight and rejoices (Vālmīki *Uttara* 66; CE 7.58.1–11). Vālmīki makes a protective rite with a handful of *kuśa* grass and its cut lower part (*lava*): the first-born is to be wiped with the consecrated kuśa, hence **Kuśa**; the younger is to be wiped by the old women with the lava, hence **Lava** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 66; CE 7.58.4–7). Kuśa is the elder. The etymology is Vālmīki's own; Kālidāsa repeats it, with Vālmīki, Daśaratha's friend and Janaka's ritual officiant (*mantrakṛt*), performing the rites (Raghu. 15.31–32).
- **Reared as ṛṣi-kumāras** - They grow up in the āśrama as Veda-learning ascetic-princes, ignorant of their royal birth and of who their father is — the dramatic-irony engine of the recognition scene to come.
- **Vālmīki teaches them the Rāmāyaṇa** - In the Bāla frame Vālmīki, having composed the whole poem "with what is to come and with its sequel" (*sabhaviṣyaṃ sahottaram*), chooses the twins, who are already Veda-trained, as its reciters and teaches it to them for singing and recitation, in three tempos and seven *jātis*, to the lute (Vālmīki *Bāla* 4; CE 1.4.1–12). So the epic's first reciters are the hero's own unrecognised sons singing his life back to him. The size is given as **24,000 ślokas**, 500 sargas and six books with the Uttara in the vulgate (Vālmīki *Uttara* 94); the CE keeps "five hundred sargas" (CE 7.85.20) but has no 24,000 figure. In the vulgate Śatrughna, returning after twelve years, already hears the Rāma-song at the āśrama (Vālmīki *Uttara* 71); the CE's sarga has no such scene (CE 7.63). This is the tradition's foundational image of itself: poetry born of grief (*śoka → śloka*, Vālmīki *Bāla* 2), transmitted by the wronged son.
- **The "two boys, one face" motif** - Vālmīki calls them "like images lifted from an image — from Rāma's body" (CE 1.4.10), and at the sacrifice the audience says they could not be told from Rāma but for their matted hair and bark garments (Vālmīki *Uttara* 94; CE 7.85.7–8). The resemblance is exploited heavily in drama and folk theatre.
  - *Bhavabhūti (Acts IV–VI):* Lava, twelve years old and at play near the āśrama, captures the sacrificial horse, routs its guard with the magic *jṛmbhaka* missiles and duels **Candraketu**, Lakṣmaṇa's son (Acts IV–V). Rāma, returning from the Daṇḍaka, stops the fight and embraces Lava; Kuśa arrives; Rāma's suspicions grow (the twins, the missiles he once promised Sītā's children) but are not confirmed (Act VI). There is no father–son combat in the play. [scholarly]

## VI. The Aśvamedha and the capture of the horse

- **Why an Aśvamedha** - Bharata dissuades Rāma from a Rājasūya as ruinous to the kings of the earth; Lakṣmaṇa proposes the Aśvamedha instead, illustrated by the tales of Indra freed from the guilt of killing Vṛtra and of Ila (Vālmīki *Uttara* 83–90; CE 7.74–81). The sacrifice is held in the **Naimiṣa** forest on the Gomatī; the black-spotted horse is loosed with **Lakṣmaṇa** guarding it, and no one seizes it (Vālmīki *Uttara* 91–92; CE 7.82.14, 7.83.1–9). Lacking an acknowledged consort at court, Rāma uses a **golden image of Sītā** (*kāñcanī patnī*) in the wife's ritual seat, and he never takes another wife (Vālmīki *Uttara* 91, 99; CE 7.82.19, 7.89.4).
- **The twins chant the epic at the sacrifice** - Vālmīki arrives with his pupils and sends the twins to sing "the whole Rāmāyaṇa" through the camp, twenty sargas a day, to accept no gifts, and if asked to say they are Vālmīki's pupils (Vālmīki *Uttara* 93; CE 7.84.1–15). Rāma assembles sages, kings and scholars to hear them; offered 18,000 pieces of gold, they refuse it as useless to forest-dwellers, and name Vālmīki as the poem's author (Vālmīki *Uttara* 94; CE 7.85.4–20). After hearing the song for many days Rāma recognises them as Sītā's sons (Vālmīki *Uttara* 95; CE 7.86.1–2). In Vālmīki this **recognition by recitation** is the climax — gentle, not martial.
- **The capture of the horse — the folk/Purāṇic amplification** - In Vālmīki the horse is never captured; the popular imagination did not leave it there. Later tellings have the boys **seize the wandering sacrificial horse** and defeat its escort, but the shape of the war differs by source:
  - *Padma Purāṇa (Pātāla-khaṇḍa):* the fullest Purāṇic battle-narrative, told by Śeṣa to Vātsyāyana. **Agastya** prescribes the Aśvamedha as expiation for killing Rāvaṇa, a brāhmaṇa by birth (Pātāla 8); **Vasiṣṭha** looses the horse, with **Śatrughna** leading its army, Bharata's son **Puṣkala** guarding the rear and **Hanumān** among the escort (Pātāla 10–11). The horse's long circuit has its own wars, as when Vīramaṇi's son Rukmāṅgada seizes it and Vīramaṇi later surrenders it (Pātāla 39, 46). At Vālmīki's āśrama **Lava** binds the horse (Pātāla 54); in the battles that follow Lava kills the general Kālajit, the boys strike Puṣkala and Śatrughna down senseless and destroy much of the army (Pātāla 60–64), and lead Hanumān and Sugrīva to the āśrama tied by their tails. **Sītā** makes her sons free the monkeys and the horse, and by an act of truth revives the fallen army (Pātāla 64–65). Rāma never takes the field: Vālmīki tells him who the boys are and they sing the Rāmāyaṇa in his assembly (Pātāla 66). This is the principal Purāṇic home of the "Lava-Kuśa war." [late text]
  - *Variant (Jaiminīya Aśvamedha / folk Lava-Kuśa cycle, stage & cinema):* the form most popular in South Asia has the twins defeat army after army under Rāma's brothers, bind Hanumān, and finally defeat **Rāma himself** before the truth emerges. A museum catalogue entry for a Rāmāyaṇa painting of this battle names the "Jaimini Bhārata" — the *Jaiminīya Aśvamedha*, narrated by Jaimini to Janamejaya — as the earliest of the versions that tell it; that Sanskrit text was not checked for this page. It is the backbone of the blockbuster **Lava Kusa** films and innumerable Harikathā / Yakṣagāna performances. [late text / folk]
  - *Note:* the martial version subtly **rehabilitates Sītā's line** — her sons prove unbeatable, vindicating her honour through their valour rather than through any further ordeal. In the Padma it is Sītā's own act of truth that ends the war.

## VII. The second public ordeal and Sītā's return to the Earth

- **The summons** - Rāma sends messengers to Vālmīki: if Sītā is of pure conduct and sinless, let her, with the sage's consent, prove it by an oath (*śapatha*) before the assembly at dawn (Vālmīki *Uttara* 95; CE 7.86.3–6). Vālmīki agrees: "a husband is a woman's deity" (CE 7.86.10). *This* — not any event before the banishment — is the actual "second ordeal" of the cycle.
- **Vālmīki's oath** - Vālmīki leads Sītā in before sages, kings, vānaras, rākṣasas and people of all four varṇas and swears to Rāma: "I am the tenth son of Pracetas; I do not remember ever speaking an untruth — these two are your sons"; may he lose the fruit of thousands of years of tapas if Maithilī is not sinless (Vālmīki *Uttara* 96; CE 7.87.1–20). Rāma answers that he believes it, that Sītā had already given proof before the gods, that he abandoned her knowing her sinless out of fear of the world, asks the sage's pardon, and acknowledges the twins (Vālmīki *Uttara* 97; CE 7.88.2–4).
- **Bhūmi receives her daughter** - Sītā, in an ochre robe, eyes lowered, makes her oath into a final act of self-vindication and refusal: "As I have never thought, even in my mind, of any but Rāghava, so may the goddess **Mādhavī** (Bhūmi-Devī, the Earth) grant me an opening." A divine throne rises from the ground borne on the heads of nāgas; the Earth-goddess takes her onto it, and Sītā **descends into the earth forever** amid a rain of flowers (Vālmīki *Uttara* 97; CE 7.88.9–16).
- **The ring closed** - Sītā was **earth-born** (found in Janaka's furrow — see [Bāla backstory](ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md)); her return *to* the Earth completes that arc and is widely read as her unanswerable verdict: she proves her truth and simultaneously **refuses to remain** with the husband who twice made her prove it. In the vulgate Rāma, grieving, threatens to plough up the Earth unless she gives Sītā back, and Brahmā calms him: Sītā has reached the nāga world, they will be reunited in heaven, and he should hear the rest of the poem, the Uttara (Vālmīki *Uttara* 98). The CE's constituted text has no such scene: it passes from the descent straight to the close of the sacrifice and the reign that follows (CE 7.88 → 7.89).
- **Treatments of the ending — the great divergence:**
  - *Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa 15):* follows Vālmīki in outline. Rāma tells Vālmīki that Sītā was proved pure in the fire before them, but the people here did not believe it; let her convince them by her conduct and he will take her back with her sons (15.72–73). When she arrives in an ochre robe, the onlookers stand with lowered eyes like ripe rice; she sips water brought by Vālmīki's pupil and swears by speech, mind and deed (*vāṅmanaḥkarmabhiḥ*) — where Vālmīki's oath names the mind alone; the Earth takes her on her lap while Rāma cries "No, no!"; Rāma's anger at the Earth is calmed by his guru (15.77–85). Rāma is left to rule on in sorrow before his own departure. [tragic ending]
  - *Bhavabhūti (Uttararāmacarita, Act VII):* **breaks with the tragedy** — the play stages a happy reunion. Vālmīki has his account of Sītā's later history performed by apsarases as a **play-within-the-play** before Rāma and the people; in it the Earth and the Gaṅgā receive Sītā and the newborn twins. When Rāma swoons, the Gaṅgā and the Earth rise from the river with the real Sītā and give her into **Arundhatī's** charge; Arundhatī rebukes the people, they accept Sītā, Rāma takes her back, and Vālmīki presents the sons. Bhavabhūti deliberately overturns the canonical *viraha* (separation) into *milana* (reunion), refusing to leave his audience in the karuṇa he had so masterfully built. It is the most celebrated reunion ending in Sanskrit literature, though not the only one: S. K. Belvalkar notes that the Padma's version need not be later than Bhavabhūti and that two endings may have been current in his day. [scholarly]
  - *Padma Purāṇa (Pātāla-khaṇḍa 66–67):* also ends in **reunion**, without a second ordeal. Sītā first declines Lakṣmaṇa's summons, calling herself the king's infamy rather than his fame, and sends the boys to their father (Pātāla 66); then Lakṣmaṇa brings her to Ayodhyā, Agastya seats her in the sacrificial enclosure in place of the golden image, and Rāma completes the Aśvamedha with her (Pātāla 67). [late text]
  - *Bhāgavata Purāṇa (9.11.15–18):* no recital and no oath — Sītā leaves her sons with the sage and, meditating on Rāma's feet, enters the earth; Rāma, unable to master his grief, keeps brahmacarya and an unbroken agnihotra for 13,000 years. [late text]
  - *Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa (Uttara 7):* keeps the recital (Rāma's gift here is 10,000 gold coins), Vālmīki's oath as the tenth son of Pracetas, and Sītā's descent on an elephant-borne throne, now as the return planned in Uttara 4; Rāma grieves "as if he knew nothing," for the sake of worldly example (Adhyātma *Uttara* 7.2–50). [late text]
  - *Variant (folk / Lava-Kuśa cinema):* most popular versions also end in **reunion** — Sītā restored, the family whole — because audiences would not accept her final loss; the Earth-swallowing is softened or replaced. [folk]
  - *The Māyā-Sītā doctrine does not change the ending where it is explicit:* it answers the abduction and the first ordeal (the real Sītā comes back out of the fire in Laṅkā), but in the Adhyātma Sītā still goes into the earth (Adhyātma *Araṇya* 7.1–4; *Uttara* 7.40–47). [late text]

## VIII. The Śambūka episode — the dharma-controversy

- **The dead brāhmaṇa boy** - An old brāhmaṇa of the countryside lays his dead five-year-old son at the palace gate and blames the king: such untimely death has never been seen in Rāma's realm, so "there is some great wrong of Rāma's" — no untimely death should occur under a righteous king (Vālmīki *Uttara* 73; CE 7.64.2–15).
- **Nārada's diagnosis** - The sage **Nārada** explains the text's varṇa-yuga logic: in the Kṛta only brāhmaṇas practised tapas; in the Tretā kṣatriyas did too, while the others served; in the Dvāpara vaiśyas took it up; śūdra austerity belongs to the Kali. Somewhere a **śūdra is performing austerities** at the edge of the realm, and this is the cause of the boy's death (Vālmīki *Uttara* 74; CE 7.65.9–23).
- **Rāma slays Śambūka** - Rāma has the body kept in a trough of oil, then searches west, north and east on the **Puṣpaka**; in the south, by a lake north of Mount Śaivala, he finds an ascetic hanging head-down. Asked his birth, he says he is a śūdra named **Śambūka**, seeking to reach the gods' world in his own body; while he is still speaking, Rāma draws his sword and **beheads him** (Vālmīki *Uttara* 75–76; CE 7.66.2–7.67.4). In the CE the boy revives at that moment and Rāma goes straight on to Agastya's āśrama, where Agastya says the gods are talking of the śūdra-slayer (CE 7.67.5–10). The vulgate adds the gods' rain of flowers and an offered boon, which Rāma spends on the boy's life (Vālmīki *Uttara* 76).
- **The controversy — among the most contested passages in Hindu literature [disputed]:**
  - *Anti-caste critique:* read as the scriptural enforcement of varṇa-exclusion from spiritual practice — **B. R. Ambedkar** (in *Riddles in Hinduism*), the **Self-Respect / Dravidian** movement and the Telugu rationalist **Tripuraneni Ramaswamy** (*Śambuka Vadha*) treat Śambūka as the wronged victim and Rāma as the agent of caste violence; **Kuvempu's** Kannada play *Śūdra Tapasvi* (1944) lets Śambūka live and has him help his brāhmaṇa accuser to enlightenment (as summarised from Paula Richman's study in the Wikipedia *Shambuka* article). The episode is widely cited in Dalit literature. [scholarly]
  - *"Liberation by Rāma's hand" — a classical reading, not only a modern defence:* Kālidāsa says the śūdra, punished by the king himself, gained "the goal of the good" (*lebhe śūdraḥ satāṃ gatim*), which his austerity, transgressing his own path, could not win (Raghu. 15.53). Bhavabhūti has Rāma strike reluctantly — "a deed worthy only of a Rāma" — after which Śambūka rises as a celestial being, thanks him, and is granted the Vairāja worlds (Uttararāmacarita 2.10–12). Other defenders read the act as the cosmos self-correcting in the Tretā framework, or treat the passage as a **later interpolation** alien to Rāma's character — a view the Wikipedia *Shambuka* article attributes to Tagore and Gandhi, citing secondary studies. [late text / devotional]
  - *Omission in regional Rāmāyaṇas:* **Kamban** and **Tulsīdās** drop the episode entirely; many medieval and modern retellings either omit or soften it — itself evidence of long discomfort with the beat. [folk]
  - *As authenticity-evidence:* for philologists the Śambūka and banishment episodes are **Exhibit A** for the Uttara Kāṇḍa's late, interpolated character (see [§IX](#ix-the-disputed-authenticity-debate)). [scholarly]
  - *Kālidāsa & Bhavabhūti:* both **retain** the killing (Raghu. 15.42–57; Uttararāmacarita Act II), showing it was firmly in the received aftermath by the classical period — the omissions are the *later* move, not the original. [late text]

## IX. Rāma's departure — the *jala-samādhi* / Mahāprasthāna

- **Kāla comes for Viṣṇu** - **Time (Kāla)**, disguised as an ascetic, says he comes on a weighty errand and demands a private audience that no one may see or hear on pain of death; Lakṣmaṇa is set to guard the door. Kāla reveals that he is Rāma's own son, born of Māyā, sent by Brahmā: the eleven-thousand-year term Rāma fixed for himself among men is complete; he may stay or return to the gods (Vālmīki *Uttara* 103–104; CE 7.93.1–7.94.19).
- **Durvāsā and the sacrifice of Lakṣmaṇa** - At that moment the irascible **Durvāsā** demands to see Rāma at once, threatening to curse the realm, Rāma, Bharata and all their line. Lakṣmaṇa decides that his own death is better than the ruin of all and goes in; Durvāsā, it turns out, only wants a meal at the end of a thousand-year fast (Vālmīki *Uttara* 105; CE 7.95.1–15). Lakṣmaṇa asks Rāma to kill him and keep his word; Vasiṣṭha advises abandonment; Rāma, saying that for the good, abandonment and death are the same, sends him away. Lakṣmaṇa goes to the **Sarayū**, restrains his breath and senses, and **Indra bears him bodily to heaven**, where the gods honour him as "the fourth part of Viṣṇu" (Vālmīki *Uttara* 106; CE 7.96.1–18). The cycle's logic of "rāja-dharma over love" claims even the most beloved brother.
  - *Variant (Vaiṣṇava devotional):* Lakṣmaṇa as **Śeṣa / Ananta** — the identity the tradition remembers — is not in this sarga, which calls him a quarter of Viṣṇu; in the vulgate it appears in the Yuddha's closing phala-śruti ("Lakṣmaṇa is called Śeṣa", Vālmīki *Yuddha* 128.120), a passage the CE does not print. [late text]
- **Succession** - On Bharata's advice Rāma consecrates the twins — **Kuśa in Kosala at Kuśāvatī**, on a slope of the Vindhya (and the Ikṣvāku line continues through him; cf. [Sūryavaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)), and **Lava in the northern Kosalas at Śrāvastī** (spelled Śrāvatī in the CE) (Vālmīki *Uttara* 107–108; CE 7.97.7–18, 7.98.4–5). Śatrughna installs his sons Subāhu at Madhurā and Śatrughātin at Vidiśā (CE 7.98.9). The brothers' other sons were settled earlier: Bharata's Takṣa and Puṣkala at Takṣaśilā and Puṣkalāvatī (Puṣkara at Puṣkarāvatī in the CE), Lakṣmaṇa's Aṅgada and Candraketu at Aṅgadīyā and Candrakāntā (Vālmīki *Uttara* 101–102; CE 7.91.9, 7.92.6–9).
- **Rāma's last commands** - Before the walk to the river Rāma disposes of his allies: **Vibhīṣaṇa** is to rule Laṅkā as long as creatures exist; **Hanumān** is to keep his vow and live as long as Rāma's stories are told in the world; the bears and monkeys are invited to follow (Vālmīki *Uttara* 108; CE 7.98.22–26). Hanumān's **cirañjīvitva** is first granted earlier in the same book, at his own request, when Rāma bids farewell to his allies after the coronation — "as long as I hear the Rāma-story on earth, may my breath stay in my body" (Vālmīki *Uttara* 40; CE 7.39.16–19). It is therefore a plain Uttara grant, made twice, and not, as it is usually filed, a Vaiṣṇava devotional addition. The vulgate adds that Vibhīṣaṇa's rule is to last as long as sun and moon shine and men speak of Rāma, **Jāmbavān's** different term — to dwell on earth until the Kali Yuga begins — Vibhīṣaṇa's worship of the Ikṣvāku deity Jagannātha, and **Sugrīva's** request to follow after installing **Aṅgada** at Kiṣkindhā; none of these is in the CE's text of the sarga (Vālmīki *Uttara* 108) — see [The Chiranjīvis](the-chiranjivis.md).
- **The Great Departure (jala-samādhi)** - At dawn, in fine linen, holding kuśa-grass, reciting the Veda, silent, Rāma walks out of Ayodhyā with Śrī at his left, Hrī at his right, his weapons and the Vedas following in human form, and behind them the palace women, his brothers, the brāhmaṇas with their sacred fires, ministers, citizens, and the vānaras, ṛkṣas and rākṣasas (Vālmīki *Uttara* 109; CE 7.99.1–18). A yojana and a half on, at the **Sarayū**, Brahmā greets him — "Come, Viṣṇu" — and he enters Viṣṇu's radiance in his body, with his brothers. At Viṣṇu's request Brahmā assigns those who followed him the **Sāntānika worlds**, next to Brahmaloka; the vānaras and ṛkṣas return into the gods from whom they were born; and all who enter the water at the **Goprataraka** ghāṭ rise to heaven (Vālmīki *Uttara* 110; CE 7.100.1–24). **Sugrīva** entering the orb of the sun is in the vulgate (Vālmīki *Uttara* 110) but not in the CE.
  - *Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa 15):* Kāla comes in a sage's guise; Lakṣmaṇa breaks the agreement for fear of Durvāsā's curse and gives up his body by yoga on the Sarayū; Rāma "stood loosely on the earth like dharma on three legs"; Kuśa is installed at Kuśāvatī and Lava at **Śarāvatī**; the crush of citizens entering the water "like cattle at a ford" gives the tīrtha its name Gopratara; Rāma makes a separate heaven for the citizens and enters his own form, leaving Vibhīṣaṇa and Hanumān as two "pillars of his fame" on the southern and northern mountains (Raghu. 15.92–103). The dynastic continuity (the *Raghu-vaṃśa* itself) is Kālidāsa's real subject, so the departure is a transition, not a void.
- **The phala-śruti** - The vulgate closes with the merits of hearing the Rāmāyaṇa (Vālmīki *Uttara* 111). The CE ends with the ascent and a single closing verse naming the poem "with its *uttara*" as Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa (CE 7.100.25–26), with no phala-śruti, so this "integral ending" is itself a feature of one recension, which the authenticity debate must reckon with.

## IX-b. The [disputed] authenticity debate {#ix-the-disputed-authenticity-debate}

- **The interpolation thesis** - Mainstream modern philology holds the **Uttara Kāṇḍa (with the Bāla Kāṇḍa)** to be a **later addition** to a once five- or six-book core. The arguments are internal: Nārada's summary of the poem in Bāla 1 ends with the coronation and the reign, touching the end only in a one-line forecast of Rāma's going to Brahmaloka (CE 1.1.76–77); the Uttara opens with an appendix-like frame ("the sages ask Rāma about Rāvaṇa") and spends its first half on back-story; and Śambūka plus the banishment seem to **contradict the dharma and character** of the Rāma of Books 2–6. The Bāla's own table of contents does name Sītā's banishment and an "*uttara kāvya*" (*vaidehyāś ca visarjanam … tac cakārottare kāvye*, CE 1.3.28–29; *sabhaviṣyaṃ sahottaram*, CE 1.4.2), usually read as part of the same later frame. [scholarly]
- **The traditional / counter-view** - The **Baroda Critical Edition did not excise** the Uttara Kāṇḍa; it edits it as Book 7 in 100 sargas, leaving out vulgate material such as the inserted sargas after 23, 37 and 59, Brahmā's consolation after Sītā's descent (vulgate 98), Sugrīva's entry into the sun, Jāmbavān's term and the closing phala-śruti (vulgate 111). The standard critical translation is Goldman and Sutherland Goldman's Princeton volume VII. Classical commentators, the entire devotional tradition, and Kālidāsa (4th–5th c.) **already knew and retold** the after-story — so it is at minimum very old and was canonical for the whole classical period. Writers such as **Nilesh Oak** argue affirmatively for authenticity. [disputed]
- **Why the debate is itself part of the tradition** - As noted in [§I](#i-the-problem-stated-why-is-there-an-uttara-at-all), excision (Kamban, Tulsī), allegory (liberation by Rāma's hand), the Māyā-Sītā doctrine and the Adhyātma's pre-arranged līlā, and the reunion endings of Bhavabhūti and the Padma are **all strategies for resolving the same ethical scandal**. The aftermath cycle is less a fixed text than a millennium-long argument about whether the perfect king did the right thing. This file records the variants and flags the controversy rather than resolving it.
