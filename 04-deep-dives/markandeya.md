# Mārkaṇḍeya — the Deathless Sage & the Cosmic Dissolution Vision

> Cosmic address: a deep-dive episode-cycle whose span depends on the text. The *Mahābhārata* has him outlive many "ends of a thousand yugas" (kalpas); the *Bhāgavata* says he was born in the present kalpa and that his deluge was a vision. Mārkaṇḍeya is a *chiranjīvi* (long-lived one); his two great stories, conquering Death and witnessing the pralaya, are the human-scale anchor for the abstract cosmology files.
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](./) · See also [README](../README.md) · cosmology: [kalpa-and-pralaya](../00-time-and-cosmology/kalpa-and-pralaya.md), [brahma-lifetime](../00-time-and-cosmology/brahma-lifetime.md), [manvantara-system](../00-time-and-cosmology/manvantara-system.md)

Mārkaṇḍeya is the boy fated to die young who instead became the timeless witness: the sage the epic and Purāṇic tellings let survive *into* the dissolution of worlds (in the *Mahābhārata* Yudhiṣṭhira says no one but Brahmā equals his lifespan, 3.186.2). His cycle joins two of the tradition's grandest images: **Shiva as Kālāntaka / Mṛtyuñjaya** (the conqueror of Death) and **Vishnu as Bāla-Mukunda** (the infant on the banyan adrift on the pralaya ocean). He thus belongs to both Shaiva and Vaishnava theology, which credit his deathlessness to different gods.

---

## 1. Origin — the boon of a short brilliant life

- **Mṛkaṇḍu's penance and the dilemma** - The childless sage Mṛkaṇḍu propitiates Shiva, who offers a choice: a virtuous, brilliant son who lives only **16 years**, or a dull-witted son who lives long. Mṛkaṇḍu chooses the virtuous short-lived son (Śaiva retelling: Tamil Tirukkaṭavūr sthala-purāṇa per Shulman 1984, where the alternative is a hundred long-lived foolish sons; Vettam Mani, *Purāṇic Encyclopaedia* s.v. Mārkaṇḍeya, one "dull-witted evil-natured son", no primary locus given) [regional]. The wife is named **Marudvatī** in popular retellings [folk]; no primary locus found.
  - Mark: this boon-story is **not** in the *Mahābhārata* (Book 3 never names Mṛkaṇḍu), not in the *Liṅga Purāṇa* (GRETIL 1–108), and not found in Shastri's translation of the *Śiva Purāṇa* (all four volumes) or GRETIL's *Śiva Purāṇa* books 1 and 7. The Śiva Purāṇa mentions him in passing or as an interlocutor (he narrates the Pitṛ-kalpa, Umā-saṃhitā 40–42); the only passage found that accounts for his long life says he "became long-lived" by Śiva's grace (Umā-saṃhitā 2.40, Shastri tr.). Sanskrit Purāṇic locus for the 16-year version: locus not verified.
  - *Variant (Brahmāṇḍa 1.11.7, GRETIL numbering; Narasiṃha 7.10):* the mother is **Manasvinī** (*manasvinyāṃ mṛkaṇḍasya mārkaṇḍeyo babhūva*, Brahmāṇḍa; also Vāyu 28.5 per the Purāṇa Index, locus not verified) or **Sumitrā** (Narasiṃha). The father is spelled **Mṛkaṇḍa** in *Bhāgavata* 4.1.44–45 and *Brahmāṇḍa* 1.11.6–7, **Mṛkaṇḍu** in *Viṣṇu* 1.10.4, *Bhāgavata* 12.8.2, *Narasiṃha* 7.10 and *Mārkaṇḍeya* 52.16 (where the sage calls Mṛkaṇḍu "my father"). Lineage: Bhṛgu and Khyāti → Dhātā/Vidhātā (married Āyati and Niyati, daughters of Meru) → Prāṇa and Mṛkaṇḍu → Mārkaṇḍeya → Vedaśiras (*Viṣṇu* 1.10.2–4; *Brahmāṇḍa* 1.11.7 adds that Vedaśiras was born of Mārkaṇḍeya's wife Dhūmrā, *dhūmrapatnyām*).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata 4.1.45):* **Vedaśiras** is the son of **Prāṇa**, not of Mārkaṇḍeya (*mārkaṇḍeyo mṛkaṇḍasya prāṇād vedaśirā muniḥ*).
  - *Variant (Padma Purāṇa, Sṛṣṭi-khaṇḍa 33):* no Shiva and no Yama. A visiting sage foretells the child will live only **six months** (v. 8); seven sages bless him "live long", then take him to **Brahmā**, who makes their words true by granting him a life as long as Brahmā's own (v. 24).
  - *Variant (Narasiṃha Purāṇa 7.10–23):* a soothsayer (*āgamī*) foretells death **when his twelfth year is complete** (*varṣe dvādaśame pūrṇe*, 7.13); his grieving parents reveal it when he asks (7.19–21), and he vows to drive Death away by tapas (7.23).
  - *Variant (Nārada Purāṇa 1.4.50–1.5.17):* Mṛkaṇḍu does tapas to **Viṣṇu** at Śālagrāma; Viṣṇu promises to be born as his son, "long-lived" (*dīrghajīvī*, 1.4.97), and Mārkaṇḍeya is born from a portion of Hari's radiance (1.5.6). (GRETIL text is garbled around 1.5.17.)
- **Birth and naming** - The son is born radiant and is named **Mārkaṇḍeya** ("son of Mṛkaṇḍu"). He masters the Vedas precociously; his parents conceal his death-date, then reveal it as the fatal year nears (Śaiva retelling per Mani). In *Narasiṃha* 7.14–23 the parents do not hide anything deliberately: they grieve openly over the 12-year limit and tell him the prophecy when he asks why.
- **The Mṛtyuñjaya teaching** - Foreseeing his death, Mārkaṇḍeya is taught (or, by later tradition, *composes*) a death-conquering mantra and devotes himself to ceaseless Shiva worship. In the Tirukkaṭavūr legend **Brahmā** teaches him the **Mahā-Mṛtyuñjaya mantra** (the Tryambakam verse, *Rigveda* 7.59.12) [regional, per Shulman].
  - Mark: the *attribution* of the mantra to Mārkaṇḍeya is [folk/devotional tradition]; the verse predates the legend as a Vedic ṛc. The *Śiva Purāṇa* gives the Mahāmṛtyuñjaya to **Śukra**, who teaches it to **Dadhīca** (Rudra-saṃhitā, Satī-khaṇḍa 38, Shastri tr.), not to Mārkaṇḍeya; in the *Liṅga Purāṇa* it is the sage **Śveta** who recites *tryambakaṃ yajāmahe* as Death arrives (1.30.5).
  - *Variant (Narasiṃha Purāṇa 7.32–44, 7.61–74):* his grandfather Bhṛgu tells him only Nārāyaṇa can conquer death; he installs Viṣṇu at **Bhadravaṭa on the Tuṅgabhadrā**, repeats the twelve-syllable *oṃ namo bhagavate vāsudevāya*, and recites a **Viṣṇu Mṛtyuñjaya-stotra** ("*kiṃ me mṛtyuḥ kariṣyati*", "what can Death do to me?", 7.63–70) which Viṣṇu himself spoke into his ear (7.62, 7.73).

## 2. Victory over Death — Shiva as Kālāntaka / Mṛtyuñjaya

- **The arrival of Yama** - On the appointed day Mārkaṇḍeya clings to a **Shiva-liṅga** in unbroken worship. Yama's messengers cannot approach him; Yama, lord of death, arrives in person, noose in hand, to take the boy's life (Tirukkaṭavūr sthala-purāṇa per Shulman; Mani) [regional]. The *Tēvāram* hymns (Appar on Kaṭavūr; Campantar) praise Shiva's foot that kicked Death for his devotee (per Peterson, *Poems to Śiva*; patikam numbers not verified) [regional].
- **The noose round the liṅga** - Yama casts his **pāśa (noose)**; it falls around the boy's neck and, because the boy is locked in embrace of the liṅga, around the **Shiva-liṅga itself** (same sources) [regional].
- **Shiva bursts forth as Kālāntaka** - Enraged at the noose touching the liṅga, **Shiva emerges from the liṅga** in furious form and strikes with the trident and kicks Yama down, slaying Death itself. From this deed he earns the epithets **Kālāntaka** ("Ender of Time/Death"), **Kālāri**, **Kālasaṃhāra** and **Mṛtyuñjaya** ("Conqueror of Death") (Tirukkaṭavūr sthala-purāṇa; Āgamic iconography per T. A. Gopinatha Rao, *Elements of Hindu Iconography* II.1, 158–61) [regional].
  - *Variant (iconographic, S. Indian bronze/painting; Āgamic texts per Rao):* Shiva is depicted with leg extended kicking Yama, trident raised, the classic **Kālāntaka-mūrti** / **Kālāri** form; Yama collapses while Mārkaṇḍeya clutches the liṅga.
  - *Variant (death-restored; Tirukkaṭavūr and Tiruvāṉmiyūr sthala legends per Shulman; Mani):* Shiva slays Yama outright; with no one to make beings die, Earth, the devas and Mārkaṇḍeya plead, and Shiva **revives Yama** with a touch of his foot, but Yama may never again touch Shiva's devotees. [regional]
  - *Variant (Liṅga Purāṇa 1.30.1–23):* the same liṅga-and-Death story told of a different devotee, the sage **Śveta**. Reciting *tryambakam*, Śveta asks "what will Death do to me?" (*kiṃ kariṣyati me mṛtyuḥ*, 1.30.6), the refrain of the Narasiṃha stotra below. Kāla binds him with the noose (1.30.14–15) and mocks the "motionless" Shiva in the liṅga (1.30.18); Shiva issues forth with Ambā and Nandin, and Antaka dies from his mere glance (1.30.19–22). The *Śiva Purāṇa* likewise recalls "Śvetaketu bound by the noose of Kāla" as the precedent (Śatarudra-saṃhitā 15.14, Shastri tr.).
  - *Variant (Narasiṃha Purāṇa 7.56–8.10):* Vaiṣṇava version with no liṅga. Yama's servants come **noose in hand** (*pāśahastāḥ*) and are beaten back with clubs by **Viṣṇu's attendants**; Death (Mṛtyu) himself comes and is driven off while the boy chants the Viṣṇu stotra (7.56–71); Yama then explains to his servants that the sage worships Viṣṇu (ch. 8).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata 12.8.7–11):* no Yama scene at all. By lifelong celibate tapas and worship of Hṛṣīkeśa (Viṣṇu) for "myriads of years" he **conquered invincible death** (*jigye mṛtyuṃ su-durjayam*, 12.8.11).
  - *Variant (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiṣṇava-khaṇḍa, Puruṣottama-kṣetra-māhātmya 3, wisdomlib tr.):* Nārāyaṇa tells him to "propitiate my second body" (the translator supplies "[Śiva]"), promising "with my compassion you will certainly conquer Death" (vv. 46–48); by the tank that Hari's discus dug he worships Maheśvara and conquers Death (vv. 49–51). The chapter then praises a separate seashore liṅga, **Yameśvara**, which "destroys the control of Yama" (v. 55).
- **The boon of deathlessness at sixteen** - Shiva blesses Mārkaṇḍeya to remain **sixteen forever** and never to die, granting him *chiranjīvi* status (Śaiva retelling per Mani; "a youth of 16 for seven kalpas" in the Tirukkaṭavūr legend per Shulman) [regional].
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata 3.187.41, 3.187.51):* the boon comes from **Nārāyaṇa**, who says he gave it "in the form of Brahmā"; Mārkaṇḍeya says that by the grace of this god (Kṛṣṇa) his memory never fails, and he has **long life and death at will** (*dīrgham āyuḥ svacchandamaraṇam*), not strict deathlessness. The god says the sage sees him directly because he is devoted to his father, has taken refuge in him, and keeps great celibacy (3.187.2).
  - *Variant (Bhāgavata 12.10.36–37):* Shiva grants fame and freedom from old age and death **until the end of the kalpa** (*ā-kalpāntāt*). See §4a.
  - *Variant (Brahma Purāṇa 56.59–60):* after the deluge vision at Puri, Viṣṇu offers a boon and tells him "be long-lived again" (*punar dīrghāyur āpnuhi*). See §4c.
- **Why this matters cosmologically** - This boon is the narrative device that makes the *next* story possible: a witness who survives the [pralaya](../00-time-and-cosmology/kalpa-and-pralaya.md) when all else dissolves.

## 3. Chiranjīvi status — the deathless ones

- **Counted among the immortals** - Mārkaṇḍeya appears in the popular *Chiranjīvi* verse, which names seven (Aśvatthāman, Bali, Vyāsa, [Hanumān](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/hanuman.md), Vibhīṣaṇa, Kṛpa, Paraśurāma) and then adds "**Mārkaṇḍeya as the eighth**" (*saptaitān saṃsmaren nityaṃ mārkaṇḍeyam athāṣṭamam*) (*Chiranjīvi* stotra; textual source untraced) [late text]. See chiranjivi notes in [manvantara-system](../00-time-and-cosmology/manvantara-system.md).
  - *Variant:* "seven immortals" counts leave Mārkaṇḍeya out because the verse itself adds him only as an eighth; lists that count "eight" include him. [disputed membership]
  - In the epic his long life is the fruit of dharma: answering Draupadī's complaint, Yudhiṣṭhira points to "Mārkaṇḍeya of immeasurable soul, long-lived through dharma" (*Mahābhārata* 3.32.10); he arrives as "the aged sage bearing many thousands of years" (3.180.39–40).
  - *Variant (Mahābhārata Vulgate, star passage 3.180.39 *0895):* he looks like a youth of twenty-five, ageless and deathless; not in the critical text. [interpolation]
- **Function across kalpas** - Because he never dies, Mārkaṇḍeya is repeatedly cast as the **transmitter of ancient knowledge** to later ages: Yudhiṣṭhira says he has seen many ends of a thousand yugas and alone attends Brahmā at the pralaya (*Mahābhārata* 3.186.2–4); he narrates to the Pāṇḍavas in the forest and is the frame-sage of the *Markandeya Purana*. The *Bhāgavata* adds that he "even now wanders the world" (12.10.39).

## 4. The Pralaya Vision — Bāla-Mukunda on the banyan

The signature episode: Mārkaṇḍeya, alone surviving, experiences the cosmic dissolution and meets the infant Vishnu on the waters. Two principal tellings (Bhāgavata and Mahābhārata), a Puri-centred version (Brahma Purāṇa, with a Skanda parallel), and the *Markandeya Purana* frame.

### 4a. Bhāgavata Purāṇa version (Skandha 12, chs. 8–10)

- **Frame — a puzzle (SB 12.8.2–5)** - Śaunaka asks how Mārkaṇḍeya can have survived a kalpa-end when he was born in Śaunaka's own Bhṛgu line *in this kalpa*, and no dissolution has happened yet. The answer is that the deluge was a vision of the Lord's māyā (12.10.1–2, 12.10.40); in the BBT reading of 12.10.41, the text calls unintelligent those who equate it with ordinary beginningless saṃsāra (the Sanskrit, *kādācitkaṃ pracakṣate*, is terse).
- **Austerity and temptation (SB 12.8.7–31)** - After his father's rites of initiation, Mārkaṇḍeya keeps lifelong celibacy and worships Hari; **six manvantaras** pass while he meditates (12.8.14). In the **seventh**, the present one, Indra grows fearful and sends **Kāma, the gandharvas and apsarases, Spring (Vasanta), the Malaya breeze**, and *rajas-toka* and *mada* ("Greed and Intoxication" in the BBT gloss) to his hermitage north of the Himālaya, on the **Puṣpabhadrā** river by the rock called **Citrā** (12.8.15–17). The apsaras **Puñjikasthalī** plays with balls; Kāma shoots; all fail and feel scorched by his power (12.8.26–29). [parallels the standard "tempted ascetic" motif]
- **Nara-Nārāyaṇa and the boon (SB 12.8.32–12.9.7)** - The Lord appears as the sage-pair **Nara and Nārāyaṇa**; Mārkaṇḍeya praises them (12.8.40–49). Nārāyaṇa offers any boon; seeing the Lord is enough, he says, but he asks to **behold the Lord's māyā**, by which the world sees difference in reality (12.9.2–6). The Lord says "so be it" and leaves for Badarikāśrama (12.9.7).
- **The deluge descends (SB 12.9.10–19)** - One evening, at his worship on the bank of the Puṣpabhadrā, a great wind rises; clouds pour rain; the **four oceans** (*catuḥ samudrāḥ*) appear on all sides and swallow the earth, its islands, mountains and continents, then sky and heaven (12.9.10–15). He alone survives, hungry, thirsty, attacked by sea-monsters, wandering blind in darkness for "**myriads of years**" (12.9.16–19). Cross-ref [kalpa-and-pralaya](../00-time-and-cosmology/kalpa-and-pralaya.md).
- **The banyan and the child (SB 12.9.20–25)** - On a rise of land (*pṛthivyāḥ kakudi*; "a small island" in the BBT gloss) he sees a **young banyan (vaṭa)**; on its **north-eastern branch** a luminous **infant** lies in a **fold of a leaf** (*parṇa-puṭake*), emerald-dark, sucking the toe of his own foot, his radiance swallowing the darkness. This is **Bāla-Mukunda / Vaṭa-patra-śāyī** (the child reclining on the banyan leaf).
- **Inhaled into the cosmos (SB 12.9.27–30)** - The child **inhales** Mārkaṇḍeya into his body "like a mosquito"; inside, the sage sees the **entire universe**: worlds, oceans, mountains, beings, time with its yugas and kalpas, and even the Himālaya, the Puṣpabhadrā and **his own hermitage**. He is then **exhaled** back onto the ocean of dissolution.
- **Vanishing and aftermath (SB 12.9.31–34; 12.10)** - He sees the child again smiling at him and runs to embrace him; the child vanishes, then the banyan and the waters, and Mārkaṇḍeya finds himself back in his own hermitage as before (12.9.31–34). **Shiva and Pārvatī (Umā)** pass overhead; at Umā's urging Shiva enters the sage's heart and then appears before him (12.10.3–18). Mārkaṇḍeya asks only for unfailing devotion to Viṣṇu, his devotees and Shiva (12.10.34); Shiva grants fame and freedom from old age and death until the end of the kalpa, knowledge of past, present and future, and the office of **teacher of the Purāṇas** (*purāṇācāryatā*) (12.10.36–37).
  - Note: the Bhāgavata **braids Vaishnava and Shaiva endings**: the vision is of Vishnu's māyā, but the closing boon-giver is Shiva. It is not unique in this; the *Brahma Purāṇa* (§4c below) and the *Skanda* Puruṣottama-māhātmya also join the two.

### 4b. Mahābhārata version (Vana Parva, Mārkaṇḍeya-samāsyā Parva)

Critical-edition numbering c. 3.179/180–221 (Mārkaṇḍeya arrives at 3.180.39; the vision is 3.186–187); the Vulgate/Ganguli sections run higher (the sacred-texts link below is to the Ganguli sections; exact section mapping not verified).

- **Frame — counsel to Yudhiṣṭhira** - At Kāmyaka in the forest exile, **Kṛṣṇa** arrives with **Satyabhāmā**; then the ancient Mārkaṇḍeya comes, and Kṛṣṇa asks him to tell holy old stories of kings, women and sages; **Nārada** joins them (*Mahabharata* 3.180.6–49). Mārkaṇḍeya consoles the Pāṇḍavas with stories of dharma, devoted women and cosmic time, setting their suffering against the vast canvas of yugas (*Mahabharata*, Vana Parva, Mārkaṇḍeya-samāsyā Parva). See [Mahabharata deep-dive](./mahabharata/).
- **The dissolution narrated** - Mārkaṇḍeya describes living through the **end of a thousand yugas**: seven blazing suns and the Saṃvartaka fire burn the world, then clouds rain for twelve years into a single ocean (3.186.58–79). He is left the **sole wandering survivor** on the shoreless waters, weary and afraid (3.186.78–80).
- **The boy on the banyan branch** - He sights a vast **banyan (nyagrodha)**; on its broad branch, on a **couch spread with divine coverlets** (*paryaṅke divyāstaraṇa-saṃstṛte*), sits a moon-faced, lotus-eyed **boy**, dark as the flax flower and bearing the śrīvatsa mark (3.186.81–87). (The epic has a couch, not a leaf; the leaf is the *Bhāgavata*'s image.) The child calls him by name and invites the exhausted sage to **enter his body and rest** (3.186.88–89).
- **The universe within** - The child suddenly opens his mouth and Mārkaṇḍeya is drawn in "helplessly, by fate" (3.186.91). Inside he roams the whole earth, its rivers, oceans, mountains, peoples and gods for **more than a hundred years** without finding the body's end (3.186.92–111); he takes refuge in the god and is blown out of the mouth by a gust of wind (3.186.112–113).
- **The child's self-revelation** - The boy, now in yellow garments, explains that the waters are called *nārā* and so he is **Nārāyaṇa**, creator and destroyer, who sleeps in child-form for a thousand yugas until Brahmā wakes and then emits creation again (3.187.1–47). He tells the sage that even the gods do not truly know him, and he reveals himself out of affection for him (3.187.1–2). Mārkaṇḍeya then tells the Pāṇḍavas that **this god is Kṛṣṇa**, their kinsman sitting there, and bids them take refuge in him (*sa eṣa kṛṣṇo vārṣṇeyaḥ*, 3.187.50–55). The identification is the epic's own, not only a later Vaiṣṇava reading.
  - *Variant emphasis:* the Mahābhārata stresses the **didactic frame** (teaching Yudhiṣṭhira perspective and dharma, with Kṛṣṇa present); the Bhāgavata stresses the **theological vision of māyā**. The core image (sole survivor and divine child on the banyan) is shared.

### 4c. Puri version — Brahma Purāṇa (Puruṣottama-māhātmya)

- **The eternal banyan at Puruṣottama** - At the kalpa-end fire, Mārkaṇḍeya flees and takes shelter at the root of the eternal banyan, **Vaṭarāja**, called "Puruṣeśa", where the fire cannot reach (*Brahma Purāṇa* 52.11–19). Clouds rain for twelve years; he is left floundering on the single ocean (53.1–18; the cloud and rain verses 53.1–13 repeat *Mahābhārata* 3.186.65–76 almost word for word).
- **The child who calls him "boy"** - Viṣṇu calls out "come, child Mārkaṇḍeya"; the aged sage is offended at being called a child (53.19–24), then takes refuge, sees the banyan again with a golden divine couch on its branch made by Viśvakarman, and on it the four-armed **Kṛṣṇa in a child's body** with śrīvatsa, conch, discus and mace (53.26–33). Even knowing past and future, he fails to recognise him, deluded by his māyā (53.35–36). [late text]
- **The Mārkaṇḍeya tank** - Mārkaṇḍeya offers to build a Śiva shrine at Puruṣottama so that Śaivas and Bhāgavatas stop disputing (56.63–65); Viṣṇu tells him to build a Śiva temple bearing his name and, north of the Lord's shrine, a tīrtha, the **Mārkaṇḍeya-hrada**, since "Rudra is Viṣṇu himself" (*yo rudraḥ sa svayaṃ viṣṇuḥ*) (56.65–73); bathing there opens the Puri pañca-tīrtha rite (57.1–10).
  - *Variant (Skanda Purāṇa, Vaiṣṇava-khaṇḍa, Puruṣottama-kṣetra-māhātmya 3, wisdomlib tr.):* the sage "with a life of seven kalpas" finds no shelter; a child's voice invites him in; the Lord is "in the form of a baby" lying on a leaf-cup on the **Kalpavaṭa**; the tank, dug by Hari's discus north-west of the banyan, becomes famous by the sage's name, and he worships Maheśvara there (vv. 2–11, 28–29, 49–51). The name Mārkaṇḍeśvara for that liṅga comes from the translator's footnote, not the verses.

### 4d. Markandeya Purana frame

- **Mārkaṇḍeya as narrator-sage** - The *Markandeya Purana* opens with **Jaimini** (Vyāsa's pupil) bringing four questions about the *Mahābhārata* to **Mārkaṇḍeya** (why Kṛṣṇa took human form, Draupadī's five husbands, Balarāma's penance, the deaths of Draupadī's sons; 1.12–16). Mārkaṇḍeya says it is his time for ritual and sends him to four wise **birds**, the sons of Droṇa (**Piṅgākṣa, Vibodha, Suputra, Sumukha**), in the Vindhya (1.18–22). From ch. 45 the birds relay what Mārkaṇḍeya once taught **Krauṣṭuki** on cosmology, manvantaras, dharma and karma (45.15–17).
- **The Devī-Māhātmya embedded** - This Purāṇa contains the **Devī-Māhātmya (Durgā Saptaśatī, chs. 81–93 in the GRETIL/Pargiter numbering; chapter numbers vary by edition)**, the foundational Śākta text of the Goddess's victories. In the text itself it is **Mārkaṇḍeya who narrates it** (to Krauṣṭuki), beginning with the story of the eighth Manu, Sāvarṇi (81.1). See [devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md).
  - Mark: direct *authorship* of the Devī-Māhātmya by Mārkaṇḍeya is [devotional attribution], not historical; the text is embedded in the Purāṇa that bears his name. [scholarly: the Purāṇa is composite, layered over centuries].

## 5. Sectarian framing — who "owns" Mārkaṇḍeya

- **Shaiva reading** - Mārkaṇḍeya is *the* proof-story of Shiva's power over Death: **Kālāntaka / Mṛtyuñjaya** rescues his devotee, and the Mahā-Mṛtyuñjaya mantra is invoked for healing and longevity to this day (Tamil Śaiva tradition: Tirukkaṭavūr, *Tēvāram* [regional]; *Śiva Purāṇa* Umā-saṃhitā 2.40 for his long life by Śiva's grace). The Sanskrit *Liṅga Purāṇa* tells the liṅga-and-noose rescue of Śveta instead (1.30).
- **Vaishnava reading** - Mārkaṇḍeya is the witness of **Vishnu's māyā** and the deathless devotee who sees **Bāla-Mukunda** sustaining the cosmos through pralaya (*Bhagavata* 12.8–10; *Mahabharata* 3.186–187, where the child is Kṛṣṇa). He conquers death by Viṣṇu worship (*Bhāgavata* 12.8.11; *Narasiṃha* 7) and is even Viṣṇu's own portion (*Nārada* 1.4–1.5). See [dashavatara](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md) and [trimurti-and-tridevi](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md).
- **Śākta reading** - Through the *Markandeya Purana* / **Devī-Māhātmya**, he is the narrator of the Goddess's glory.
- **Synthesis** - The Bhāgavata's ending (Vishnu's vision, Shiva's boon), the *Brahma Purāṇa*'s Śiva temple ordered by Viṣṇu to end Śaiva–Bhāgavata disputes (56.63–73), and the *Skanda*'s Viṣṇu calling Śiva "my second body" all reconcile the claims, making Mārkaṇḍeya a rare pan-sectarian sage.

## 6. Sacred geography & living tradition

- **Vaṭa-patra-śāyī temples** - The infant-on-banyan-leaf form is enshrined notably at **Srivilliputhur (Tamil Nadu)** and depicted across S. Indian temple art. [regional]
- **Markandeya at Puri** - The **Mārkaṇḍeya tank (Mārkaṇḍeya-hrada) and Mārkaṇḍeśvara shrine** at Puri commemorate the sage surviving the deluge at the eternal banyan of Puruṣottama-kṣetra (*Brahma Purāṇa* 52–57; *Skanda Purāṇa*, Vaiṣṇava-khaṇḍa, Puruṣottama-kṣetra-māhātmya 3). [regional māhātmya] [late text]
- **Kālāntaka shrines** - Tamil tradition places the rescue at **Tirukkaṭavūr** (Thirukkadaiyur), one of the eight *vīraṭṭāṉam* sites of Shiva's heroic deeds; other temples claim it too (Triprangode in Kerala; Kaithi near Varanasi). [regional] The epic already knows a "tīrtha of Mārkaṇḍeya" at the world-famed confluence of the Gomatī and the Gaṅgā, after Vārāṇasī in the pilgrimage list (*Mahābhārata* 3.82.70), though without the Yama story.
- **Mahā-Mṛtyuñjaya practice** - The mantra tied to his rescue remains among the most-recited Shaiva mantras for warding off untimely death and illness. [living tradition]
