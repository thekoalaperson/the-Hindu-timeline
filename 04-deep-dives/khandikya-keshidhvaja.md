# Khāṇḍikya and Keśidhvaja — the Vishnu Purana's concluding yoga dialogue

> *Cosmic address: the philosophical coda of the [Viṣṇu Purāṇa](../90-literature-corpus/puranas/vishnu-purana.md), **Aṃśa VI, chapters 5–7** (Wilson Book VI, ch. V–VII) — narrated by **Parāśara** to **Maitreya** as the Purāṇa's final teaching, after the pralaya chapters, on how liberating knowledge is actually won. A dialogue between two cousins of the **Videha (Janaka) line** of Mithilā ([lineage hub](../03-lineages-vamsha/videha-mithila-line-janaka.md)): **Khāṇḍikya**, master of the path of works (karma), and **Keśidhvaja**, master of Self-knowledge (ātma-jñāna).*
> Parent: [04 Deep Dives](README.md)

The **Khāṇḍikya–Keśidhvaja** dialogue is the note on which the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* ends — the passage that turns the whole cosmological work toward its declared goal, *mokṣa*. Two royal cousins of the Janaka dynasty embody the two great "paths": one is the perfect ritualist, the other the perfect knower of the Self. In a chiasmus the text builds deliberately, the **jñāna**-master must beg the **karma**-master for a ritual expiation, and the karma-master — twice given the chance to kill his rival or reclaim the throne he has lost — asks instead only to be freed from the ignorance that binds him to rebirth. Keśidhvaja's answer is a compact manual of yoga and contemplation on Viṣṇu that the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition, above all **Rāmānuja**, would quote as scriptural bedrock. It is the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*'s final argument that neither works nor knowledge is complete without the other, and that release, not dominion, is the one prize worth asking for.

---

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — The cousins belong to the **Videha (Maithila) branch of the Solar dynasty**, descended from Ikṣvāku's son Nimi through Janaka; the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* assigns their episode **no yuga-address** — it is deployed as a timeless doctrinal exemplum, not a dated reign. The narration itself sits in the **Dvāpara-yuga frame** of the Purāṇa: Parāśara (grandfather of the Pāṇḍavas' contemporaries, father of Vyāsa) relating it to his pupil Maitreya. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.5–7)
- **Academic / historical frame** — The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* is reckoned one of the **oldest and most systematic Mahāpurāṇas**, its core often dated **[scholarly] c. 1st century BCE – 4th century CE** (frequently c. 3rd–4th century CE), ascribed to Parāśara and structurally the model *pañcalakṣaṇa* Purāṇa. H. H. Wilson's translation (1840) fixed the chapter numbering used here. The reception that makes this chapter famous is later still: **Rāmānuja**, [scholarly] c. 1017–1137 CE, cites the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* more than any other Purāṇa and quotes the Keśidhvaja discourse directly. ([Rāmānuja profile](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/ramanuja-and-madhva.md))

## The frame: three miseries and the meaning of "Bhagavat" (VI.5)

- **Why the story is told** — Parāśara has just described the three dissolutions; Maitreya asks how a being escapes the **threefold misery (tāpa-traya)** of existence for good. Parāśara answers that the *ātyantika* (final, individual) release comes only through **knowledge of the supreme spirit**, and that all worldly remedies for pain merely postpone it. The dialogue of the two kings is offered as the demonstration of *how that knowledge is obtained*. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.5)
- **The three pains** — *ādhyātmika* (bodily and mental), *ādhibhautika* (from other creatures), and *ādhidaivika* (from the elements and fate); Parāśara insists that wealth, medicine and pleasure are counterfeit cures, since possession breeds fresh grief — only the knowledge that dissolves the "I and mine" ends suffering at the root. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.5)
- **The definition of *Bhagavat*** — In this same prelude the Purāṇa gives its celebrated etymology of the divine name: the one who possesses in full the **six excellences (bhaga)** — sovereignty (*aiśvarya*), might (*vīrya*), glory (*yaśas*), splendour (*śrī*), knowledge (*jñāna*) and dispassion (*vairāgya*) — and who "knows the origin and end, the coming and going of beings, and knowledge and ignorance," is *Bhagavat*. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.5.74–79) — this ṣāḍguṇya passage is a load-bearing proof-text for the whole Vaiṣṇava theology of the Lord's auspicious attributes. [scholarly]

## The rival cousins of the Janaka line (VI.6)

- **The two houses** — Of the Videha kings descended from Janaka, **Kṛtadhvaja**'s son was **Keśidhvaja**, and **Mitadhvaja**'s son was **Khāṇḍikya** — the fathers being brothers, so the two are first cousins. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6; the Bhāgavata runs the same line **Kuśadhvaja → Dharmadhvaja → Kṛtadhvaja & Mitadhvaja**, *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.13.19–21.)
  - *Note:* the corpus keeps the Videha "-dhvaja" kings distinct from **Sīradhvaja Janaka** (Sītā's father, Tretā-yuga) and from the **Janaka Dharmadhvaja** tested by Sulabhā; the shared title "Janaka" is a throne-name, not one man. See the [Videha–Mithilā lineage hub](../03-lineages-vamsha/videha-mithila-line-janaka.md). [disputed]
- **The division of learning** — **Keśidhvaja** was accomplished in **spiritual science (adhyātma-vidyā / ātma-jñāna)**; **Khāṇḍikya** was supreme in the **science of works and ritual (karma-mārga)**, the practical religion of sacrifice and its rules. The whole plot turns on the fact that each is deficient exactly where the other is master. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)
- **Conquest and exile** — Ambitious for dominion, **Keśidhvaja conquered Khāṇḍikya** and drove him from his kingdom; the deposed Khāṇḍikya withdrew into deep forest with his ministers and priests, biding his time. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)

## The slain cow and the chain of ignorance (VI.6)

- **The interrupted sacrifice** — Keśidhvaja, secure on the throne, undertakes a great **sacrifice to conquer death (to win a deathless world)**. While the rite is in progress a **tiger kills the milch-cow** whose milk is required for the offering — an omen and a ritual defilement that halts the whole sacrifice. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)
- **No one knows the expiation** — Keśidhvaja asks his **officiating priests (ṛtvijes)** for the *prāyaścitta* (expiation) that would purify the interrupted rite; they confess ignorance and send him to the sage **Kaśeru**; Kaśeru sends him on to **Śunaka**; and each in turn admits he does not know — until they conclude that the **one living man who knows the expiation is Khāṇḍikya**, the very enemy Keśidhvaja has dethroned. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)
  - *The chiasmus:* the master of *knowledge of the Self* is helpless before a matter of *ritual works* and must go, cap in hand, to the master of *works* — setting up the mirror-exchange to come. [scholarly]
- **The resolve to face the enemy** — Keśidhvaja reasons that dharma outweighs danger: if Khāṇḍikya kills him he will die completing a sacrifice (a good death); if Khāṇḍikya answers, the rite is saved. Clad in deerskin, he drives alone into the forest to his rival. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)

## The enemy's dilemma — vengeance or the world beyond (VI.6)

- **The ministers cry for blood** — Seeing his conqueror defenceless before him, Khāṇḍikya seizes his bow; his counsellors urge him to **kill Keśidhvaja and take back the earth** — the enemy is delivered into his hand. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)
- **Khāṇḍikya's first renunciation** — Khāṇḍikya lays the bow down. To slay Keśidhvaja, he reasons, would win only **this world**; to let him complete his sacrifice and to give him the expiation would win the **world beyond** — and the imperishable is worth more than a kingdom. He tells his enemy the exact *prāyaścitta* for the interrupted rite and sends him away unharmed. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)

## The teacher's fee: not the throne but yoga (VI.6)

- **The debt of instruction** — Keśidhvaja returns, performs the expiation, and completes his sacrifice. But dharma holds that a rite is unfinished until the **instructor receives his fee (dakṣiṇā)** — and Khāṇḍikya has functioned as his teacher in the matter of the expiation. Keśidhvaja drives back into the forest a second time to discharge the debt, offering Khāṇḍikya **whatever he desires**. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)
- **The ministers cry for the kingdom** — Now Khāṇḍikya's counsellors see their chance: demand the **restoration of the whole kingdom** as the teacher's fee — Keśidhvaja is bound by his own word to give it. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)
- **Khāṇḍikya's second renunciation** — Again Khāṇḍikya refuses the worldly prize. A kingdom, he says, is the fruit of ignorance and craving, enjoyed only in this transient life; the wise seek not empire but the **knowledge that severs the bonds of rebirth**. So he asks Keśidhvaja for the one thing his ritual mastery cannot give him: **the yoga by which the affliction of existence is ended.** (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)
- **The victor's delight** — Keśidhvaja is overjoyed: by declining the kingdom Khāṇḍikya has in truth **conquered** — choosing the eternal over the transient — and there is nothing Keśidhvaja would rather bestow than the teaching. He sits to instruct his cousin. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6)

## Keśidhvaja's yoga (VI.7)

- **The root of bondage is ignorance** — Keśidhvaja begins where the frame began: **avidyā** is the false identification of the Self with what is not the Self — taking the body, wealth, house and kin for "I" and "mine." This misidentification is the seed of *karma* and rebirth; **discriminative knowledge** that the Self is other than all of these is release. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.7)
- **Act that does not bind, knowledge that liberates** — Works done from craving forge the chains of *saṃsāra*; works offered without desire, and above all knowledge of Brahman, undo them. The teaching threads the needle between the two paths the cousins personify — *karma* purifies, but only *jñāna* liberates. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.7; cf. the concept map at [karma, saṃsāra & mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md) and on ignorance [māyā & avidyā](../05-concepts-and-dharma/maya-and-avidya.md))
- **The definition of yoga** — Yoga is the **union of the individual soul with the supreme Brahman**; the practitioner is the *yogin* who is *yoga-yuj* (still striving) or *viniṣpanna-samādhi* (accomplished), and the aspirant advances through **restraint (yama), observance (niyama), posture (āsana), breath-control (prāṇāyāma), withdrawal of the senses (pratyāhāra), fixing of the mind (dhāraṇā), meditation (dhyāna) and absorption (samādhi)**. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.7) — the Purāṇa's ladder parallels the classical eight limbs; see [yoga paths & Patañjali](../05-concepts-and-dharma/yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md).
- **The support for the mind: the embodied Viṣṇu** — Because the untrained mind cannot rest on the formless, Keśidhvaja prescribes **dhāraṇā on the "gross" (perceptible) form of Bhagavān Viṣṇu** — the benign human form bearing conch, discus, mace and lotus, the Śrīvatsa on his breast, crowned and garlanded — held steadily before the mind. As concentration deepens, the meditator **withdraws from the emblems and limbs to the subtle**, until the support itself dissolves into formless contemplation of Brahman. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.7; on Viṣṇu's iconic forms see [Viṣṇu's forms & vyūhas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishnu-forms-and-vyuhas.md))
- **The two forms of Brahman** — Keśidhvaja teaches that Brahman-Viṣṇu has **two aspects, the embodied (mūrta) and the unembodied (amūrta), the perishable and the imperishable** — both real, the perceptible form the appointed doorway to the imperceptible. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.7, the *dve rūpe brahmaṇaḥ* passage)
- **The Self is essentially knowledge** — Among the discourse's most-quoted lines: the Self is by its very nature **pure, stainless consciousness (jñāna-svarūpa)**, which the deluded wrongly perceive as if it were an object; realization is simply the Self resting in its own knowing nature. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.7)
- **The parting** — His teaching complete, Keśidhvaja returns to his city and Khāṇḍikya, entrusting his throne to his son, retires to the forest to practise the yoga he has received; by steady contemplation on Viṣṇu he purges his mind of every taint and **attains liberation**, while Keśidhvaja, freed likewise by knowledge, is released at the fall of the body. (*Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.7) — with this the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* closes and pronounces its *phala-śruti*.

## Reception: the discourse Rāmānuja quotes

- **The Śrīvaiṣṇava proof-text** — The *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* is the favoured Purāṇa of the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition (styled the *Purāṇa-ratna*), and **Rāmānuja** cites it throughout the *Śrī-Bhāṣya* and *Vedārtha-saṅgraha*. The Keśidhvaja material supplies him two pillars: the **VI.5 definition of *Bhagavat*** (the six auspicious excellences of the Lord) and the **VI.7 doctrine that the self is *jñāna-svarūpa*** — essentially knowledge, and also a knower — which he wields against the Advaitic reading of the self as attributeless consciousness. (*Śrī-Bhāṣya*; *Vedārtha-saṅgraha*) [scholarly]
- **A charter for karma-jñāna harmony** — Read whole, the episode is the tradition's neat allegory that **works and knowledge are not rivals but a sequence**: Khāṇḍikya the ritualist perfects himself only by receiving *jñāna*, and Keśidhvaja the knower is shown still bound by ritual duty — an image later Vedāntins invoke on the relation of *karma-kāṇḍa* to *jñāna-kāṇḍa*. [scholarly]
- **Kindred dialogues** — The tale sits beside the *Viṣṇu Purāṇa*'s own preference for teaching through royal exempla and among the tradition's philosopher-king dialogues of the Janaka house — the Sulabhā-Janaka debate ([Mokṣadharma dialogues](mokshadharma-dialogues.md)), the [Nimi and the Nine Yogendras](nimi-nava-yogendra-samvada.md) *saṃvāda*, and, as a mirror-image of a spouse teaching a king to renounce renunciation, the [Cūḍālā–Śikhidhvaja](chudala-shikhidhvaja.md) tale of the *Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*.

## Sources

### Primary (scriptural)
- ***Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.5** — the three miseries (*tāpa-traya*), the supremacy of knowledge of the supreme spirit for final (*ātyantika*) release, and the definition of *Bhagavat* by the six excellences (VI.5.74–79); Parāśara's frame that introduces the dialogue to Maitreya.
- ***Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.6** — the story: the Videha cousins Khāṇḍikya (karma-mārga) and Keśidhvaja (adhyātma-vidyā); the conquest and exile; the tiger-slain sacrificial cow; the chain of sages (priests → Kaśeru → Śunaka) unable to give the expiation; Keśidhvaja's journey to his enemy; Khāṇḍikya's twin refusals (of vengeance, then of the kingdom) and his request for liberating knowledge as the teacher's fee.
- ***Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.7** — Keśidhvaja's exposition of yoga: avidyā as false self-identification, the definition of yoga and its stages, contemplation (dhāraṇā) on the perceptible form of Viṣṇu withdrawn toward the imperceptible, the two forms (mūrta/amūrta) of Brahman, the self as *jñāna-svarūpa*, and the liberation of both kings; the concluding *phala-śruti* of the Purāṇa.
- ***Bhāgavata Purāṇa* IX.13.19–21** — parallel Videha genealogy (Kuśadhvaja → Dharmadhvaja → Kṛtadhvaja & Mitadhvaja → Keśidhvaja & Khāṇḍikya), naming Keśidhvaja master of ātma-vidyā and Khāṇḍikya master of karma. [cross-reference]
- *Rāmānuja*, ***Śrī-Bhāṣya*** and ***Vedārtha-saṅgraha*** — citations of *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* VI.5 (the six excellences of Bhagavān) and VI.7 (the self as essentially knowledge) in the Viśiṣṭādvaita defence of the qualified, attribute-bearing Self and Lord. [scholarly]
