# The seven brahmins reborn — the Pitṛkalpa chain of births (Brahmadatta of Kāmpilya)

> Where this sits: a *Pitṛ-kalpa* (ancestor-rite) exemplum embedded in the **Harivaṃśa** and retold in the **Matsya**, **Shiva** and **Padma** Purāṇas — how seven brahmin students who ate their teacher's cow keep **memory across successive births** (hunters → deer → cakravāka birds → swans) because, even in transgression, they **offered it to the ancestors**, and so end as **King Brahmadatta of Kāmpilya** and his companions. The classic charter for **śrāddha as karma that follows the soul**.
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · Doctrine home: [Śrāddha & the Pitṛ Rites](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md) · Frame text: [Harivaṃśa](harivamsha.md)

This is one of the great **jātismara** ("memory-of-former-births") narratives of the Purāṇic corpus, and its explicit purpose is didactic: it is told to prove that a *śrāddha* offered to the **pitṛs** — even one improvised over a sin — is a deed of such weight that its fruit clings to the doer through birth after birth. Seven students devour a cow they were set to guard, but in the act they dedicate its flesh to the ancestors; that single pious reflex earns them **unbroken recollection** through a descent into the animal world and back up to a royal human birth, culminating in the yogically-gifted Pāñcāla king **Brahmadatta of Kāmpilya**. The corpus has carried only a storyless roster line for this tale in the [Harivaṃśa map](harivamsha.md); this is its first full telling.

---

## A note on names — which Brahmadatta

The name *Brahmadatta* ("given by Brahman/Brahmā") is one of the most over-used royal names in the tradition; the corpus keeps the bearers strictly separate.

- **This Brahmadatta — the Pāñcāla king of Kāmpilya** — son of **Aṇuha** and **Kṛtvī / Kīrtimatī**, daughter of the sage **Śuka** (Vyāsa's son). A yogically gifted ruler who understands the speech of all beings. This is the Brahmadatta of the present tale. (Harivaṃśa, Harivaṃśa Parva; see [Śuka](shuka-shukadeva.md) for the Kīrtimatī–Aṇuha marriage.) [scholarly]
- **NOT the Rāmāyaṇa's Brahmadatta** — the gandharvī Somadā's mind-born son who weds Kuśanābha's hundred hunchbacked daughters and heals their crookedness at the marriage-touch. A wholly separate figure with no Pāñcāla pedigree. See [Kuśanābha's Hundred Daughters & Brahmadatta](../03-lineages-vamsha/kushanabha-daughters-and-brahmadatta.md). [disputed: identity — the shared throne-city Kāmpilya/Kāmpilī invites conflation]
- **NOT Brahmadatta of Śālva** — father of the demon-brothers Haṃsa and Ḍimbhaka, enemies of Kṛṣṇa. See [Haṃsa & Ḍimbhaka](hamsa-dimbhaka.md).
- **NOT the Jātaka stock-king** — the Pāli *Jātakas* open tale after tale with "when Brahmadatta was reigning in Vārāṇasī," a formulaic name unconnected to any of the above. [folk]

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — the whole chain unfolds in the **deep past of the current 28th Mahāyuga**. The seven brahmins begin in **Kurukṣetra** (some texts say the Naimiṣa/Kuru region) in a famine; their final human birth as Brahmadatta places him in the **Lunar dynasty's Pāñcāla line** (the North-Pāñcāla kings of Kāmpilya), i.e. a Dvāpara-era genealogical king generations before the Bhārata war. The intervening animal births are timeless-feeling but are narrated as a single continuous soul-history within this Mahāyuga. [scripture]
- **Academic / historical frame** — the tale belongs to the **Pitṛ-kalpa stratum of the Harivaṃśa**, the *khila* ("appendix") to the Mahābhārata. Text-critically the Harivaṃśa is **[scholarly] c. 1st–4th c. CE** at its core with later accretion; the Matsya recension of the same story sits in that Purāṇa's early **śrāddha block [scholarly] c. 250–500 CE**; the Śaiva reframing in the Shiva Purāṇa's Umā-saṃhitā is **[late text]**, later still. The narrative is a **doctrinal charter for śrāddha and jātismaratva**, not datable history.

## The frame — Mārkaṇḍeya on the power of the Pitṛs

- **The Pitṛ-kalpa setting** — in the Harivaṃśa Parva the sage **Mārkaṇḍeya** expounds the **greatness of the pitṛs and of śrāddha**; to show that ancestral rites bear fruit beyond a single life, he narrates the history of seven students and their long chain of births. (Harivaṃśa, Harivaṃśa Parva, Pitṛ-kalpa; vulgate ~ adhyāyas 21–24; Brodbeck 16–19) [scholarly]
- **The teacher and the cow** — the seven were disciples of the sage **Garga / Gārgya** (Matsya/Padma: *Garga*), studying in **Kurukṣetra**. Set to graze and guard their teacher's **milch-cow** (named **Kapilā** in one telling; the cow's soul later returns as a woman — see below), they were overtaken during a **famine** by killing hunger. (Harivaṃśa 21; Matsya Purāṇa 20) [scripture]

---

## The transgression that became a merit

- **They kill and eat the cow** — starving, the seven slaughter the guru's cow. This is a compound sin: theft of a teacher's property, **go-hatyā** (cow-slaughter), and betrayal of a trust. (Harivaṃśa 21; Matsya 20) [scripture]
- **The saving reflex — offering to the pitṛs** — before eating, they **dedicate the flesh as a śrāddha to the ancestors** (and tell their teacher/mother the cow was killed by a tiger, or died). The pivotal doctrinal point: because the deed, however sinful, was **oriented to the pitṛs**, the *śrāddha-merit* attaches to them and yields **unbroken memory of former births (jātismaratva)** through everything that follows. The sin sends them down the ladder of rebirths; the śrāddha-piety lets them **remember**, repent, and climb back. (Harivaṃśa 21; Matsya 21; Shiva Purāṇa, Umā-saṃhitā 40–41) [scholarly]
- **The seven named** — the Padma/encyclopaedic tradition names them **Śvarūpa, Krodhana, Hiṃsra, Piśuna, Kavi, Vāgduṣṭa, and Pitṛvartti** — the last, "the one devoted to the fathers," true to his name, the seed of the future king. (Padma Purāṇa III.10; cf. Harivaṃśa 21)
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa expanded recension):* before the animal births they first pass through births as **sons of Bharadvāja** and then **sons of Kauśika (Viśvāmitra)**, only then descending to the hunter birth — an extra brahmin-to-brahmin stage. [late text]

## The ladder of births

The soul-history is recited as a fixed sequence, and the tale's most quoted moment is the **recognition-verse** listing every rung.

- **1. Seven hunters (vyādha)** — reborn as **fowlers/tribal hunters** in the **Daśārṇa** country (Matsya/Shiva: **Daśapura**), living on **Mount Kālañjara** and its forests. Sickened by the killing life and remembering all, they practise detachment and **cast off their bodies by yoga/fasting**. (Harivaṃśa 22; Matsya 21; Shiva Purāṇa 41) [scripture]
- **2. Seven deer (mṛga)** — reborn as **deer on Mount Kālañjara**. Still remembering, indifferent to the herd, they **fast to death at Prapatana** (the "leaping-place" cliff). (Harivaṃśa 22; Shiva Purāṇa 41) [scripture]
- **3. Seven cakravāka birds (ruddy geese)** — reborn as **cakravāka (Brahminy sheldrakes)** on **Śaradvīpa** (some texts: on the **Mānasa lake**). Here the recollection is vivid; some of the flock recite the past-birth verse aloud. The Shiva/encyclopaedic tradition renames the birds **Sumanas, Kusuma, Vasu, Citradarśin, Sudarśin, Jñāta, and Jñānapāraga**. (Harivaṃśa 23; Shiva Purāṇa 42) [scripture]
  - *Variant (Harivaṃśa 23):* the trigger is a passing king or the cakravāka pair's own longing — a bird laments watching **King Aṇuha of Pāñcāla** disport with wealth, army, and women, and recites the verse; this is the seed of the birds' resolve to be reborn as men able to complete their yoga.
- **4. Seven swans (haṃsa)** — a further avian birth as **royal swans on the Mānasa lake**, engaged in "sacred rites and yoga," before they finally cast off the bird-body for a human one. (Harivaṃśa 23–24; Shiva Purāṇa 42)
  - *Variant:* several tellings collapse stages 3–4 or omit the swan-birth, giving the shorter "hunters → deer → cakravāka → men" chain; the **Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha** vairāgya-verse, however, preserves the swans. [scholarly]

---

## The recognition-verse

- **The stanza** — the reborn birds/brahmins recite a mnemonic gāthā that names every rung of the ladder; on hearing it, whoever is meant to remember **swoons with the flood of recollection**. In the wisdom-tradition's rendering: *"Seven [were we as] hunters among the Daśārṇas / Daśapura; deer on Mount Kālañjara; cakravāka-birds on Śaradvīpa; swans on the Mānasa lake — and now [we are] here."* (Harivaṃśa 23–24; and famously quoted in the **Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha / Laghu-Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha** as proof of jātismaratva) [scholarly]
- **The verse's afterlife** — this "sapta vyādhāḥ…" stanza became a **stock proof-text for rebirth and yogic recollection**, cited in vairāgya and śrāddha literature far beyond its Harivaṃśa home (Garuḍa Purāṇa, Brahma-khaṇḍa; Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha). The tale is, in effect, the narrative behind a widely-quoted verse.

---

## The final human births — Brahmadatta and his companions

- **Pitṛvartti becomes Brahmadatta** — the seventh and most pious, **Pitṛvartti**, is reborn as **Brahmadatta**, king of **Kāmpilya** in Pāñcāla, son of **Aṇuha** and **Kṛtvī/Kīrtimatī** (Śuka's daughter). By the strength of his kept memory he is a **yogin on the throne** and understands the language of all creatures. (Harivaṃśa 24; Matsya 21) [scripture]
- **The companions** — two of the seven are reborn as his **ministers/friends**, named **Subālaka** and **Puṇḍarīka (Pāñcāla)**, who share the recollection and later renounce for liberation. (Harivaṃśa 24; Shiva Purāṇa 42)
  - *Variant (Shiva Purāṇa, Umā-saṃhitā 42):* four of the swan-souls take birth in a **pious brahmin family of the same Kāmpilya** and become **Veda-masters — Dhṛtimān, Sumanas, Vidvān, and Tattvadarśin** (elsewhere Chidradarśin/Citradarśin & Sunetra) — who live unentangled by power and attain **mokṣa**; three others are "deluded," having **lost the memory**, showing that the boon was not automatic but proportioned to piety. [late text]
- **The cow returns as the queen** — the soul of the slaughtered cow is reborn as **Sannati**, daughter of **Sudeva/Devala**, who becomes **Brahmadatta's wife**; their son is **Viṣvaksena**. The karmic circle closes: the victim of the founding sin is bound into the sinner's own household. (Padma Purāṇa III.10; Harivaṃśa) [scholarly]
- **The release** — recognising one another through the recognition-verse, the companions **complete the yoga they had been attempting since the hunter-birth**; Brahmadatta rules righteously and at last the memory-bearing souls **attain liberation**, the animal-descent finally redeemed. (Harivaṃśa 24; Shiva Purāṇa 42) [scripture]

## Reading the tale

- **A charter for śrāddha-karma** — the doctrinal thesis is blunt: **an offering to the pitṛs is karma so potent it outlasts death and species-change.** The seven fall for cow-slaughter but rise on the merit of the śrāddha performed inside that very act — the tale literally weighs *pitṛ-piety* against *go-hatyā* and lets the piety carry memory through five or six bodies. It is the Purāṇic **narrative proof of the śrāddha doctrine**; see [Śrāddha & the Pitṛ Rites](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md). [scholarly]
- **Sin and merit held together, never merged** — like the [Jantu–Somaka](jantu-somaka.md) sacrifice, the story refuses to let the pious end launder the terrible means: the **sin still drives the descent** into hunter, deer, and bird bodies, even as the **merit grants the memory** that eventually saves. Punishment and reward run on parallel tracks. [scripture]
- **Memory as the engine of liberation** — the whole apparatus exists to dramatise **jātismaratva**: it is *remembering* the chain, not merely suffering it, that lets the souls cultivate vairāgya and finish their yoga. This is why the tale migrated into pure vairāgya literature (Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha) shorn of its śrāddha frame. [scholarly]
- **A chain-of-births companion piece** — set it beside [Jaya & Vijaya](jaya-vijaya.md), the corpus's other great multi-birth arc: there a curse drives three demonic births toward Viṣṇu; here a sin-with-merit drives an ascent through the animal kingdom toward mokṣa. Both use rebirth as a **moral through-line** rather than mere transmigration. [scholarly]
- **Not a curse-tale but a mercy-tale** — unlike the [Gautama curse on the brahmins](gautama-curse-on-the-brahmins.md) or [Bhṛgu's curse](bhrigus-curse-on-vishnu.md), no sage pronounces doom here; the "sentence" is the automatic working of karma, and its softening is the automatic working of śrāddha. It belongs among the [brāhmaṇa legends](brahmana-legends-catalog.md) as a **teaching-story about ritual efficacy**, not a grievance. [scholarly]
