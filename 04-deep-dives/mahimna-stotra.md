# Shiva Mahimna Stotra

> *Up one level: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · A celebrated Śaiva hymn-with-frame-story, companion to the [Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra](shiva-tandava-stotra.md) and catalogued among the song-discourses of the [Lesser Gītās catalog](lesser-gitas-catalog.md).*

The **Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra** ("Hymn to the Greatness of Śiva"), opening *mahimnaḥ pāraṃ te paramavidūṣo yady asadṛśī...* ("If, not knowing the limit of Thy greatness, my praise be unworthy..."), is one of the most widely recited Śaiva hymns in the tradition. Its **43 verses** (in the long Puṣpadanta recension) are organized around a single confessional theme — that **Śiva's greatness lies utterly beyond speech and mind**, so that even the gods' praise can only approximate it. The hymn is framed by, and self-attributes its authorship to, the **gandharva Puṣpadanta**, who composed it in atonement after losing Śiva's boon of invisibility. [scholarly]

---

## The frame-story: Puṣpadanta and the trampled garden

- **Puṣpadanta the gandharva** — A celestial musician (in some tellings styled *king of the gandharvas*) and a devotee of Śiva, granted by the god the **boon of invisibility** and the power to move unseen wherever he wished. (Puṣpadanta frame, stotra tradition / Purāṇic legend) [folk]
- **The flower-theft** — Roaming invisibly, Puṣpadanta took to gathering choice flowers from a magnificent royal garden each night to offer to Śiva, slipping past the guards unnoticed because of his boon. (legend) [folk]
  - *Variant (garden's owner):* the garden is most often that of **King Citraratha** (himself a gandharva-king); other retellings make it a generic royal pleasure-garden. [regional]
- **The fatal misstep** — Among the offerings the king habitually laid out were **bilva (bel) leaves / flowers already consecrated to Śiva**. Puṣpadanta, not noticing them, **trod on this sacred Śiva-offering** — and the sacrilege instantly **stripped him of his invisibility**. (legend) [folk]
  - *Variant (what was trampled):* some versions say he trampled **Śiva's own nirmālya** (used worship-offerings); others, the bilva-grove or the flowers dedicated to the god. The constant is that the trespass is against *Śiva's sacred flowers*, which is why the power vanishes. [regional]
- **Caught and punished** — Made visible, Puṣpadanta was seized by the king's guards; in many tellings he is imprisoned, and in all he is left bereft of the gift that defined him. (legend) [folk]
- **The hymn as atonement** — Realizing the offense was against **Śiva**, Puṣpadanta composed the **Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra** in praise of the god to win back His grace — and, the hymn pleasing Śiva, his powers were restored. The stotra thus functions both as confession and as the means of expiation. (Puṣpadanta frame) [folk]
- **Self-naming in the text** — The hymn names its own author in its closing verses: the colophon (v. 43) and the signature-verses attribute it to **Puṣpadanta** ("flower-toothed," also given as *Kusumadanta*), a rare instance of a stotra embedding its composer's name and frame-legend within the text itself. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra*, signature/colophon verses) [scholarly]

## Content and structure

- **The opening confession of inadequacy** — The first verse sets the governing logic: if even **Brahmā's wearied speech, exhausting the Vedas, cannot reach the end of Śiva's greatness**, then the poet's own praise is necessarily defective — yet praise offered with devotion is never wholly in vain, for it purifies the speaker's own mind. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* 1–3)
- **The limits of language and the gods' praise** — A recurring move: the **Vedas, Brahmā, and the greatest sages fall silent** before Śiva's nature; speech "returns without reaching" Him (echoing the Upaniṣadic *yato vāco nivartante*). The hymn makes its own inexpressibility its central rhetorical and theological device. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra*, esp. 2–7) [scholarly]
- **Cosmic functions and paradoxes** — Śiva is praised as creator, sustainer, and destroyer; the verses delight in **paradox** — the naked ascetic who is lord of all wealth, the cremation-ground dweller who is supremely auspicious, the formless one worshipped in form. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* 8–14)
- **The three guṇas and the one reality** — Several verses harmonize sectarian names, declaring that the various paths (Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Pāśupata, Vaiṣṇava, Vaidika) and even the names **Hari and Hara** point to a single supreme reality — an inclusivist note often quoted. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra*, the *trayī-sāṃkhya-yoga* verse, ~v. 7) [scholarly]
- **The myths of Śiva woven in** — The hymn alludes to the great Śaiva narratives as proofs of His power: the **drinking of the hālāhala poison** at the churning, the burning of the **Tripura** cities, the chastening of **Kāma**, the bearing of the **Gaṅgā** in His matted hair, the **tāṇḍava** cosmic dance, and the **humbling of Rāvaṇa** beneath Kailāsa. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra*, narrative verses ~14–30)
  - *Cross-reference:* the cosmic-dance theme is treated at length in the [Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra](shiva-tandava-stotra.md).
- **The Bāṇa / arrow imagery and the cosmos as Śiva's chariot** — A famous passage pictures the gods' war on Tripura with the **earth as chariot, sun and moon as wheels, Brahmā as charioteer, Meru as bow** — and Śiva needing none of it, destroying the cities by a mere smile, to show that His power is independent of instruments. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra*, the Tripura verse) [scholarly]
- **The closing dedication and phala-śruti** — The final verses give the author's signature (Puṣpadanta), declare the hymn's power to purify and please Śiva, and promise the fruit of recitation. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* 38–43)

## Themes: the inexpressibility of Śiva's greatness

- **Apophasis as devotion** — The hymn's signature theme is **theological apophasis**: the greatness (*mahiman*) of Śiva is *aparimeya* (immeasurable), so the only honest praise is praise that confesses its own insufficiency. Far from defeating the hymn, this becomes its highest tribute — the devotee offers the *effort*, not a claim to have captured the deity. [scholarly]
- **Devotion over capacity** — A consoling corollary much loved in the tradition: a child's babble or a poet's stammer, offered sincerely, reaches the god as surely as Bṛhaspati's eloquence — what counts is *bhāva*, not skill. (*Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra* 2–3) [scholarly]
- **Inclusivism (Hari–Hara samanvaya)** — By declaring the rival names and systems to be facets of one supreme reality, the stotra is frequently cited as a model of **sectarian reconciliation**, recited by Śaivas and non-Śaivas alike. [scholarly]

## Recitation tradition

- **A daily and festival hymn** — The Śiva-mahimnaḥ stotra is among the **most popular of all Śaiva stotras**, recited daily by devotees, during **Śiva-pūjā**, and especially on **Mahā-śivarātri** and through the month of **Śrāvaṇa**. (living tradition)
- **The gandharva's hymn, the worshipper's words** — Tradition holds that, because a gandharva composed it to *regain divine grace*, its recitation is peculiarly efficacious for **removing offenses (aparādha)** and faults committed in worship; many liturgies append it as a *kṣamā* (forgiveness) prayer at the close of a pūjā. (devotional tradition) [folk]
- **Recensions and length** — The familiar Puṣpadanta text runs to **43 verses** (some printings count 43–45 with the concluding phala-śruti / dedicatory ślokas); a distinct **shorter "Mahimna" attributed in some traditions to the gandharva and recited in Jain circles** also circulates, showing the hymn's reach across communities. (textual tradition) [disputed]
  - *Variant (Jain adaptation):* a **Jain Mahimna-stotra** modeled on the same opening and meter, redirecting the praise to a Tīrthaṅkara, is preserved in Jain liturgy — a measure of the original's prestige. [regional] [disputed]
- **Place among the great stotras** — It is grouped with the foremost recited praise-poems of Śaivism alongside the [Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotra](shiva-tandava-stotra.md), the Rudra-praśna, and the Liṅgāṣṭakam, and is treated in the wider [Lesser Gītās catalog](lesser-gitas-catalog.md) as part of the corpus of independent devotional song-discourses outside the epics' named Gītās. (liturgical tradition)

## Cosmic placement — the "two clocks"

- **In-narrative time** — The frame-legend is a **trans-yuga / heavenly episode**: a gandharva of Śiva's retinue offending and atoning belongs to the timeless celestial register of the Purāṇic world rather than to a fixed point in a single Mahāyuga; it is best read as **cross-yuga**. [scholarly]
- **Text-critical time** — As a polished Sanskrit *stotra* in elaborate meter (chiefly *śikhariṇī*), the hymn as we have it is a work of the **classical/medieval devotional period**, not of Vedic antiquity; its attribution to a primordial gandharva is a traditional frame, not a manuscript date. Keep the scriptural attribution distinct from the literary dating. [scholarly]
