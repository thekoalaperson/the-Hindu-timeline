# The Yoga Sūtras of Patañjali

> Deep dive within [04-deep-dives](README.md) of *The Hindu Timeline* — the foundational text of classical (*pātañjala*) Yoga, one of the six orthodox *darśanas*.
> For Yoga as **lived practice and the paths to mokṣa** (the four mārgas, the tantric streams, the eight limbs walked limb-by-limb), see [../05-concepts-and-dharma/yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md); for Yoga's place among the darśanas see [../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md) and [../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md](../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md). The doctrinal goal itself: [../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md).

The *Yoga Sūtras* (*Yogasūtra*, also *Pātañjalayogaśāstra* when counted together with its commentary) are a compact aphoristic manual — **196 sūtras in 4 *pādas* (books)** — that crystallise classical Yoga as the *practical* twin of [Sāṃkhya](../90-literature-corpus/upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md) metaphysics, while grafting on a distinctive theism (Īśvara) Sāṃkhya lacks. The defining sūtra is *"yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ"* — "Yoga is the stilling (*nirodha*) of the fluctuations (*vṛtti*) of the mind-stuff (*citta*)" (YS 1.2); when the mind is stilled, *"tadā draṣṭuḥ svarūpe 'vasthānam"* — "then the Seer abides in its own nature" (YS 1.3). This file catalogs the text's architecture, key doctrines, and its dense commentarial and dating debates.

**Dating** (note: this is a *darśana* text outside the cosmological timeline; cf. our constant that Kali Yuga began 3102 BCE — that frame does not apply here). Academic estimates range from **c. 2nd c. BCE to c. 4th–5th c. CE**, with most scholars favoring **c. 1st–2nd c. CE**; a recent philological position (Philipp Maas) dates the *sūtra*-plus-*bhāṣya* whole to **c. 400 CE**. [scholarly] [disputed]

## 1. The Author(s) — Patañjali

- **Patañjali** - The traditional author/redactor of the *Yoga Sūtras*. Tradition often conflates him with the grammarian Patañjali (author of the *Mahābhāṣya* on Pāṇini, c. 2nd c. BCE) and even with a physician of Āyurveda — the "three sciences" (grammar, medicine, yoga) legend — but most scholars treat these as **distinct persons** sharing a name. [scholarly] [disputed]
  - *Variant (devotional):* Patañjali is venerated as a partial incarnation of **Ādiśeṣa / Śeṣa-nāga**, the cosmic serpent of [Viṣṇu](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/), who fell (*pat*) into the cupped hands (*añjali*) of the sage-grammarian's mother Goṇikā — hence "Patañjali." Iconography shows him half-human, half-coiled-serpent, multi-hooded. [folk]
  - *Variant (single-author thesis, Maas):* the *sūtras* and the canonical *Bhāṣya* were composed by **the same hand** (the unit being the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra*), so "Vyāsa" is a later honorific ascription, not a separate author. [scholarly] [disputed]
  - *Variant (compilation thesis):* the text is a **redaction of pre-existing yoga material** (note the apparent seams — e.g. the *vibhūti* powers list, and the *kriyā-yoga* / *aṣṭāṅga* doublet — suggesting stitched sources). [scholarly]

## 2. The Four Pādas (Books) — Architecture

The 196 sūtras (standard count; some recensions give 194–196) divide into four books. [scholarly]

- **I. Samādhi Pāda — "On Absorption" (51 sūtras)** - Defines yoga (1.2–4), enumerates the five *vṛttis* (1.5–11), prescribes *abhyāsa* (practice) and *vairāgya* (dispassion) (1.12–16), distinguishes *samprajñāta* from *asamprajñāta* samādhi (1.17–18), treats Īśvara and **OM** (1.23–29), lists the nine *antarāyas* (obstacles) (1.30–31), and closes with the *samāpatti* (engrossment) stages culminating in *nirbīja* (seedless) samādhi (1.41–51). Addressed to the already-advanced practitioner.
- **II. Sādhana Pāda — "On Practice" (55 sūtras)** - The practical core. Opens with **Kriyā Yoga** (*tapas*, *svādhyāya*, *īśvara-praṇidhāna*; 2.1–2), the five **kleśas** (2.3–9), karma and its fruits, the doctrine of *duḥkha* (suffering) and *viveka* (discrimination) (2.15–27), then introduces the **eight limbs (aṣṭāṅga)** and details the first five "external" limbs (*yama* through *pratyāhāra*; 2.28–55).
- **III. Vibhūti Pāda — "On Powers/Manifestations" (56 sūtras)** - Defines the three "internal" limbs — *dhāraṇā*, *dhyāna*, *samādhi* — and their fusion as **saṃyama** (3.1–8), then catalogs the **siddhis / vibhūtis** (supernormal powers) that arise from saṃyama on various objects (3.9–55), warning that these are **obstacles** to liberation (3.37, 3.51).
- **IV. Kaivalya Pāda — "On Isolation/Liberation" (34 sūtras)** - Treats the mind's nature, the sources of siddhis, the theory of mind (*citta*) and its modifications, karma and latent impressions (*saṃskāra*/*vāsanā*), a refutation of idealism (defending the reality of objects against a Vijñānavāda-Buddhist position), and ends in **kaivalya** — the final isolation of Puruṣa (4.34). Often thought a later addition because of its anti-Buddhist polemic. [scholarly] [disputed]
  - *Variant (sūtra count):* most editions = 196; some = 195 (merging two) or 194; Bhoja's recension and certain manuscripts differ slightly. [scholarly]

## 3. The Core Doctrines

### 3a. The five vṛttis and their stilling
- **The five vṛttis (mental fluctuations, YS 1.5–11)** - *pramāṇa* (valid cognition — perception/inference/testimony), *viparyaya* (error/misperception), *vikalpa* (conceptualisation/imagination without object), *nidrā* (sleep), *smṛti* (memory). Yoga = stilling all five.
- **The two means (YS 1.12)** - **abhyāsa** (sustained practice, "firmly grounded by long, uninterrupted, earnest application," 1.13–14) and **vairāgya** (dispassion / non-attachment, with its higher *paravairāgya* born of knowing Puruṣa, 1.15–16).

### 3b. Samādhi — the typology of absorption
- **Samprajñāta samādhi** ("cognitive" / with-seed) - Absorption that still rests on an *object*, in four ascending grades: **vitarka** (gross object), **vicāra** (subtle object), **ānanda** (bliss of the cognising instruments), **asmitā** (bare I-am-ness/buddhi) (YS 1.17).
- **Asamprajñāta samādhi** ("super-cognitive" / objectless) - Absorption beyond all object-support, the cessation of all content, leaving only latent *saṃskāra* (YS 1.18); deepening into **nirbīja** ("seedless") samādhi when even those impressions are burnt (1.51).
- **Saṃāpatti grades** (YS 1.41–48) - engrossment shading from *savitarka*/*nirvitarka* through *savicāra*/*nirvicāra*, culminating in *ṛtambharā prajñā* ("truth-bearing insight").

### 3c. The five kleśas (afflictions)
- **The five kleśas (YS 2.3–9)** - **avidyā** (ignorance — misidentifying the impermanent body-mind with the eternal Self; the *root* of the rest), **asmitā** (egoism — conflating the seer with the instrument of seeing), **rāga** (attachment), **dveṣa** (aversion), **abhiniveśa** (clinging to life / instinctive fear of death). Avidyā is "the field" of all the others (2.4).
- **Kriyā Yoga (YS 2.1–2)** - The "yoga of action" that attenuates the kleśas: **tapas** (austerity), **svādhyāya** (self-study / recitation of sacred texts incl. OM), **īśvara-praṇidhāna** (devotional surrender to the Lord). [Note: these three also recur as the last three *niyamas* — a deliberate doublet.]

### 3d. Kaivalya — liberation
- **Kaivalya** ("aloneness, isolation") - The *summum bonum*: the absolute **disjunction of Puruṣa from Prakṛti**, pure witness-consciousness standing in its own nature, freed from the *guṇa*-flux and the wheel of rebirth (*saṃsāra*). It is *isolation*, not "union" — classical Yoga's goal is separative, not unitive. (YS 4.34) [scholarly]
  - *Variant (Vedāntic reading):* later non-dualist commentators (and modern teachers) reinterpret kaivalya as *union* with Brahman/Īśvara, importing Vedānta into a dualist text. [late text] [scholarly]

## 4. Relation to Sāṃkhya — and the Īśvara Difference

- **Shared metaphysics** - Yoga is "Sāṃkhya with God and method." It inherits the **dualism of Puruṣa (pure consciousness, plural, inactive witness) and Prakṛti (the one active material matrix)**, the **three guṇas** (*sattva*, *rajas*, *tamas*), and the apparatus of *citta* = *buddhi* (intellect) + *ahaṃkāra* (ego-sense) + *manas* (mind). Bondage = mistaken identification of Puruṣa with the *vṛttis* of citta; liberation = discriminative knowledge (*viveka-khyāti*) of their utter distinctness. [scholarly]
  - *Note:* much of this 25-*tattva* vocabulary is **sparse in Patañjali's bare sūtras** and is supplied by the *Bhāṣya*; classical Yoga is sometimes called *seśvara-sāṃkhya* ("Sāṃkhya-with-Lord"). [scholarly]
- **Īśvara — the theistic anchor (YS 1.23–29)** - A **special, ever-free Puruṣa** "untouched by afflictions (*kleśa*), actions (*karma*), their fruits (*vipāka*), and latent deposits (*āśaya*)" (1.24); the supreme, unsurpassed teacher unconditioned by time (1.25–26); **designated by the syllable OM (praṇava)**, whose *japa* with contemplation of its meaning removes obstacles and turns the mind inward (1.27–29).
  - *Variant (reading):* Patañjali's Īśvara is **not a cosmic creator** but a perfect model-soul and object of devotion — a "minimal theism" or focus for meditation, distinct from the creator-gods of Purāṇic Vaiṣṇavism/Śaivism. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (sufficiency):* *Īśvara-praṇidhāna* (surrender to the Lord) is offered as a **complete alternative path** to samādhi all by itself (1.23, 2.45) — Yoga's embedded *bhakti* element. [scholarly]
  - *Contrast (classical Sāṃkhya):* the older Sāṃkhya of the *Sāṃkhya-kārikā* is **atheistic** (or god-agnostic); Yoga's addition of Īśvara is its chief doctrinal divergence. [scholarly]

## 5. The Eight Limbs (Aṣṭāṅga, YS 2.28–3.3)

The first five are *bahiraṅga* (external aids); the last three, fused as **saṃyama**, are *antaraṅga* (internal). (Walked in full detail, with cross-tradition variants, in [../05-concepts-and-dharma/yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md).)

- **1. Yama — restraints (the "great vow," *mahāvrata*)** - *ahiṃsā* (non-violence), *satya* (truthfulness), *asteya* (non-stealing), *brahmacarya* (continence), *aparigraha* (non-possessiveness) (YS 2.30); binding regardless of birth, place, time, circumstance (2.31).
- **2. Niyama — observances** - *śauca* (purity), *santoṣa* (contentment), *tapas*, *svādhyāya*, *īśvara-praṇidhāna* (YS 2.32).
- **3. Āsana — posture** - *"sthira-sukham āsanam"* — a seat **steady and comfortable** (YS 2.46–48). In Patañjali a *meditation seat* only; the elaborate postural repertoire is later hatha. [scholarly]
- **4. Prāṇāyāma — breath regulation** - Control of the in/out/retained breath after āsana is mastered (YS 2.49–53).
- **5. Pratyāhāra — sense-withdrawal** - The senses disengage from their objects, "imitating the mind" (YS 2.54–55).
- **6. Dhāraṇā — concentration** - Binding the mind to a single locus/object (YS 3.1).
- **7. Dhyāna — meditation** - Unbroken flow of cognition toward that object (YS 3.2).
- **8. Samādhi — absorption** - Object alone shines, the mind "empty of its own form" (YS 3.3).
- **Saṃyama (YS 3.4)** - The three internal limbs practised together upon one object; its mastery yields the **vibhūtis/siddhis** (e.g. knowledge of past/future, invisibility, strength of an elephant, levitation, omniscience) — repeatedly flagged as *distractions* to be renounced for kaivalya (YS 3.16–55, esp. 3.37, 3.51). [folk: many siddhis read as legendary]

## 6. The Commentarial Tradition (catalog)

The sūtras are terse to the point of unintelligibility without commentary; an unbroken chain of *bhāṣya*, *vārttika*, *ṭīkā*, and *vṛtti* shaped how they were read. [scholarly]

- **Yogabhāṣya / Vyāsabhāṣya** - The foundational commentary, traditionally ascribed to **Vyāsa** (here a title, not the *Mahābhārata* compiler), **c. 4th–5th c. CE**. Near-canonical; supplies the Sāṃkhya metaphysics and the standard reading for ~1500 years. The sūtras + this *bhāṣya* are jointly the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra*.
  - *Variant (Maas):* identical authorship with the sūtras — i.e. **Patañjali wrote his own bhāṣya**, "Vyāsa" being a later honorific. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Tattvavaiśāradī** - Sub-commentary by **Vācaspati Miśra** (**c. 900–980 CE**), the most influential gloss on the *Bhāṣya*; the polymath Vācaspati also wrote on Nyāya, Vedānta, Mīmāṃsā. [scholarly]
- **Yogavārttika & Yogasārasaṃgraha** - By **Vijñānabhikṣu** (**16th c. CE**), who reads Yoga through a theistic, Vedānta-leaning lens (his *avibhāga-advaita*). [scholarly]
- **Rājamārtaṇḍa (Bhojavṛtti)** - Direct commentary on the sūtras by **King Bhoja of Dhārā** (**11th c. CE**), bypassing the *Bhāṣya*. [scholarly]
- **Pātañjala-yogaśāstra-vivaraṇa** - A commentary on sūtras-plus-bhāṣya **ascribed to Śaṅkara** (the Advaitin); its authenticity is **disputed** — if genuine it is the earliest Advaita engagement with Yoga, c. 8th c.; if not, by a later "Śaṅkara." [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Maṇiprabhā** - By **Rāmānanda Sarasvatī** (**16th c.**), a concise gloss. [scholarly]
- **Bhāsvatī** - By **Hariharānanda Āraṇya** (**1869–1947**), founder of the Kāpil Maṭh, the major modern Sanskrit commentary continuing the classical-dualist line. [scholarly]
- **Modern translators & expositors:**
  - **Swami Vivekananda — *Rāja Yoga* (1896)** - Hugely influential Western-facing rendering that fixed the equation "Yoga Sūtras = Rāja Yoga" in modern usage and recast the text in a Vedāntic, experiential frame. [late text]
  - **B. K. S. Iyengar — *Light on the Yoga Sūtras of Patañjali* (1993)** - Practitioner's translation linking the sūtras to modern postural yoga. [late text]
  - **Edwin F. Bryant — *The Yoga Sūtras of Patañjali* (2009)** - Scholarly translation drawing the whole commentarial tradition into the notes. [scholarly]
  - *Other modern editions:* Rama Prasada (Vyāsa + Vācaspati, 1910s), Georg Feuerstein, I. K. Taimni (*The Science of Yoga*), Christopher Chapple.

## 7. Reception, Eclipse, and Revival

- **Medieval eclipse** - After Vijñānabhikṣu, the *Yoga Sūtras* faded somewhat as Yoga-as-practice migrated to the **haṭha** corpus (*Haṭhayogapradīpikā*, *Gheraṇḍa Saṃhitā*, etc.) and the Nāth lineages; the *Yoga Sūtras* survived more as a *darśana* text than a practice manual. [scholarly] (See haṭha streams in [../05-concepts-and-dharma/yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md).)
- **Modern revival** - The text's global prominence is largely a **late-19th/20th-c. phenomenon**, propelled by Vivekananda, the Theosophical Society, and modern transnational yoga; some scholars argue its centrality to "Yoga" was *retrojected* by the modern movement. [scholarly] [disputed]
