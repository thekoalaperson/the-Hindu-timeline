# Rantideva's Fourfold Charity

> Cosmic address: a *Manvantara-frame exemplary tale* of the present **[7th — Vaivasvata — Manvantara](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md)**, set among the **[Bhārata (Lunar-line) kings](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)** descended from Bharata Dauṣyanti — Rantideva being the son of Saṅkṛti. It is one of the tradition's supreme parables of **dāna, atithi-sevā, and boundless compassion (para-duḥkha-kātaratā)**, told at length in the 9th Canto of the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* and in a strikingly different key across the *Mahābhārata*.
> Parent: [04-deep-dives](README.md) · See also: [Śibi — the King & the Dove](shibi.md) · [Ṣoḍaśarājakīya (the Sixteen Kings)](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md) · [Śveta & the Mongoose (dāna-upākhyānas)](dana-upakhyanas-shveta-and-the-mongoose.md) · [Ambarīṣa & Durvāsā](ambarisha-durvasa.md) · [ahiṃsā & Hindu ethics](../05-concepts-and-dharma/ahimsa-and-hindu-ethics.md)

The story of **King Rantideva** is the *locus classicus* of selfless giving in which a starving king, on the point of breaking a 48-day fast, surrenders his last food and last water to a succession of hungry strangers — a **brāhmaṇa, a śūdra, an outcaste with his dogs, and finally a caṇḍāla begging water** — asking of God not liberation but only *to enter into the suffering of all beings so that they be freed from it*. In the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* (9.21.1–18) he is a near-ascetic of measureless compassion whom the **Trimūrti themselves** test in disguise. In the *Mahābhārata* (Droṇa Parva; Śānti Parva) the *same* king is remembered very differently — as a magnificent host at whose kitchens **two thousand cattle were slaughtered daily**, from the hides of which the river **Carmaṇvatī (the Chambal)** is said to have flowed. The corpus preserves both portraits side by side; this file catalogs them without merging them.

## Who Rantideva was — placement in the Lunar line

- **The Bhārata descent** — Rantideva belongs to the **Pauravā / Bhārata branch of the Chandravaṃśa (Lunar dynasty)**, a descendant of **Bharata**, son of Duṣyanta and Śakuntalā; the *Bhāgavata* names him **son of Saṅkṛti**, and his fame is glorified "in this world and the next" (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.1–2*). See [Śakuntalā & Bharata](shakuntala-bharata.md) and the [Lunar dynasty](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **A "sixteen kings" cakravartin** — He is one of the great emperors of old catalogued in the **[Ṣoḍaśarājakīya](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md)**, the *Mahābhārata*'s consolation-list of mighty, generous, dharmic kings who nonetheless all died — recited in the Droṇa Parva to teach the vanity of grief (*Mahābhārata, Droṇa Parva ~67*). [scripture]
- **The two clocks** — Scripturally he reigns in the deep dynastic past of the **28th Mahāyuga** of the present Manvantara, generations after Bharata (traditionally a Tretā-into-Dvāpara antiquity); academically the narratives that fix his fame are texts of the common era — the *Mahābhārata*'s core [scholarly] c. 400 BCE – 400 CE and the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* [scholarly] c. 9th–10th century CE — so his "biography" is a composite of strata a millennium apart.
- **Kingdom & river** — Tradition seats his realm in the Mālwa / Daśapura country along the **Carmaṇvatī (Chambal)**, the river the epic ties to his hospitality (see below). [regional]

## The fourfold charity — the Bhāgavata famine narrative (SB 9.21.1–18)

- **The king who kept nothing** — Rantideva **never stored wealth**; whatever came to him he gave away, keeping nothing for the morrow, and so lived in voluntary poverty, he and his family suffering want with perfect equanimity (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.3–4*).
- **Forty-eight days without food or water** — A great **famine** struck. For **forty-eight days** Rantideva, with his household, took **neither food nor even water**; wasted and trembling with hunger and thirst, he never lost his composure (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.5*).
- **The single meal at dawn** — On the morning that ended the fast there came to him, as if by grace, a little **ghee, milk-rice / kheer (ghṛta-pāyasa), some other food, and water** — just enough for his starving family to break their fast (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.5–6*).
- **First guest — the brāhmaṇa** — As they were about to eat, a **brāhmaṇa guest** arrived. Seeing God present in all beings, Rantideva received him with joy and **gave him a portion of the food** with devotion; the brāhmaṇa ate and departed (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.7*).
- **Second guest — the śūdra** — Dividing what remained, Rantideva was about to eat when a **śūdra** arrived hungry; the king **fed him too**, again seeing the Lord in him (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.8–9*).
- **Third guest — the man with dogs** — Next came an **outcaste surrounded by a pack of dogs (śva-bhiḥ pariṛtaḥ)**, pleading, "Give food to me and my starving dogs." Rantideva reverently **gave away all the remaining food** to the man and his hounds, bowing to them (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.9–11*).
- **Fourth guest — the caṇḍāla and the last water** — **Only the water was left**, barely enough for one thirsting man. As Rantideva raised it, a **caṇḍāla (pulkasa)** appeared, gasping, "I am low-born — give me water." Moved to the depths by the wretch's suffering, the king **gave away his last water** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.11–12*).
- **The reveal in disguise** — These four guests were **no ordinary beings**: the *Bhāgavata* discloses that the controllers of creation, maintenance, and dissolution — **Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva** — had come in illusory forms (*māyā-manuṣya*) to test and grace him (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.13–16*).

## "I desire not the eight siddhis" — the great compassion verse

- **The verse itself** — As he surrendered the last water to the caṇḍāla, Rantideva uttered the utterance for which he is most remembered — a renunciation not only of wealth but of **liberation itself**, in favour of shouldering the pain of all creatures:
  > *na kāmaye 'haṃ gatim īśvarāt parām*
  > *aṣṭardhi-yuktām apunar-bhavaṃ vā |*
  > *ārtiṃ prapadye 'khila-deha-bhājām*
  > *antaḥ-sthito yena bhavanty aduḥkhāḥ ||*
  > "I do not pray to the Lord for the supreme state endowed with the **eight siddhis**, nor for release from rebirth. I choose only to **enter into and bear the suffering of all embodied beings**, that they may be freed from misery." (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.12*)
- **What it renounces** — He explicitly turns down **animā and the rest of the aṣṭa-siddhi** (the eight yogic perfections) and even **apunar-bhava (final liberation)** — the two highest prizes of the ascetic path — asking instead to **indwell all beings and absorb their pain**. It is the scriptural charter of *para-duḥkha-duḥkhitā*, compassion that suffers another's grief as one's own. [scripture]
- **Effect of the gift** — By that supreme renunciation, the *Bhāgavata* says, **hunger, thirst, weariness, distress, grief, delusion and dejection all left him**; his heart, purified by devotion to Vāsudeva, became utterly serene (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.13*).

## The Trimūrti revealed and the liberation

- **The gods stand forth** — The three disguised guests **revealed themselves** to the king. Rantideva, however, **desired no boon**: fixed in devotion to Nārāyaṇa beyond the three guṇas, he sought nothing from Brahmā, Viṣṇu, or Śiva (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.14–16*).
- **Māyā dissolved** — For those whose consciousness rests wholly in **Vāsudeva**, the *Bhāgavata* observes, the Lord's illusory energy (*māyā*) **falls away of itself**, as a dream vanishes on waking — which is why the tempting powers held no lure for him (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.16–17*).
- **The yoga of his followers** — All who **followed Rantideva** — his kinsmen and dependents — became, by his association, **great yogīs devoted to Nārāyaṇa**, attaining the supreme goal (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.18*).
- **Vaiṣṇava reading** — In the Bhāgavata frame the tale sits beside its neighbouring devotee-exempla — [Ambarīṣa](ambarisha-durvasa.md), [Khaṭvāṅga](khatvanga-one-muhurta.md) — as proof that **bhakti transmutes even destitution and death into liberation**, and that the true devotee prizes service to beings above mokṣa. [scripture]

## The Mahābhārata Rantideva — the lavish host and the mortality-list

The epic remembers Rantideva by a **different signature virtue**: not famine-charity but stupendous *hospitality*, feeding multitudes of guests with vast slaughter of cattle. The two frames genuinely diverge and the corpus keeps them apart.

- **The Ṣoḍaśarājakīya entry (Droṇa Parva)** — Amid the consolation for the slain [Abhimanyu](mahabharata/07-drona-parva.md), Vyāsa lists Rantideva among the **sixteen great dead kings**. His mark of greatness: at his kitchens **two thousand cattle (and other beasts) were dressed each day**, and with the flesh he fed his countless brāhmaṇa and other guests — "yet he died," the lesson runs, so grief is vain (*Mahābhārata, Droṇa Parva ~67*). See [Ṣoḍaśarājakīya](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md).
- **The cook's famous cry** — A celebrated detail: so lavish were the feasts that the servers would call out, *"There is meat in plenty today, but of **sūpa (broth / cooked pulse)** only a little!"* — a byword for boundless open-handedness (*Mahābhārata, Droṇa Parva ~67; echoed Śānti Parva*). [scripture]
- **The Śānti Parva remembrance** — In the **Śānti Parva** (~ch. 29), within Bhīṣma's teaching to Yudhiṣṭhira, Rantideva recurs as an exemplar of **dāna and atithi-sevā** and again in the register of mighty kings who, for all their sacrifices and gifts, **passed away** — reinforcing the same mortality-and-generosity theme as the Droṇa list (*Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva ~29*).
- **A carnivorous, kṣatriya hospitality** — Here Rantideva is emphatically a **flesh-giving royal host**, not a fasting ascetic: the virtue lauded is the scale and openness of his table, not the renunciation of food. This is the frame that generates the river-aetiology below.

## The Carmaṇvatī (Chambal) aetiology

- **The river of hides** — From the **heaps of hides (carman) and the blood, fat, and offal** of the thousands of animals slaughtered daily at Rantideva's kitchens, a stream is said to have formed and flowed — named **Carmaṇvatī**, "she who is furnished with hides," the modern **Chambal** of Mālwa and Rājasthān (*Mahābhārata* tradition). [scripture] [regional]
- **The etymology** — *carman* = "hide, skin"; *Carmaṇvatī* = "possessing / made of hides." The epic frame ties the river's very name to the by-products of Rantideva's boundless feasts — a rare instance of a major river's origin ascribed to a king's hospitality rather than a divine descent. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (river-origin traditions):* other Purāṇic geographies simply list the **Carmaṇvatī among the rivers rising from the Ṛkṣa / Pāriyātra ranges** with no Rantideva connection; the "river of hides" aetiology is the *Mahābhārata*-linked legend, not a universal one. [scholarly]
- **The river's reputation** — In some regional lore the Chambal's association with **animal slaughter and blood** underlies its later reputation as an **inauspicious river to bathe in or cross**, and its exclusion from the roll of "holy" waters — folk memory reading the epic aetiology forward. [folk] [regional]

## Variants, tensions, and reception

- **Fasting ascetic vs. flesh-feasting host** — The single most striking divergence: the **Bhāgavata's Rantideva starves and gives away his last morsel**, and no animal is killed; the **Mahābhārata's Rantideva slaughters two thousand cattle a day** to feed his guests. Both are held up as the *summit of generosity*, from opposite material premises. The corpus records both; it does not reconcile them. [disputed]
  - *Reading (traditional harmonisation):* some commentators treat them as **two phases or two facets** — the mighty host of his reigning years, the destitute renunciant of his tapas-tested days — but the texts themselves make no such bridge; the harmonisation is interpretive. [scholarly]
- **The identity of the four guests** — The Bhāgavata names the testers as **Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva** in disguise (three gods for four guests, the fourth caṇḍāla folded into the same divine testing). Retellings vary in whether the dogs' master and the water-begging caṇḍāla are one theophany or several; the core text's point is that **God came as the lowest of the low** (*Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.13–16*). [scripture]
- **The Śibi parallel** — Rantideva stands beside **[Śibi](shibi.md)** as the paired archetype of the **self-emptying dāna-king**: Śibi gives his own **flesh** to ransom a refugee dove; Rantideva gives his last **food and water** and asks to bear all beings' pain. Both are Lunar-line kings, both tested by gods in disguise, both cited across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain *dāna* literature. [scholarly]
- **A dāna-exemplum across the tradition** — Rantideva's compassion-verse (SB 9.21.12) is quoted in Vaiṣṇava discourse and modern devotional writing (Gandhi and others invoked the "enter into all beings' suffering" ideal) as the high-water mark of **selflessness over even salvation**. He is grouped with the corpus's other gift-parables — [Śveta & the mongoose](dana-upakhyanas-shveta-and-the-mongoose.md) and the [Ṣoḍaśarājakīya](shodasarajakiya-sixteen-kings.md) roster. [scholarly]

- **Śrīmad Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Canto 9, Chapter 21, verses 1–18 (SB 9.21.1–18)** — the definitive fourfold-charity narrative: Rantideva son of Saṅkṛti in the Bhārata line (9.21.1–2), his refusal to hoard (9.21.3–4), the 48-day fast and the dawn meal (9.21.5–6), the four guests — brāhmaṇa, śūdra, the man with dogs, the caṇḍāla for water (9.21.7–11), the great compassion verse *na kāmaye 'haṃ gatim īśvarāt parām…* (9.21.12), the departure of hunger and grief (9.21.13), the Trimūrti in disguise and Rantideva's desirelessness (9.21.14–17), and the yogic liberation of his followers (9.21.18).
- **Mahābhārata, Droṇa Parva ~67 (Ṣoḍaśarājakīya)** — Rantideva among the sixteen great dead kings; the two thousand cattle dressed daily, the "meat in plenty, little broth" hospitality, and the mortality-lesson consoling the bereaved.
- **Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva ~29** — Bhīṣma's remembrance of Rantideva as an exemplar of dāna and hospitality among the mighty kings who, for all their gifts and sacrifices, died.
- **Mahābhārata tradition** — the **Carmaṇvatī (Chambal)** aetiology: the river said to arise from the hides and blood of the animals slaughtered at Rantideva's kitchens (*carman* = "hide").
