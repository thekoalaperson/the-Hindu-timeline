# Dharma-Nibandhas — the medieval digests

> Where this sits: `90-literature-corpus/` — the **secondary** dharma-literature that sits one layer above the root smṛtis. A **nibandha** ("binding-together," a digest) does not reveal new dharma; it collates, harmonises and topically re-arranges the scattered Dharma-sūtras, Manu/Yājñavalkya/Parāśara smṛtis and Purāṇic ritual passages into usable manuals. All are Kali-Yuga products by both clocks: scripturally, smṛti-elaboration for the degraded age (post-3102 BCE); academically **[scholarly] c. 1100–1800 CE**. This node expands the single digest-bullet in [agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md](agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md) §II. Parent: [The Literature Corpus](README.md).

The root smṛtis are terse and mutually contradictory; by the early second millennium a jurist could not simply "apply Manu." The nibandha-kāras (digest-authors) — usually court ministers or Banaras/Deccan paṇḍits — solved this by writing vast topical encyclopedias that quote hundreds of authorities verbatim, resolve conflicts by the Mīmāṃsā rules of interpretation, and file the result under practical headings (saṃskāra, āhnika, vyavahāra, śrāddha, prāyaścitta, vrata, dāna, tīrtha, śānti, śuddhi). These digests — not the root smṛtis — were the **operative "Hindu law"** the British courts inherited and codified as *Anglo-Hindu law*. They are also the codifying backbone behind this corpus's festival, vrata and ancestor-rite files.

---

## Orientation: what a nibandha is (and is not)

- **Nibandha vs. bhāṣya (commentary).** A **bhāṣya/ṭīkā** glosses one root text line-by-line (e.g. the *Mitākṣarā* on Yājñavalkya); a **nibandha** is a **topical digest** that ranges across the whole smṛti canon and re-organises it by subject. The *Mitākṣarā* straddles both — a commentary on Yājñavalkya so comprehensive it functions as a digest. (Kane, *History of Dharmaśāstra*)
- **Method.** The digest-authors adjudicate conflicting texts by **Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā** hermeneutics — distinguishing binding injunction (vidhi) from mere praise (arthavāda), applying the yuga-dharma doctrine (a rule valid for Satya may lapse in Kali), and invoking "custom of the good" (sadācāra) where texts are silent. [scholarly]
- **The two clocks.** Traditionally these are Kali-Yuga aids for humans too short-lived and low in dharma to master the whole Veda — the same rationale the Āgama traditions use. Academically they cluster **c. 12th–18th c. CE**, produced under Gāhaḍavāla, Cālukya, Yādava, Bundela and Peśvā/Deccan patronage. Never collapse the frames: a digest can be "a smṛti of Kali" *and* datable to 1612 CE at once.

## I. The major nibandhas (chronological)

- **Kṛtyakalpataru** (also *Kalpataru*, "the wish-fulfilling tree of religious acts") — the **earliest of the great encyclopedic digests**, by **Bhaṭṭa Lakṣmīdhara**, minister (mahāsāndhivigrahika) of the **Gāhaḍavāla king Govindacandra of Kānyakubja (Kanauj)**. Cast in **fourteen kāṇḍas**, each a self-contained treatise: Brahmacārin, Gṛhastha, Niyatakāla (daily rites), Śrāddha, Dāna, Vrata, Pūjā, Pratiṣṭhā (consecration), Tīrtha, Śānti, Vyavahāra (law), Śuddhi, Rājadharma, and Mokṣa. Its scheme became the template every later digest followed.
  - *Dating (academic):* **[scholarly] c. 1110–1150 CE**. Its citation of authorities is a prime tool for critically dating the smṛtis and Purāṇas it quotes. (Kane; Rangaswami Aiyangar's critical edition, GOS Baroda)
- **Smṛticandrikā** ("moonlight on the smṛtis") — the **authoritative digest of the South**, by **Devaṇṇa-bhaṭṭa** (Devaṇa Bhaṭṭa), a Karnataka/Dravida jurist. Organised into sections on saṃskāra, āhnika (daily observance), vyavahāra, śrāddha, āśauca (impurity) and prāyaścitta. Together with the *Mitākṣarā* it is the principal source of the **Dravida (Madras) sub-school** of Hindu law.
  - *Dating (academic):* **[scholarly] c. 1150–1225 CE** (12th–early 13th c.). [disputed] — some place Devaṇṇa-bhaṭṭa slightly after Vijñāneśvara, whom he cites. (Kane)
- **Caturvarga-cintāmaṇi** ("the wish-jewel of the four human goals" — dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣa) — the **largest of all nibandhas**, by **Hemādri (Hemādpant)**, chief minister (śrīkaraṇādhipa) of the **Yādava kings of Devagiri, Mahādeva and Rāmacandra**. Planned in five khaṇḍas; the surviving giants are the **Vrata-khaṇḍa** (an exhaustive calendar of hundreds of vows/observances), the **Dāna-khaṇḍa** (gift-giving) and the **Parisesha-khaṇḍa** (supplement, incl. kāla, śrāddha, tīrtha, and substantial prāyaścitta material). Both the **Tīrtha-khaṇḍa** and the **Mokṣa-khaṇḍa** are lost / unavailable today — some scholars think Hemādri never completed them.
  - *Dating (academic):* **[scholarly] c. 1260–1270 CE**. Hemādri is also remembered in Maharashtra for the **Hemāḍpanti** temple style and (by tradition) the **Moḍī** script — a folk-memory that fuses the historical minister with regional legend. [regional]
  - *Significance for this corpus:* the Vrata-khaṇḍa is the single densest medieval codification of the vow-and-festival year that stands behind [../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md) and the [../04-deep-dives/vrata-kathas-catalog.md](../04-deep-dives/vrata-kathas-catalog.md).
- **Vīramitrodaya** ("the rise of Vīra's friend") — a sprawling multi-part digest by **Mitra Miśra (Mitramiśra)** of Banaras, named for and composed under his patron **Bīr Singh Deo (Vīrasiṃha) of Orchhā**, the Bundela king. Its many *prakāśas* (books) cover saṃskāra, āhnika, pūjā, tīrtha, śrāddha, vyavahāra, lakṣaṇa and more; the **Vyavahāra-prakāśa** is a leading authority of the **Banaras (Kāśī) sub-school** of Mitākṣarā law.
  - *Dating (academic):* **[scholarly] early 17th c., c. 1610–1620 CE**. (Kane)
- **Nirṇayasindhu** ("the ocean of [ritual] determinations") — the standard authority on **kāla-nirṇaya**: fixing the correct calendrical time for every festival, fast, saṃskāra and vrata (which tithi prevails, how to reconcile lunar/solar reckoning, what to do when observances collide). By **Kamalākara Bhaṭṭa** of the great Banaras Bhaṭṭa family, in three **paricchedas**.
  - *Dating (academic):* composed **1612 CE** (Śaka 1534). It, with the *Dharmasindhu*, still governs how much of the Hindu festival almanac (pañcāṅga) is calculated today. (Kane)
- **Dharmasindhu** ("the ocean of dharma") — the most-used **practical household manual** of the modern Deccan, by **Kāśīnātha Upādhyāya (Bābā Pāḍhye / "Kāśīnātha-bhaṭṭa")**, in three paricchedas covering the ritual year, the saṃskāras life-cycle, śrāddha and prāyaścitta. Written in an accessible, prescriptive style, it — not the root smṛtis — is what a Maharashtrian purohita actually consults.
  - *Dating (academic):* completed **1790–1791 CE** (Śaka 1712), the late Peśvā period. Alongside the *Nirṇayasindhu* it is the working authority behind everyday domestic ritual. (Kane)
- **The wider genre.** Other major digests in the same tradition: **Parāśara-Mādhavīya** (Mādhavācārya/Vidyāraṇya's commentary-digest on the Parāśara-smṛti, Vijayanagara, 14th c.); **Madana-pārijāta** and **Madana-ratna** (Madanapāla, 14th c.); **Smṛtitattva** and the **Aṣṭāviṃśati-tattva** of **Raghunandana Bhaṭṭācārya** (Bengal, 16th c., the great codifier of the Dāyabhāga province); **Nṛsiṃha-prasāda**; **Śuddhikaumudī / Nirṇaya** works of the Mithilā school. (Kane, *History of Dharmaśāstra*, vol. I)

## II. The two inheritance schools: Mitākṣarā vs. Dāyabhāga

The one place where digest-theory became **living civil law** administered by courts is inheritance (dāya). Two rival systems split pre-modern Hindu India.

- **Mitākṣarā** — a **commentary on the Yājñavalkya-smṛti** by **Vijñāneśvara**, written at the court of the Western **Cālukya king Vikramāditya VI of Kalyāṇī**. So authoritative it names an entire school; it governed **all of Hindu India except Bengal and Assam**.
  - *Dating (academic):* **[scholarly] c. 1076–1127 CE** (late 11th–early 12th c.).
  - *Core doctrine — birthright (janma-svatva-vāda):* a son (and son's son, and son's son's son) acquires an interest in ancestral/coparcenary property **by birth**, jointly with the father. Property is held by the **undivided joint family as coparceners**; on a coparcener's death his share passes by **survivorship** to the surviving coparceners, not by inheritance. The father is only a co-owner, not absolute master, of ancestral wealth.
  - *Heirship by consanguinity:* Vijñāneśvara defines the heir by **propinquity of blood (sapiṇḍa = shared bodily particles)** — the nearest by consanguinity inherits.
- **Dāyabhāga** — a **free-standing digest on inheritance** by **Jīmūtavāhana** of Bengal; the sole authority of the **Bengal (and Assam) school**. (The same author's *Kālaviveka* and *Vyavahāra-mātṛkā* are companion works.)
  - *Dating (academic):* **[scholarly] c. 1090–1130 CE** [disputed] — Kane places Jīmūtavāhana roughly contemporary with the *Mitākṣarā*, but his date is one of the most contested in dharmaśāstra and several scholars (e.g. Rocher) put him meaningfully later than Vijñāneśvara; his influence in any case peaked when **Raghunandana** systematised the Bengal school in the 16th c.
  - *Core doctrine — inheritance only on death (uparama-svatva):* a son acquires **no right by birth**; the father is **absolute owner** of all property (ancestral or self-acquired) during his lifetime and may dispose of it at will. On the owner's death the property devolves by **inheritance (succession), not survivorship**; heirs take defined shares as tenants-in-common.
  - *Heirship by spiritual benefit:* Jīmūtavāhana determines the heir not by blood but by **religious efficacy** — whoever confers the **greatest spiritual benefit on the deceased by offering piṇḍa** (the śrāddha rice-ball) is the preferential heir. This ties the law of succession directly to the ancestor-cult (see [../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md) and [../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md](../08-beings-and-bestiary/pitrs-and-ancestors.md)).
  - *Practical consequences:* under Dāyabhāga a father can partition or alienate freely and sons cannot demand partition in his lifetime; the widow's position is comparatively stronger. Under Mitākṣarā the coparcenary restrains the father but classically excludes women from the coparcenary body. [scholarly]
- **Sub-schools of the Mitākṣarā:** regional digests refined it into four sub-schools — **Banaras** (Vīramitrodaya), **Mithilā** (Vivāda-cintāmaṇi, Vivāda-ratnākara), **Maharashtra / Bombay** (Vyavahāra-mayūkha of Nīlakaṇṭha), and **Dravida / Madras** (Smṛticandrikā, Sarasvatī-vilāsa). (Kane; *Anglo-Hindu law*)
- **Afterlife of the schools:** British courts froze these digests into a case-law "Hindu law." The Mitākṣarā/Dāyabhāga divide governed Indian succession until the **Hindu Succession Act, 1956** absorbed and largely superseded both — the coparcenary itself was only opened to daughters by the **2005 amendment**. [scholarly] — [Kali Yuga, documented history](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md).

## III. How the digests shape this corpus

- **The festival/vrata year.** The observances catalogued in [../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md) draw their prescriptive form — which tithi, which mantra, which dāna, which katha — from Hemādri's **Vrata-khaṇḍa**, the **Nirṇayasindhu** and the **Dharmasindhu**, not directly from the Purāṇas they quote. When two festivals collide on the calendar, it is a nibandha's nirṇaya that decides which prevails. [scholarly]
- **The ancestor rites.** The procedures in [../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md) and the tīrtha-śrāddha at [../06-sacred-geography/gaya-pitru-tirtha.md](../06-sacred-geography/gaya-pitru-tirtha.md) follow the śrāddha-kāṇḍas of the *Kṛtyakalpataru*, *Smṛticandrikā* and *Dharmasindhu* — and, as noted, the Dāyabhāga's very theory of who inherits rests on who offers the piṇḍa.
- **Penance and purification.** The prāyaścitta and śuddhi sections of these digests are the operative source behind the doctrine mapped in [../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md](../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md).
- **Root-text link.** For the smṛtis these digests systematise — Manu, Yājñavalkya, Nārada, Parāśara — see [manusmriti-map.md](manusmriti-map.md) and [agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md](agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md) §II.
