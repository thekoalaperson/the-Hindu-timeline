# Regional & Oral Epics & Folk Traditions

> Literature-corpus node. The **vernacular underside** of the canon — the great regional, oral, and folk-deity cycles that run *beneath and alongside* the Sanskrit [śruti/smṛti](./README.md) corpus and the [Tamil canon](./tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md). Where the [Itihāsa](./smriti-itihasa-epics.md) and [Purāṇas](./puranas/) are pan-Indian and (in principle) fixed-text, these are **sung, region-locked, caste-/community-owned, and openly multiform** — performed by hereditary bards (bhopā, paṇa, gondhaḷī, Pardhān, kaṃsale-singer), often before a painted scroll or in all-night possession-ritual. Most deify a **local hero/heroine** or a **grāma-devatā (village deity)** as an *avatāra* or *aṃśa* of a great Purāṇic god, knitting folk religion into the orthodox frame. By traditional reckoning all sit deep in [Kali Yuga](../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md); academically the heroes are medieval (c. 10th–16th c. CE) and the texts are living/uncrystallised. Variant policy here is total: **no two performances are the same**. See siblings [tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md](./tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md), [smriti-itihasa-epics.md](./smriti-itihasa-epics.md) and the master map [README.md](./README.md).

## Orientation: where the oral/folk stream sits

- **A third canon, neither Shruti nor "classical."** Beyond Sanskrit *and* beyond even the classical regional literatures (classical Tamil, Telugu, Kannada *kāvya*) lies a vast **oral epic** layer: long sung narratives, transmitted by hereditary singer-castes, recorded only recently (often by 20th-c. folklorists), and existing in *many recensions by design*. They are smṛti-class at most, and frequently **[non-canonical]** / **[folk]** entirely — yet they carry the lived religion of most of rural India. (Stuart Blackburn & A.K. Ramanujan, *Another Harmony*; multiple sources below)
- **The grāma-devatā layer.** Below the "great gods" sits the **grāma-devatā** (village/guardian deity) — usually a goddess (Māriyammaṉ, Ellammā, Gangamma) or a deified hero (kāval deyvam, "guardian deity"), tutelary protector against plague, famine, drought and demons, worshipped at an open-air or boundary shrine, often non-Brahmin in priesthood and blood-offering in rite. The oral epics are largely the **scriptures of this layer**. (Gramadevata — Wikipedia; *Village deities of South India* — Wikipedia) **[folk]**
- **Hero-into-god (the deification engine).** The recurring structure: a historical or legendary **martyr-hero** (often dying violently/young, or self-sacrificing) becomes a worshipped deity, then is retro-fitted as an *avatāra* or *aṃśa* of Viṣṇu/Śiva/Devī. This is the folk mechanism by which Pābūjī "is" Lakṣmaṇa, Devnārāyaṇ "is" Viṣṇu, and the Elder Brothers serve Śiva. **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
- **Performance is the text.** These epics are inseparable from their performance frame — the **phaḍ** scroll (Rajasthan), **udukkai-pāṭṭu** drum-song (Tamil), **kaṃsale** cymbals (Kannada), **burrakatha** (Telugu), **jāgar** possession-ritual (Himalaya). The "book" is a transcription of one singer's one night. **[oral tradition]**
- **Scripture vs. datable history.** Traditional devotion places these heroes in a timeless sacred past and links them to epic figures; academically most are **medieval** men and women (12th–16th c. CE) whose cults grew over centuries. Both frames flagged below.

## I. Tamil oral epics & folk deities (beyond the Sangam canon)

The classical twin epics **Silappatikāram** and **Maṇimēkalai** are already cataloged under the [Tamil canon](./tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md). Below them runs a living oral stratum, sung mostly to the **udukkai** (hourglass drum) and tied to village goddess and guardian-deity cults.

- **Aṇṇaṉmār Katai (The Elder Brothers' Story / Ponnivala epic)** - a major Tamil/Koṅgu oral epic of the twin warrior-brothers **Poṉṉar and Caṅkar** and their sister **Taṅkāḷ**, set in **Ponnivala Nāḍu** ("land of the golden river," Koṅgu region). Three generations of a farmer-clan: migration, the jealous nine younger brothers coveting fertile land, childlessness, magic, monsters, and the brothers' willing **self-sacrifice to the gods** (they serve Śiva/Viṣṇu), with the sister performing their funeral rites. Performed by two bards in **udukkai-pāṭṭu** style (lead bard + cymbals, assistant repeats end-phrases on the udukkai). (VMIS "Annanmar Katai"; Brenda Beck, *The Three Twins* / Ponnivala project)
  - *Date (traditional/[folk]):* narrative held to reach back at least to the **15th c. CE**; the brothers worshipped as kāval/guardian deities in Koṅgu Nāḍu.
  - *Variant ([scholarly], sister's POV):* the epic also circulates "from the sister's point of view" (Taṅkāḷ-centred recensions) — see *What the Sister Knew* (Religious Studies & Theology). The hero-count, the brothers' divine patron, and the ending vary by troupe.
- **Maturai Vīraṉ (Madurai Veeran, "warrior of Madurai")** - a Tamil Hindu **folk guardian deity** (kāval deyvam) of southern Tamil Nadu, venerated as protector of villages and farmlands. Legend: a low-caste/outcaste hero and warrior in the service of Madurai who is executed (dismembered) over a forbidden love, then deified for his valour and martyrdom; worshipped with his consorts **Vellaiyammāḷ and Bommakkāḷ**. (Madurai Veeran — Wikipedia; HinduPad) **[folk]**
  - *Variant (origin):* recensions make him variously a Tirunelveli prince, a sweeper-caste guardsman, or a foundling raised by cobblers — caste origin is itself a contested marker.
- **Aravāṉ / Iravāṉ (Kūttāṇṭavar)** - the Mahābhārata son of **Arjuna** and the Nāga princess **Ulūpī**, raised in Tamil folk cult into a major **village god**, especially of the Aravani (transgender/*ali*) community. Folk *Aravāṉ Kaḷappali* ("Aravan's battlefield sacrifice"): he offers himself to **Kālī** before the war to guarantee Pāṇḍava victory, asking to be married for one night first — Kṛṣṇa as **Mōhinī** weds and then mourns him; re-enacted at the **Koovagam** festival. (Iravan — Wikipedia; *Parata Veṇpā*, Peruntēvaṉār, 9th c.; Villiputtūrār *Makāparatam*, 14th c.) **[folk]**
  - *Variant (textual):* the self-sacrifice episode is **absent from the Sanskrit Mahābhārata** and is a distinctively Tamil-tradition expansion, attested from Peruntēvaṉār's 9th-c. *Parata Veṇpā* onward.

## II. Rajasthani oral epics — the phaḍ tradition

Rajasthan's epics are sung by **bhopā** priest-bards (and their **bhopī** wives) before a long painted cloth scroll, the **phaḍ** — a "portable temple" — to the **rāvaṇhattā** (two-string fiddle); the unrolled scroll *is* the deity's shrine for the night. Originated in the Bhilwara region, c. 700+ years old. (Pabuji ki Phad — MAP Academy; Sarmaya; Scroll.in)

- **Pābūjī (Pabuji ki Phad)** - the great Rajasthani folk epic of **Pābūjī Rāṭhoṛ**, a Rajput hero-chieftain (held to have lived early **14th c. CE**) deified as a **grāma-devatā** and **camel-protector god**, worshipped especially by the Rebārī/Raika herding community. Sung over the ~15-foot phaḍ; central exploits include cattle-rescue and his death in battle. Traditionally regarded as an **incarnation of Lakṣmaṇa** (Rāma's brother). (Pabuji ki Phad — MAP Academy; *Folk Epics of Rajasthan*, Meenakshi) **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant ([scholarly]):* John D. Smith's *The Epic of Pābūjī* documents the full sung text and its variability across bhopā lineages — episode order and length differ per performer/night.
- **Devnārāyaṇ (Devnarayan ki Phad)** - one of the **oldest living oral epic traditions of India**; the saga of **Devnārāyaṇ**, deified hero-king of the Gūjar community, held to be an **incarnation of Viṣṇu** (in some tellings, of the warrior **Sāvāī Bhoj**'s lineage avenging the Bagaṛāvat brothers). Sung over a giant ~**35-foot phaḍ** (larger than Pābūjī's), the longest of the scroll-epics. (Pabuji ki Phad — MAP Academy; *Folk Epics of Rajasthan*) **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (avatāra-claim):* sources variously make Devnārāyaṇ an avatāra of Viṣṇu directly, or of **Lakṣmaṇa/Śeṣa** — the Vaiṣṇava grafting is itself multiform.
- **Ḍholā–Mārū (Ḍholā Mārū rā Dūhā)** - a celebrated Rajasthani **romance-epic** rather than a hero-cult: the love of **Ḍholā** (prince Sāl Kuṃvar) and **Mārvaṇī (Mārū)**, betrothed as children, separated, and reunited across desert and rival queens — preserved in *dūhā* (couplet) verse and sung widely across the Marwar/Malwa belt. (Rajasthani folk-literature tradition; widely anthologised) **[folk]**
  - *Note:* unlike Pābūjī/Devnārāyaṇ it is **not** a deified-hero phaḍ cult but a courtly-romantic ballad cycle; flagged here as the romantic counterpart in the Rajasthani oral corpus.

## III. North Indian (Hindi belt) — the Alhā cycle

- **Ālhā-Khaṇḍ (Ālhā–Ūdal)** - the great martial oral epic of **Bundelkhand**, a cycle of ~**52 gāthās** (ballads) recounting the **52 battles / "52 forts" (bāvan gaṛh vijay)** of the brother-heroes **Ālhā and Ūdal**, Banāphar commanders of **King Paramardideva (Parmāl)** of Mahoba, culminating in their resistance to **Pṛthvīrāj Cauhāṇ** of Ajmer (assoc. with the **1182 CE** conflict). Attributed to the court-poet **Jagnik (Jaganika)** of Mahoba; entirely oral, surviving in many recensions differing in language and episode. (Alha-Khand — Wikipedia; *The Lays of Alha*) **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (immortality motif, [folk]):* a widespread tradition holds **Ālhā never died** — granted immortality (sometimes by the goddess **Śāradā** of Maihar), he withdraws rather than falls, and is awaited still.
  - *Variant (recension):* Waterfield/Grierson's *The Lays of Alha* (English) vs. the printed **Baṛā Ālhā-Khaṇḍ** (Hindi) vs. countless village versions — number and order of the 52 *laṛāī* (battles) are not fixed.

## IV. Telugu — the Palnāḍu cycle

- **Palnāṭi Vīrula Katha (Palnāḍu epic / "The Epic of Palnāḍu")** - the foremost Telugu **oral martial epic**, narrating the fratricidal **Battle of Palnāḍu** (c. **1178–1182 CE**) between two factions of the Haihaya/Kalachuri-line chiefs of Palnāḍu (in modern Andhra Pradesh). Centred on the statesman-warrior **Brahma Nāyuḍu** (Palanāṭi Brahmanāyuḍu), who preaches an **egalitarian, anti-caste "Vīra-Vaiṣṇava" order** (the *Cāpa-kūḍu* common-meal across castes), and the boy-hero **Bālacandruḍu** who dies heroically. The fallen heroes (**Palnāṭi Vīrulu**) are worshipped as deities, with a cult centre at **Kārempūḍi**. (Palanati Brahmanaidu — Wikipedia; Gene Roghair, *The Epic of Palnadu*; ThePrint) **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (literary crystallisation):* the oral *katha* was given literary form by the poet **Śrīnātha** (15th c.) as *Palnāṭi Vīra Caritra* — sung also as **burrakatha**; oral and literary versions differ in emphasis and detail.
  - *Variant (theology):* Brahma Nāyuḍu is variously framed as a social reformer, a Vīraśaiva-adjacent figure, or an **incarnation of Viṣṇu**, and his rival minister **Nāgammā** as a Śaiva/Devī-aligned antagonist.

## V. Kannada oral epics — the kaṃsale singers

Karnataka's oral epics are sung mostly by professional possession-prone singers — **Devara Guḍḍaru** ("god's children") and **Kaṃsaleyavaru** (those who keep time on the **kaṃsale**, bronze cymbals) — to cymbals, snake-shaped string instruments and leather drums, in honour of Vīraśaiva/folk hero-saints. (*Oral Epics of Kannada*, shastriyakannada.org; Hi.Chi. Boralingayya) **[oral tradition]**

- **Male Mādēśvara (Male Mahadeshwara)** - the major Kannada oral epic of the spiritual hero-saint **Mādēśvara** of the **Male Maḍeśvara hills**, a miracle-working Vīraśaiva (Liṅgāyat-adjacent) **siddha**, held to date from the late **12th–early 13th c. CE**. Sung by Mādēśvara devotees to the kaṃsale; the saint is worshipped as a form of **Śiva**, his hill-shrine a major pilgrimage centre. (Documented from Hebbani Madayya's troupe by K. Keshavan Prasad.) **[folk]**
- **Manteswamy (Manṭeswāmy)** - a Kannada oral epic cycle of the wandering Vīraśaiva guru-saint **Manṭeswāmy** and his miracles/social challenges to caste orthodoxy; collected (single-narrator version) by **Hi.Chi. Boralingayya** in 1997 at Kannada University, Hampi. Sung in the same kaṃsale tradition as Male Mādēśvara. **[folk]**
- **Juñjappa** - oral epic of **Juñjappa**, a native cattle-herding **cultural hero/cowherd deity** of the Kāḍu Golla (pastoralist) community of Karnataka, protector of cattle — one of the Kannada hero-cult cycles kept alive by community singers. **[folk]**

## VI. Bengali Maṅgal-kāvyas — the indigenous-deity poems

A distinct hybrid: **written but folk-rooted**. The **Maṅgal-kāvya** ("auspicious poems," c. **13th–18th c. CE**) are long episodic Bengali narrative poems that **eulogise indigenous/non-Vedic deities of rural Bengal** to promote their worship; the recurring plot is the **indigenous deity forcing a reluctant merchant/king to worship them**, ending in the local god's triumph over the "great" Sanskritic gods. (Mangal-Kavya — Wikipedia; Banglapedia; Britannica) **[folk]/[late text]**

- **Manasā-maṅgal** - poems of **Manasā**, the **serpent-goddess** who protects from snakebite; the core tale: the merchant **Cā̃d Sadāgar** refuses her worship, so she destroys his sons (incl. **Lakhindar**, slain by snakebite on his wedding night), until **Behulā**, his widowed bride, floats her husband's corpse to the gods and wins his life back — forcing Cā̃d to worship Manasā. Among the most important works of Bengali literature; poets **Vijaya Gupta, Bipradāsa Pipilāi, Ketakādāsa Kṣemānanda**. **[folk]**
- **Caṇḍī-maṅgal (Caṇḍīmaṅgal)** - poems of **Caṇḍī**, a fierce form of **Durgā/Devī**, establishing her worship among hunters and merchants (the **Kālaketu** hunter and **Dhanapati/Śrīmanta** merchant cycles). The masterwork is **Mukundarāma Cakravartī "Kavikaṅkaṇa"'s** *Caṇḍīmaṅgal* (16th c.). **[folk]**
- **Dharma-maṅgal** - poems of **Dharma Ṭhākur**, a uniquely Bengali folk deity (aniconic, often a stone/tortoise form) associated with **justice, fertility and disease-cure**, worshipped especially by lower-caste communities; the **Lausen** hero-cycle is central. Dharma's origins are debated (Buddhist-survival theory vs. indigenous Sun/folk deity). **[folk]/[disputed]**
- **Bonbibī (Bonbibi Jahuranama)** - the **forest-goddess of the Sundarbans**, protector of honey-gatherers and woodcutters against the tiger-demon **Dakṣiṇ Rāy**; her cult is **shared by Hindus and Muslims** of the delta (her brother **Shah Jongoli**, her text in *dobhāṣī* Bengali). A grāma-devatā of the mangrove frontier rather than a classical maṅgal-kāvya deity, but of the same indigenous-protector type. (Bengali mythology — Wikipedia) **[folk]**
  - *Variant (syncretic):* Bonbibī is invoked as both a Hindu *debī* and an Islamic *pīr*-figure; her legend exists in Hindu and Muslim recensions side by side.

## VII. Adivasi / tribal & Himalayan cycles

The deepest stratum: tribal re-tellings of pan-Indian epic, and possession-ritual deity cults outside the Brahmanical mainstream.

- **Pandavani** - a sung **Mahābhārata** tradition of **Chhattisgarh**, performed especially by the **Pardhān** bards (hereditary genealogist-bards of the **Gond** Adivasi) and famously by **Teejan Bai**. In the tribal *Kapālik* style, epic figures are localised — **Bhīma** becomes an **Adivasi rain-deity who beats the maṇḍar drum to summon rain** — fusing the Sanskrit epic into Gond cosmology. (Pandavani — Grokipedia; *Adivasi Mahabharata Pandavani of Chhattisgarh Gond Tribe*) **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (style):* two performance schools — **Vedmatī** (seated, restrained) and **Kapālik** (standing, dramatic, more localised/improvised).
- **Pardhān Gond bardic cult** - the **Pardhāns**, itinerant bard-genealogists of the Gonds of central India, sing the genealogy and myths of the Gond clan-gods (incl. the great culture-hero **Liṅgo Pen / Pahandi Kapar Lingal**) to the **bāna/kiṅgrī** fiddle — a tribal "Veda" of clan origin entirely outside the Sanskrit canon. (IGNCA "Gond/Koitur cultural roots"; *Intangible heritage of the Pardhan Gonds*) **[folk]/[non-Vedic]**
- **Jāgar (jāgar gāthā)** - the **Himalayan possession-ritual** of **Uttarakhand** (Garhwal & Kumaon): an all-night ceremony in which a singer (**jāgariyā**) to dhol-damau drums "awakens" (*jāgar* = "to keep awake") a local deity or deified ancestor-hero — **Golū Devtā** (the justice-god on his white horse), **Bhairav**, the **Pāṇḍavas** (Pāṇḍav Līlā / Pāṇḍav Nṛtya), or **Nāgarjā** — into the body of a human medium (*ḍaṅgariyā*) to give judgement and remedy. The epic and the ritual are one event. (Jagar (ritual) — Wikipedia; *The Jaagars of Uttarakhand*) **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (Pāṇḍav Līlā):* in Garhwal the Mahābhārata itself is danced/possessed as the **Pāṇḍav Līlā**, with villagers possessed by individual Pāṇḍavas (esp. Bhīma/Nakula) and the weapon-cult of Arjuna's *gaṇḍiva*.

## VIII. The grāma-devatā layer (cross-regional)

The connective tissue under all the above: the **village guardian deities** whose cults these epics largely serve.

- **Grāma-devatā (village deity)** - the tutelary guardian of a specific village/town, protector against demons, plague, famine and calamity; typically enshrined at the **village boundary**, served by **non-Brahmin priests**, and honoured with **blood-offering** and possession — the ground-floor of lived Hindu religion. Often female (**Māriyammaṉ**, **Ellammā/Reṇukā**, **Gaṅgammā**, **Śītalā** the smallpox-goddess), often a **deified martyr-hero** (kāval deyvam). (Gramadevata — Wikipedia; *Village deities of South India* — Wikipedia) **[folk]**
  - *Theology (Sanskritisation):* a recurring process **folds** the village goddess into **Durgā/Pārvatī/Śakti** and the guardian-hero into an *aṃśa* of **Śiva/Viṣṇu** — the same hero-into-god / local-into-pan-Indian engine seen above. Catalog the local identity *and* its claimed Sanskritic equivalence as variants, never collapse one into the other. **[folk]**
  - *Cross-link:* these cults populate the sacred-geography of [`../06-sacred-geography/`](../06-sacred-geography/) at the village scale, below the great tīrthas.
