# The Dhangar god-epics — Biroba, Mhaskoba and Dhuloba

> Literature-corpus node, the **Marathi shepherd-pastoralist chapter of the oral-epic layer** — sibling of the pan-Indian survey [regional-and-oral-epics.md](regional-and-oral-epics.md) and of the master map [README.md](README.md). These are the **ovī** (sung religious narratives) of the **Dhangars**, the sheep-and-blanket pastoralists of the Maharashtra Deccan, whose tutelary gods — **Biroba** the shepherd-god, **Mhaskoba** of Vīr (Kāḷ-Bhairava as the buffalo-lord), and **Dhuloba** — form, on G.-D. Sontheimer's classic reading, the **pastoral substratum beneath the Sanskritized warrior-god [Khaṇḍobā / Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md)**. By traditional reckoning these gods are folk descents of Śiva-Bhairava, timeless or vaguely placed in [Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md); academically they are living cults, first documented in colonial ethnography and studied definitively by Sontheimer in the 1960s–70s. Everything here is **[folk]/[oral tradition]/[regional]** unless tagged otherwise.

## Orientation — the Dhangars and the ovī

- **Who the Dhangars are** — The **Dhangars** (Marathi *dhangar*, from *dhan* "livestock/wealth") are the great **pastoralist community of the Deccan** — herders of sheep and goats and weavers of the black wool blanket (*ghongaḍī*), transhumant across the Maharashtra plateau. They are subdivided into many endogamous groups (Hāṭkar, Khuṭekar, Zhāḍe, Sangar, Ahir, Khilārī and others), each carrying its own *kuḷdaivat* (clan-god) and its own body of sung myth. Their religion is a **pastoral, non-Brahmin, blood-sacrificing** stratum only secondarily touched by Sanskritic Śaivism. (Sontheimer, *Pastoral Deities in Western India*) **[scholarly]/[folk]**
- **The ovī as sung scripture** — The Dhangar **ovī** is not the short domestic grindmill-song verse of the same name but a **long sung god-epic**: the narrative of a god's birth, deeds, marriage and cult, performed through the **night (*jāgaraṇ*, vigil)** at festivals and vows, chanted by Dhangar men to the **ḍaph/frame-drum** and often culminating in **possession** of a devotee-medium by the god. Epic, genealogy and liturgy are one event, as across the [oral-epic belt](regional-and-oral-epics.md). **[oral tradition]**
- **A pastoralist counter-pantheon** — The three gods cataloged here are the Marathi peers of the Deccan's other herder-hero cycles — the Kannada cowherd-god [Juñjappa](regional-and-oral-epics.md) of the Kāḍu Golla and the Telugu cattle-king [Katamaraju](katamaraju-katha.md) — and stand beside the closely-related folk-Śaiva guardians [Jyotiba/Kedārliṅg](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/jyotiba-kedarling-kolhapur.md) of Kolhapur. Their common subject is the **flock**: the god who guards sheep against disease, wolf and thief, and who is himself imagined as, or fostered among, shepherds. **[folk]/[regional]**

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — These are **folk deities without yuga-arithmetic**. Where Brahmanical identification reaches them it equates them with **Śiva as Bhairava** (Mhaskoba = **Kāḷ-Bhairava / Kāla-Bhairava**; Khaṇḍobā = **Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava**), a timeless form; folk memory otherwise places their deeds "long ago," by default deep in Kali Yuga (which began **3102 BCE**). Biroba and Dhuloba resist any stable *avatāra*-equation — their divinity rests on the pastoral hero/ghost logic itself, not on a Purāṇic descent. **[folk]**
- **Academic / historical frame** — **[scholarly]** The cults are **living oral traditions of undatable medieval-to-early-modern formation**, kin to the wider Deccan **Mailāra–Mallāri–Khaṇḍobā complex** that surfaces epigraphically and in the *Mallāri Māhātmya* in the later medieval period. They entered the record in **colonial-era ethnography** and were studied systematically only in the 20th century — above all by **Günther-Dietz Sontheimer (1934–1992)**, whose Habilitation *Biroba, Mhaskoba und Khandoba* (Wiesbaden, **1976**; English as *Pastoral Deities in Western India*, trans. Anne Feldhaus, OUP **1989**) rests on extensive fieldwork among Dhangar singers and shrines in the **1960s–70s**. **[scholarly]**

## I. Biroba — the shepherd-god

- **The god and his names** — **Birobā** (also **Bīrā, Bīrāppā, Vīrappā** in the Kannada-influenced south; the suffix *-obā* is the affectionate honorific also seen in Viṭhobā, Khaṇḍobā) is the **chief tutelary god of many Dhangar clans**, the shepherd-god par excellence. His great cult-centre is **Ārevāḍī (Arewadi)** in Sāngli district; his shrines dot the Kṛṣṇā–Bhīmā pasture-country of western Maharashtra and the Kannada border. (Biroba ovī; Sontheimer, *Pastoral Deities*) **[folk]/[regional]**
- **Biroba and the goddess Māyavā** — The Biroba ovī binds him inseparably to the goddess **Māyavā (Māyakkā / Mayamma)**, his divine mother and the female pole of the Dhangar cult; the god is born of, or given by, her, and mother and son are worshipped together. The narrative turns on a **miraculous birth** and the god's **fosterage in a Dhangar shepherd household**, from which he emerges to herd sheep, work wonders over the flock, and establish his worship. (Biroba ovī, as transcribed by Sontheimer) **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (singer-dependent):* the details of the birth — the foster-parents' names, the manner of the miraculous conception, the god's boyhood ordeals — **differ irreconcilably between recensions and singers**; the ovī has no fixed text, and each performer owns his version. Traditions are cataloged, not merged. **[oral tradition]/[disputed]**
- **Weak Sanskritic grafting** — Unlike Khaṇḍobā (firmly Bhairava) or Mhaskoba (firmly Kāḷ-Bhairava), Biroba carries **no standardized *avatāra*-equation**; where he is glossed as a form of Śiva/Bhairava the identification is local, late and inconsistent. Sontheimer treats him as one of the **"purest" surviving pastoral gods**, still owned overwhelmingly by shepherds rather than absorbed into the cross-caste settled pantheon. **[scholarly]/[folk]**

## II. Mhaskoba of Vīr — Kāḷ-Bhairava, the buffalo-lord

- **The name — the buffalo-god** — **Mhaskobā** (also **Mhaṅkobā**; from Marathi *mhasā* "buffalo" + the *-obā* honorific) means, literally, the **"buffalo-lord."** He belongs to the wide Deccan family of **Mhasobā** — the ubiquitous aniconic field-and-boundary godling, a red-lead-smeared stone guardian of the fields, at once demon and protector. At **Vīr** the local Mhasobā is raised into a full temple-god and **identified with Kāḷ-Bhairava (Kāla-Bhairava)**, the fierce skull-bearing form of Śiva. (Sontheimer, "Between Ghost and God"; *Pastoral Deities*) **[folk]/[scholarly]**
- **The god of Vīr** — The pre-eminent Mhaskoba shrine is at **Vīr** (Purandar taluka, Pune district), whose annual **yātrā** draws Dhangar and wider Deccan pilgrims. Mhaskoba of Vīr is a fierce, **blood-demanding *kṣetrapāla*** — historically propitiated with **buffalo/goat sacrifice**, red lead (*śendūr*) and the vow-fulfilment of possessed devotees; his consort is the goddess **Jogāī / Jogeśvarī**. **[folk]/[regional]**
- **The migration from Sonāri, and the Dhangar devotee Kāmājī** — The cult's charter-myth is a **migration story**: the god (Kāḷ-Bhairava) originally resided at **Sonāri** in the Bhīmā valley and **followed his Dhangar devotee — remembered as Kāmājī — to Vīr**, where his power took root. Sontheimer reads the Vīr cult as a textbook case of the Deccan **"ghost-to-god" process**: a fierce spirit (*bhūt*) attached to a devotee is progressively **deified into a temple-god and Sanskritized as Bhairava**, the raw pastoral-demonic layer never fully effaced. (Sontheimer, "Between Ghost and God: A Folk Deity of the Deccan") **[folk]/[scholarly]**
  - *Variant:* the precise identity of the devotee, the route of the migration, and the god's original nature vary between the Vīr temple tradition and the sung ovī; the corpus records the disagreement rather than resolving it. **[oral tradition]/[disputed]**

## III. Dhuloba — the Dhangar Bhairava of the Vīr–Phalṭaṇ country

- **The god and his name** — **Dhūlobā** (glossed from *dhūḷ*, "dust" — the dust of the flock and the field) is a **Dhangar god of the Vīr–Phalṭaṇ (Phaltan) tract** of the western Deccan, cognate in type with Mhaskoba: a fierce, blood-taking **Bhairava-form guardian** owned by the shepherd community, propitiated at his shrine with sacrifice and possession, and celebrated in his own sung **ovī**. (Dhuloba ovī; Sontheimer, *Pastoral Deities*) **[folk]/[regional]**
- **The Dhuloba ovī** — Like Biroba's, the Dhuloba cycle narrates the god's **birth, deeds, marriage and the founding of his cult**, sung by Dhangar performers through the night vigil. Sontheimer gave the Dhuloba ovī sustained attention as one of the fullest recorded Dhangar god-epics, a window onto the **pastoral religious world** — its transhumance, its sheep-lore, its social charter — encoded as sacred narrative. **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (recensional multiformity):* names, episodes and the god's consort shift between singers and localities; no canonical Dhuloba text exists, and the multiformity is constitutive of the tradition. **[oral tradition]/[disputed]**
- **Twin of Mhaskoba** — In the religion of the Vīr country Dhuloba and Mhaskoba stand as a **paired fierce-god complex** — both Bhairava-type, both blood-sacrificing, both bound to the Dhangar community — so that the two ovī cycles illuminate one another and share a common grammar of the pastoral fierce god. **[folk]/[regional]**

## IV. Sontheimer's thesis — the pastoral substrate beneath Khaṇḍobā

- **The core argument** — Sontheimer's abiding claim is that the widely-worshipped, cross-caste, Sanskritized warrior-god **[Khaṇḍobā (Mārtāṇḍa Bhairava / Mailāra / Mallanna)](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md)** sits atop an **older, still-living pastoral stratum** — a layer of Dhangar shepherd-gods (Biroba, Mhaskoba, Dhuloba and their goddess Māyavā) whose worship remains predominantly pastoral, blood-sacrificing and non-Brahmin. Khaṇḍobā represents that stratum **caught in the act of Brahmanization**; Biroba and Dhuloba show what it looks like largely **before** that absorption. (Sontheimer, *Biroba, Mhaskoba und Khandoba*, 1976 / *Pastoral Deities*, 1989) **[scholarly]**
- **The Dhangar hooks into the Khaṇḍobā cult** — The thesis is anchored in the cult's own structure: Khaṇḍobā's second wife **[Bāṇāī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/khandoba-martanda-bhairava.md)** is a **Dhangar shepherdess**, won by the god in pastoral guise (herding sheep to win her), and the Mhāḷsā–Bāṇāī two-wife pattern encodes the **union of the settled-agrarian and the wild-pastoral** under one deity — the sung *ovī* of Bāṇāī being a Dhangar possession. The pastoral layer is thus written into the "high" god's own biography. **[scholarly]/[folk]**
- **The disputed Viṭhobā extension** — Sontheimer pressed the argument further and controversially, reading even **Viṭṭhal / Viṭhobā of Paṇḍharpūr** — the god who stands arms-akimbo on a brick — as carrying a **pastoral / hero-stone (*vīragaḷ*) substratum** beneath his later Vaiṣṇava identity, a cowherd-and-cattle god of the same Deccan pastoral world. This reading is influential but **contested**; the corpus records it as Sontheimer's, not as settled. **[scholarly]/[disputed]**
- **Ghost, hero-stone, god** — Across all three cycles Sontheimer isolates a single **deification engine**: a fierce spirit or a fallen pastoral hero (often first a *bhūt* or a memorial *vīragaḷ*) is bound to a devotee and a place, propitiated through **possession and blood-sacrifice**, and over time **raised to a temple-god and equated with Bhairava** — the pastoral-demonic origin surviving under the Sanskritic veneer. This "between ghost and god" process is his master-key to the Deccan folk pantheon. **[scholarly]**

## V. Shared grammar of the Dhangar god-epics

- **Community-owned scripture** — Each ovī is the possession of the Dhangar community that sings it; none has a fixed text, and **no two performances are the same** — the multiformity is constitutive, as across the whole [oral-epic layer](regional-and-oral-epics.md). **[oral tradition]**
- **Possession as liturgy** — The night-long ovī is **rite, not entertainment**: the god descends into a devotee-medium at the vigil to heal, judge and protect the flock and the family — epic, priesthood and shrine in a single institution, exactly as in the Maithili [gahwar](maithili-oral-epics-salhesh-dina-bhadri.md) and Telugu [Ganga Jātara](katamaraju-katha.md) cults. **[folk]**
- **The god of the flock** — The recurring charter-value is **the protection of sheep and shepherds**: the god who guards against murrain, wolf and thief, who is fostered among or married into the Dhangar world, and whose cult sanctifies the pastoralists' actual economy and dignity beneath the settled, Brahmin-curated order. **[scholarly]/[folk]**
- **Blood, red lead, and the fierce god** — Mhaskoba and Dhuloba embody the Deccan's **fierce blood-taking guardian** — the buffalo-lord, the red-lead stone, the *kṣetrapāla* — a type only thinly reconciled with Sanskritic Śaivism, where Biroba shows the same stratum in a milder, more purely pastoral key. **[folk]/[regional]**
