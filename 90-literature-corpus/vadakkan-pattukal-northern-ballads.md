# Vadakkan Pattukal — the Northern Ballads of Malabar

> Parent: [Literature Corpus](README.md). Literature-corpus node, the **Malayalam chapter of the oral-epic layer** — the martial hero-song tradition of **North Malabar** (Kannur–Kozhikode–Vatakara country, northern Kerala), sibling of the pan-Indian survey [regional-and-oral-epics.md](regional-and-oral-epics.md), of the Maithili gāthās [salhesh & dinā-bhadrī](maithili-oral-epics-salhesh-dina-bhadri.md), and of the Tamil hero-katai world [tamil-canon](tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md). These are the songs of the **kaḷari** (the fencing-schools of Kerala's martial art *kaḷarippayattu*) and of the **aṅkam** duel — sung over the deified champions whose theyyam-cousin, the martyr-god [Kathivanur Veeran](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/kathivanur-veeran.md), the corpus already carries. By traditional reckoning their heroes belong to the deep human past of Kali Yuga; academically they are North-Malabar warrior-heroes of **[scholarly] c. 16th–17th century CE**, sung in Northern Malayalam and first put into print in the colonial era. Everything here is **[folk] / [oral tradition] / [regional]** unless tagged otherwise.

---

## Orientation: what the "Northern Songs" are

- **Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ = "the Northern Songs"** — a body of Malayalam ballads (*pāṭṭu*) native to **North Malabar**, celebrating warriors, warrior-women and blood-feuds of the region's martial castes. The name is geographic and contrastive: it is set against the **Tekkan Pāṭṭukaḷ** ("Southern Songs") of Travancore–Venad (the Iravikkutti Piḷḷa Pōr and kindred lays), a separate southern hero-song stratum. This file treats only the Northern corpus. **[regional]**
- **Two clocks** — *Traditional:* folk memory presents the heroes as champions "of old," with no yuga-arithmetic attached — Kali-Yuga hero-lore, not śāstric chronology; their divinity is **hero-worship** (*vīra-ārādhana*), and their tutelary powers are the Purāṇic goddess of [Lokanārkāvu](#the-thacholi-cycle-othenan-of-thacholi-manikkoth). *Academic:* scholarship places the heroes in **[scholarly] c. 16th–17th-century North Malabar** — the presence of the **matchlock gun** in the Othenan story fits the post-Portuguese firearm era — while the songs themselves are living oral compositions, systematically collected and edited only from the late 19th–20th centuries. **[scholarly]**
- **A working people's epic** — the ballads were sung by ordinary Malabar people at labour, at harvest, at weddings and at *kaḷari* gatherings, in a rhythmic Northern-Malayalam meter; they are the **community's own chronicle** of its martial past, owned not by Brahmin curators but by the Tīyya/Cēkava and Nāyar houses whose ancestors they name. **[folk]**
- **The aṅkam institution** — the ballads' central engine is the **aṅkam** (aṅkam-pōr): a **judicial duel** by which the *nāṭuvāḻi* chieftains of North Malabar settled disputes over honour, boundary or right. Neither lord fought in person; each **hired a professional champion** — an *aṅkakkāran* — from the martial **Cēkavar** community, and the two hired swordsmen fought to the death on a raised platform, the **aṅkattaṭṭu**, before assembled crowds. To die in another man's aṅkam was the Cēkavar's trade and glory; the songs are the memory of those deaths. **[scholarly] [folk]**
- **The two great cycles** — the corpus organises around two family sagas: the **Puttūram Pāṭṭu** (the Cēkava house of Puttūram, whose champions are Ārōmal Cēkavar and his sister Uṇṇiyārcca) and the **Tacōḷi Pāṭṭu** (the Nāyar house of Tacōḷi Maṇikkōth, whose hero is Tacōḷi Ōtēnan). Around them cluster subordinate lays of Payyampaḷḷi Cāntu, Kuññirāman and others. **[folk] [oral tradition]**

## The Puthooram cycle — the house of the Cēkavars

- **The Puttūram Vīṭu** — the songs' first family is the **Puttūram** house of **Cēkavars** (the Tīyya martial lineage of the *kaḷari*, the same warrior-milieu that produced [Kathivanur Veeran](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/kathivanur-veeran.md)), of the Kaṭattanāṭu country. Its head is the old master **Kaṇṇappa Cēkavar** (Kannappan), and from his house come the two most famous figures of all the Northern Songs: **Ārōmal Cēkavar** and his sister **Uṇṇiyārcca**. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, Puttūram cycle) **[folk] [oral tradition]**
- **Ārōmal Cēkavar, the peerless champion** — Ārōmal is the supreme *aṅkakkāran* of the ballads, a Cēkava swordsman of flawless *kaḷari* skill, sought by chieftains to fight their duels. His saga is the **aṅkam of Ārōmal** — the fight he wins and the treachery by which he dies. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, *Ārōmal Cēkavar pāṭṭu*) **[oral tradition]**
- **Cāntu, the kinsman-traitor** — **Cāntu (Chandu) Cēkavar** is Ārōmal and Uṇṇiyārcca's **cousin, raised in the Puttūram house** — and, in the received tradition, the great villain of the Northern Songs. His stated motive is thwarted desire: he loved **Uṇṇiyārcca** and was refused, she being wedded instead to **Kuññirāman of Attummanammēl**; his jealousy of the family's glory curdles into betrayal. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, Puttūram cycle) **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (the modern sympathetic Cāntu):* the popular 20th-century retelling — most influentially M. T. Vāsudēvan Nāyar's screenplay *Oru Vaṭakkan Vīragātha* (1989) — **rehabilitates Cāntu**, arguing the ballads slander him and telling the aṅkam from his side. This is an authored modern reinterpretation, not a sung recension; the traditional lays keep him the traitor. **[late text] [disputed]**
- **The aṅkam against Aṟiṅṅōṭar** — Ārōmal is hired to fight the aṅkam on behalf of one *nāṭuvāḻi* against the champion of a rival: the veteran master **Aṟiṅṅōṭar (Aringodan)**, himself a renowned swordsman. Cāntu goes with Ārōmal as his kinsman and second. On the aṅkattaṭṭu Ārōmal fights and **kills Aṟiṅṅōṭar** — but victory is the moment of his undoing. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, *Ārōmal Cēkavar pāṭṭu*) **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (the broken sword):* in widely-sung tellings **Ārōmal's sword snaps** in the duel and he calls for another blade; singers differ on whether Cāntu had deliberately supplied a **flawed weapon**, and on whether Cāntu was **bribed by the enemy side** — the sabotage is present in some recensions and merely jealousy in others. The traditions are cataloged, not harmonised. **[oral tradition] [disputed]**
- **The betrayal and death** — the wounded, exhausted victor is **treacherously stabbed by Cāntu** — the kinsman striking the man he came to guard. Dying, Ārōmal sends Cāntu to fetch his people; instead Cāntu, in the ballad, **tries to strip the dying hero of his gold ring / arms** and carries home a lying account. The treachery of Cāntu is the emotional pivot of the whole Puttūram cycle. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, *Ārōmal Cēkavar pāṭṭu*) **[oral tradition]**
- **Uṇṇiyārcca, the warrior-sister** — Ārōmal's sister **Uṇṇiyārcca** is the most celebrated **woman warrior** of Malayalam tradition: trained in the *kaḷari* like her brother, mistress of the *uṟumi* (the flexible whip-sword) and of the war-dances, fierce and unafraid. She is wife of **Kuññirāman of Attummanammēl**. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, *Uṇṇiyārcca pāṭṭu*) **[oral tradition]**
- **Uṇṇiyārcca at Nāṭāpuram** — her signature lay: journeying with her husband to the festival at the **Aḷḷimalarkāvu** temple, Uṇṇiyārcca is blocked at **Nāṭāpuram** by a gang of **Jōnaka (Māppiḷa) rowdies** who bar the road and menace her; when her husband hesitates, **she herself routs them**, cutting her way through — the archetypal image of the Malabar woman who fights her own battle. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, *Uṇṇiyārcca pāṭṭu*) **[oral tradition] [regional]**
- **Ārōmaluṇṇi and the revenge** — the cycle closes on vengeance: **Ārōmaluṇṇi**, Ārōmal's posthumous son, is reared and drilled in the *kaḷari* by the Puttūram elders, grows into a champion in his father's mould, and at last **hunts down and kills Cāntu**, redeeming the family's blood-debt. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, *Ārōmaluṇṇi pāṭṭu*) **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (who avenges):* singers differ on the avenger's exact place in the house — Ārōmal's own son in the common telling, but in other recensions a **nephew** or the son of Uṇṇiyārcca and Kuññirāman; the revenge is constant, the genealogy shuffles. **[oral tradition] [disputed]**

## The Thacholi cycle — Othenan of Thacholi Manikkoth

- **Tacōḷi Ōtēnan** — the hero of the second cycle is **Tacōḷi Mēppayil Kuññi Ōtēnan**, scion of the **Tacōḷi Maṇikkōth** house (a **Nāyar** martial *taṟavāṭu* of the Vaṭakara / Kaṭattanāṭu country, Kozhikode district). Unlike the hired-champion Cēkavars of Puttūram, Ōtēnan is a **chieftainly Nāyar warrior** — a *kaḷari* master, adventurer, duellist and lover — around whom the largest single body of Northern Songs gathers. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, Tacōḷi cycle) **[folk] [oral tradition]**
- **Devotee of Lōkanārkāvu Bhagavatī** — Ōtēnan's tutelary deity is **Lōkanārkāvu Bhagavatī**, the goddess (a Durgā / Bhadrakāḷī form) of the **Lōkanārkāvu temple** near Vaṭakara — the traditional patron-shrine of the Tacōḷi and Puttūram fighting houses. The ballads have Ōtēnan **worship at Lōkanārkāvu before his combats**, his valour underwritten by the Devī; the temple's association with the *kaḷari* warriors of North Malabar is one of the cycle's fixed points. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, Tacōḷi cycle; Lōkanārkāvu tradition) **[folk] [regional]**
  - This is where the folk hero-song touches the Purāṇic frame: the martyr-champion's power flows from the **Great Goddess**, tying the North-Malabar hero-cult into the [Bhadrakāḷī / Bhagavatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/bhadrakali-kerala-bhagavati.md) complex of Kerala. **[scholarly]**
- **The exploits of Ōtēnan** — his cycle is a string of *kaḷari* adventures — duels fought and won, feuds, and the love-intrigues for which the hero is as famous as for his sword; his kinsman-companions and rival champions (among them **Payyampaḷḷi Cāntu**, hero of his own subordinate lay) recur across the songs. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, Tacōḷi cycle) **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (the kinsmen):* the names and roles of Ōtēnan's brother, nephew and companions (a Tacōḷi Ampu, a Tacōḷi Kuññikēḷu and others) shift between singers and lays; the Tacōḷi house is a **cluster of hero-songs**, not a single fixed genealogy. **[oral tradition] [disputed]**
- **Death by the gun** — the cycle's defining tragedy: **invincible with the sword, Ōtēnan is felled only by a firearm.** In the received tradition he is **shot dead with a matchlock**, killed young by treachery because no swordsman could beat him in fair *kaḷari* combat — the ballad's bitter comment that the gun ends the age of the sword. (Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ, Tacōḷi cycle) **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (the assassin):* singers name the gunman variously (a Māppiḷa marksman in common tellings); the constant is the **manner** — the sword-hero brought down by a bullet, not a blade. The detail is also the cycle's best dating-clue, placing Ōtēnan in the **post-16th-century firearm era**. **[oral tradition] [scholarly]**

## Shrine, theyyam and the deified hero

- **Vīra-ārādhana in North Malabar** — the Northern Songs belong to the same soil as the region's **theyyam** hero-cults, whose signature act is the **deification of a dead martial hero** (*vīra-ārādhana*): the champion who dies in valour or by treachery is worshipped at village shrines and embodied by possessed dancers. The ballad heroes are venerated in this idiom — remembered at family shrines and *kaḷari* precincts, and honoured (locally and to varying degrees) as **theyyam** or hero-figures alongside cousins like [Kathivanur Veeran](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/kathivanur-veeran.md), [Pottan Theyyam](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/pottan-theyyam.md) and [Muchilottu Bhagavathy](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/muchilottu-bhagavathy.md). (K. K. N. Kurup, *The Cult of Teyyam and Hero Worship in Kerala*; Vaṭakkan Pāṭṭukaḷ) **[folk] [scholarly] [regional]**
- **The kaḷari as living context** — the songs are inseparable from **kaḷarippayattu**: they name its schools, its masters, its weapons (the long sword-and-shield, the *uṟumi* whip-sword, the dagger), and its ethic of the hired duel. The corpus is, among other things, the **oral scripture of the Malabar martial tradition**. **[folk] [regional]**

## The corpus in scholarship

- **Oral composition, colonial collection** — the ballads had no fixed text; each was sung and re-sung, multiform by nature, until collectors began transcribing them. **[oral tradition]**
- **C. Achyuta Menon, *Ballads of North Malabar* (1935)** — the landmark scholarly edition: **C. Achyuta Menon** collected, edited and published the Northern Songs as *Ballads of North Malabar* (University of Madras, 1935, in two volumes), the standard critical gathering that made the corpus available for study and fixed much of its received shape. **[scholarly]**
- **Afterlife in modern Malayalam culture** — the ballads became a wellspring of modern Kerala's popular imagination: Uṇṇiyārcca is the paradigm woman warrior, Ārōmal and Ōtēnan household names, and the aṅkam of Ārōmal reached a mass audience through **M. T. Vāsudēvan Nāyar's *Oru Vaṭakkan Vīragātha* (1989)** — whose sympathetic re-reading of Cāntu is a deliberate modern counter-song to the traditional lay. **[late text] [scholarly]**
