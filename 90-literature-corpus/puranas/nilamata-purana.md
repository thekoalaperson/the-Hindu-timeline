# Nīlamata Purāṇa — The Charter Text of Kashmiri Hinduism

> Hierarchy location: `90-literature-corpus/puranas/` — a per-text source map in the literature corpus, for the regional purāṇa of Kashmir (outside the 18+18 canon; see [`upapuranas-overview.md`](upapuranas-overview.md)).
> Up-link: [`README.md`](README.md) (the Purāṇas index) · [`../README.md`](../README.md) (the literature corpus).
> Canonical home of the **Satīsaras / Jalodbhava draining myth**: [`../../04-deep-dives/kashyapa.md`](../../04-deep-dives/kashyapa.md) — this file maps the *text*; that one owns the story.

The **Nīlamata Purāṇa** (*Nīlamata*, "the doctrine/opinion of Nīla"; also styled *Kaśmīra-māhātmya*) is the foundational religious text of **Kashmiri Hinduism** — part origin-myth, part sacred gazetteer, part liturgical calendar. Framed as **Vaiśampāyana's answer to king Janamejaya** (the same narrator-listener pair as the Mahābhārata), it records the **ordinances (dharmas) of Nīla**, king of the Kashmir nāgas, taught to the brahmin **Candradeva**: the complete round of festivals, rites and pilgrimages by which humans may lawfully dwell in the valley. Around that core it carries Kashmir's cosmogony (the lake **Satīsaras** and the demon **Jalodbhava**), the divinization of the land (Kashmir as the goddess **Umā**; the river **Vitastā** as Pārvatī), a roster of sacred **nāga-springs**, and — remarkably early for a brahmanical text — an ordinance for celebrating **the Buddha's birthday with image-bathing**, the Buddha being counted an avatāra of Viṣṇu. Kalhaṇa opens the **Rājataraṅgiṇī** (1148–49 CE) by naming the Nīlamata among his sources, making it the mythic porch of Kashmir's historical chronicle. [regional]

## The text — identity, structure, and the two clocks

- **A regional purāṇa outside the canon** — the Nīlamata appears in **no standard list** of the 18 Mahāpurāṇas or 18 Upapurāṇas (for those rosters see [`README.md`](README.md), [`../smriti-puranas.md`](../smriti-puranas.md), [`upapuranas-overview.md`](upapuranas-overview.md)); it is generically a **māhātmya-cum-dharma-charter** for a single land, the same sect/region-charter genre as many Upapurāṇas, and Kashmiri tradition treats it with purāṇic authority. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; Ghai, *The Nīlamata Purāṇa*) [regional] [scholarly]
- **Form and size** — a single continuous dialogue, **Janamejaya questioning Vaiśampāyana**, of roughly **1,400–1,450 extant ślokas** (Ghai's edition prints c. 1,453 verses); the transmitted text is incomplete and composite, with lacunae and likely accretions. (de Vreese ed. 1936; Ghai ed. 1968–73) [scholarly]
- **Nested frames** — Vaiśampāyana's answer itself encloses older dialogues: the sage **Bṛhadaśva** instructing king **Gonanda** on the valley's origins, and innermost, **Nīla-nāga** declaring the ordinances to the brahmin **Candradeva** — the *nīla-mata* proper. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, frame narrative)
- **Sectarian character** — broadly **Vaiṣṇava-leaning** (Viṣṇu slays Jalodbhava; the avatāra list includes the Buddha) but saturated with **Śaiva, Śākta and nāga-cult** material — Kashmir itself is Umā, the Vitastā is Pārvatī, and the real ritual substrate is the worship of serpent-spirits of the waters. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; Ghai) [scholarly]
- **Scriptural clock** — the frame sits in **early Kali Yuga**: Janamejaya, great-grandson of Arjuna, asks his question in the generation after the Bhārata war (the same king whose snake-sacrifice is mapped in [`../../04-deep-dives/astika-sarpa-satra.md`](../../04-deep-dives/astika-sarpa-satra.md)); the lake-cosmogony it reports reaches back to the **beginning of the kalpa** (see below).
- **Academic clock [scholarly]** — composed in Kashmir **c. 6th–8th century CE**: Bühler (1877), who first brought the text to European notice, and **Ved Kumari Ghai**, its principal modern editor-translator, argue for the **6th–7th century**, with some scholars allowing the 8th; the firm *terminus ante quem* is **Kalhaṇa's use of it in 1148–49 CE**. Editions: **K. de Vreese** (Leiden, 1936); **Ved Kumari Ghai**, 2 vols. with English translation (1968, 1973).

## The frame — why no Kashmir king fought at Kurukṣetra

- **Janamejaya's question** — the opening problem: the Bhārata war drew kings of every land, yet **no king of Kashmir is named at Kurukṣetra**. Janamejaya asks Vaiśampāyana why the ruler of so eminent a country stood apart. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, opening; cf. Rājataraṅgiṇī 1) — the war itself: [`../../04-deep-dives/mahabharata/README.md`](../../04-deep-dives/mahabharata/README.md).
- **Gonanda I falls to Balarāma** — Vaiśampāyana answers: the Kashmir king **Gonanda I**, kinsman-ally of **Jarāsandha**, joined the siege of Kṛṣṇa's **Mathurā** and was slain in battle by **Balarāma**. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, opening; Rājataraṅgiṇī 1.57–60) [regional]
- **Dāmodara falls to Kṛṣṇa** — his son **Dāmodara I**, burning for revenge, attacked Kṛṣṇa's party at a **svayaṃvara in the Gāndhāra country** and was killed by Kṛṣṇa's discus. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, opening; Rājataraṅgiṇī 1.62–66) [regional]
- **Kṛṣṇa crowns the widow — because the land is the Goddess** — instead of annexing the kingdom, **Kṛṣṇa had Dāmodara's pregnant queen Yaśovatī consecrated** to the throne, silencing objectors on the ground that **Kashmir is Pārvatī herself and its king partakes of Śiva** — the land's divinity outranks politics. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; Rājataraṅgiṇī 1.67–72, with the maxim "Kaśmīrā is Pārvatī; its king a portion of Śiva" at 1.42) [regional]
- **The infant king — hence the absence** — her son **Gonanda II** was a **child on the throne** when the Kuru–Pāṇḍava war came; too young to be courted by either side, he was summoned by neither. Thus Kashmir, uniquely, sat out Kurukṣetra. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, opening; Rājataraṅgiṇī 1.82) [regional]
- **Function of the frame [scholarly]** — the device stitches a frontier valley into the pan-Indian epic universe while explaining an embarrassing epic silence — a classic regional-charter move, legitimizing Kashmir's kings as an ancient line running parallel to (not subordinate to) the epic dynasties.

## Cosmogony — Satīsaras, Jalodbhava, and the making of the valley

*Summary only — the canonical treatment of the draining myth is [`../../04-deep-dives/kashyapa.md`](../../04-deep-dives/kashyapa.md).*

- **The lake of Satī, six manvantaras long** — from the beginning of the kalpa the valley was the mountain-lake **Satīsaras**, "the lake of Satī (Umā)"; the Nīlamata has it remain water **through the first six manvantaras**, becoming habitable land only in the present **Vaivasvata Manvantara**. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, cosmography) [regional]
- **Jalodbhava and the draining at Varāhamūla** — the water-demon **Jalodbhava**, invincible within his element, is destroyed when **Kaśyapa's** austerities bring the gods: the barrier at **Varāhamūla (Baramulla)** is breached, the waters run out, and **Viṣṇu** slays the demon on drained ground; the land is **Kaśmīra, "of Kaśyapa."** (Nīlamata Purāṇa; Rājataraṅgiṇī 1.25–27) [regional] — full variants, including Ananta's plough-work, at [`../../04-deep-dives/kashyapa.md`](../../04-deep-dives/kashyapa.md).
- **First peoples: nāgas and piśācas** — the emptied valley is assigned to the **nāgas under Nīla** (Kaśyapa's serpent-progeny via Kadru — see [`../../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md`](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md)) together with the **piśācas**; the Nīlamata is the key source making piśācas aboriginal to Kashmir (see [`../../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md`](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md)). (Nīlamata Purāṇa) [regional]
- **The half-year land** — under the piśāca king **Nikumbha**, whose hordes occupied the valley for the **six cold months** each year (campaigning away in the sand-ocean the other six), humans could dwell in Kashmir **only in summer**, evacuating before winter or perishing. (Nīlamata Purāṇa) [regional]
- **The Vitastā brought down** — at Kaśyapa's entreaty to purify the piśāca-defiled land, **Śiva** causes **Pārvatī to flow forth as the river Vitastā (Jhelum)**, emerging through a channel opened with his trident **one *vitasti* (span) deep — hence her name**; vexed by pollution she vanishes and must be coaxed to re-emerge, and other goddesses accompany her as the valley's rivers (Lakṣmī as the **Viśokā**, in the text's pairings). (Nīlamata Purāṇa) [regional] — the Vitastā among the rivers of the Sapta-Sindhu: [`../../06-sacred-geography/sapta-sindhu-and-the-indus-rivers.md`](../../06-sacred-geography/sapta-sindhu-and-the-indus-rivers.md).

## Nīla's ordinances — Candradeva and the charter of year-round life

- **Candradeva winters among the piśācas** — the aged brahmin **Candradeva**, of Kaśyapa's own line, stays behind one winter, witnesses the piśāca horrors, and takes refuge in austerity at the abode of **Nīla-nāga**; the serpent-king shelters him. (Nīlamata Purāṇa) [regional]
- **The nīla-mata proper: the ordinances declared** — Nīla teaches Candradeva the **dharmas of Kashmir**: the full cycle of rites, festivals, and observances the valley's people must keep. His pledge: where these ordinances are observed, **the piśāca terror ends and humans may dwell the whole year** — ritual as the literal terms of settlement. This body of teaching is the "**doctrine of Nīla**" that names the text. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, ordinance section) [regional]
- **A covenant between species** — the ordinances bind humans to honor the **nāgas of the springs** and even to remember the piśāca past (rites of the snow-months retain a propitiatory, apotropaic cast); Kashmiri religion is framed from the start as a **treaty among humans, serpents and spirits**, brokered by a brahmin and a nāga-king. (Nīlamata Purāṇa) [regional] [scholarly]

## The nāga and spring roster — sacred waters of the valley

- **Springs as nāgas** — the Nīlamata catalogs the valley's sacred waters as **named nāgas by the hundred**, each spring or lake the seat of a serpent-deity — the textual root of the living Kashmiri usage in which springs are still called **"nāg."** (Nīlamata Purāṇa, nāga-roster) [regional]
- **Nīla's own seat — the Nīlakuṇḍa (Verinag)** — Nīla-nāga dwells in the deep blue spring at the valley's head (**Verinag**), revered as the **source of the Vitastā**. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; living Kashmiri tradition) [regional]
- **The great serpent-waters** — among the roster's most enduring identifications in Kashmiri tradition: **Ananta-nāga** (the springs of Anantnāg town), **Mahāpadma-nāga** in the great lake **Mahāpadmasaras (Wular)**, **Takṣaka-nāga** at Jayavana (Zewan), and **Śeṣa's** high lake **Śeṣanāga (Sheshnag)** on the Amarnāth route. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; Kashmiri tradition) [regional]
- **Amareśvara / Amarnāth** — the Nīlamata is part of the earliest textual footprint of pilgrimage to **Amareśvara**, the ice-liṅga cave — see [`../../06-sacred-geography/amarnath-ice-lingam-and-amar-katha.md`](../../06-sacred-geography/amarnath-ice-lingam-and-amar-katha.md). (Nīlamata Purāṇa; Rājataraṅgiṇī) [regional]
- **Goddess-tīrthas of the valley** — the Nīlamata's sacred map underlies the later goddess-geography of Kashmir — **Kṣīr Bhavānī (Rājñā devī) at Tulmul** and **Śāradā** of the upper Kishangangā among its most famous heirs; for the tīrtha-network see the sacred-geography section [`../../06-sacred-geography/README.md`](../../06-sacred-geography/README.md). [regional]

## The festival catalog — including the Buddha's birthday

- **A complete liturgical year** — the heart of Nīla's teaching is a **long calendar of festivals and vratas** (several dozen), fixed by tithi through the twelve months: sowing and harvest rites, snow rites, river worship, deity-days, and communal feasts — effectively the oldest surviving portrait of Kashmiri popular religion. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, festival ordinances) [regional] — pan-Indian festival frame: [`../../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md`](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md).
- **The first-snowfall festival** — a feast at the **first fall of snow** (*nava-hima-pāta*), with enjoyment of the new snow and honoring of kin — a purely Himalayan ordinance with no pan-Indian counterpart. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, festival ordinances) [regional]
- **The Vitastā's birthday** — worship of the **Vitastā on the day of her manifestation** (Bhādrapada bright 13th in living usage), kept by Kashmiri Pandits as **Vyeth Truvah**. (Nīlamata Purāṇa; living Kashmiri Pandit practice) [regional] [folk]
- **The Buddha's birthday — an early Buddha-image ordinance** — the catalog directs that on the **Buddha's birthday** (in Vaiśākha) the **image of the Buddha is to be bathed**, the shrine whitewashed and adorned, and **śramaṇas honored with food and gifts** — the Buddha being reckoned an **incarnation of Viṣṇu**. This is among the **earliest brahmanical prescriptions of Buddha-worship and of the Buddha-avatāra doctrine** (6th–8th c. CE), and a snapshot of Hindu–Buddhist coexistence in early Kashmir. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, festival ordinances, vv. c. 684 ff. in Ghai's ed.) [regional] [scholarly] — the avatāra question in full: [`../../04-deep-dives/buddha-avatara.md`](../../04-deep-dives/buddha-avatara.md).
- **Śivarātri — and the living Herath** — Śiva's night in Phālguna stands in the ordinance cycle; its Kashmiri Pandit descendant is **Herath** (< *Hararātri*), kept on the **trayodaśī** (a day before the pan-Indian caturdaśī) with the **vaṭuk** rite — water-vessels installed as **Vaṭuka Bhairava**, walnut offerings, and days of household liturgy. The trayodaśī timing and vaṭuk specifics are the living regional form rather than the letter of the text. (Nīlamata Purāṇa, festival ordinances; living Kashmiri Pandit practice) [regional] [folk] — the pan-Indian vigil myth: [`../../04-deep-dives/shivaratri-hunter-legend.md`](../../04-deep-dives/shivaratri-hunter-legend.md).
- **Winter spirit-rites** — the dark-half observances of the snow months (e.g. the Pauṣa new-moon food-offerings kept by Pandits as **Khetsimāvas**, rice-and-pulse left for the yakṣa) are traced by the tradition to the Nīlamata's ordinances for propitiating the valley's spirit-populations. (living Kashmiri Pandit practice, referred to the Nīlamata) [folk] [regional]

## Kalhaṇa and the afterlife of the text

- **Named source of the Rājataraṅgiṇī** — **Kalhaṇa** (writing **1148–49 CE**) lists the **Nīlamata Purāṇa** among the works he inspected for his chronicle, alongside eleven earlier scholars' king-lists. (Rājataraṅgiṇī 1.14–16) [scholarly]
- **The chronicle opens inside the Nīlamata** — Book 1 of the Rājataraṅgiṇī begins with the Nīlamata's world: Satīsaras, Jalodbhava, Kaśyapa's desiccation of the land (1.25–27), the land-as-Pārvatī maxim (1.42), and the Gonanda–Dāmodara–Yaśovatī sequence explaining the Kurukṣetra absence — myth flowing directly into dynastic history. (Rājataraṅgiṇī 1) [regional] [scholarly]
- **Continuity into Kashmir's later religion** — the valley the Nīlamata charters is the same soil that later produced the **Kashmir Śaiva** corpus (9th–11th c. CE) — see [`../kashmir-shaiva-texts.md`](../kashmir-shaiva-texts.md); the nāga-springs, Vitastā rites, and Herath remain live threads of **Kashmiri Pandit** practice into the present. [regional] [scholarly]
- **Modern recovery [scholarly]** — **Georg Bühler's** 1877 Kashmir manuscript report first signaled the text's importance for dating Kashmiri religion; **K. de Vreese** produced the critical edition (1936), and **Ved Kumari Ghai** the standard study and translation (2 vols., 1968–73).
