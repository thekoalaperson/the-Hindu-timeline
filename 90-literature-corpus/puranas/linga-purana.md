# Liṅga Purāṇa

> Hierarchy location: `90-literature-corpus/puranas/` — a per-Purāṇa source file in the literature corpus.
> Up-link: [`../README.md`](../README.md) (the Purāṇas index) · [`../../README.md`](../../README.md) (the literature corpus).
> See also the parent survey [`../smriti-puranas.md`](../smriti-puranas.md), the sibling [`shiva-purana.md`](shiva-purana.md), and the [`../bhagavata-purana-skandha-map.md`](../bhagavata-purana-skandha-map.md).

The **Liṅga Purāṇa** (*Liṅga Mahāpurāṇa*) is one of the eighteen *Mahāpurāṇas* and a core **Śaiva** text. Its organizing symbol, announced in the title, is the **liṅga** (*liṅga* = "mark, sign"). The text defines it in Sāṃkhya terms: "the unmanifest is called the liṅga", while Śiva himself is the *aliṅga*, the one without a mark (1.3.1), and "*pradhāna* is called the liṅga; the bearer of the liṅga is Parameśvara" (1.17.5). Where the Śiva Purāṇa is a sprawling devotional encyclopedia, the Liṅga Purāṇa is tighter and more **doctrinal and cosmological**. It fuses a **Sāṃkhya cosmogony** with Śaiva theism and frames Śiva-worship through **Pāśupata yoga**. The outer frame is the usual one: the bard **Sūta Romaharṣaṇa**, a disciple of Kṛṣṇa Dvaipāyana, arrives at **Naimiṣa**, where Nārada has just been telling the sages about the glory of the liṅga, and the sages ask him for the Purāṇa (1.1.1–10). Inside that frame several speakers narrate in turn: Brahmā tells the Liṅgodbhava in the first person (1.17), and Nandin (Śailādi) tells the yoga lore to Brahmā's son Sanatkumāra (1.7.8; named at 1.25.3–4). The text is distinctive for its **28 yoga-avatāras (Yogeśvaras)** of Śiva ending with **Nakulīśa/Lakulī**, its **Liṅgodbhava** pillar myth told as an **Oṃ**-theophany, **two separate thousand-name hymns to Śiva** (the first explicitly of 1,008 names) and a **108-name hymn**, and a strongly **tattva**-based metaphysics. *(Verse references below are to the Pūrva-bhāga as `1.chapter.verse` in the GRETIL e-text, which follows the Veṅkaṭeśvara edition (Bombay 1906) as revised against the Calcutta 1960 (Gurumandal) edition. Uttara-bhāga references `2.chapter` follow the chapter list of J. L. Shastri's 1951 translation as hosted on wisdomlib.)*

- **Structure (two *bhāgas*)** — the printed text has a **Pūrva-bhāga of 108 adhyāyas** and an **Uttara-bhāga of 55 adhyāyas**, **163 chapters** in all. The text gives its own size as **11,000** (*ekādaśa-sāhasra*) and calls itself the eleventh Purāṇa (1.2.3–5). The Nāradīya Purāṇa's summary of the Liṅga agrees on 11,000 verses and two parts (Nārada 1.102.3–4). Shastri's introduction counts only **9,185 verses** in the Veṅkaṭeśvara edition. The GRETIL Pūrva-bhāga has about 6,830 numbered verses.
  - **Pūrva-bhāga** — the doctrinal and cosmological heart. It covers the origin of the liṅga and the creation from *pradhāna* (1.2–4, 1.17.28–31; 1.70 "the expansion of creation"), the **Liṅgodbhava** (1.17–19), and the **28 Yogeśvaras** with Pāśupata yoga (1.7–9, 1.24). Time-reckoning comes in 1.4 (Brahmā's day and night) and 1.39–40 (the yugas and their dharmas). Cosmography fills 1.45–53 (the *pātālas*, the *dvīpas*, Bhāratavarṣa, the mountains) and astronomy 1.54–62: the *jyotiś-cakra*, the chariots of the sun and moon, the planets and Dhruva. The genealogies run through 1.63–69. Liṅga-worship and vow chapters come next (1.25–27, 1.73–85), and **tīrtha** glorifications of Vārāṇasī/Avimukta and Śrīśaila (1.77, 1.92). The cycle of Śiva's deeds (1.93–108) closes the part.
  - **Uttara-bhāga** — ritual and devotional. Following Shastri's chapter titles and the Nāradīya's summary (Nārada 1.102.13–16), it **opens with Vaiṣṇava material**: the greatness of Nārāyaṇa and Viṣṇu, Nārada's mastery of music, the tale of Ambarīṣa and Śrīmatī, the origin of Alakṣmī, and the twelve- and eight-syllable Viṣṇu-mantras (2.1–8). Śaiva doctrine follows, including the Pāśupata rite (2.9, 2.18), Śiva's eight bodies (2.13) and the five Brahmans (2.14). Then come **dīkṣā** (initiation) and *pūjā* procedure (2.19–25), the worship of **Aghora** (2.26, 2.49–50) and the Jaya ablution (2.27). A long run of **chapters on gifts** (2.28–44: Hiraṇyagarbha, the sesame mountain, the golden cow, the kalpa tree and others) leads to the *jīvacchrāddha* (2.45), the **installation of the liṅga** (2.46–47), the forms of the **Gāyatrī** (2.48), the **Vajreśvarī vidyā** (2.51), rites of subjugation (2.52), and rites for conquering death and with the Tryambaka mantra (2.53–54).
  - *Variant (the second part's chapter count):* the closing chapter itself gives the second part as **46 chapters** (*ṣaṭcatvāriṃśad adhyāyam*, 2.55, vv. c. 36–43). The *Śivatoṣiṇī* commentary reads the compound so as to get 55. Shastri's footnote rejects that reading, notes that his text actually has 56 chapters (as the wisdomlib rendering reports), and suggests the part originally had 46 chapters, the rest being added later. Some scholars go further and treat the whole Uttara-bhāga as a **later attachment** (Rocher 1986, p. 187, as reported by Wikipedia). By contrast, the Nāradīya's summary already knows a two-part Liṅga with Uttara-bhāga contents much like the extant ones (Nārada 1.102.12–17). [interpolation] [scholarly]
- **Pañca-lakṣaṇa coverage** — strong for a sectarian Purāṇa. *Sarga* and *pratisarga* (creation and dissolution) are central and systematic (1.3–4, 1.70). *Manvantara* coverage includes the fourteen Manus, named with their colours (1.7.23–28). *Vaṃśa/vaṃśānucarita* covers the lineages of the gods and sages (1.63–64), the solar line (1.65–66) and the lunar line, from Yayāti down to Kṛṣṇa (1.67–69). All of these are subordinated to Śaiva doctrine. The real centre of gravity is **liṅga-theology, Pāśupata yoga, and māhātmya and ritual procedure**. Cross-link the framework: [`../../00-time-and-cosmology/`](../../00-time-and-cosmology/) and [`../../01-manvantaras/`](../../01-manvantaras/).
- **Sectarian leaning** — emphatically **Śaiva**, but **integrative**. Viṣṇu and Brahmā arise from Śiva's own body (1.19.2–3, 1.19.12). The first eight chapters of the Uttara-bhāga are Vaiṣṇava. The **Goddess** is prominent: Satī is "the nurse/support of the worlds" (*jagatāṃ dhātrī*, 1.5.27), all women are portions of her and "all that is feminine is she, all that is masculine is Nīlalohita" (1.5.29–30). **Ardhanārīśvara** is the form from which she is separated (1.5.28, 1.99.8–12). The philosophical idiom is **Sāṃkhya cosmology refitted to Śaiva theism**, with a clear **Pāśupata** layer: the Yogeśvara/Lakulī lineage, and the Pāśupata *vrata* (1.80) and rite (2.9, 2.18). [scholarly]
- **Dating** — *traditional:* the text says Brahmā first composed it concerning the **Īśāna kalpa** and that the Vyāsas abridged it in each Dvāpara (1.2.1–5).
  - *Variant (tradition):* the Nāradīya instead says Hara told it while standing in the **fire-liṅga**, concerning the **Agni kalpa**, and that Vyāsa arranged it in two parts (Nārada 1.102.2–3).
  - *Academic [scholarly]:* the oldest core is commonly placed **c. 5th–10th century CE** (Rocher 1986, pp. 187–188, and Clothey 1978, as reported by Wikipedia). The text "exists in many versions" and was revised and expanded over time. Shastri's introduction argues that the redaction process ran until about the beginning of the 5th century CE, taking the reign of Candragupta II (c. 380–412 CE) as its lowest limit. Internally, the Lakulī prophecy is set in the generation of Kṛṣṇa and Dvaipāyana (1.24.124–129), which presupposes the Lakulīśa Pāśupata tradition. [late text]
- **Canon-list note** — the Pūrva-bhāga's own list of the eighteen Mahāpurāṇas (1.39.61–63) reads Brāhma, Pādma, Vaiṣṇava, **Śaiva**, Bhāgavata, Bhaviṣya, Nāradīya, Mārkaṇḍeya, Āgneya, Brahmavaivarta, **Laiṅga** (11th), Vārāha, Vāmana, Kūrma, Mātsya, Gāruḍa, Skānda, Brahmāṇḍa. So it **names the Śiva (Śaiva) Purāṇa and not the Vāyu**. Shastri's translation of the same verses agrees, and no mention of a Vāyu Purāṇa was found anywhere in the Pūrva-bhāga e-text. This corrects the earlier statement here, repeated in sibling pages, that the Liṅga list names Vāyu. For the wider 18th-seat dispute see [`../smriti-puranas.md`](../smriti-puranas.md) → "Canonical-list variants" and [`shiva-purana.md`](shiva-purana.md) §"The major canon variant."

## Notable / distinctive stories and sections

### Liṅgodbhava — the cosmic pillar of light (the charter myth)
- **Liṅgodbhava ("the rising of the liṅga")** (1.17–19) — **Brahmā** tells the gods and sages this story in the first person, in answer to their question "what is the liṅga, and who is its bearer?" (1.17.3–5).
  - **The quarrel.** At the end of a cosmic cycle Viṣṇu lies asleep on the single ocean. Brahmā, deluded by Viṣṇu's *māyā*, wakes him with a blow of the hand, and the two quarrel over which of them is the creator (1.17.10–32). Viṣṇu sets out a Sāṃkhya cosmology with himself as its source: the 24-fold unmanifest, *buddhi*, the threefold *ahaṅkāra*, the *tanmātras* and the elements (1.17.28–31).
  - **The pillar.** To end the quarrel a blazing liṅga appears between them. It is wreathed in thousands of flames, without beginning, middle or end, and is called "unmanifest, the source of the universe" (1.17.33–35).
  - **The search.** Viṣṇu takes the form of a huge black **boar** and goes downward. Brahmā becomes a white **swan** (*haṃsa*) and flies upward. Each searches for a thousand years and neither finds an end (1.17.36–45). Both return exhausted and bow before the pillar (1.17.46–48). Neither lies.
  - **The Oṃ theophany.** A sound, **"Om Om"**, arises. They see its letters **a**, **u** and **m** on the liṅga, with Śiva above them, clear as crystal (1.17.49–55). A sage named **Veda** appears, through whom Viṣṇu comes to know Parameśvara (1.17.57–58). The letters are tied to Brahmā, Hari and Nīlalohita (1.17.60–63). Śiva then shows himself in a form made of sound, each letter of the alphabet a part of his body (1.17.73–81). Viṣṇu receives five mantras and praises him (1.17.82–92ff).
  - **The boon.** Śiva declares that both were born from his body, Brahmā from his right side and Viṣṇu from his left. He grants them the boon they ask for, unwavering devotion (1.19.1–7), and tells Viṣṇu that he himself is divided threefold as Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Bhava (1.19.12). "From that time liṅga-worship was established in the worlds." The pedestal (*vedī*) is the Great Goddess and the liṅga is Maheśvara himself. It is called *liṅga* from *laya*, dissolution (1.19.15–16).
  - **No curse.** In the Liṅga version **no one lies, there is no ketakī flower, and Brahmā is not cursed**.
  - *(Chronology: framework/cosmology — a primordial, timeless theophany at the dawn of creation.)* Cross-link the shared treatment and variants: [`../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md`](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/trimurti-and-tridevi.md), [`../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md`](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md), and [`shiva-purana.md`](shiva-purana.md) §Liṅgodbhava.
  - *Variant (Śiva Purāṇa — the lie and the ketakī):* in the Śiva Purāṇa's Vidyeśvara-saṃhitā, Brahmā meets a falling **ketakī** flower on his way up and persuades it to bear false witness that he reached the top. Viṣṇu, believing him, worships him. Śiva appears from the fire-liṅga. Bhairava, whom Śiva creates, cuts off Brahmā's lying fifth head and is about to kill him when Viṣṇu intercedes. Śiva declares that Brahmā shall have **no temple and festival worship** (*sthāna-utsava*). The ketakī is barred from Śiva's worship, and Viṣṇu's truthfulness is rewarded with equality to Śiva (Śiva P. 1.7.19–33, 1.8.1–21 in the GRETIL numbering). This is the version popular retellings usually tell; it is **not** the Liṅga Purāṇa's.
  - *Variant (cross-text spread):* the Kūrma Purāṇa tells the episode without the lie: both search and fail, then praise "Śiva, the Brahman whose form is the liṅga" (Kūrma 1.25.72–82). Secondary surveys also list the Vāyu, Skanda, Brahmāṇḍa and Vāmana Purāṇas; those were not re-checked here. The **Mahāśivarātri** link is not made in the Liṅga Purāṇa. Its Pūrva-bhāga prescribes worship on the eighth and fourteenth lunar days of both fortnights (1.83.3), and in the month of Phālguna a fast on the fourteenth and eighth (1.83.23–24), but never names Śivarātri, so the festival association belongs to other texts and later practice. (*Śaiva Purāṇic tradition*)
  - *Variant (the unmanifest liṅga):* the Liṅga Purāṇa reads the pillar as the **manifest sign of the unmanifest**. The text itself says so: *pradhāna* is the liṅga and Parameśvara the *liṅgin* (1.17.5); the unmanifest is called the liṅga and Śiva is *aliṅga* (1.3.1). The etiology is therefore **metaphysical** and not merely the settling of a quarrel. (*Liṅga Purāṇa, Pūrva-bhāga*)
  - *Variant (a second Brahmā–Viṣṇu theophany, Pādma kalpa):* chapters 1.20–22 open a further account set in the Pādma kalpa, which 1.19.13–14 announces in advance. There Brahmā is born from the lotus of Viṣṇu's navel and both again see Bhava. No pillar appears in this account: Śiva comes in person, ithyphallic and roaring, Viṣṇu explains to Brahmā who he is, and the two praise him (1.20.60–97, 1.21). The text's own table of contents also lists a separate "renewed rising of the liṅga among the sages" (1.2.16) and, as a separate entry, Śiva's entry into the Deodar forest (1.2.38). (*Liṅga Purāṇa*)

### The 28 Yoga-avatāras (Yogeśvaras) and Lakulīśa — the Pāśupata charter
- **The twenty-eight Yogeśvaras** — in the present **Vārāha kalpa** and **Vaivasvata manvantara**, a **Vyāsa** appears at the end of each Dvāpara and Śiva (Śūlin) incarnates as a **yoga-teacher in each Tiṣya (Kali) age**. Each has **four disciples** and many disciples of disciples (1.7.9–13, 1.7.19–21, 1.7.36–37). The 28 Vyāsas are listed from Kratu to Kṛṣṇa Dvaipāyana (1.7.15–19). The disciples are "Pāśupata *siddhas*, their bodies smeared with ash" (1.7.53). Śiva is Paśupati because all beings from gods to *piśācas* are his *paśus* (1.7.55–56). Chapter 1.24 repeats the whole sequence as Śiva's prophecy to Brahmā. It begins in the first Kali of the Vaivasvata manvantara, with Śveta on the Chāgala peak of the Himālaya (1.24.8–14), and gives each avatāra's place and disciples, for example Ugra at Gaṅgādvāra (1.24.52), Lāṅgalī at Vārāṇasī (1.24.104), Śūlī at Naimiṣa (1.24.112) and Somaśarmā at Prabhāsa (1.24.121). *(Chronology: each appears in the **Kali-Yuga of its mahāyuga**, within the current **Vaivasvata Manvantara** of the **Śveta-Varāha Kalpa**.)* Cross-link the manvantara/mahāyuga frame: [`../../01-manvantaras/`](../../01-manvantaras/) and the Kali-Yuga file under [`../../01-manvantaras/`](../../01-manvantaras/).
  - The 28 (Liṅga Purāṇa 1.7.31–35): **Śveta, Sutāra, Madana, Suhotra, Kaṅkaṇa, Lokākṣi, Jaigīṣavya, Dadhivāhana, Ṛṣabha, Muni, Ugra, Atri, Subālaka, Gautama, Vedaśīrṣa, Gokarṇa, Guhāvāsī, Śikhaṇḍabhṛt, Jaṭāmālī, Aṭṭahāsa, Dāruka, Lāṅgalī, Mahākāya, Śūlī, Daṇḍī Muṇḍīśvara, Sahiṣṇu, Somaśarmā**, and **Nakulīśa**, "the world-teacher" (*nakulīśo jagadguruḥ*). "Jagadguru" is an epithet of the 28th, not another name. "Muni" (10th) and "Daṇḍī Muṇḍīśvara" (25th, a single avatāra) are confirmed by the numbered prophecy in 1.24.48 and 1.24.114–115.
  - *Variant (the Liṅga Purāṇa's own second roster, 1.24):* the prophecy chapter names several avatāras differently from 1.7. It has **Damana** for Madana (3rd), **Kaṅka** (5th), **Logākṣi** (6th), and **Vāli**, at the Vālakhilya hermitage on Gandhamādana, for Subālaka (13th, 1.24.59–60). For the 23rd it has "**Śveta**, the great-bodied (*mahākāya*) son of a sage" at Kālañjara, so 1.7's "Mahākāya" appears to be that Śveta's epithet (1.24.107–109). (*Liṅga Purāṇa*)
  - *Variant (Kūrma Purāṇa roster):* Kūrma 1.51.2–10 has **Subhāna** and **Damana** in 2nd and 3rd place, **Bhṛgu** 10th (where the Liṅga's Muni lives on Bhṛgu's peak, 1.24.49), **Balī** 13th, **Śveta** 23rd and **Ḍiṇḍī Muṇḍī** 25th. It also ends with Nakulīśa, at the *tīrtha* **Kāyāvatāra** (1.51.9–10). (*Kūrma Purāṇa*)
  - *The 28th, Lakulī (Liṅga Purāṇa 1.24.124–134):* in the 28th turn of the yugas, when Parāśara's son Dvaipāyana is Vyāsa and Kṛṣṇa is born as Vāsudeva, Śiva will see an abandoned corpse in a cremation ground. He will enter it by yogic power as a celibate student and will be called **Lakulī**. The place will be famed as the *siddha-kṣetra* **Kāyāvatāra**, "the descent into the body". His four disciples, **Kuśika, Garga, Mitra and Kauruṣya**, will be ash-smeared Pāśupatas devoted to liṅga-worship who reach Rudra's world (1.24.131–134). The 27th avatāra, Somaśarmā at Prabhāsa, has the disciples Akṣapāda, Kumāra, Ulūka and Vatsa (1.24.121–123). (*Liṅga Purāṇa*)
  - *Variant (parallel prophecies and the historical sect):* the Kūrma Purāṇa also names the place **Kāyāvatāra** (1.51.10) and elsewhere a Śiva shrine **Kāyāvarohaṇa** where the sages set the Māheśvara dharmas going (2.42.7), followed by a Nakulīśvara *tīrtha* (2.42.12). A Vāyu Purāṇa parallel (ch. 23) is reported in secondary sources but was not re-checked here; the Vāyu is not on GRETIL. Modern scholarship identifies the site with **Kārvān (Gujarat)** and Lakulīśa with the historical founder, or the first formal preceptor, of the **Pāśupata** sect. One conditional estimate places him around 125 CE. (*Kūrma; Vāyu per Wikipedia "Lakulisha"*) [scholarly]

### Śiva-tattva cosmology — the liṅga as the unmanifest source
- **Sāṃkhya-Śaiva cosmogony** — creation proceeds from the **unmanifest (*avyakta*/*pradhāna*, called the liṅga)** through *mahat* and the **tattvas** to the manifest world. The text's own table of contents gives the sequence as primary, *prākṛta* and *vaikṛta* creations, the cosmic egg and its eight coverings (1.2.6–7). Śiva is the ground beyond them as the *aliṅga* (1.3.1), and dissolution folds the cosmos back into the liṅga ("called *liṅga* from *laya*", 1.19.16). The text gives *kalpa/manvantara/yuga* reckoning (1.4, 1.7.23–28, 1.39–40) and astronomy of the sun, moon, planets, *nakṣatras* and Dhruva (1.54–62). *(Chronology: framework/cosmology — the metaphysics of the cosmic clock itself.)* Cross-link: [`../../00-time-and-cosmology/`](../../00-time-and-cosmology/).
- **Ardhanārīśvara and the Goddess** — at the beginning of creation Brahmā sees Śiva as **Ardhanārīśvara** and asks him to divide himself. The Goddess who comes forth becomes Dakṣa's daughter **Satī**, "the nurse of the worlds" (1.5.27–31). The fuller account says the Goddess is the pedestal and the liṅga is Śiva, that **Ardhanārīśvara** arises from their union, and that Śiva creates the Goddess from his left side (1.99.6–12). After reviling Dakṣa, Satī is reborn as the daughter of Menā (1.99.13–14). This is a Śaiva framing that folds Śākta theology into the liṅga-cosmology. *(Chronology: framework/cosmology — divine theophany, timeless.)* Cross-link: [`../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md`](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-mahatmya-and-the-great-goddess.md).

### The demon-slayings and divine deeds
- **Andhaka-vadha — the blind demon** (1.93; the chapter's colophon calls it the story of "Andhaka's elevation to gaṇa-chief") — Andhaka is the son of **Hiraṇyākṣa**. A boon from Brahmā makes him invulnerable; he conquers the three worlds and torments Indra, and the gods, led by Nārāyaṇa, take refuge on **Mount Mandara** (1.93.3–7). Śiva comes with his gaṇas, burns the demon's hosts, and **impales Andhaka on his trident**. The fire burns away Andhaka's sins (1.93.11–15). Turning to a *sāttvika* mood, Andhaka remembers that he worshipped Śiva in a former life, praises him, and asks only for devotion. Śiva grants him faith and makes him a **gaṇa-chief**, and the gods bow to him (1.93.15–26). The Liṅga version **has no lust for Pārvatī, no thousand-headed form and no blood-drop clones**. *(Chronology: framework/cosmology — divine deed, timeless.)* Cross-link the variants (each blood-drop spawning clones, the Saptamātṛkā, Andhaka's redemption as the gaṇa Bhṛṅgi): [`../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md`](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) §3 and [`shiva-purana.md`](shiva-purana.md) §"Andhaka-vadha."
  - *Variant (redemption):* the Liṅga Purāṇa ends with Andhaka **made a devotee and gaṇa-chief** and not annihilated (1.93.24–26). The Kūrma ends the same way, with Andhaka as Nandīśvara's attendant (Kūrma 1.15.202–211). (*Liṅga Purāṇa; Kūrma Purāṇa*)
  - *Variant (the motive):* in the Kūrma Purāṇa, Andhaka, "tormented by desire", comes to carry off **Girijā (Pārvatī)** (Kūrma 1.15.168). This motive, which the Liṅga account lacks, is the one the cross-linked pages follow. (*Kūrma Purāṇa*)
- **The Dakṣa-yajña and Vīrabhadra** (1.99–100) — grieving at separation from the Goddess, Śiva sends the gaṇa **Bhadra (Vīrabhadra)**, who creates hosts of gaṇas from his body-hair (*romaja*). Dakṣa's sacrificial ground lies near **Gaṅgādvāra at Kanakhala** in the Himālaya (1.100.3–7).
  - **The wrecking.** Vīrabhadra burns the sacrificial hall. He plucks out **Bhaga's eyes** with his nails, knocks out **Pūṣan's teeth** with his fist, grinds **Candra** under his toe and beheads **Indra** (1.100.13–18). He fights **Viṣṇu** and cuts off his head, which falls into Rasātala (1.100.23–32). He beheads the sacrifice itself as it flees skyward in the form of a deer (1.100.34–35), and cuts the nose-tips of Sarasvatī and the Mother of the gods (1.100.38).
  - **The restoration.** Brahmā pacifies Vīrabhadra. At Brahmā's request Śiva restores the slain, including the heads of Indra, Viṣṇu and Dakṣa, and grants boons, and all praise him (1.100.39–51).
  - **The cause.** The Liṅga Purāṇa prepares the episode earlier. In the **Kṣupa–Dadhīca** dispute Viṣṇu takes the king's side against Dadhīca, deliberately provoking the sage so that his curse will bring about Viṣṇu's and the gods' downfall at Dakṣa's sacrifice (1.36.29–30). Dadhīca then curses the gods, with Viṣṇu, to be destroyed by Rudra's wrath at Dakṣa's sacrifice (1.36.72–74). That is why 1.100.1 says the gods were overthrown "by Dadhīca's word".
  - No **Bhadrakālī** appears in the Liṅga account (she belongs to other retellings). *(Chronology: framework/cosmology — divine biography, timeless; pre-Pārvatī cycle.)* Cross-link the full event and variants: [`../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md`](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md).
  - *Variant (memorialized in name-lists):* the deed survives in Śiva-epithets in the Liṅga Purāṇa's hymns:
    - *bhaganetrāntaka* ("ender of Bhaga's eyes"), *pūṣadantavināśa* ("destroyer of Pūṣan's teeth") and *dakṣayajñāntaka* (1.21.57–59);
    - *dakṣayajñavināśana* (1.22.2);
    - *bhaganetranipātana* (1.82.101);
    - *bhaganetrabhit* (1.98.39);
    - *makhadveṣī* ("hater of the sacrifice") and *viṣṇukandharapātana* ("who felled Viṣṇu's head") (1.98.125). (*Liṅga Purāṇa*)
- **Tripura-dahana, Jālandhara, and the burning of Kāma** — the Liṅga Purāṇa also carries the standard Śaiva cosmic deeds. Śiva, as Paśupati, burns Maya's three cities of gold, silver and iron (**Tripura**, 1.71–72; "Tripurāntaka", 1.72.114). He slays **Jalandhara** with a wheel he draws in the waters with his toe (1.97, esp. 1.97.16). That wheel is the one Viṣṇu later seeks and receives as the Sudarśana (1.98.15–19, 1.98.170). **Kāma** is burnt by the third eye during the Tāraka crisis. At Rati's lament Śiva promises that her husband, now bodiless, will return as the son of Vāsudeva, and she departs having regained Madana (1.101.8–46). *(Chronology: framework/cosmology — Śaiva cosmic deeds, timeless.)* Cross-link: [`../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md`](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) §3.

### Jyotirliṅgas and sacred geography
- **The pillar of light and the *tīrthas*** — the Liṅga Purāṇa's "liṅga of light" is the cosmic pillar of 1.17. The Pūrva-bhāga contains **no list of twelve jyotirliṅgas**: the word does not occur in the e-text, and the Uttara-bhāga's chapter titles show none. What it does contain is *tīrtha* glorification:
  - Nārada's round of liṅga-shrines before he comes to Naimiṣa, including Avimukta, Kedāra and Pāśupata (1.1.2–4);
  - the merit of dying at **Vārāṇasī/Avimukta, Kedāra, Prayāga and Kurukṣetra** (1.77.38–41);
  - a full glorification of **Vārāṇasī (Avimukteśvara) and Śrīśaila** (1.92);
  - the count of Rudra's and Viṣṇu's shrines on earth (1.2.18).

  It also places the Pāśupata avatāras at named sites: Chāgala on the Himālaya, Gaṅgādvāra, Vārāṇasī, Kālañjara, Naimiṣa, Prabhāsa, and Kāyāvatāra (1.24). The canonical twelve-jyotirliṅga roster comes from other texts and later tradition. *(Chronology: framework — sacred geography active across yugas; founding legends set in mythic antiquity.)* Cross-link the dedicated catalog: [`../../06-sacred-geography/12-jyotirlingas.md`](../../06-sacred-geography/12-jyotirlingas.md) and [`../../06-sacred-geography/sapta-puri-and-sacred-cities.md`](../../06-sacred-geography/sapta-puri-and-sacred-cities.md).

### Name-litanies, ritual, and yoga
- **Two thousand-name hymns and a 108-name hymn** — all three are in the **Pūrva-bhāga**:
  - **(1) The Rudra-sahasranāma of Taṇḍi (1.65).** Brahmā first told the thousand names to the sage Taṇḍi, who won gaṇa-status with them. The Ikṣvāku king Tridhanvan, a descendant of the Anaraṇya whom Rāvaṇa slew, received them from Taṇḍi (1.65.44–51). The litany of "1,008 names" (*aṣṭottarasahasram*, 1.65.53) opens *sthiraḥ sthāṇuḥ prabhur bhānuḥ pravaro varado varaḥ* (1.65.54).
  - **(2) Viṣṇu's sahasranāma (1.98).** Viṣṇu worships Śiva with a thousand names (the hymn runs to 1.98.159), offering a lotus at each. Śiva hides one lotus, so Viṣṇu plucks out his own eye to complete the worship. Śiva gives him the **Sudarśana** discus, and Viṣṇu is called **Padmākṣa**, "lotus-eyed" (1.98.160–177).
  - **(3) A hymn of 108 names (1.96).** Narasiṃha addresses it to Śiva in his **Śarabha** form after being subdued (1.96.94–95).

  The hymns compress Śiva's deeds and attributes into worship formulae, for example *Paśupati* and *Ugra* (1.98.39) and *Andhakāri* (1.98.125). *(Chronology: framework — living ritual, applicable across yugas.)* Cross-link the deities survey: [`../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md`](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) and [`../../05-concepts-and-dharma/`](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/).
  - *Variant (shared litanies):* the Taṇḍi hymn of 1.65 is the same tradition as the Mahābhārata's Śiva-sahasranāma. The epic also credits the thousand names to "Taṇḍi, born of Brahmā" (MBh 13.14.3; Taṇḍi in the Kṛta age, 13.16.12), and its litany opens with the identical line *sthiraḥ sthāṇuḥ prabhur bhānuḥ pravaro varado varaḥ* (MBh 13.17.30, Critical Edition). Other Śiva thousand-name lists, such as those in the Śiva Purāṇa, the Vāyu and the Mahābhārata's Śānti-parvan, are reported in secondary literature to differ in many seats; they were not compared here. No source in the Pūrva-bhāga e-text was found for the popular *Śiva-aṣṭottara-śata-nāmāvali* (the 108-name list in common use). (*cross-text*) [scholarly]
- **Liṅga-worship, *bhasma*, *rudrākṣa*, *praṇava*, and Pāśupata yoga** — there is extensive procedure for bathing, the five daily sacrifices and worship of the liṅga (1.25–27). The text also treats the kinds and installation of liṅgas (1.73–76), worship and its fruits (1.77–79), the **Pāśupata vow** (1.80), freeing the bound soul (*paśu*) from its fetters (*pāśa*) through liṅga-worship (1.81), and Śiva-vows including the Umā-Maheśvara vow (1.83–84). The **Pañcākṣara** (*namaḥ śivāya*) has a whole chapter of its own (1.85). The **praṇava (Oṃ)** is Śiva's sound-body in the Liṅgodbhava (1.17.49–81). Ash-smearing marks the Pāśupata *siddhas* (1.7.53, 1.24.133) and is praised in 1.34. **Rudrākṣa**, by contrast, appears only in passing, as beads for *japa* that multiply its merit (1.85.111), so the long rudrākṣa-māhātmya belongs to the Śiva Purāṇa tradition. The **eightfold Pāśupata yoga** (*prāṇāyāma* and the other "eight means", 1.7.3), with its *yamas* and *niyamas* and its places of yoga, is set out in 1.8–9. The Uttara-bhāga adds the Pāśupata rite, *dīkṣā* and Aghora worship (2.9, 2.18, 2.21, 2.26). *(Chronology: framework — living ritual + soteriology.)* Cross-link: [`../../05-concepts-and-dharma/`](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/) and [`../../06-sacred-geography/meru-kailash-and-cosmic-mountains.md`](../../06-sacred-geography/meru-kailash-and-cosmic-mountains.md).

### Other episodes in the Pūrva-bhāga
- **Kṣupa and Dadhīca** (1.35–36) — King Kṣupa and his friend the sage Dadhīca argue over whether the king or the brahmin is greater. Through Śiva's grace Dadhīca gains **bones hard as the thunderbolt** and is unbeatable even by Viṣṇu, who has taken Kṣupa's side. Dadhīca's curse then foreshadows the Dakṣa-yajña (1.36.29–30, 1.36.72–74). (*Liṅga Purāṇa*)
- **Nandikeśvara** (1.42–44) — the birth of **Nandin** as son of the sage **Śilāda**, who had asked Indra and then Śiva for a son not born of a womb (1.2.12–13), and Nandin's consecration as chief of the gaṇas. (*Liṅga Purāṇa*)
- **Varāha, Narasiṃha and Śarabha** (1.94–96) — Viṣṇu's boar and man-lion descents are retold (1.94–95). When Narasiṃha's fury becomes a danger, Śiva first sends Vīrabhadra and then takes the form of **Śarabha**, part beast and part bird with wings and beak (1.96.1–5, 1.96.66–68). Śarabha subdues Narasiṃha, who praises him with 108 names (1.96.94–95). It is a pointed sectarian episode. (*Liṅga Purāṇa*) [disputed]
- **The Goddess's marriage and her sons** — Pārvatī is born to Menā, does her austerities and holds her **svayaṃvara**, and the marriage follows (1.101–103). The gods praise Śiva (1.104), and **Vināyaka** is born (1.105). (*Liṅga Purāṇa*)
- **Dāruka, Kālī and the Tāṇḍava** (1.106) — the asura **Dāruka** can be slain only by a woman. Pārvatī enters Śiva's body and shapes herself from the poison in his throat, and Śiva creates **Kālī**, dark-throated, with his third eye; she kills Dāruka (1.106.2–19). To calm her wrath Śiva first drinks it as an infant at her breast, then dances the **Tāṇḍava** at twilight (1.106.20–26), and the colophon titles the chapter "the account of Śiva's Tāṇḍava". (*Liṅga Purāṇa*)
- **Upamanyu** (1.107) — the boy Upamanyu, denied milk at his uncle's hermitage and fed by his mother on a paste of gleaned grain, does penance and is granted an **ocean of milk** by Śiva. (*Liṅga Purāṇa*)
- **Kṛṣṇa's Pāśupata initiation** (1.108) — Kṛṣṇa Vāsudeva goes to Upamanyu's hermitage, practising austerities to obtain a son, and receives the divine **Pāśupata vow** from Upamanyu, "elder brother of Dhaumya" (1.108.1–4). (*Liṅga Purāṇa*; cf. the parallel in MBh Anuśāsana-parvan, where Upamanyu teaches Kṛṣṇa the Taṇḍi sahasranāma.)
