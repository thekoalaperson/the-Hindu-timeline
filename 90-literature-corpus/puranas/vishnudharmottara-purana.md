# Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa — text profile

The arts of Khaṇḍa III live in [`../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/chitra-kala-painting-traditions.md`](../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/chitra-kala-painting-traditions.md).

The **Viṣṇudharmottara Purāṇa** (*Viṣṇudharma-uttara*, "the further/supplementary Viṣṇu-dharma") is an **encyclopedic Vaiṣṇava upapurāṇa** styled as a **khila** (appendix) to the **Viṣṇu Purāṇa** — its name announces itself as the *uttara* ("latter") continuation of an older *Viṣṇudharma* text. It is unlike almost any other Purāṇa in **breadth**: three long *khaṇḍas* run from cosmogony and genealogy through the whole apparatus of a courtly education — statecraft, law, grammar, prosody, lexicography, astronomy, medicine, veterinary science, warfare, yoga — and culminate in the **most complete surviving early-medieval manual of the fine arts**, containing the **Citrasūtra**, the **earliest extant Indian treatise on painting**. It is cited across the corpus not for a single famous myth but as a **reference work**: the source layer behind early Vaiṣṇava iconography, *tālamāna* (iconometry), and the classical theory that binds painting, sculpture, dance, and music into one interdependent chain. Its canon-rank floats: counted among the Upapurāṇas in the "Major Upapurāṇa" rosters, it is elsewhere treated simply as a *khila* of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa (see [`upapuranas-overview.md`](upapuranas-overview.md)). **[scholarly]**

- **The frame — Mārkaṇḍeya instructs King Vajra** — the entire text is spoken by the *cirañjīvī* sage **Mārkaṇḍeya** (and his line) to **Vajra**, the **last surviving Yādava**, great-grandson of **Kṛṣṇa** through **Pradyumna → Aniruddha → Vajra**. After the Yādavas destroy themselves at Prabhāsa and Kṛṣṇa departs, Arjuna installs the boy Vajra as king; needing to govern — and to *worship* rightly by making images of the gods — Vajra seeks out Mārkaṇḍeya, who answers with a total curriculum for a righteous Vaiṣṇava king. *(Chronology: the frame sits at the **Dvāpara–Kali junction** — the aftermath of the Mahābhārata war, when Kali begins; scripturally 3102 BCE. Composition itself is far later — see dating.)* Cross-link the sage [`../../04-deep-dives/markandeya.md`](../../04-deep-dives/markandeya.md) and the Kṛṣṇa era [`../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md`](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md).
- **Three khaṇḍas** — the text divides into **Khaṇḍa I** (cosmogony, geography, legend-cycles, Vaiṣṇava lore), **Khaṇḍa II** (rājadharma, law, and the sciences), and **Khaṇḍa III** (the arts and image-making). Traditional adhyāya counts run roughly **269 / 183 / 355** chapters (counts vary by recension). **[disputed]**
- **Sectarian character** — nominally **Vaiṣṇava** (Viṣṇu as supreme, the vyūhas and avatāras, Vaiṣṇava *vrata* and image-worship), but so **omnivorous** that it absorbs Śaiva, Śākta, and Saura iconography and ritual wholesale — a deliberately **encyclopedic**, near-*śāstric* text rather than a partisan cult-charter.
- **Dating — the two clocks** — *Traditional:* an eternal supplement of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, narrated by Mārkaṇḍeya at the opening of the Kali age. *Academic [scholarly]:* the core is placed **c. 400–600 CE**, with the arts material of Khaṇḍa III commonly set **c. 5th–7th c. CE**; **Priyabala Shah** (its critical editor) argued a **Kashmir / north-Indian** provenance around **c. 6th c. CE**; later strata extend the transmission toward the **9th–10th c.** **[late text]** relative to the older Mahāpurāṇas.

## Khaṇḍa I — cosmogony, genealogy & the legend-cycles

- **Creation, kalpa-reckoning, and the geography of the worlds** — standard Purāṇic *sarga/pratisarga*: the emanation of the cosmos from Viṣṇu, the measures of cosmic time, the *dvīpas* and oceans, Mount Meru, Bhārata-varṣa, and the rivers and *tīrthas* — the *bhuvana-koṣa* framework shared with the Viṣṇu Purāṇa it supplements. *(Chronology: framework — cosmic geography of the present kalpa.)* Cross-link [`../../06-sacred-geography/README.md`](../../06-sacred-geography/README.md).
- **The avatāras and vyūhas of Viṣṇu** — the descents of Viṣṇu and the four **vyūha** emanations (Vāsudeva–Saṃkarṣaṇa–Pradyumna–Aniruddha) of Pāñcarātra theology, later reused in Khaṇḍa III as the doctrinal basis for how each form is to be *depicted*. *(Chronology: framework — avatāra spine across the yugas.)* Cross-link [`../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishnu-forms-and-vyuhas.md`](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vishnu-forms-and-vyuhas.md) and [`../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md`](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/dashavatara.md).
- **Solar and Lunar genealogies and the Yādava line** — the *vaṃśa/vaṃśānucarita* of the Sūrya- and Candra-vaṃśa, converging on the **Yādava** house of Kṛṣṇa from which the frame-king Vajra descends — the genealogical thread that makes Vajra a *credible* last heir rather than a bare narrative device. *(Chronology: legendary antiquity through the Dvāpara close.)* Cross-link [`../../03-lineages-vamsha/README.md`](../../03-lineages-vamsha/README.md) and [`../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md`](../../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md).
- **Legend-cycles and *māhātmyas*** — Khaṇḍa I carries the text's narrative payload: episodes of Viṣṇu's greatness, the merit of Ekādaśī and other *vrata*, tales of devotees and kings, and *tīrtha-māhātmyas* — the "story" book before the two "science/art" books that follow. *(Chronology: mixed legendary settings.)* **[folk]** elements sit alongside the śāstric core.

## Khaṇḍa II — rājadharma & the sciences

- **Rājadharma — the education of a king** — the practical heart of the frame: **statecraft, the duties of the sovereign, ministers and *daṇḍa* (law/punishment), *vyavahāra* (judicial procedure), diplomacy and the fort/army**, plus *prāyaścitta* (expiation), *śrāddha*, and *dāna* — everything Vajra must know to rule the remnant of the Yādava world. *(Chronology: taught in the frame at the dawn of Kali.)* Cross-link [`../../05-concepts-and-dharma/README.md`](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/README.md).
- **The classical sciences catalogued** — a compact **encyclopedia of the *śāstras***: **vyākaraṇa** (grammar), **chandas** (metrics/prosody), **kośa** (lexicography), **jyotiṣa** (astronomy & the calendar), and **nīti** (polity) — a rare Purāṇic attempt to *summarize the whole curriculum* of Sanskrit learning under one Vaiṣṇava roof. *(Chronology: framework — timeless *vidyā*.)*
- **Medicine, veterinary science, and warfare** — sections on **āyurveda** (the Viṣṇudharmottara is quoted in later medical compendia), **aśva- and gaja-āyurveda** (the care of horses and elephants), and **dhanurveda** (archery and the arts of war) — the martial and healing knowledge a ruling house required. *(Chronology: framework/applied science.)* **[scholarly]** value as an early witness to these disciplines.
- **Yoga and philosophy** — expositions of **yoga**, **Sāṃkhya**, and Vedānta-flavored metaphysics folded into the Vaiṣṇava frame, so that the king's outward *dharma* is grounded in inward *jñāna*. *(Chronology: framework — soteriology across the yugas.)*

## Khaṇḍa III — the Citrasūtra & the encyclopedia of the arts

- **The chain of the arts** — when Vajra asks to learn **image-making** so he can fashion icons for worship, Mārkaṇḍeya lays down the text's most-quoted principle: the arts are **interdependent and hierarchically dependent** — *the canon of image-making (pratimā-lakṣaṇa) cannot be grasped without painting (citra); painting without dance (nṛtya); dance without instrumental music (ātodya); and instrumental music without vocal song (gīta)*. To teach the icon he must therefore first teach the whole tree of the arts. (Source: *Viṣṇudharmottara* III.2) **[scholarly]** Cross-link [`../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md`](../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/natya-shastra-and-the-classical-arts.md), [`../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/sangita-classical-music-svara-raga-tala.md`](../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/sangita-classical-music-svara-raga-tala.md), and the 64 arts [`../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/sixty-four-kalas.md`](../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/sixty-four-kalas.md).
- **The Citrasūtra — the earliest Indian treatise on painting** — Khaṇḍa III, chapters **35–43**, constitute the **Citrasūtra**, the **foundational Sanskrit *śāstra* of painting**: the **types (bheda) of citra** (the *satya, vaiṇika, nāgara, miśra* classes), the **proportion and iconometry (tālamāna)** shared with sculpture, the **eight standing/seating measures (sthāna)**, the depiction of the **rasas and bhāvas** (emotion in the image), the **defects (doṣa) of a picture**, and the preparation of **grounds, brushes, and pigments (varṇa)**. Its governing dictum — **`yathā nṛtte tathā citre`, "as in dance, so in painting"** — makes the painted body a frozen moment of dance. (Source: *Viṣṇudharmottara* III.35–43; **Stella Kramrisch, *The Vishnudharmottara, Part III*, 1928**) **[scholarly]** Full living-tradition treatment: [`../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/chitra-kala-painting-traditions.md`](../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/chitra-kala-painting-traditions.md).
  - *Note on the ṣaḍaṅga:* the "**six limbs of painting**" (*ṣaḍaṅga* — *rūpabheda, pramāṇa, bhāva, lāvaṇya-yojana, sādṛśya, varṇikā-bhaṅga*) are the classic six-fold theory of Indian painting associated with the Citrasūtra tradition; the canonical enumeration is preserved in **Yaśodhara's *Jayamaṅgalā*** commentary on the Kāmasūtra, and the Citrasūtra elaborates the same set of principles (form, proportion, expression, grace, likeness, and coloring). **[scholarly]** — attributions of the exact six-item list vary, so it is cataloged here at the tradition level, not fixed to a single verse.
- **The origin-of-painting legend — Nārāyaṇa and Urvaśī** — the Citrasūtra grounds the very *origin of citra* in a myth of the sage **Nārāyaṇa** (of the Nara–Nārāyaṇa pair at Badarikāśrama): to humble the pride of the **apsarases** sent to disturb his austerities, Nārāyaṇa **painted the figure of a surpassingly beautiful woman on his own thigh (*ūru*) with the juice of a mango**, and from that painted image sprang **Urvaśī** — lovelier than all the celestial nymphs, and named for the thigh she rose from. The first *picture* thus predates and out-does the apsarases themselves: painting is figured as the art that *creates* beauty rather than merely copying it. (Source: *Viṣṇudharmottara*, Khaṇḍa III / Citrasūtra) **[scholarly]** This is the corpus's canonical home for the legend; cross-link Nārāyaṇa [`../../04-deep-dives/nara-narayana.md`](../../04-deep-dives/nara-narayana.md), the apsaras class [`../../08-beings-and-bestiary/apsaras.md`](../../08-beings-and-bestiary/apsaras.md), and Urvaśī's later love-story [`../../04-deep-dives/pururavas-urvashi.md`](../../04-deep-dives/pururavas-urvashi.md).
  - *Variant (older Vedic/epic tradition):* elsewhere Urvaśī is simply **born from Nārāyaṇa's thigh** (the *ūru*-etymology) with **no painting** involved — the Viṣṇudharmottara's distinctive contribution is to make that birth an act of *citra*, retrofitting the etymological myth into a charter for painters. Do not merge the two: the "mango-juice painting" detail is specific to this text.
- **Pratimā-lakṣaṇa — the canon of image-making** — following painting, Khaṇḍa III lays out the **iconography (pratimā-/mūrti-lakṣaṇa)** of the gods: the multi-armed forms and attributes of **Viṣṇu** (including the four-faced **Vaikuṇṭha / Caturmūrti** with man, lion, and boar faces), of **Sūrya, Śiva, the Goddess, the grahas, and the guardians** — one of the **richest early textual sources for Hindu iconography**, used to date and read surviving Gupta- and post-Gupta-period images. (Source: *Viṣṇudharmottara* Khaṇḍa III) **[scholarly]** Cross-link [`../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/murti-lakshana-and-iconometry.md`](../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/murti-lakshana-and-iconometry.md).
- **Prāsāda-lakṣaṇa and consecration** — the closing arts material treats **temple architecture (prāsāda/vāstu)**, the making and **installation (pratiṣṭhā)** of images, and the ritual that turns a crafted form into a worshipable deity — the endpoint of the whole chain that began with song. (Source: *Viṣṇudharmottara* Khaṇḍa III) Cross-link [`../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/temple-architecture-nagara-dravida-vesara.md`](../../09-artifacts-symbols-and-arts/temple-architecture-nagara-dravida-vesara.md).

## Dating, transmission & editions

- **Provenance [scholarly]** — the linguistic and iconographic content points to **Kashmir / the north-Indian northwest**; **Priyabala Shah** situated the text there around the **6th c. CE**, consistent with the Gupta-to-early-medieval art it codifies. The absence of the developed later Tantric and sectarian polemic found in the true late Upapurāṇas argues against a very late date for the core. **[disputed]** on the precise century.
- **Canon status** — floats between **"an Upapurāṇa"** (the modern "Major Upapurāṇa" lists) and **"a *khila* of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa"**; unlike the fiercely sectarian upapurāṇas surveyed in [`upapuranas-overview.md`](upapuranas-overview.md), its encyclopedic, *śāstra*-like ambition sets it apart. **[disputed canon-rank]**
- **Standard editions/translations** — critical edition and study by **Priyabala Shah** (Gaekwad's Oriental Series, Baroda); the arts book edited and translated by **Stella Kramrisch, *The Vishnudharmottara (Part III): A Treatise on Indian Painting and Image-Making*** (Calcutta University, 2nd ed. 1928) — the source that made the **Citrasūtra** central to modern Indian art history.
