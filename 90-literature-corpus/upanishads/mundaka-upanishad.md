# Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad

> Part of [`90-literature-corpus/`](../README.md) — a per-text deep-dive into the **Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad**, a verse *mukhya* Upaniṣad of the **Atharvaveda** ([Śaunaka śākhā](../vedas/atharvaveda.md)), structured as **three muṇḍakas of two khaṇḍas each**. ## 1. Identity, Placement, Dating

- **The name "Muṇḍaka"** — from *muṇḍa*, "shaven (head)." Tradition reads it as the Upaniṣad **"of the shaven ones,"** i.e. the teaching that "shaves off" error and ignorance, and which is fit for the head-shaven renouncer (saṃnyāsin). Its sections are called **muṇḍakas** rather than the usual *adhyāyas*. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up.; Śaṅkara bhāṣya)*
- **Parent Veda** — belongs to the **Atharvaveda**, specifically the **Śaunaka** recension; it is one of the cluster of Atharvan mukhya Upaniṣads (with Praśna and Māṇḍūkya). *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up.)* — see [`vedas/atharvaveda.md`](../vedas/atharvaveda.md).
- **Structure** — **three muṇḍakas, each of two khaṇḍas (sections)**, entirely in **verse (metrical)** apart from a few prose-like passages; ~64–65 mantras in all. The first muṇḍaka frames the two knowledges; the second and third expound parā vidyā and the path to it. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1–3)*
- **Placement in the canon** — listed **5th** in the **Daśopaniṣad** (the ten Śaṅkara commented on), in the mnemonic *"Īśa-Kena-Kaṭha-Praśna-**Muṇḍa**-Māṇḍūkya-Taittiri..."*; counted among the principal/mukhya set. *(Source: Daśopaniṣad mnemonic; Muktikā Up.)* — catalogued in [`principal-upanishads-detail.md`](../principal-upanishads-detail.md).
- **Dating (two clocks)** —
  - *Academic:* a relatively **late mukhya Upaniṣad** among the metrical group, commonly placed in the **mid-to-later 1st millennium BCE** (often c. 5th–2nd c. BCE), after the old prose Upaniṣads; its polished critique of ritual presupposes a mature śrauta system. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (orthodox / traditional):* eternal **apauruṣeya Śruti**, "seen" by the ṛṣis and re-compiled by **Vyāsa** at the Dvāpara–Kali junction (~3102 BCE, the Kali onset constant); not a "BCE-stratum composition." This file records both frames and corrects neither. [scripture vs. academic dating]

## 2. The Lineage of Transmission (1.1.1–2)

- **Brahmā, first of the gods** — the Upaniṣad opens by naming its chain of teachers: **Brahmā**, the maker of the universe and the protector of the world, arose **first among the gods** and **taught the knowledge of Brahman, the foundation of all knowledges, to his eldest son Atharvan.** *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.1.1)*
- **Atharvan → Aṅgir(as)** — what **Brahmā** told **Atharvan**, that very Brahma-vidyā Atharvan in old time declared to **Aṅgir** (Aṅgiras). *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.1.2a)*
- **Aṅgiras → Satyavaha → Aṅgiras (the seeker)** — Aṅgir passed it to **Bhāradvāja Satyavaha**, and Satyavaha to **Aṅgiras** (the descendant), in the descending teacher-pupil (guru-śiṣya) succession. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.1.2b)*
- **Śaunaka the great householder approaches Aṅgiras** — **Śaunaka, the great householder (mahāśāla),** approaches **Aṅgiras** in the proper manner and asks the pivotal question: **"kasmin nu bhagavo vijñāte sarvam idaṃ vijñātaṃ bhavati"** — *"What is that, O blessed one, through the knowing of which all this becomes known?"* The whole Upaniṣad is Aṅgiras's answer. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.1.3)* — Śaunaka is the eponym of the recension this text belongs to.

## 3. Parā vs. Aparā Vidyā — The Two Knowledges (1.1.4–9)

- **The two knowledges to be known** — Aṅgiras replies that **two knowledges are to be known (dve vidye veditavye)**: **parā** (the higher) and **aparā** (the lower) — as the knowers of Brahman declare. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.1.4)*
- **Aparā vidyā — the lower knowledge** — comprises the **four Vedas (Ṛg, Yajur, Sāma, Atharva)** together with the six **Vedāṅgas** (śikṣā, kalpa, vyākaraṇa, nirukta, chandas, jyotiṣa). Strikingly, the **Vedas themselves and all ritual learning are classed as "lower."** *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.1.5)* [a famous demotion of ritual scripture relative to Self-knowledge]
- **Parā vidyā — the higher knowledge** — that **by which the Imperishable (akṣara) is reached**: the **Brahman that is invisible, ungraspable, without origin or attributes, without eye or ear, without hands or feet, eternal, all-pervading, subtle, undecaying — the womb of beings** which the wise perceive everywhere. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.1.6)*
- **The spark-from-fire / web-from-spider creation simile** — *"yathā sudīptāt pāvakād visphuliṅgāḥ..."* — **"As from a blazing fire thousands of sparks of like form leap forth, so from the Imperishable, O dear one, manifold beings arise and into it again return."** Joined to the parallels: **as a spider sends out and draws in its thread, as plants grow from the earth, as hair grows from a living person — so from the Imperishable arises all this universe.** Creation as emanation, not fabrication. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.1.7–9; 2.1.1)*

## 4. The Critique of Sacrifice — "Leaky Boats" (1.2)

- **The ritual machinery described** — the second khaṇḍa first sets out, in full and respectful detail, the **fire-sacrifices (the iṣṭāpūrta)**: the seven tongues of the offering-fire (Kālī, Karālī, Manojavā, Sulohitā, Sudhūmravarṇā, Sphuliṅginī, Viśvarucī), the right times for oblations, the heavenly fruits of correctly poured offerings. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.2.1–6)*
- **"Plavā hy ete adṛḍhā yajña-rūpāḥ" — the leaky boats** — then the verdict: **"But unsteady, verily, are these boats in the form of the eighteen sacrifices (plavā hy ete adṛḍhā yajña-rūpāḥ aṣṭādaśoktam avaraṃ yeṣu karma)."** The fools who hail them as the highest good **go again and again to old age and death (jarā-mṛtyuṃ te punar evāpi yanti).** *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.2.7)* [the central anti-ritualist image of the text]
- **The blind led by the blind** — those dwelling in ignorance, fancying themselves wise and learned, **stagger about like blind men led by the blind (andhenaiva nīyamānā yathāndhāḥ).** *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.2.8)*
- **The remedy — renunciation and the teacher** — let the seeker, **fuel in hand (samit-pāṇi),** approach a teacher who is **learned in scripture and established in Brahman (śrotriyaṃ brahma-niṣṭham);** examining the worlds won by works and concluding that **"the uncreated is not got by what is done" (nāsty akṛtaḥ kṛtena),** he should turn in **dispassion (nirvedam āyāt)** to the higher knowledge. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 1.2.12–13)*

## 5. The Imperishable & the Two Birds (2–3)

- **The cosmic Puruṣa** — the **divine, formless Puruṣa** is described: from him arise breath, mind, the senses, the elements, the Vedas, sacrifices, worlds. **"Brahmaivedam amṛtam"** — all this is Brahman, the immortal. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 2.1.1–10)*
- **The bow-and-arrow meditation (praṇava)** — *"Take the great weapon of the Upaniṣad as a bow, fix on it the arrow sharpened by meditation; drawing it with the mind absorbed in That, hit the mark — the Imperishable. The bow is **Oṃ**, the arrow the self (ātman), the target Brahman."* *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 2.2.3–4)*
- **Dvā suparṇā — two birds on one tree** — *"dvā suparṇā sayujā sakhāyā samānaṃ vṛkṣaṃ pariṣasvajāte"* — **"Two birds, companions ever united, cling to the self-same tree. One of them eats the sweet fruit; the other looks on without eating."** The eating bird is the **jīva** (the embodied, enjoying self); the watching bird is the **witnessing Self (the supreme Ātman/Īśa).** *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 3.1.1)* — this verse is shared with the [Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad](./svetashvatara-upanishad.md) (4.6) and ultimately Ṛgveda 1.164.20.
- **The grief that ends in joy** — on the same tree the deluded jīva, sunk in its own helplessness, **grieves; but when it sees the other — the worshipped Lord — and his glory, its grief departs (juṣṭaṃ yadā paśyaty anyam īśam asya mahimānam, iti vīta-śokaḥ).** Recognising its identity with the witness is the end of sorrow. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 3.1.2)*
- **The Self won by truth, not by force** — **"This Ātman is not attained by the weak (nāyam ātmā balahīnena labhyaḥ),"** nor by heedlessness nor by tapas without the right marks; but the wise who strive by the proper means enter the abode of Brahman. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 3.2.3–4)*
- **The river-into-ocean simile of liberation** — **"yathā nadyaḥ syandamānāḥ samudre..."** — as flowing rivers disappear into the ocean, losing name and form, **so the knower, freed from name and form, attains the divine Puruṣa, higher than the high.** *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 3.2.8)*

## 6. "Satyam eva jayate" — Truth Alone Triumphs (3.1.6)

- **The national motto of India** — **"satyam eva jayate nānṛtaṃ"** — **"Truth alone triumphs, not falsehood."** The full verse: *"By truth is laid the divine path (deva-yāna) by which the sages, their desires fulfilled, ascend to where lies the supreme treasure won by truth."* *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 3.1.6)*
- **Adoption as state emblem** — the phrase **"Satyameva Jayate"** (in Devanāgarī) was adopted as the **national motto of the Republic of India**, inscribed below the **Lion Capital of Aśoka (Sārnāth)** in the State Emblem. It is among the most widely reproduced lines of any Upaniṣad. *(Source: Muṇḍaka Up. 3.1.6; State Emblem of India)* [scholarly]

## 7. Commentary & Reception

- **Śaṅkara's bhāṣya** — **Ādi Śaṅkara** (c. 8th c. CE) wrote a commentary (*Muṇḍakopaniṣad-bhāṣya*) on the text; it is a core member of his **Daśopaniṣad** commentary cycle and a principal source for the **parā/aparā** distinction in Advaita epistemology. Śaṅkara treats the Atharvan trio (Muṇḍaka, Praśna, Māṇḍūkya) as a connected set. *(Source: Śaṅkara, Muṇḍakopaniṣad-bhāṣya)* [scholarly]
- **Later commentators** — sub-commentaries (e.g. **Ānandagiri's** ṭīkā on Śaṅkara) and the wider Vedāntic tradition gloss the text; it is rendered in the standard scholarly editions (Hume's *Thirteen Principal Upanishads*, Radhakrishnan's *The Principal Upaniṣads*). *(Source: principal set)* — cross-text table in [`principal-upanishads-detail.md`](../principal-upanishads-detail.md).
- **Doctrinal afterlife** — the **two-knowledges** schema (parā/aparā), the **leaky-boats** critique of karma-kāṇḍa, and the **dvā suparṇā** image become standard reference-points across Vedānta; the spark-from-fire simile is a recurring scriptural warrant for emanationist cosmology.
