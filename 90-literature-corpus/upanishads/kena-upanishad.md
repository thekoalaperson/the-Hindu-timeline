## 1. Identity, Placement, Dating

- **Two names, one text** — Known as **Kena** from its opening word *kena* ("by whom?"), and as **Talavakāra** from its location: it is embedded in the **Talavakāra (Jaiminīya) Brāhmaṇa** of the **Sāmaveda** (specifically the Talavakāra-Āraṇyaka / Jaiminīya-Upaniṣad-Brāhmaṇa, 4th adhyāya). *(Source: Kena Up.; Jaiminīya / Talavakāra Brāhmaṇa)*
- **Parent Veda** — Belongs to the **Sāmaveda**, the only mukhya Upaniṣad of that Veda apart from the Chāndogya; both attach to Sāmavedic Brāhmaṇa material. *(Source: Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa)* — see [`vedas/samaveda.md`](../vedas/samaveda.md).
- **Placement in the canon** — Listed **2nd** in the **Daśopaniṣad** (the ten Śaṅkara commented on), in the mnemonic *"Īśa-Kena-Kaṭha-Praśna-Muṇḍa-Māṇḍūkya-Taittiri..."*. *(Source: Daśopaniṣad mnemonic; Muktikā Up.)* — catalogued in [`principal-upanishads-detail.md`](../principal-upanishads-detail.md).
- **The oldest verse-group Upaniṣad** — Academically grouped with the **metrical (verse) Upaniṣads** and generally placed **first** among them, ahead of Kaṭha, Īśa, Śvetāśvatara and Muṇḍaka. *(Source: Kena Up.; principal set)*
- **Dating (two clocks)** —
  - *Academic:* a **middle-period Upaniṣad**, roughly **mid-1st millennium BCE** (often c. 6th–5th c. BCE), composed after the older prose Upaniṣads (Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Chāndogya) but among the earliest of the verse texts. [scholarly]
  - *Variant (orthodox / traditional):* eternal **apauruṣeya Śruti**, "seen" (not authored) by ṛṣis and re-compiled by **Vyāsa** at the Dvāpara–Kali junction (~3102 BCE; the Kali-Yuga onset constant); not a "BCE-stratum composition." This file records both frames and corrects neither. [scripture vs. academic dating]
- **Structure** — **Four sections (khaṇḍas).** The **first two are in verse** (a terse philosophical hymn), the **last two in prose** (the yakṣa narrative and a closing teaching). This verse-then-prose composite is itself part of the dating discussion. *(Source: Kena Up. 1–4)*

## 2. The Verse Khaṇḍas (1–2) — Brahman Behind Mind & Senses

- **The opening question** — *"Keneṣitaṃ patati preṣitaṃ manaḥ / kena prāṇaḥ prathamaḥ praiti yuktaḥ..."* — **"By whom willed and directed does the mind alight on its objects? By whom commanded does the first breath (prāṇa) go forth? By whom is this speech (vāc) impelled? What god yokes the eye and the ear?"** A disciple asks: what is the conscious power *behind* the instruments of perception and action? *(Source: Kena Up. 1.1)*
- **The teacher's answer — the unseen seer** — Brahman is **"the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind, the speech of speech, the breath of breath, the eye of the eye."** The wise, withdrawing the senses from their objects and recognising this inner ground, become immortal. *(Source: Kena Up. 1.2)*
- **That which the mind cannot think** — the core formula, stated for each faculty in turn: **"That which the mind cannot think, but by which the mind thinks — know That alone to be Brahman, not this that people worship here."** And likewise: *"That which one cannot speak with speech, but by which speech is spoken... that which one cannot see with the eye, but by which the eye sees... cannot hear with the ear, but by which the ear hears... cannot breathe with breath, but by which breath is impelled — know That to be Brahman."* Brahman is the subject that can never become an object of its own instruments. *(Source: Kena Up. 1.4–8)*
- **"If you think you know Brahman well, you know little"** — the famous paradox of khaṇḍa 2: *"If you think 'I know it well,' you know but little of Brahman's form... I think not that I know it well, nor do I think I do not know it — he among us who knows that, knows it."* Brahman is **"unknown to those who know, and known to those who do not know"** (*avijñātaṃ vijānatāṃ vijñātam avijānatām*) — i.e. it cannot be grasped as an ordinary object of knowledge. *(Source: Kena Up. 2.1–3)*
- **Awakening as the fruit** — *"It is truly known when known in every state of consciousness (pratibodha-viditam)... by the Self one gains strength, and by knowledge immortality."* Realisation here-and-now, not after death, secures the deathless. *(Source: Kena Up. 2.4–5)*
- **Tad-vanam doctrine** — Brahman is named **Tad-vanam — "the Adorable / the Yearned-for," that which all beings long for (vana = "desire, longing").** It is to be **worshipped/contemplated as Tad-vanam**, and one who knows it thus is loved and yearned for by all beings. The name encodes Brahman as the ultimate object of all desire, the ground every craving secretly reaches toward. *(Source: Kena Up. 4.6 / closing verses)* [Tad-vanam upaniṣad — meditation-name]

## 3. The Prose Khaṇḍas (3–4) — The Yakṣa Parable

- **The setting — the gods grow proud** — **Brahman wins a victory for the gods (devas)**, and in that victory the gods exult, thinking the glory and greatness were **their own**, not seeing that their power was borrowed from Brahman. *(Source: Kena Up. 3.1)*
- **The appearance of the yakṣa** — To teach them, **Brahman manifests before them as a mysterious spirit, a yakṣa**, a being they cannot identify. The gods, unsettled, send their strongest to find out *"what is this yakṣa (spirit)?"* *(Source: Kena Up. 3.2–3)*
- **Agni fails** — **Agni** (the fire-god, called *Jātavedas*) is sent first. The yakṣa lays a blade of grass before him: *"Burn this."* Agni rushes at it with all his might **but cannot burn it**, and returns defeated, unable even to learn what the spirit is. *(Source: Kena Up. 3.4–6)*
- **Vāyu fails** — **Vāyu** (the wind-god, *Mātariśvan*) goes next. The yakṣa sets down the same blade: *"Blow this away."* Vāyu charges with all his force **but cannot move it**, and likewise returns having failed. *(Source: Kena Up. 3.7–9)*
- **Indra and the disappearance** — **Indra**, king of the gods, is sent last. As he approaches, **the yakṣa vanishes**; Indra alone does not flee but waits in that very spot. *(Source: Kena Up. 3.10–4.1)*
- **The revelation through Umā Haimavatī** — In the sky where the spirit had stood there appears **a woman, Umā Haimavatī** (Umā "daughter of Himavat," radiant and golden), the goddess of wisdom. She reveals to Indra that the yakṣa **was Brahman**, and that it was *in Brahman's victory* that the gods had been exulting. From her, the gods learn the truth. *(Source: Kena Up. 4.1–4)* — Umā here functions as the disclosing power of knowledge; later traditions identify her with Pārvatī/Śakti, but the text simply names her the revealer.
- **The hierarchy of the gods** — Because **Agni, Vāyu and Indra** drew nearest to Brahman and were first to touch the truth (Indra most of all, since he learned it directly), they are held to **excel the other gods**. *(Source: Kena Up. 4.2–3)*
- **The teaching of the parable** — the narrative dramatises the verse-doctrine: even the great elemental powers (fire, wind, the king of gods) have **no power of their own** — they cannot burn or blow a mere straw — apart from Brahman, the conscious ground from which all their faculties borrow strength. The senses' lesson (khaṇḍas 1–2) is re-staged on the cosmic stage (khaṇḍas 3–4). *(Source: Kena Up. 3–4; Śaṅkara bhāṣya)*

## 4. Commentaries & Reception

- **Śaṅkara's two bhāṣyas** — uniquely, **Ādi Śaṅkara** (c. 8th c. CE) wrote **two separate commentaries** on the Kena: a **Pada-bhāṣya** (a word-by-word gloss) and a **Vākya-bhāṣya** (a sentence/discourse commentary treating the meaning of whole passages). Both survive and are studied together; it is the only principal Upaniṣad he is held to have commented on twice in two distinct modes. *(Source: Śaṅkara, Kenopaniṣad pada- & vākya-bhāṣya)* [scholarly]
- **Other commentators** — later Vedāntic ācāryas and sub-commentators (e.g. **Ānandagiri's** ṭīkā on Śaṅkara) treat the text; it figures in the standard Daśopaniṣad commentary cycle. *(Source: commentarial tradition)*
- **Place in the principal set** — included in every traditional list of the **mukhya / principal Upaniṣads** and in the **Daśopaniṣad**; rendered in the standard scholarly editions (Hume's *Thirteen Principal Upanishads*, Radhakrishnan's *The Principal Upaniṣads*). *(Source: principal set)* — see the cross-text table in [`principal-upanishads-detail.md`](../principal-upanishads-detail.md).

## 5. Cross-Links & Significance

- **The epistemology of the witness** — the "mind of the mind, eye of the eye" formula is a foundational Vedānta statement of Brahman/Ātman as the **un-objectifiable witness (sākṣin)** — that by which all knowing occurs and which therefore can never itself be a known object. It pairs naturally with the four-states analysis of the Māṇḍūkya: [`mandukya-upanishad.md`](./mandukya-upanishad.md).
- **Sāmavedic siblings** — the Kena and the **Chāndogya** are the two mukhya Upaniṣads of the Sāmaveda; both arise from Sāmavedic Brāhmaṇa-Āraṇyaka strata. See [`chandogya-upanishad.md`](./chandogya-upanishad.md) and the parent [`vedas/samaveda.md`](../vedas/samaveda.md).
- **Devas as borrowers of power** — the yakṣa parable is a key scriptural locus for the doctrine that **the gods' powers are derivative**, not self-existent — a theme that recurs across Purāṇic theology. The episode's goddess **Umā Haimavatī** is an early Śruti appearance of the wisdom-revealing feminine.
- **Śruti two-clocks** — for the scriptural-vs-academic framing of when this text "is," see [`shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md`](../shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md).
