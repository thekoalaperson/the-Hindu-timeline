# Śukasaptati — the Seventy Tales of the Parrot

> Parent: [Literature Corpus](README.md) · A Sanskrit frame-tale collection (**kathā**) in which a household **parrot** holds the merchant's wife **Prabhāvatī** back from adultery for seventy cliff-hanger nights until her husband returns — a worldly, often bawdy **clever-wife** cycle, sibling to the [Pañcatantra/Hitopadeśa fable stream](panchatantra-hitopadesha-and-fable-literature.md) and the [Bṛhatkathā story-ocean](brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md). It claims **no yuga or manvantara address**: it sits wholly in ordinary human **Kali-Yuga documented history** on the academic clock (**[scholarly]** extant recensions c. 12th c. CE, older core). Its chief afterlife is the Persian **Ṭūṭī-nāma**, ancestor of the illustrated Mughal parrot-books.

---

## Orientation: what the Śukasaptati is

- **"Seventy tales of the parrot"** — *śuka* ("parrot") + *saptati* ("seventy"): a Sanskrit **frame-narrative** of the same architecture as the Pañcatantra, the Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati and the Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā — an outer story that boxes a fixed numbered set of inner tales, each sealed on a dilemma. Here the boxing device is a **talking parrot that tells one tale per night** to a wife on the point of infidelity, breaking off at the crisis so she must wait, and waiting saves her. (Śukasaptati, frame) **[scholarly]**
- **A "clever-wife" cycle, not a nīti-śāstra** — where the [Hitopadeśa](hitopadesha.md) teaches statecraft through beast-fable, the Śukasaptati is **secular entertainment with a moral alibi**: its inner tales are overwhelmingly about **women (and men) caught in adultery who wriggle free by sheer wit** — equivocal oaths, disguises, ready lies, staged accidents. The frame's ostensible lesson is chastity; the tales' actual delight is cunning. It is the corpus's frankest, most **erotic and comic** *kathā*. **[scholarly]**
- **Rank on the canon ladder** — neither Śruti nor Smṛti: worldly **kathā/kāvya**, didactic-entertainment literature, **[late text]** relative to the Vedic–epic core. Its stories are magpie-gathered from older reservoirs — Jātaka tales, the [Kathāsaritsāgara](brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md), the Pañcatantra — so the *collection* is late even where individual *tales* are ancient. **[scholarly]**
- **The count vs the title** — the title advertises **seventy**, but as transmitted the work runs to roughly **72 narrative units** (the outer frame + about 71 parrot-told tales); recensions differ in exactly which tales are counted and included, so "seventy" is the round emblematic number, not a fixed manuscript tally. **[scholarly]/[disputed]**

## The two clocks

- *Traditional / narrative frame* — the work invokes **no cosmic address at all**: no kalpa, no manvantara, no yuga is named. Its world is the everyday human bazaar — a merchant's house, a trading voyage, a lonely wife, a procuress next door. This placelessness in cosmic time is itself a genre-marker of *kathā*: pure worldly *kāma/artha* story, set in ordinary un-dated human time.
- *Academic / historical frame* — the Śukasaptati reached its **surviving shape by about the 12th century CE**; the **oldest known manuscript is only 15th-century**, and the individual tales are older still. **Csaba Töttössy** argued on sociopolitical grounds for an archetype in the **early 9th century CE**; there is no external anchor for an author or a firm date. **[scholarly]/[disputed]** For the documented-history spine, see [recorded-history.md](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/recorded-history.md).

## The frame-story: how the parrot keeps the vigil

*(Śukasaptati, outer frame; details vary between the simplicior and ornatior recensions.)*

- **Madana Vinoda, the merchant's wayward son** — in a merchant household, the son **Madana Vinoda** (Madanavinoda) is dissolute and neglects his duties. A **Brahmin friend gives the family a wise, talking parrot** (in some tellings a pair of birds, a parrot **śuka** and a myna-hen **sārikā**) to steady him. By its counsel the parrot reforms Madana and redirects him to honest livelihood. (Śukasaptati, frame)
- **The husband sails, the wife is left with the bird** — restored to purpose, Madana **departs on a long trading voyage**, leaving his wife **Prabhāvatī** alone in the house — and, crucially, **in the charge of the parrot**, entrusted as guardian of the household and of her virtue. (Śukasaptati, frame)
  - *Variant (two-bird recension):* where a **myna (sārikā)** shares the frame, she reproves Prabhāvatī **bluntly** for contemplating a lover and is nearly killed for it — teaching the parrot that only **indirection**, not rebuke, will work. The parrot's whole seventy-night strategy is a lesson in *how* to counsel a determined heart. **[scholarly]**
- **Prabhāvatī tempted** — pining in her husband's absence and egged on by **dissolute women / a procuress**, Prabhāvatī resolves to slip out by night to a lover. Each evening she dresses and moves to leave. (Śukasaptati, frame)
- **The parrot's stratagem** — the parrot does **not forbid** her. It **grants the legitimacy of desire** — "go, by all means" — but adds that a woman of spirit should be able to extricate herself from any scrape as cleverly as the heroine of *this* little story… and begins a tale, **breaking off at the crisis** and asking, in effect, *could you match her wit if you were caught?* Fascinated, and unwilling to leave the tale unfinished, **Prabhāvatī stays to hear the end** — and the night is safely spent. (Śukasaptati, frame) **[scholarly]**
- **Seventy nights, then the return** — the device repeats **night after night for seventy nights**, each tale postponing the assignation, until on the **seventieth night Madana returns home**. Prabhāvatī, her intended sin never consummated, **confesses and thanks the parrot** for guarding her. (Śukasaptati, frame)
  - *Variant (the parrot's plea):* in the closing movement the parrot's last tale functions as **an intercession** — a plea for forgiveness on the ground that Prabhāvatī was **not wholly responsible** for a lapse she never completed, so the marriage is restored without punishment. **[scholarly]**
  - *Note (no divine-parrot reveal):* unlike the Kathāsaritsāgara frame, the standard Śukasaptati does **not** end by unmasking the parrot as a cursed celestial returning to heaven; it remains, in the received text, simply the wisest member of the household. Claims of a curse-release ending are **not supported** by the main recensions. **[disputed]**

## Character of the inner tales

*(Śukasaptati, the seventy parrot-narrations; individual tales are numbered within each recension but their contents and order differ between simplicior and ornatior — cited here at the recension level, not by fabricated tale-numbers.)*

- **The equivocal-oath ordeal** — the cycle's signature tale-type: a wife suspected of adultery must **pass a public chastity ordeal** — walking between the legs of a **Yakṣa image** (or grasping it), which only the truly faithful can do. She has her **lover disguise himself as a madman** and embrace her in the crowd on the way, so that she can swear, with literal truth, that **"no man has touched me but my husband and this lunatic"** — and passes the ordeal. The identical "ambiguous oath" motif travels west into the medieval European Tristan-and-Isolde ordeal. (Śukasaptati, simplicior) **[scholarly]**
- **The father-and-son lovers** — a tale of a woman entangled with **both a father and his son** as lovers, and the comic machinery by which the overlapping affairs are concealed — an ancestor of the "who-is-in-the-house" bedroom farce. (Śukasaptati) **[folk]**
- **The Gaṇapati idol and the ass** — one of the frankly transgressive tales, mingling a wife's ruse with a **Gaṇeśa image and a donkey**, blending scatological comedy and irreverence toward sanctimony — a reminder that the collection was told for **amusement**, its piety worn lightly. (Śukasaptati, simplicior) **[folk]/[disputed]**
- **Borrowed tales** — several inner stories are **lifted or reworked from the Pañcatantra** and the wider *kathā* stock, refitted to the parrot's chastity-frame; the Śukasaptati is as much an **anthology** of migrating tale-types as an original work. **[scholarly]**
- **The recurring engine** — across the seventy, the constant is the **quick-witted woman (or man) who converts an about-to-be-fatal exposure into escape** by a ready word or a staged trick; the tales prize *pratyutpanna-mati* (presence of mind) exactly as the Pañcatantra prizes *nīti*. **[scholarly]**

## The recensions

- **Textus simplicior** — the **plainer, prose-dominant** recension, surviving in multiple manuscripts; it is the version behind most early European translations. Its authorship is **uncertain**; a Brahmin named **Cintāmaṇi Bhaṭṭa** is sometimes named, but the ascription is **not secured by the colophons**. **[scholarly]/[disputed]** (Śukasaptati, simplicior; ed. Richard Schmidt)
- **Textus ornatior** — the **more elaborate, ornamented** recension, richer in verse and rhetorical polish; more moralizing and literary in cast. **[scholarly]** (Śukasaptati, ornatior; ed. Richard Schmidt)
- **Textus elegantior** — a **minor third** recension, known from a single incomplete manuscript. **[scholarly]**
- **Not a parent-and-child pair** — scholarship treats the simplicior and ornatior as **parallel developments from a lost common archetype**, not as one derived directly from the other; hence the corpus rule applies — **their divergences are cataloged, not merged**. **[scholarly]**

## Transmission and afterlife: from parrot to Ṭūṭī-nāma

- **Into Persian: the Ṭūṭī-nāma** — the Sanskrit parrot-book is the **direct ancestor of the Persian *Ṭūṭī-nāma* ("Book of the Parrot")**. **Ziyāʾ al-Dīn Nakhshabī**, a Persian physician and Sufi, produced the standard Persian recasting **c. 1330–1335 CE**, reworking the material into **fifty-two tales told over fifty-two nights** — the seventy of the Sanskrit compressed and Islamized. **[scholarly]**
  - *Note (an earlier Persian layer):* Nakhshabī worked from an **older, cruder Persian version** of the parrot-cycle (the *Javāhir al-asmār* of ʿImād ibn Muḥammad, made for the Delhi Sultanate court **c. 1313–1315 CE**), which he refined and shortened — so the Sanskrit→Persian passage runs through at least **two Persian stages**. **[scholarly]/[disputed]**
- **The illustrated Mughal parrot-books** — the Ṭūṭī-nāma became a favourite of the **Mughal atelier**. An early illustrated manuscript was made for the young emperor **Akbar** in the years after his 1556 accession, its miniatures directed by the master painters **Mīr Sayyid ʿAlī and ʿAbd al-Ṣamad**; it survives **almost entirely in the Cleveland Museum of Art** (with leaves elsewhere). A **second Akbar-period Ṭūṭī-nāma (c. 1580)** is held mainly at the **Chester Beatty Library, Dublin**. **[scholarly]**
- **Onward diffusion** — from Persian the parrot-cycle passed into **Turkish** and other languages; the **first Western scholarly access** came through Richard Schmidt's German edition and translation of the Sanskrit recensions (1890s). The Śukasaptati thus sits in the same **great eastward-and-westward tale-migration** as its cousin the Pañcatantra (which travelled as *Kalīla wa-Dimna*). **[scholarly]**
- **Place in the frame-tale family** — within Sanskrit, the Śukasaptati is one of the four great **numbered emboxing cycles** — beside the Pañcatantra (five books), the **Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati** (25 corpse-riddles) and the **Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā** (32 throne-tales) — all sharing the "fixed set of tales released one at a time by a narrating device" architecture. See the [Bṛhatkathā / Kathāsaritsāgara & Vikramāditya cycle](brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md). **[scholarly]**
