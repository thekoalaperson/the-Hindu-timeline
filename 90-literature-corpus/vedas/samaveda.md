# Samaveda — Per-Text Profile (Chant & Melody)

[Vedas](./README.md) · [Literature Corpus](../README.md) · [Master Index](../../README.md)
The "Veda of melodies" (sāman): a liturgical songbook of ~1,875 chant-units whose words are almost entirely borrowed from the Rigveda but resung to fixed melodies (sāman). Sung by the **Udgātṛ** priest at the Soma sacrifice; the historical fountainhead of Indian classical music. Companion profiles: [rigveda.md](./rigveda.md), [yajurveda.md](./yajurveda.md). Broader maps: [shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md](../shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md), [vedic-deities-and-suktas.md](../vedic-deities-and-suktas.md), [yajnas-and-vedic-rituals.md](../yajnas-and-vedic-rituals.md).

## Overview and Scale

- **Definition** - *Sāma* ("song, melody, chant") + *veda* ("knowledge"); the Samhita of melodies (sāman) to which Rigvedic verses (ṛc) are set for singing at the Soma sacrifice. A sāman = a melody; the ṛc supplies the words. The maxim: "the ṛc is the word, the sāman is the song laid upon it." (Samaveda Samhita)
- **Scale** - **~1,875 chant-verses** in the Kauthuma recension as ordinarily counted (Griffith's translation), reducing to **1,549 distinct verses** once repetitions are removed. (Samaveda — Kauthuma count)
- **Almost all borrowed** - **All but ~75 verses** are taken from the Rigveda; only ~75 are original ("apparently new") to the Samaveda. The Samaveda is thus less a new corpus of words than a new way of *singing* the Rig. (Samaveda Samhita)
- **Source mandalas** - The borrowed verses are drawn overwhelmingly from **Rigveda Mandala 9 (Soma Pavamana) and Mandala 8 (Kanva)** — exactly the Soma-pressing and strophic material that suits Soma-sacrifice chant. See [rigveda.md](./rigveda.md). (Samaveda)
  - *Variant (count):* the "75 new verses" figure is the most cited; some sources give 78 or 99 unique verses depending on recension and method of counting variant readings. [disputed]
- **Two clocks** - *Scripture clock:* apaurusheya shruti, "heard" by the rishis at the dawn of the present order, transmitted orally without corruption; the sāman-melody is held to be eternal sound. *Academic clock:* the chant-collection took shape **c. 1200–1000 BCE (or slightly later)**, roughly contemporary with the Yajurveda and Atharvaveda and after the Rigveda; Witzel cautions there is "no absolute dating" for the Samaveda. [scholarly]
- **The Udgātṛ priest** - The Samaveda is the manual of the **Udgātṛ** ("singer aloud"), one of the four chief Soma-sacrifice priests; he *sings* the sāmans while the Hotṛ (Rigveda) recites, the Adhvaryu (Yajurveda) performs the acts, and the Brahman oversees. See [yajnas-and-vedic-rituals.md](../yajnas-and-vedic-rituals.md). (Vedic priesthood)

## Two-Part Architecture: Ārcika (Verse-Books) + Gāna (Song-Books)

- **The dual structure** - The Samaveda has two complementary halves: the **Ārcika** (verse collections — the bare texts/ṛc with their accents) and the **Gāna** (song collections — the actual melodies, with vowel-stretching, syllable-insertion, and refrains). A verse in the Ārcika corresponds to a melody in the Gāna; one must read them together to reconstruct a chant. (Samaveda structure)
- **Ārcika → two parts** - Subdivided into **Pūrvārcika** ("former verse-book") and **Uttarārcika** ("latter verse-book"). (Samaveda)
- **Gāna → four collections** - Subdivided into four melody-books: **Grāmageya-gāna, Araṇyageya-gāna, Ūha-gāna, Ūhya-gāna** (the last sometimes called Rahasya-gāna). (Samaveda — gānas)

## Pūrvārcika (Former Verse-Book)

- **Pūrvārcika** - The opening collection, **585 single stanzas**, arranged in order of **deity** (Agni group, then Indra, then Soma Pavamana, etc.) — a praise-anthology used to learn the basic melodies. Each stanza is sung to one of the Grāmageya/Araṇyageya tunes. (Samaveda — Purvarchika)
- **Single-verse basis** - The Pūrvārcika is constituted on a **single-verse** basis (one ṛc = one melodic unit), serving as the primer of sāman tunes from which the ritual chants are later built. (Samaveda)
- **Internal divisions** - Traditionally parsed into the Āgneya-, Aindra-, and Pāvamāna-kāṇḍas (Agni / Indra / Soma sections), plus an Āraṇyaka-kāṇḍa whose stanzas belong to forest (esoteric) chanting. (Purvarchika sub-kandas)

## Uttarārcika (Latter Verse-Book)

- **Uttarārcika** - The larger collection, arranged not by deity but by **ritual sequence** (the order in which chants fall during the Soma rites). (Samaveda — Uttararchika)
- **Triplet basis** - Constituted on **units of three verses** (tṛca) — the standard liturgical building block, since a single sāman in performance typically spreads across a verse-triplet. The Uttarārcika supplies the texts that the Ūha/Ūhya song-books then melodically realize for actual sacrifices. (Samaveda)

## The Four Gānas (Melody-Books)

- **Grāmageya-gāna ("village-sung")** - Melodies meant for **public chanting in the settlement** (grāma); the exoteric, openly-taught tunes set to the Pūrvārcika verses. Sometimes called Veya-gāna. (Samaveda — gana classes)
- **Araṇyageya-gāna ("forest-sung")** - Melodies for **private, meditative chanting in the forest** (araṇya); esoteric tunes regarded as potent/secret, learned and sung apart from the village. (Samaveda — gana classes)
- **Ūha-gāna ("modified/transposed song")** - The Grāmageya melodies **re-adapted (ūha = "rearrangement")** to the actual verse-order of the Soma rituals as found in the Uttarārcika; the working performance-book for the public Soma chant. (Samaveda — Uha)
- **Ūhya-gāna / Rahasya-gāna ("secret modified song")** - The Araṇyageya melodies **re-adapted** to ritual order; the esoteric counterpart of the Ūha. Ūha and Ūhya were the books a chanter actually used when officiating at Soma sacrifices. (Samaveda — Uhya/Rahasya)
- **Seven ritual divisions of Ūha/Ūhya** - The Ūha and Ūhya gānas of the Uttarārcika are classed by sacrifice-type into seven parts: **Daśarātra, Saṃvatsara, Ekāha, Ahīna, Sattra, Kṣudra, and Prāyaścitta** — i.e. ten-day rites, year-long rites, one-day rites, multi-day rites, sacrificial sessions, minor chants, and expiatory chants. (Samaveda — Uha/Uhya divisions)

## Anatomy of a Sāman (How a Verse Becomes a Song)

- **Five (or seven) parts (bhakti)** - A fully sung sāman is divided into melodic limbs (bhakti): typically **Prastāva** (prelude, by the Prastotṛ), **Udgītha** (the main loud song, by the Udgātṛ, opened with Om), **Pratihāra** (response, by the Pratihartṛ), **Upadrava**, and **Nidhana** (the cadential close); an introductory **Hiṃkāra** (the syllable "hum") and **Ādi** are sometimes counted, giving seven. (Sama chant structure)
- **Stobha syllables** - Melodic filler-syllables with no lexical meaning — *hā u, hā i, o vā, hum, hoyi, irā* — inserted to carry the tune and stretch vowels; the sung text therefore differs markedly from the written ṛc. (Stobha)
- **Vikāra / transformation** - The four standard alterations a ṛc undergoes to become singable: vowel lengthening (atikrama), insertion of stobhas, repetition (abhyāsa), and breaking words across notes (vikāra/viśleṣaṇa). (Sama transformation)

## The Udgātṛ and His Assistants

- **Udgātṛ ("the singer aloud")** - Chief singing-priest of the Soma sacrifice; he sings the Udgītha, the central loud portion of each sāman, always beginning with **Om**. The Samaveda exists to equip him. (Vedic priesthood)
- **Three assistant singers** - The Udgātṛ heads a quartet: **Prastotṛ** (sings the Prastāva/prelude), **Pratihartṛ** (sings the Pratihāra/response), and the **Subrahmaṇya** (utters the special Subrahmaṇyā invocation summoning Indra). (Soma-sacrifice priesthood)
- **Tripartite knowledge (trayī vidyā)** - The Chāndogya likens Om to the threefold Veda because "the Adhvaryu invokes with it, the Hotṛ recites with it, and the Udgātṛ sings with it" — the Udgātṛ's act being singing (gāna). (Chandogya Upanishad)

## The Udgītha and Om

- **Udgītha = Om** - The Chāndogya Upanishad (opening chapters) identifies the syllable **Om** with the **Udgītha** itself ("om ity etad akṣaram udgītham upāsīta" — "let one meditate on this syllable Om as the udgītha"). Om is the seed and essence of all chant. (Chandogya Upanishad 1.1)
- **Hierarchy of essences** - The Chāndogya teaches a ladder of essences: earth → water → plants → man → speech → Ṛgveda → **Sāmaveda → and the essence of the Sāmaveda is the Udgītha (Om)** — making Om the innermost sap of the entire ritual world. (Chandogya Upanishad)
- **Cosmogonic Om** - "Heaven and Earth were born at the opening of the Om"; in later musical theology the single primal Om, unfolded, manifests as the seven notes — as white light disperses into a spectrum. [folk/late gloss]

## Surviving Shakhas (Recensions)

- **Three surviving branches** - Of the many Samavedic shakhas tradition once knew (Patañjali's number is famously large), only three substantially survive: **Kauthuma, Rāṇāyanīya, and Jaiminīya (Talavakāra)**. (Samaveda shakhas)
- **Kauthuma shakha** - The most prominent and widely studied recension, prevalent in **northern and western India** (Gujarat); supplies the standard ~1,875-verse text and the Pañcaviṃśa/Chāndogya Brahmana literature; melodies notated with **numerals above the syllables**. [scholarly]
- **Rāṇāyanīya shakha** - A close cousin of the Kauthuma, prevalent in **Maharashtra, Karnataka, and parts of Odisha / Andhra**; shares the Pañcaviṃśa (Tāṇḍya) Brahmana with the Kauthuma; differs mainly in melodic detail and notation. (Ranayaniya)
- **Jaiminīya / Talavakāra shakha** - The most distinct recension, preserved in the **south (Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka)** by a dwindling number of families; its own Samhita, the **Jaiminīya Brahmana** and **Jaiminīya Upaniṣad Brahmana (Talavakāra)**, and a notably different, more elaborate chant-style. The Kerala Jaiminīya tradition is now critically endangered. [folk/endangered]

## Attached Brahmanas

- **Pañcaviṃśa / Tāṇḍya Mahābrahmana** - The principal Brahmana of the **Kauthuma–Rāṇāyanīya** shakhas; named "of twenty-five" (pañcaviṃśa) for its 25 chapters; the great manual of Soma-sacrifice chant (incl. the year-long Sattra rites and the legendary Vrātya-stomas). (Panchavimsha Brahmana)
- **Ṣaḍviṃśa Brahmana** - "The twenty-sixth," an appendix to the Pañcaviṃśa; its last part is the **Adbhuta Brahmana**, a treatise on portents and omens. (Shadvimsha)
- **Jaiminīya Brahmana** - The principal Brahmana of the **Jaiminīya** shakha; rich in narrative myth and ritual lore, partly parallel to the Pañcaviṃśa. (Jaiminiya Brahmana)
- **Other Samavedic Brahmanas (Kauthuma)** - **Ārṣeya** (an index of sāman-names and their seers), **Devatādhyāya** (deities of the chants), **Saṃhitopaniṣad** (theory of chant-construction), **Vaṃśa** (teacher-lineage), and **Sāmavidhāna** (magical/ritual application of sāmans) Brahmanas. (Samaveda Brahmana corpus)
- **Jaiminīya Upaniṣad Brahmana (Talavakāra)** - The Jaiminīya school's esoteric Brahmana, embedding the Kena Upanishad. (Talavakara Brahmana)

## Attached Upanishads

- **Chāndogya Upanishad** - One of the oldest and largest principal Upanishads, embedded in the Chāndogya Brahmana of the **Kauthuma (Tāṇḍya)** school; named from *chandoga* ("singer of sāmans"). Home of the Udgītha-Om meditations and the great mahāvākya **"tat tvam asi" ("That thou art")** taught by Uddālaka Āruṇi to Śvetaketu. Foundational for Vedānta. See [principal-upanishads-detail.md](../principal-upanishads-detail.md). (Chandogya Upanishad)
- **Kena Upanishad (Talavakāra Upanishad)** - A short principal Upanishad embedded in the **Jaiminīya Upaniṣad Brahmana**; opens "kena-īṣitam" ("by whom impelled?") and probes the Brahman behind mind, speech, and senses (the "Brahman that the senses cannot grasp but by which they grasp"). See [principal-upanishads-detail.md](../principal-upanishads-detail.md). (Kena Upanishad)

## Root of Indian Classical Music

- **Oldest notated melodies** - Samavedic chant carries the **world's oldest surviving musical notation**, written as numerals or syllables above (or within) the verse-line; different shakhas use different notation systems. (Samaveda — notation)
- **The seven svaras (sapta-svara)** - Sāmagāna is sung on a scale of **seven notes**, the historical seed of the Indian heptatonic scale (sā ṛ ga ma pa dha ni). In the Samavedic naming they are **Krushṭa, Prathama, Dvitīya, Tṛtīya, Caturtha, Mandra, Atisvārya** — and, distinctively, the sāman scale **descends** (highest note first), the reverse of the later ascending classical sargam. (Sapta-svara; Sama notation)
- **Descent into the gandharva tradition** - Indian classical music (and dance) is traditionally rooted in the sonic dimension of the Samaveda; the rules of melody, instrument (the **vīṇā**), and rhythm were later codified in the **Gandharva-Veda**, the upaveda attached to the Samaveda. See [upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md](../upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md). (Gandharva-Veda)
- **Three to seven notes, historically** - Earliest sāman-singing may have used three notes (udātta–anudātta–svarita accents), expanding to four, five, six, and finally seven — the line of descent later scholars trace to the full rāga system. [scholarly]

## Traditional vs Academic Dating

- **Scripture clock** - Apaurusheya and eternal; the melody (sāman) is uncreated sound "heard" by rishis; no human date — the Vedas span the current Vaivasvata Manvantara. (Traditional view)
- **Academic clock** - The chant-collection assembled **c. 1200–1000 BCE**, drawing on an already-fixed Rigveda; contemporary with Yajurveda/Atharvaveda. The Brahmanas follow (c. 1000–700 BCE) and the Chāndogya/Kena Upanishads cluster **c. 8th–6th century BCE**. No absolute date is recoverable (Witzel). [scholarly]
