# Rigveda Samvada & Secular Hymns — the Dialogue and Narrative Genre

[Vedas](./README.md) · [Literature Corpus](../README.md) · [Master Index](../../README.md)
Companion to the mandala map [rigveda.md](./rigveda.md): a catalog of the Rigveda's **akhyana/samvada** (narrative/dialogue) hymns and its "secular" poems — gambling, frogs, a chariot race, a ribald marital quarrel, a wedding, an ecstatic long-haired muni, and the gift-praise (*danastuti*) genre. *Scripture clock:* shruti, heard by the rishis at the dawn of the present order; *academic clock:* most of these sit in the late strata (Mandalas 1, 8, 10), c. 1200–1000 BCE, with the frog hymn (Mandala 7) and Vamadeva's birth-hymn (Mandala 4) in the older family books. [scholarly]

## The Genre: Akhyana and Samvada

- **Samvada-sukta ("dialogue hymn")** — hymns cast as exchanges between two or more speakers with no narrative framing inside the text itself: Yama–Yami (10.10), Pururavas–Urvashi (10.95), Sarama–Panis (10.108), Agastya–Lopamudra (1.179), Indra–Indrani–Vrishakapi (10.86), Vishvamitra and the rivers (3.33), Indra and his mother (4.18). Later tradition (Yaska's *Nirukta*; the *Brihaddevata*) supplies the connecting stories the hymns presuppose. (Nirukta; Brihaddevata)
- **The akhyana theory [scholarly]** — Hermann Oldenberg's hypothesis: these hymns are the fixed *verse* portions of old prose-plus-verse narrations (akhyana), whose prose links were improvised and lost — explaining why the dialogues read as disconnected speeches. Widely influential, still debated. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Seeds of epic and drama [scholarly]** — the samvadas are commonly treated as ancestors of the itihasa dialogue-frame (the Mahabharata is built of samvadas) and, in one older theory (L. von Schroeder's ritual-drama reading), of Sanskrit theatre performed as ritual mime. Suggestive, not proven. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **"Secular" with a caveat** — hymns like the dice lament or the frog hymn are the Rigveda's closest approach to non-liturgical poetry, but each still ends in prayer or was given a ritual use (rain-charm, expiation); "secular" is a modern label of convenience. [scholarly]

## The Gambler's Lament (RV 10.34)

- **The poem** — a gambler's first-person confession in 14 verses: the **vibhidaka-nut dice** ("the trembling hazel-brown ones, born on the great tree in the windy place") intoxicate him like Soma from **Mount Mujavant**; he keeps returning to the gaming-hall vowing "this time I win." One of the very few Rigvedic hymns with no god as its subject. (Rigveda 10.34)
- **The ruin** — the dice are "hooked, piercing, deceitful"; they burn the loser yet seem honey-sweet. His devoted wife is estranged and pushed away, his mother-in-law hates him, others fondle the wife of the man whose wealth the dice have seized; he wanders at night an anxious debtor while his family declares "we know him not — lead him away bound." (Rigveda 10.34.2–11)
- **Savitr's counsel** — the famous turn (v.13): the god **Savitr** admonishes, *"akshair ma divyah, krishim it krishasva"* — "Play not with dice; plough your field instead; delight in your property, esteeming it enough." The gambler begs the dice to release him and befriend another. (Rigveda 10.34.13–14)
- **Seer attribution** — the Anukramani names the seer as **Kavasha Ailusha** (or, in an alternate reckoning, "Aksha Maujavant" — the dice themselves personified as seer). [disputed]
- **Afterlife of the motif** — the dicing-ruin theme re-erupts in the epics: Nala's possession by Kali at dice ([nala-damayanti.md](../../04-deep-dives/nala-damayanti.md)) and Yudhishthira's disastrous match; the Vedic lament is the oldest Indian text on gambling addiction. [scholarly]

## The Frog Hymn (RV 7.103)

- **The scene** — in the Vasishtha mandala: frogs who have lain silent for a year "like brahmins keeping a vow" find their voices when the monsoon breaks; one repeats the call of another "as a pupil repeats his teacher's words," their chorus around the pool likened to brahmins around the Soma bowl at the overnight *atiratra* rite. (Rigveda 7.103)
- **Gomayu and Ajamayu** — one frog "lows like a cow," another "bleats like a goat" (*gomayu*, *ajamayu*); speckled and green, they raise their voices together when the rains fulfil their year-long longing. (Rigveda 7.103.6–7, 10)
- **The boon** — the closing verses pray that the frogs — "the cow-voiced, the goat-voiced, the speckled, the green" — grant riches and **prolong life**, having "given cows in hundreds" in the rain-bringing rite. (Rigveda 7.103.10)
  - *Variant (ritual reading — Sayana and successors):* a **rain-charm**, the frogs praised as genuine rain-bringing powers (a reading supported by the Atharvan use of frogs in cooling/rain rites). [scholarly]
  - *Variant (satirical reading — Max Müller and others):* an early **satire of priests**, the croaking brahmin-frogs mocking mechanical recitation; modern scholarship often holds both at once — a real rain-panegyric with a knowing smile. [scholarly] [disputed]

## The Keshin Hymn — the Long-Haired Muni (RV 10.136)

- **The Keshin ("long-haired one")** — seven verses on ecstatic silent sages (**munis**) outside the sacrificial mainstream: long-haired, clothed in tawny/soiled robes (*mala*) or "girdled with the wind" (**vatarashana**, i.e., naked), they "ride the winds" where the gods have entered them. (Rigveda 10.136)
- **The flight** — "Crazed with silent sagehood (*mauneya*) we have mounted the winds — you mortals see only our bodies"; the muni flies through the mid-region, comrade and "sweetest friend" of **Vayu**, sent on the errand of the gods, dwelling in both eastern and western oceans, walking the paths of apsarases, gandharvas, and wild beasts. (Rigveda 10.136.2–6)
- **The poison-cup with Rudra** — the final verse: the Keshin "drank with **Rudra** from the poison-cup" (*keshi vishasya patrena yad rudrena apibat saha*) — the earliest textual pairing of the ecstatic ascetic with Rudra, read as seed of the later Shiva-yogi complex; some see a proto-reference echoed in Shiva the poison-drinker of the ocean-churning. See [shiva-myth-cycle.md](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md). (Rigveda 10.136.7) [scholarly on the linkage]
- **Proto-yogi status [scholarly]** — the standard modern citation for pre-Upanishadic ecstatic asceticism: evidence that a non-priestly path of trance, wandering, and psychotropic or breath-borne ecstasy ("he drinks with Rudra") existed alongside the yajna cult; frequently placed at the head of histories of yoga. See [yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/yoga-paths-and-patanjali.md). [scholarly] [disputed as to how much "yoga" is really present]
- **Vatarashana rishis later** — the Bhagavata and Taittiriya traditions reuse *vatarashana* for naked ascetic rishis (linked by Jain tradition to Rishabha's followers) — a later reception of this hymn's imagery. [late text] [disputed]

## Indra, Indrani, and Vrishakapi (RV 10.86)

- **The quarrel** — a 23-verse three-way dialogue: **Indrani** rages that the tawny ape **Vrishakapi** ("bull-ape"), Indra's pet/favourite, has defiled her cherished things; Indra placates both wife and ape in turn; each verse closes with the deadpan refrain ***"vishvasmad Indra uttarah"*** — "Indra is supreme over all." (Rigveda 10.86)
- **The ribaldry** — the exchange is famously obscene by Vedic standards: Indrani boasts of her sexual desirability and threatens the ape; sexual taunts fly in both directions before reconciliation and Vrishakapi's return. The Anukramani lists **Indra, Indrani, and Vrishakapi themselves as the seers**, making Indrani a canonical rishika — see [rigvedic-women-seers-brahmavadinis.md](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/rigvedic-women-seers-brahmavadinis.md). (Rigveda 10.86; Anukramani)
- **What it "is"** — no consensus: readings include a fertility-ritual libretto (von Schroeder's ritual-drama), court comedy, a solar/monkey allegory, and a satire on cult rivalry (Vrishakapi's offerings diverting Indra's). Catalogued, not resolved. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Vrishakapi later** — the name resurfaces as an epithet in epic-Puranic lists (of Shiva, and among Rudras), a reuse rather than the same figure. [late text]

## Suryaa's Wedding — the Vivaha Charter (RV 10.85)

- **The marriage of Suryaa** — 47 verses: **Suryaa**, daughter of **Surya/Savitr**, is wed to **Soma**; the **Ashvins** were the wooers/groomsmen, and the wedding procession — her chariot, the two shining wheels, the mind-made vehicle — carries her to the husband's house. Cross-link [surya-and-his-family.md](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/surya-and-his-family.md), [chandra-soma.md](../../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/chandra-soma.md). (Rigveda 10.85.6–20)
- **Soma the moon vs Soma the plant** — the hymn opens with the riddle that "one thinks one has drunk Soma when the plant is crushed; the Soma the brahmins know, of him no earthly one tastes" — an explicit moon/plant double identity of the bridegroom. (Rigveda 10.85.3–5)
- **The sequence of husbands** — the bride's guardians in order: "**Soma** obtained her first; the **Gandharva** obtained her next; **Agni** was your third husband; your fourth is born of man" — the classical charter verse for the bride's passage through divine guardianships to the human groom. (Rigveda 10.85.40–41)
- **The vivaha liturgy** — verses of this hymn are the core of the Hindu wedding rite as codified in the Grihya-sutras and still recited: the hand-clasp "I take your hand for good fortune" (*gribhnami te...*, v.36), the blessing "be queen (*samrajni*) over your father-in-law, mother-in-law, sisters- and brothers-in-law" (v.46), and the prayer for the couple's unbroken union. The **Suryaa-sukta is thus the charter text of the vivaha samskara** — see [varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md](../../05-concepts-and-dharma/varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md). (Rigveda 10.85.36, 42–47; Grihya-sutra usage)
- **Seer** — the Anukramani assigns the hymn to **Suryaa Savitri herself**, the bride as rishika. (Anukramani) [late text as attribution]

## Mudgala and Mudgalani's Race (RV 10.102)

- **The bull-and-club chariot** — **Mudgala** (Anukramani: Mudgala Bharmyashva) wins a race — and a thousand cattle — with a cart yoked to his **bull**, with a wooden club/hammer (**drughana**) set as the second "draft animal," while his wife **Mudgalani** drives as charioteer; Indra is invoked to aid the improbable team. (Rigveda 10.102)
- **Mudgalani the charioteer** — the hymn celebrates the wife as the winning driver ("the wife of Mudgala drove in the contest... won the stake"), one of the Rigveda's few images of a woman in athletic-heroic action. (Rigveda 10.102.2, 6)
- **Obscurity** — among the most debated hymns in the Samhita: read literally (a real ox-cart race), as fertility allegory, as rain-magic, or as humorous folk-tale in verse; the text's details (the club, the loincloth verse, the "eunuch's" taunt) resist any single reconstruction. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Name-collision caution** — this Vedic Mudgala is **distinct** from the gleaner-sage Mudgala of the Mahabharata's Vana Parva who refused heaven — see the caution in [mudgala.md](../../04-deep-dives/characters/mudgala.md) — and from the Maudgalya-gotra progenitor. [scholarly] [disputed identification]

## Vamadeva's Birth-Hymn (RV 4.18)

- **The womb-dialogue** — in the Vamadeva family book: a dialogue around **Indra's birth**. The voice in the womb is told "this is the ancient proven path by which all the gods were born; by it shall he be born"; the unborn one refuses — "not by this way: I shall come out **crosswise, through the side**" — an unnatural hero-birth; the mother conceals the newborn, who rises in his own might. (Rigveda 4.18.1–5)
- **The dark verses** — the hymn's harsh flashes: "Who made your mother a widow? Who sought to slay you sleeping and moving?" and Indra's extremity even to his father — allusions to a violent birth-legend never fully told in the Samhita; the Brihaddevata and commentators fill the story variously. (Rigveda 4.18.12; Brihaddevata) [disputed narrative]
- **"I cooked a dog's entrails"** — v.13: "In distress I cooked the entrails of a dog; among the gods I found none who showed mercy; I beheld my wife dishonoured; then the falcon brought me the sweet Soma." Tradition reads this as the seer **Vamadeva speaking in famine** — the classic proof-text of *apad-dharma* (emergency ethics), cited as such by Manusmriti 10.106. (Rigveda 4.18.13; Manusmriti 10.106 [late text as interpretation])
- **Vamadeva's prenatal gnosis** — the Aitareya Upanishad quotes Vamadeva declaring that "while yet lying in the womb I knew all the births of the gods" (drawing on RV 4.27.1), making him tradition's exemplar of knowledge before birth — the legend echoed in the Mandala-4 profile in [rigveda.md](./rigveda.md). (Aitareya Upanishad 2.1.5; Rigveda 4.27.1)

## The Danastuti Genre (Gift-Praise)

- **The form** — *danastuti*, "praise of the gift": closing verses (rarely whole hymns) in which the poet names his royal patron and itemizes the fee — cows, horses, gold, chariots, slave-women — received for the hymn; concentrated in Mandalas 1, 5–10 and thick in the Kanva Mandala 8. (Rigveda, danastuti verses; cf. [rigveda.md](./rigveda.md) on Mandala 8)
- **Kakshivat and Svanaya Bhavya** — the showpiece: **Kakshivat** (son of [Dirghatamas](../../04-deep-dives/dirghatamas.md)) praises the lavish gifts of king **Svanaya Bhavya** on the Sindhu (RV 1.125–126) — hundreds of cows, chariots, teams — with the bride-verse of **Romasha** attached at 1.126.7 (see [rigvedic-women-seers-brahmavadinis.md](../../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/rigvedic-women-seers-brahmavadinis.md)). (Rigveda 1.125–126; Anukramani)
- **Historical window [scholarly]** — because danastutis name real patrons (Trasadasyu, Divodasa, and others prominent in the family books), they are prime evidence for the poet-patron economy, tribal geography, and chronology of the Vedic clans — the most "documentary" verses in the Samhita. [scholarly]
- **Ambivalence in tradition** — later ritualists ranked danastutis low (praise of men, not gods), and the genre fed the smriti-era anxiety about priests who praise for pay; yet the *dakshina* (sacrificial fee) they celebrate remained integral to yajna — see [yajnas-and-vedic-rituals.md](../yajnas-and-vedic-rituals.md). [scholarly]

## Two Clocks

- **Scripture clock** — as shruti, these hymns are apaurusheya, "seen" by their rishis (Kavasha, Vasishtha, Vamadeva, Suryaa Savitri, Indrani...) in the mantra-drashta age deep in the cyclical past; the events they voice (Suryaa's wedding among the gods, Indra's birth) are devaloka events outside yuga chronology. (traditional frame)
- **Academic clock [scholarly]** — the frog hymn (7.103) and Vamadeva's 4.18 belong to the older family-book stratum (c. 1500–1200 BCE); the dice lament, Keshin, Vrishakapi, Suryaa's wedding, and Mudgala hymns sit in the late Mandala 10 layer (c. 1200–1000 BCE); the Grihya-sutra codification of 10.85 as wedding liturgy is mid-1st-millennium BCE. [scholarly]
