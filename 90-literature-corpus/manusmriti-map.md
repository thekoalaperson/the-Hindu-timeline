# Manusmriti — chapter map of the Mānava-Dharmaśāstra

> Literature-corpus node · a per-text **adhyāya map** of the *Manusmṛti* / *Mānava-Dharmaśāstra* ("Laws of Manu"), the most influential of the metrical Dharmaśāstras.
> Parent: [Literature Corpus](README.md) · summary entry: [`agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md` §II](agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md) (this file is the canonical chapter-map home; the §II bullet links here).

The **Manusmṛti** (*Mānava-Dharmaśāstra*, "the Law-Treatise of Manu") is the earliest, most cited, and most contested of the versified **Dharmaśāstras** — smṛti-class law-codes that recast the older prose **Dharma-sūtras** (Āpastamba, Gautama, Baudhāyana, Vasiṣṭha; see [`agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md` §I](agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md)) into a comprehensive treatise on **dharma**: cosmogony, the four **varṇas**, the four **āśramas**, marriage and household ritual, women's conduct, kingship (**rājadharma**), civil and criminal procedure (**vyavahāra**), penance (**prāyaścitta**), and the karmic mechanics of rebirth. It is a single continuous poem of **12 adhyāyas** and ~**2,685 verses** in Olivelle's critical edition (the received vulgate has ~2,694). Unlike the Purāṇas it is not a story-book; it is a legal-ritual code that nonetheless opens with a full **creation narrative** and closes with an **eschatology of transmigration**, framing law between cosmos and karma.

- **The double frame — who is speaking.** The great sages (*maharṣis*) approach **Manu** as he sits absorbed, and ask him to declare the dharma of all the varṇas. Manu narrates the creation himself (ch. 1); then, at **1.58–60**, he states that he taught the whole śāstra to his mind-born son the sage **Bhṛgu**, and that *Bhṛgu* will now recite it to the assembled sages. So chapters **2–12 are formally spoken by Bhṛgu**, repeating Manu — a nested narration typical of smṛti/itihāsa. (Manu 1.58–60; Olivelle 2005)
  - *Variant (which Manu?):* the framing "Manu" is by most readings **Svāyambhuva Manu**, the first Manu, mind-born son of the Self-existent Brahmā ([`../01-manvantaras/manvantara-01-svayambhuva.md`](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-01-svayambhuva.md)); but tradition and the §II summary also identify the code with **Vaivasvata Manu**, the current 7th Manu and survivor of the flood ([`../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md`](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/README.md); the flood-frame [`../04-deep-dives/matsya-pralaya.md`](../04-deep-dives/matsya-pralaya.md)). The text names Manu the son of the Self-existent, so the primordial Svāyambhuva reading is textually favoured; the conflation is old and unresolved. [disputed]

## Two clocks — dating the code

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame.** A **smṛti of the current Manvantara** — the eternal law re-declared by Manu at the dawn of the human age; by traditional reckoning binding across the yugas of the present 28th Mahāyuga, with law itself said to weaken by a quarter each yuga (Manu 1.81–86). Parāśara-smṛti later assigns Manu's code specifically to **Kṛta/Satya Yuga** in the per-yuga law-code scheme (see [`agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md` §II](agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md)). [scriptural]
- **Academic / historical frame [scholarly].** **Patrick Olivelle** dates the compiled text to the **2nd–3rd century CE**, arguing (against Georg Bühler's older "gradual accretion" view) that it is a **coherent, single-author (or single-redactor) composition** with a deliberate ring-structure, not a loose stratified anthology. Popular sources give a wider **c. 200 BCE – 200 CE** or **300 BCE – 600 CE** range. Its verse core reworks the lost **Mānava Dharma-sūtra** tradition of a Vedic school. (Olivelle 2005; Manusmriti — Wikipedia)
- **Critical edition & commentators.** Olivelle's *Manu's Code of Law* (Oxford, 2005) is the standard **critical edition**, reconstructing an earlier text behind the medieval vulgate. The vulgate itself rests on the commentary of **Kullūka Bhaṭṭa** (*Manvarthamuktāvalī*, c. 12th–13th c.); earlier commentators include **Medhātithi** (*Manubhāṣya*, c. 9th c., the oldest surviving), **Govindarāja**, **Nārāyaṇa**, **Rāghavānanda**, and **Nandana**. Kullūka's text (via **Bühler's** 1886 *Sacred Books of the East* vol. 25 translation) shaped every colonial and modern popular reading. [scholarly]

## The twelve adhyāyas — chapter map

### Adhyāya 1 — Cosmogony, the yugas, and the plan of the book
- **The account of creation (*sṛṣṭi*)** — the Self-existent (Svayambhū) dispels darkness, deposits his seed as a golden **cosmic egg (Hiraṇyagarbha)** from which **Brahmā** is born; the elements, the mind (*manas*), ahaṅkāra, the senses, and the beings emerge — a Sāṃkhya-tinged cosmogony prefacing the law. (Manu 1.5–1.57) Cross-link the cosmology [`../00-time-and-cosmology/README.md`](../00-time-and-cosmology/README.md).
- **The four varṇas from the body of the Primeval One** — for the sake of the worlds the Self-existent brings forth **Brāhmaṇa from the mouth, Kṣatriya from the arms, Vaiśya from the thighs, Śūdra from the feet**, and assigns each its innate duty (teaching, protecting, trade/husbandry, service) — a direct echo of the **Puruṣa-sūkta** (Ṛgveda 10.90). (Manu 1.31, 1.87–91) Cross-link [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md).
- **The four yugas and the decline of dharma** — the measures of Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara and Kali, and the doctrine that **dharma, imaged as a four-legged bull, loses one foot per yuga** (full in Kṛta, one-quarter in Kali); different dharmas suit different ages. (Manu 1.81–86)
- **Manu appoints Bhṛgu; the greatness of the Brāhmaṇa; contents summary** — the transfer of narration to Bhṛgu (1.58–60) and a table of the topics to follow. (Manu 1.58–1.119)

### Adhyāya 2 — The sources of dharma and the student (brahmacārin)
- **The four roots of dharma (*dharma-mūla*)** — **Veda (śruti), Smṛti (tradition), the conduct of the good (*sadācāra/śīla*), and what is agreeable to one's own conscience (*ātmatuṣṭi*)** — the classic hierarchy of legal authority. (Manu 2.6, 2.12)
- **Sacred geography of dharma** — the graded holiness of **Brahmāvarta**, **Brahmarṣi-deśa**, **Madhya-deśa**, and **Āryāvarta** as the lands whose custom is normative. (Manu 2.17–24)
- **Saṃskāras, upanayana, and studentship** — the life-cycle rites, initiation with the sacred thread, the syllable **Oṃ** and the **Gāyatrī**, and the conduct owed to the **guru** and the Veda. (Manu 2.26–2.249) Cross-link [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/varna-ashrama-and-samskaras.md).

### Adhyāya 3 — Marriage and the householder (gṛhastha)
- **The eight forms of marriage** — **Brāhma, Daiva, Ārṣa, Prājāpatya, Āsura, Gāndharva, Rākṣasa, Paiśāca** — ranked from the meritorious gift of a daughter to abduction and rape, with the varṇas for which each is lawful. (Manu 3.20–3.44)
- **The five great sacrifices (*pañca-mahāyajña*)** and śrāddha — the householder's daily debts to gods, ancestors, beings, humans and the Veda, and the ancestral rites, with rules on whom to feed and exclude. (Manu 3.67–3.286) Cross-link [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/pancha-maha-yajnas-and-the-three-debts.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/pancha-maha-yajnas-and-the-three-debts.md) and [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/shraddha-and-pitr-rites.md).
- **"Where women are honoured…"** — the much-quoted **3.56**, *yatra nāryastu pūjyante ramante tatra devatāḥ* ("where women are honoured, there the gods rejoice"), routinely set against the code's restrictive verses. (Manu 3.55–3.60)

### Adhyāya 4 — Livelihood and the observances of the snātaka
- **Right means of living and the daily rule** — permitted modes of subsistence for the Brāhmaṇa (**ṛta, amṛta, mṛta, pramṛta** gleaning/gift modes), continual Veda-study (*svādhyāya*), and a long code of personal observance, purity, speech, and prohibitions for the graduate householder. (Manu 4.1–4.260)

### Adhyāya 5 — Food, impurity, and the duties of women
- **Dietary law and ritual impurity** — lawful and forbidden foods, the rules and defence of meat-eating, and the periods of **death/birth impurity (āśauca)** and their purification. (Manu 5.5–5.145)
- **The dependence of women (*strī-dharma*)** — the notorious **5.147–5.149**, *na strī svātantryam arhati* ("a woman is not fit for independence"), guarded in childhood by her father, in youth by her husband, in age by her sons; the pativratā ideal and rules for widows. (Manu 5.147–5.166) — a principal locus of modern controversy (see Reception below). [disputed]

### Adhyāya 6 — The forest hermit and the renouncer (vānaprastha, sannyāsa)
- **The last two āśramas** — the retreat to the forest as a **vānaprastha**, and the wandering **sannyāsin/yati** who abandons fire and home for liberation — completing the four-āśrama scheme begun in chs. 2–4. (Manu 6.1–6.97) Cross-link [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/sannyasa-and-the-renunciate-tradition.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/sannyasa-and-the-renunciate-tradition.md).

### Adhyāya 7 — Rājadharma: the duties of the king
- **The origin and necessity of the king** — the Lord fashions the king from **eternal particles of Indra, Vāyu, Yama, Sūrya, Agni, Varuṇa, Candra and Kubera**; kingship exists so that the strong not devour the weak like fish (*mātsya-nyāya*). (Manu 7.3–7.7)
- **Daṇḍa — the rod of punishment** — **Daṇḍa** personified as the true ruler and the guarantor of all dharma; its just, proportionate application is the king's supreme science. (Manu 7.14–7.31)
- **Statecraft — the seven limbs and the six measures** — the **seven constituents of the state (saptāṅga:** king, minister, realm, fortress, treasury, army, ally), the **maṇḍala** of neighbouring powers, taxation, and the **six measures of foreign policy (ṣāḍguṇya:** peace, war, marching, halting, dividing forces, seeking shelter). (Manu 7.54–7.215)

### Adhyāya 8 — Vyavahāra: judicial procedure and the eighteen titles of law
- **The eighteen grounds of litigation (*vyavahāra-pada*)** — the eighteen heads under which all lawsuits fall, beginning with non-payment of debt (**ṛṇādāna**) and running through deposits, sale without ownership, partnerships, wages, boundary disputes, defamation (**vākpāruṣya**), assault (**daṇḍapāruṣya**), theft, violence (**sāhasa**), adultery (**strīsaṃgrahaṇa**), the duties of husband and wife, partition, and gambling. (Manu 8.4–8.7)
- **Evidence, witnesses, ordeals, and graded punishment** — rules of testimony, oath and ordeal, and a schedule of fines and corporal penalties that is **explicitly graded by varṇa** (heavier penalties down the scale, lighter for Brāhmaṇas). (Manu 8.8–8.420) [disputed]

### Adhyāya 9 — Domestic law, inheritance, and the "removal of thorns"
- **Husband, wife, and the guarding of offspring** — duties within marriage, **niyoga** (levirate) and its restriction, and the anxious protection of legitimate lineage. (Manu 9.1–9.103)
- **Partition and the twelve kinds of son** — the law of inheritance and the twelve recognised categories of sons (aurasa, kṣetraja, and so on), the primacy of the eldest, and women's property (**strīdhana**). (Manu 9.104–9.220)
- **Kaṇṭaka-śodhana ("clearing thorns") and the closing duties of Vaiśya and Śūdra** — the king's suppression of thieves, cheats and criminals, and the concluding svadharma of the two lower varṇas. (Manu 9.252–9.336)

### Adhyāya 10 — Mixed castes and law in adversity (āpad-dharma)
- **The origin of the mixed jātis (*varṇa-saṃkara*)** — the offspring of anuloma ("with the grain") and pratiloma ("against the grain") unions, deriving groups such as the **Sūta, Māgadha, Ambaṣṭha, Niṣāda** and — as the worst pratiloma issue (Śūdra father, Brāhmaṇa mother) — the **Caṇḍāla**, textual charter of the later untouchability doctrine. (Manu 10.8–10.56) [disputed]
- **Āpad-dharma — occupations in distress** — the trades a member of each varṇa may lawfully take up when his own livelihood fails, and the limits on doing so. (Manu 10.81–10.130)

### Adhyāya 11 — Prāyaścitta: sins and their expiation
- **The great sins (*mahāpātaka*) and minor sins (*upapātaka*)** — the five cardinal sins: **killing a Brāhmaṇa (brahmahatyā), drinking liquor (surāpāna), stealing gold (steya), violating the guru's bed (guru-talpa-gama), and associating with those who do these** — followed by a long catalogue of upapātakas. (Manu 11.55–11.71) Cross-link [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md).
- **The specific penances** — graded expiations including the **Prājāpatya / Kṛcchra** fasts, **Atikṛcchra**, **Sāntapana**, **Parāka**, and the lunar **Cāndrāyaṇa** vow (food increased and decreased with the waxing and waning moon), plus gifts, japa and homa as purifiers. (Manu 11.72–11.266)

### Adhyāya 12 — Karma, transmigration, and the supreme good
- **The three kinds of action and their fruit** — action is **mental, verbal and bodily (kāyika, vācika, mānasa)**, each yielding good or evil rebirth; a catalogue of the specific animal, low-caste and hellish births produced by specific sins (e.g. a Brāhmaṇa-killer reborn as a dog, pig or ass). (Manu 12.3–12.81)
- **The three guṇas and the ladder of rebirth** — **sattva** leads upward to godhood and knowledge, **rajas** to human/active states, **tamas** downward to beasts, plants and hells — the qualities determining transmigratory destiny. (Manu 12.24–12.51) Cross-link [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md) and [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/the-three-gunas.md`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/the-three-gunas.md).
- **Pravṛtta and nivṛtta action; niḥśreyasa** — the distinction between "active" karma (leading to heaven and return) and "cessative" karma (leading to release), and the final teaching that knowledge of the **Ātman** and devotion to the **Veda** yield the supreme good (*niḥśreyasa*) — the code closing on mokṣa. (Manu 12.83–12.126)

## Reception, use, and controversy [scholarly]

- **From treatise to "Hindu law."** Under **Warren Hastings**, **Sir William Jones** translated the text as *Institutes of Hindu Law, or the Ordinances of Menu* (1794), and colonial courts made Manu (through Kullūka and the later **nibandha** digests) a pillar of **Anglo-Hindu personal law** — a status far beyond any it held as one smṛti among many. Cross-link [`dharma-nibandhas-and-digests.md`](dharma-nibandhas-and-digests.md). (Manusmriti — Wikipedia)
- **Ambedkar's protest.** On **25 December 1927**, during the **Mahad Satyāgraha**, **B. R. Ambedkar** publicly **burned a copy of the Manusmṛti** as the symbolic charter of caste hierarchy and untouchability — commemorated as *Manusmṛti Dahan Din*; the code remains the central text in Dalit and feminist critiques of Brahmanical dharma. (documented history) → see the future/history events sidecar.
- **Contested verses.** The dependence-of-women verses (**5.147–149**, **9.3**), the varṇa-graded punishments (ch. 8) and the Caṇḍāla/untouchability provisions (ch. 10) are the flashpoints; apologists counter with **3.56** ("where women are honoured…") and stress the text's descriptive-plus-idealising, internally inconsistent character. The corpus records both readings without merging them. [disputed]
- **Textual instability as a caveat.** Because the received vulgate differs materially from Olivelle's reconstructed earlier text, individual "shocking verses" cited in polemic may be **later interpolations or variant readings** — attribution to "Manu" should specify recension. [interpolation]
