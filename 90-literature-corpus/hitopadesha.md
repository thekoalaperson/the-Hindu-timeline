# Hitopadeśa — Nārāyaṇa's Book of Good Counsel

> Parent: [Literature Corpus](README.md) · A Bengal reworking of the [Pañcatantra](panchatantra-hitopadesha-and-fable-literature.md) into a self-standing four-book **nīti-kathā** — worldly, statecraft-and-friendship wisdom taught through emboxed animal fables. No revelation and no yuga-address is claimed: it sits wholly in ordinary human **Kali-Yuga documented history** on the academic clock (**[scholarly]** c. 800–1373 CE), a sibling of the [Itihāsa](smriti-itihasa-epics.md) and the [Bṛhatkathā story-stream](brihatkatha-kathasaritsagara-and-vikramaditya-cycle.md), its ethics rooted in [artha and the puruṣārthas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md).

---

## Orientation: what the Hitopadeśa is

- **A "beneficial instruction"** — the title *Hitopadeśa* means "salutary/beneficial teaching" (*hita* + *upadeśa*). It is a Sanskrit **nīti-śāstra in story form**: not mokṣa-teaching but the wise conduct of life — winning allies, reading flatterers, surviving kings, and the paired arts of *vigraha* (war) and *sandhi* (peace). It teaches through the genre's signature **frame-story engine**: an outer tale opens, a character quotes a maxim, is asked "how was that?", and a new tale opens inside it, each sealed with a gnomic verse. (Hitopadeśa, prastāvikā/preamble)
- **A gnomic-verse treasury** — beyond its prose fables the Hitopadeśa is prized as an anthology of roughly **700+ nīti-ślokas** (subhāṣitas), many quarried from earlier authorities — Manu, the Mahābhārata, Cāṇakya, and above all **Kāmandaki's Nītisāra** — which is why it became the standard first reader for Sanskrit students: it teaches grammar, vocabulary, and worldly ethics at once. (Hitopadeśa, verses throughout)
- **Source-transparent by its own admission** — the author names his sources in the preamble: the **Pañcatantra** and "another work" (*anyasmāc ca granthād*), long identified as **Kāmandaki's Nītisāra** (a verse epitome of the Arthaśāstra tradition, cataloged with the [statecraft/dharma manuals](agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md)). The Hitopadeśa is thus a *conscious* reworking, not a rival origin-legend. **[scholarly]** (Hitopadeśa, preamble v. 9)
- **Rank on the canon ladder** — neither Śruti nor formally listed Smṛti; secular, didactic, kāvya-adjacent literature — **[late text]** relative to the Vedic–epic core, yet historically **among the most-copied and most-read of all Sanskrit texts**.

## The two clocks

- *Traditional / narrative frame* — the work claims only a wise human teacher (**Viṣṇuśarman**) instructing a king's sons in ordinary time; no manvantara or yuga is invoked. Its world is the everyday human polity of forests, courts, and merchant roads.
- *Academic / historical frame* — composed in **Bengal** (eastern India) by the poet **Nārāyaṇa** under a patron named **Dhavala-candra**, in a window bracketed by scholars as **c. 800–1373 CE**: the later bound is fixed by its **earliest dated manuscript, a Nepalese copy of 1373 CE**; many place the composition in the **Pāla–Sena period, c. 9th–12th centuries**. **[scholarly]/[disputed]** — the wide bracket reflects that nothing external dates Nārāyaṇa himself. See the documented-history spine at [recorded-history.md](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/recorded-history.md).

## The frame-story (prastāvikā)

- **King Sudarśana's despairing resolve** — on the banks of the Ganges at **Pāṭaliputra** (Pāṭaliputra/Patna), King **Sudarśana** overhears a recited couplet on the worthlessness of learning that goes unpracticed, and despairs of his own grown but ignorant, pleasure-addicted sons. He convenes his pandits to ask who can turn princes into masters of *nīti*. (Hitopadeśa, prastāvikā)
- **Viṣṇuśarman's undertaking** — the aged, accomplished brahmin **Viṣṇuśarman** promises to accomplish it and takes the princes in charge, teaching them the science of polity through the four books of animal fables that follow. The frame openly recycles the Pañcatantra's Viṣṇuśarman device — the *same teacher-name* is reused, a deliberate homage rather than a claim of identity. (Hitopadeśa, prastāvikā)
  - *Note (name-collision):* this Viṣṇuśarman is the **Hitopadeśa's** narrator-frame, borrowed from the Pañcatantra's; the two texts, teachers, and their pupil-kings (Sudarśana here vs **Amaraśakti** in the Pañcatantra) are distinct — see the parent map [Pañcatantra, Hitopadeśa & the nīti-kathā corpus](panchatantra-hitopadesha-and-fable-literature.md).

## The four books (not five)

*(The Hitopadeśa condenses the Pañcatantra's five tantras into four, drops the last two Pañcatantra books, and — most visibly — reverses the order of the two friendship-books, opening with the winning of friends before their sundering.)*

### Book I — Mitralābha ("The Acquisition of a Friend")

- **The four friends: mouse, crow, tortoise, deer** — the book-frame, set beneath a great tree on the **Godāvarī**: the mouse **Hiraṇyaka** gnaws free the netted pigeon-king **Citragrīva** and his flock; the awed crow **Laghupatanaka** befriends the mouse; the two join the tortoise **Mantharaka** at his lake, and the deer **Citrāṅga** completes the quartet. When Citrāṅga is snared and Mantharaka captured while helping, the four cooperate in a staged rescue — the book-length argument that **alliance among the weak defeats the strong**. (Answers to Pañcatantra Book II, *Mitra-samprāpti*.) (Hitopadeśa I)
- **The traveller and the tiger** — the opening cautionary tale: an old, decrepit tiger, feigning piety, dangles a **gold bracelet** from the far bank to lure a greedy traveller into the muddy shallows, where the man sinks fast and is devoured — greed blinds prudence. (Hitopadeśa I, embedded)
- **The deer, the crow, and the jackal Kṣudra-buddhi** — a treacherous jackal ("Little-wit") ingratiates himself with the deer Citrāṅga to fatten and betray him; the loyal crow's warning goes unheeded until the jackal springs a trap, and quick wit turns the snare back on the schemer — beware the newly-met flatterer. (Hitopadeśa I, embedded)
- **"The world is one family"** — Book I carries the celebrated verse *ayaṃ nijaḥ paro veti gaṇanā laghucetasām / udāra-caritānāṃ tu **vasudhaiva kuṭumbakam*** — "'this one is kin, that one a stranger' is the reckoning of the small-minded; to the noble-hearted the whole earth is but one family." One of the corpus's most-quoted lines, it lives in the Hitopadeśa's friendship-book. (Hitopadeśa I, subhāṣita)

### Book II — Suhṛdbheda ("The Splitting of Friends")

- **The lion, the bull, and the two jackals** — the frame (Pañcatantra Book I, reversed into second place): the merchant's abandoned bull **Sañjīvaka** thrives in the forest, and his bellow terrifies the lion-king **Piṅgalaka** until the jackal ministers **Karaṭaka and Damanaka** broker their friendship. **Damanaka**, resenting the favor shown the newcomer, poisons each against the other with insinuation until Piṅgalaka kills his friend — how calumny sunders alliance at court. (Hitopadeśa II)
- **The crows, the black snake, and the golden chain** — a crow-pair, their nestlings devoured by a tree-hole cobra, are advised by a jackal to steal a **gold necklace** from a bathing prince and drop it at the snake's hole, so that the pursuing servants kill the serpent — indirection defeats brute strength. (Hitopadeśa II, embedded)
- **The heron, the fish, and the crab** — a scheming heron ferries the pond's fish one by one to "a safe lake," eating them on a rock, until a suspicious **crab**, carried last, grips the heron's throat and beheads it — the deceiver undone by the one victim who watches. (Hitopadeśa II, embedded)

### Book III — Vigraha ("War")

- **The war of the swans and the peacocks** — the frame: **Hiraṇyagarbha**, king of the swans/geese (*haṃsa*) on the island **Karpūra-dvīpa**, and **Citravarṇa**, king of the peacocks on the mainland, fall into war after their spies and envoys trade provocations; the swan-king's far-sighted vulture-minister **Dūradarśī** counsels the maxims of fortification, alliance, and the six-fold policy (*ṣāḍguṇya*). (Reworks the Pañcatantra's crow–owl war, Book III.) (Hitopadeśa III)
- **Vīravara's loyalty** — the text's most famous embedded tale: the warrior **Vīravara** enters the service of King **Śūdraka**, drawing a princely daily wage that he silently divides among gods, brahmins, the poor, and his own family. One night the king, following him, sees Vīravara confront a weeping woman — the embodied **Royal Fortune (Rājya-Lakṣmī)** of the realm — who foretells the king's death in three days unless a hero freely offers a son marked with the auspicious signs to the goddess **Caṇḍikā/Bhagavatī**. Vīravara's son **Śaktidhara** consents to the sacrifice; grief then takes the wife and Vīravara himself upon the goddess's altar — whereupon the goddess, moved, relents and the watching king restores them all to life, an exemplum of absolute loyalty. **[scholarly]** (Hitopadeśa III, embedded)
  - *Variant (cross-text):* a father-mother-son-daughter self-immolation to a goddess recurs in the [Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati and the Kathāsaritsāgara](../04-deep-dives/kathasaritsagara-famous-tales.md), where a like tale poses the riddle "who was the greatest?"; the Vīravara motif of the loyal retainer tested by a divine visitor circulates widely across the [Vikramāditya story-cycles](../04-deep-dives/simhasana-dvatrimshika-vikramaditya-cycle.md). **[folk]/[scholarly]**

### Book IV — Sandhi ("Peace")

- **The negotiated peace** — the closing book: through the counsel of the swan-king's ministers and a captured, cannily-managed enemy envoy, the swans and peacocks conclude terms and **make peace** — the book that theorizes treaty, the kinds of *sandhi*, and when peace serves better than victory, completing the war-and-peace diptych that is the Hitopadeśa's structural signature. (Hitopadeśa IV)
- **The frame closes** — the four books delivered, King Sudarśana's princes are declared accomplished in *nīti*, and the work ends with a benedictory prayer for the prosperity, learning, and good counsel of its hearers. (Hitopadeśa IV, colophon)

## Transmission and afterlife

- **The Sanskrit student's first reader** — for centuries the Hitopadeśa served across India as the standard primer of literary Sanskrit prose, its blend of easy narrative and quotable verse making it ideal for beginners; this pedagogical role, more than courtly prestige, drove its extraordinary manuscript spread. **[scholarly]**
- **First Sanskrit book into English** — **Charles Wilkins** published his translation, *The Heĕtōpădēs of Veĕshnŏŏ-Sărmā* (1787), among the earliest renderings of any Sanskrit work into a European language, under the patronage of the Bengal orientalist circle around Sir William Jones. **[scholarly]**
- **Early print history** — the Hitopadeśa was among the first Sanskrit texts printed in **Devanāgarī type at Serampore (1803–04)** in the Carey mission's press; it was later critically edited by **Max Müller** (with the *Nītisāra* verses traced) and famously re-versified in English by **Sir Edwin Arnold** as *The Book of Good Counsels* (1861). **[scholarly]**
- **Its own genealogy vs the Pañcatantra's** — because the Hitopadeśa derives *from* the Pañcatantra (already migrating westward as *Kalīla wa-Dimna*), it did not spawn the vast global fable-diffusion its parent did; its influence ran instead *inward*, through the Sanskrit schoolroom and the Indian vernacular retellings, and as a quarry of proverbs still current in modern South Asian speech. **[scholarly]**
