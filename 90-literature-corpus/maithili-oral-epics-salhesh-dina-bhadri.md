# Maithili Oral Epics — Rājā Salhesh & the Dinā-Bhadrī Brothers

> Literature-corpus node, the **Mithilā chapter of the oral-epic layer** — sibling of the pan-Indian survey [regional-and-oral-epics.md](regional-and-oral-epics.md) and of the master map [README.md](README.md). Mithilā (north Bihar + the Nepal Terai) is famous in the Sanskrit canon as the land of [Janaka's Videha line](../03-lineages-vamsha/videha-mithila-line-janaka.md) and of [Sītā](../04-deep-dives/characters/sita.md) ([Janakpur](../06-sacred-geography/janakpur-mithila-sita-janmabhumi.md)); *beneath* that canon runs a Maithili-language sung stratum owned by the region's Dalit communities, whose two chief epics — the **Rājā Salhesh gāthā** of the Dusādhs and the **Dinā-Bhadrī gāthā** of the Musahars — are cataloged here. By traditional reckoning their heroes belong to the deep human past of [Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md); academically both are living oral traditions of undatable medieval-looking hero-cults, first noticed in print in the colonial era **[scholarly]**. Everything in this file is **[folk]/[oral tradition]/[regional]** unless tagged otherwise.

---

## Orientation: the "other Mithilā"

- **Two Mithilās, one soil** — The same land that the Rāmāyaṇa remembers for King Janaka and Sītā carries a second, sung sacred history: Maithili **gāthās** (hero-songs) performed by hereditary and community singers at village shrines, weddings, melās and possession-rites. Where the Sanskrit layer is Brahmin-curated, the gāthā layer is **community-owned** — Salhesh belongs to the **Dusādh (Paswan)** community, Dinā-Bhadrī to the **Musahars** — and functions as those communities' own scripture, genealogy and charter of dignity. (Maithili folklore scholarship; Badri Narayan, *Women Heroes and Dalit Assertion in North India*) **[folk]/[scholarly]**
- **The deification engine, Mithilā edition** — Both epics run the classic hero-into-god sequence documented across the oral-epic belt (cf. [Pābūjī, Devnārāyaṇ, Koṭi-Cennaya](regional-and-oral-epics.md)): a hero of a stigmatised community lives with impossible valour, suffers injustice or violent death, and is thereafter **worshipped at village shrines through possession-priests**, protecting the very people the social order ranks lowest. **[folk]**
- **The wider Maithili gāthā corpus** — Salhesh and Dinā-Bhadrī head a larger sung repertoire of the region: the **Lorik** cycle (shared with the Bhojpuri belt — see [Lorik–Candā](regional-and-oral-epics.md)), the **Bihulā–Bisaharī** snake-goddess songs of the Anga–Kosi fringe (the regional face of [Manasā](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/manasa.md)), the **Naykā-Banijārā** merchant song, and Maithili renditions of Ālhā. This file treats only the two chief hero epics; the rest are flagged for context. **[oral tradition]**
- **Two clocks** — *Traditional:* the gāthās present Salhesh as a **king of Mahisautha** and Dinā-Bhadrī as heroes of a sacred village past, with no yuga-arithmetic attached — folk memory, not śāstric chronology, deep in Kali Yuga by default. *Academic:* the heroes cannot be securely dated; scholarship treats the cults as **medieval-to-early-modern in formation**, with the songs first entering print notice in the **late 19th century** (George A. Grierson's Maithili fieldwork of the 1880s is commonly credited with the earliest recorded specimens, including a Dinā-Bhadrī song) and systematic collection only in the 20th–21st centuries. **[scholarly]**

## I. The Rājā Salhesh gāthā (epic of the Dusādhs)

- **The hero and his name** — **Salhesh** (Maithili *Salhes*, widely glossed from Sanskrit **Śailēśa**, "lord of the mountain") is the great deified hero of the **Dusādh** community of Mithilā, remembered as **rājā of Mahisautha** (Siraha district, Nepal Terai), where a mound revered as his **gaṛh** (fort) still stands. (Rājā Salhes gāthā; Salhesh cult of Mahisautha) **[folk]/[regional]**
- **The watchman-king** — The gāthā's central dignifying move: Salhesh, though a king, serves as **guard/watchman (chowkidar) of a royal fort** — the very occupation the old order assigned to Dusādhs — and the epic makes that guardianship heroic and royal. Scholars read the whole cycle as the community's answer to its stigmatisation: *our watchman is a king, and our king is a god*. (Rājā Salhes gāthā; Badri Narayan on Dusādh hero-cults) **[folk]/[scholarly]**
- **His companions** — Salhesh acts alongside his **brothers Motirām and Budheswar**, and rides a great **elephant**, his signature mount in song, shrine-offering and painting alike. (Rājā Salhes gāthā) **[oral tradition]**
- **The false accusation** — The pivot of the epic: during Salhesh's watch the **royal jewels/necklace are stolen**, and Salhesh is **falsely accused of the theft** and imprisoned — the guard branded a thief. (Rājā Salhes gāthā) **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (singer-dependent):* who engineers the frame differs by recension — a jealous court rival in some tellings, in others the sorcery-wielding **mālin (gardener-caste flower-woman) Daunā**, who ensnares the hero she desires. Recensions differ irreconcilably here; singers own their versions. **[oral tradition]/[disputed]**
- **Kusumā Mālin and the vindication** — The heroine of the cycle is the **mālin Kusumā** (one of the mālin sisters of the gāthā), who loves Salhesh; through her wit and magic the **real thief is exposed**, Salhesh's innocence is proven, and he is freed and restored to honour and kingship. (Rājā Salhes gāthā) **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (the unconsummated love):* in widely-sung tellings Salhesh never marries Kusumā — the love stays unfulfilled across the caste line (mālin and Dusādh king), and her devotion is answered only after death, in the flower-garden miracle below. Other singers give the romance more or less weight; the mālin sisters' names (Daunā, Kusumā) and roles shuffle between recensions. **[oral tradition]/[disputed]**
  - *Variant (the rival hero Chuharmal):* the neighbouring Dusādh/Anga hero **Chuharmal** (of the Reshmā–Chuharmal gāthā, Mokama belt) is drawn into some tellings as Salhesh's wrestler-rival — traditions in Chuharmal's own country even have him **best Salhesh**; Salhesh-country singers reject this. A textbook case of rival community epics contesting each other's hero. **[oral tradition]/[disputed]**
- **Deification** — After his career of guardianship, justice vindicated, Salhesh is worshipped as a **protector-god of the Dusādhs** — a household and village deity across Mithilā on both sides of the India–Nepal border, his cult sitting alongside the community's older veneration of **Rāhu**. (Salhesh cult; ethnography of the Dusādhs) **[folk]**

### The cult: gahwar shrines, the flower-garden, the melā

- **The gahwar** — Salhesh's shrine is the **gahwar**: a simple open or thatched village sanctuary in Dusādh hamlets, its platform crowded with **terracotta elephants and horses** offered to the rider-god. There the **bhagat** (priest-medium) sings the gāthā to drum and, possessed by Salhesh, gives healing and judgement — the song and the liturgy are one event. (Salhesh gahwar tradition of Mithilā) **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
- **Salhesh Phulwārī/Phulbārī of Mahisautha** — Beside the Mahisautha gaṛh lies the **Salhesh flower-garden (phulbārī)**, held to be the garden of the mālin; it is the cult's chief sacred grove and the shrine-garden par excellence of the epic — the "gahwar flower-garden" pairing that marks Salhesh sites. (Salhesh melā of Mahisautha, Siraha) **[folk]/[regional]**
- **The once-a-year garland** — The garden's famous miracle: a rare **orchid on an old tree of the phulbārī is said to blossom once a year**, at the time of the melā, and devotees read the bloom as **Kusumā Mālin's garland, still offered to Rājā Salhesh** each new year — the unconsummated love renewed annually as a flower. (Salhesh melā tradition, Mahisautha) **[folk]**
- **The Salhesh melā (Jur Sital / Maithili New Year)** — The great annual **Salhesh melā at Mahisautha (Siraha, Nepal)** convenes at the Maithili new year (**Jur Sital**, first of Baiśākh, mid-April), drawing Dusādh and broader Maithil pilgrims from both Nepal and Bihar to the gaṛh and phulbārī for the bloom, the possession-rites and the fair. (Salhesh melā of Mahisautha) **[folk]/[regional]**
- **Salhesh nāch and Salhesh in Mithilā painting** — The epic lives in performance as the **Salhesh nāch** (dance-drama of the Terai Dusādhs) and, since the late 20th century, in **Dusādh Mithilā painting**: when Dalit painters of the Madhubani region entered the paper-painting movement, they made **Rājā Salhesh their central iconographic subject** — most famously **Chano Devi** (with Raudi Paswan), whose **godnā (tattoo-derived) style** canvases of the Salhesh cycle won national recognition and put the Dusādh epic beside the Brahmin–Kayastha painters' Rāma and Kṛṣṇa. (Mithilā-painting scholarship on the Dusādh/godnā school) **[scholarly]**
- **Cross-border note** — With the cult's centre of gravity at Mahisautha, Salhesh is as much a **Nepal Terai** deity as a Bihari one — Mithilā's folk pantheon, like its Sītā shrines, ignores the modern border (cf. Nepal's other living cults, [Kumārī–Taleju–Machhindranāth](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/nepal-kumari-taleju-machhindranath.md)). **[regional]**

## II. The Dinā-Bhadrī gāthā (epic of the Musahars, Kosi belt)

- **The twin heroes** — **Dinā and Bhadrī** are the brother-heroes (often called twins) of the **Musahar** community, sung across the **Kosi belt** of northeastern Mithilā (Saharsa–Madhepura–Khagaria–Purnia country). Born of a poor Musahar family, they grow into labourer-heroes of prodigious strength — the epic's world is that of field-labour, foraging, pig-keeping and the flood-country of the Kosi. (Dinā-Bhadrī gāthā) **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
- **Champions of the lowest** — The gāthā strings together the brothers' exploits as **protectors of the Musahar hamlets**: they defend the community's fields, grain and honour against its tormentors. (Dinā-Bhadrī gāthā) **[oral tradition]**
  - *Variant (who the enemy is):* recensions differ on the adversaries — **demonic beings** in some tellings, **oppressive landlords/strongmen** in others, and the two registers blend (the oppressor demonised, the demon read as oppressor). Singers' emphasis tracks their audience; the traditions are cataloged, not harmonised. **[oral tradition]/[disputed]**
- **Martyrdom** — The brothers **die violently in their final battle** — martyred fighting for their people rather than submitting — and this shared heroic death is the hinge of the cult: the epic of the Musahars is, like Koṭi-Cennaya's for the Billavas, a **martyr-epic**. (Dinā-Bhadrī gāthā) **[oral tradition]**
- **Deification and worship** — After death Dinā and Bhadrī become the Musahars' **ancestral protector-gods**: village shrines (**thān/sthān**) in Musahar tolas, an annual **Dinā-Bhadrī pūjā/melā** in the Kosi country, and **bhagat/bhagait singers** who perform the gāthā through the night with drum and possession. Offerings follow the community's own norms — including **pigs and liquor** — untouched by Brahmin priesthood. (Dinā-Bhadrī cult, Kosi belt) **[folk]**
- **First print notice** — The earliest recorded specimen of the Dinā-Bhadrī song is commonly credited to **George A. Grierson's Maithili collections (1880s)**, making it one of the first Dalit oral epics of the region to reach print; sustained ethnographic attention came only a century later. **[scholarly]**
- **Modern Dalit assertion** — From the late 20th century Dinā-Bhadrī (with Salhesh and Chuharmal) were consciously mobilised as **icons of Dalit identity and political assertion in Bihar** — statues, memorial melās and printed hero-pamphlets in Musahar and Dusādh localities — a process documented by Badri Narayan and others: the oral epic re-purposed as public memory and political charter. (Badri Narayan, *Women Heroes and Dalit Assertion in North India*, 2006) **[scholarly]**

## III. Shared grammar of the two epics

- **Community-owned scripture** — Each epic is the possession of one community (Dusādh / Musahar), sung by its own singers at its own shrines; neither has a fixed text, and **no two performances are the same** — the multiformity is constitutive, as across the whole [oral-epic layer](regional-and-oral-epics.md). **[oral tradition]**
- **The guard, the labourer, the god** — Both cycles sanctify the community's actual social role (watchman; field-labourer) and invert its stigma: the watchman is a wronged king vindicated, the labourers are martyr-lords of the soil. Scholarship reads both as **counter-histories of dignity** running beneath Mithilā's Sanskritic self-image. **[scholarly]**
- **Possession as liturgy** — In both cults the gāthā is not entertainment but **rite**: the bhagat's possession at the gahwar or thān makes the hero present to judge, heal and protect — epic, priesthood and shrine in a single institution. **[folk]**
- **No Sanskritic grafting (yet)** — Unlike Pābūjī ("Lakṣmaṇa") or Devnārāyaṇ ("Viṣṇu"), Salhesh and Dinā-Bhadrī remain largely **without a standard avatāra-equation** in the pan-Indian pantheon — their divinity rests on the martyr/vindicated-hero logic itself; where Purāṇic linkages are asserted locally they are late, local and inconsistent. **[folk]/[disputed]**

## Sources

**Oral/performed primary traditions (as sung and transcribed):**
- **Rājā Salhes gāthā** — Maithili oral epic of the Dusādh community; performance contexts: gahwar bhagat-singing, Salhesh nāch, and the **Salhesh melā of Mahisautha (Siraha, Nepal)** with its gaṛh, phulbārī and once-a-year orchid bloom. **[oral tradition]**
- **Dinā-Bhadrī gāthā** — Maithili oral epic of the Musahar community of the **Kosi belt**; performance contexts: thān worship, bhagait night-singing, annual Dinā-Bhadrī pūjā/melā. **[oral tradition]**

**Scholarly / documentary:**
- George A. Grierson's Maithili collections (incl. *An Introduction to the Maithilí Language of North Bihár*, with chrestomathy, 1880s) — earliest print notices credited for the region's folk songs, including Dinā-Bhadrī material. **[scholarly]**
- Badri Narayan, *Women Heroes and Dalit Assertion in North India: Culture, Identity and Politics* (Sage, 2006) — Dusādh/Musahar hero-cults (Salhesh, Chuharmal, Dinā-Bhadrī) and their modern political mobilisation. **[scholarly]**
- Mithilā-painting scholarship on the **Dusādh godnā school** (Chano Devi, Raudi Paswan et al.) and the Rājā Salhesh iconography.
- Reporting and regional documentation of the **Salhesh melā, phulbārī and gahwar tradition** of Mahisautha/Siraha and Mithilā at large.
- Stuart Blackburn, Peter Claus, Joyce Flueckiger & Susan Wadley (eds.), *Oral Epics in India* — comparative frame for community-owned martyr/hero epics (as used across [regional-and-oral-epics.md](regional-and-oral-epics.md)).
