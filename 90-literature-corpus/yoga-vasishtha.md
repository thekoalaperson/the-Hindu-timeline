# Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha (Mahā-Rāmāyaṇa)

> Where this sits: `90-literature-corpus/` — up to the [Literature Corpus overview](./README.md). A vast philosophical "Rāmāyaṇa" whose **frame** sits in [Tretā Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/02-treta-yuga.md) (sage **Vasiṣṭha** instructs a young, world-weary **Rāma** at Daśaratha's court), but whose **composition** belongs to medieval Kashmir [scholarly] [late text]. For its place among the Sanskrit secondary Rāmāyaṇas see [Adhyātma & variant Rāmāyaṇas](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md); this file is the canonical per-text profile.

Also called **Vāsiṣṭha Rāmāyaṇa**, **Jñāna-Vāsiṣṭha**, and — in its own colophons — the **Mahā-Rāmāyaṇa** ("Great Rāmāyaṇa") and **Ārṣa Rāmāyaṇa**; its earlier Kashmiri recension is the **Mokṣopāya** ("the means to liberation"). Attributed to **Vālmīki**, it is narrative only in frame: hundreds of nested *upākhyānas* (embedded tales) carry a radical idealist Advaita in which the world is mind-made appearance and was **never really born** (*ajāta-vāda*). Traditionally reckoned at **32,000 ślokas**, extant full versions run to roughly **29,000** — verse totals are recension-dependent. [disputed]

---

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural time** — The dialogue occurs in **Tretā Yuga**, in Daśaratha's assembly at Ayodhyā: Rāma, about sixteen and just returned from a pilgrimage, has fallen into deep dispassion (*vairāgya*); when [Viśvāmitra](../04-deep-dives/vishwamitra-vasishtha.md) arrives to ask for Rāma's help against demons (the same moment the [Bāla Kāṇḍa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/1-bala-kanda.md) narrates), the sages judge Rāma's despondency the perfect vessel for knowledge, and **Vasiṣṭha** delivers the discourse over successive days before the whole court. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Vairāgya & Mumukṣu-vyavahāra prakaraṇas)
- **Frame-of-the-frame** — The text opens with a Brahmin, **Sutīkṣṇa**, asking sage **Agasti (Agastya)** whether liberation comes from works or knowledge (answer: both, like a bird's two wings), and gives a transmission chain in which **Vālmīki** recounts the whole dialogue to his pupil **Bharadvāja**, the composition being commanded by **Brahmā** so that beings may cross saṃsāra. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* 1.1–2)
- **Academic time** — A long-contested dating. Older scholarship spread the text between the **6th and 14th centuries CE**; the current text-critical position (Slaje, Hanneder) is that the original **Mokṣopāya** was composed in **Kashmir, c. 950 CE**, and later reworked into the Vedānticized *Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* (see §Mokṣopāya below). Keep this entirely distinct from the Tretā-Yuga frame. [scholarly] [late text]

## Structure: the six prakaraṇas

- **1. Vairāgya-prakaraṇa** ("dispassion") — Rāma's disillusioned survey of the futility of worldly life — youth, wealth, body, lifespan — delivered to the assembly; the existential problem the rest of the text answers. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, book 1)
- **2. Mumukṣu-vyavahāra-prakaraṇa** ("conduct of the liberation-seeker") — Qualifications of the seeker; its most celebrated teaching is the primacy of **self-effort (pauruṣa/puruṣārtha) over fate (daiva)**: "fate" is only the accumulated momentum of one's own past efforts, and present effort can defeat it. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, book 2)
- **3. Utpatti-prakaraṇa** ("origination") — How the world-appearance arises in consciousness; home of the Līlā, Karkaṭī, and "three non-existent princes" tales. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, book 3)
- **4. Sthiti-prakaraṇa** ("existence/sustenance") — How the appearance persists through mental conditioning; home of the Śukra and Dāma-Vyāla-Kaṭa stories. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, book 4)
- **5. Upaśama-prakaraṇa** ("quiescence") — Dissolving the mind; stories of Janaka, Prahlāda, Gādhi, and Uddālaka's meditation. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, book 5)
- **6. Nirvāṇa-prakaraṇa** ("liberation") — By far the largest book, split into **Pūrvārdha** (first half) and **Uttarārdha** (second half); home of Bhuśuṇḍa and Cūḍālā. It ends with Rāma enlightened yet choosing to act — the charter of **jīvanmukti**, liberation while living and ruling. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, book 6)

## Doctrine

- **Ajāta-vāda ("non-origination")** — The world never actually arose: creation, persistence, and dissolution are appearances in the one infinite consciousness (*cit*), as dream-cities are to a dreamer. Nothing was ever born, so nothing is bound and nothing needs release except the conviction of bondage. Compare the identical doctrine in the [Ribhu-Gītā](../04-deep-dives/ribhu-gita.md) and Gauḍapāda's Kārikās (see [Ādi Śaṅkara](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/adi-shankara.md)). (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Utpatti & Nirvāṇa)
- **The world is mind (manas)** — "The world is nothing but a mode of the mind"; bondage is the mind's movement, liberation its stilling — hence the text's alternate reading as a *yoga* manual for dissolving thought. Its idealism overlaps [māyā/avidyā doctrine](../05-concepts-and-dharma/maya-and-avidya.md) but is more radical: appearance is not merely veiled Brahman, it is mind-stuff with no independent arising. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Utpatti)
- **The seven bhūmikās** — A ladder of seven stages of knowledge, from *śubhecchā* ("longing for the good") through *vicāraṇā*, *tanumānasā*, *sattvāpatti*, *asaṃsakti*, *padārtha-abhāvanī*, to *turyagā* — abiding in [the fourth state](../05-concepts-and-dharma/turiya-and-the-four-states.md); the higher stages describe the jīvanmukta. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Utpatti & Nirvāṇa)
- **Kāka-tālīya nyāya** — The recurring "crow and palm-fruit" maxim (a crow lands just as the fruit happens to fall): the text's emblem for the merely coincidental, cause-less way world-events associate in consciousness. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, passim)
- **Syncretism** — The received text fuses Advaita Vedānta with Sāṃkhya-Yoga vocabulary, **Trika/Pratyabhijñā** (Kashmir Śaiva — see [Śaiva schools](../07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/shaiva-schools.md)) ideas of consciousness-vibration, and turns of thought scholars compare with **Mahāyāna Vijñānavāda** idealism; how much is borrowing vs. shared milieu is debated. [scholarly] [disputed] For the darśana map see [Ātman, Brahman & the six darśanas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/atman-brahman-and-the-six-darshanas.md).

## The great upākhyānas (embedded tales)

- **Queen Līlā's nested worlds** — Queen **Līlā**, dreading her husband King **Padma's** death, wins the goddess **Sarasvatī's** favor; when Padma dies, Sarasvatī grants Līlā disembodied, mind-borne travel to find his jīva. She discovers that their entire royal universe exists **within the hut-space of a village brahmin, Vasiṣṭha** (a namesake householder, not the sage), who had once merely *wished* to be a king and, dying, became Padma — and that Padma has already died onward into a *further* world, reborn as young **King Vidūratha**, whose realm in turn exists inside the space of Padma's death-chamber. Worlds nest inside worlds like dream within dream; time runs at different rates in each (days in one frame are a lifetime in another). Līlā and Sarasvatī watch Vidūratha fall in battle, and his jīva returns to Padma's preserved corpse, which revives; Līlā, now knowing all worlds to be consciousness, lives on liberated. The corpus's most elaborate statement that creation is *nirmāṇa* — mental projection. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Utpatti prakaraṇa)
- **Karkaṭī, the rākṣasī who became a needle** — The Himālayan demoness **Karkaṭī** (of the [rākṣasa](../08-beings-and-bestiary/rakshasas.md) race), tormented by insatiable hunger, performs fierce tapas and asks Brahmā to become an **iron needle-like pin (sūcikā)** — the piercing sickness **Viśūcikā** — so she may enter and consume all creatures. Granted, she afflicts beings invisibly, but (Brahmā also having given a counter-mantra to protect the good) she grows weary of her formless misery, repents, and by renewed tapas regains her giant body — now Self-knowing. Meeting a mountain **king and his minister** by night, she tests them with a long chain of **riddles about the Ātman** (what is the atom that contains worlds? what is, and is not? …); their answers satisfy her, she becomes their friend and the land's protectress, taking only condemned criminals as food. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Utpatti prakaraṇa)
- **The tale of the three non-existent princes** — A nurse's story to a child: "In a city that never existed lived three princes — two were never born and the third was never even conceived…" — the text's own parable for how the mind furnishes a vivid world out of sheer non-existence, and believes it. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Utpatti prakaraṇa)
- **Śukra's mind-made lifetimes** — Young **Śukra**, son of **Bhṛgu**, meditating alone, glimpses an apsarā and follows her *in mind* to Indra's heaven; through purely mental experience he lives out enjoyment there, then falls through a chain of **successive rebirths** — while his actual body sits withering on the hillside. Bhṛgu, finding the husk, rises in fury against **Kāla** (Time/Death), who calmly shows him the whole chain of Śukra's imagined lives and the law that mind alone transmigrates; Śukra's jīva is recalled to the dried body, which revives — and he goes on to become the asuras' preceptor. One consciousness, many bodies, all mind-spun. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Sthiti prakaraṇa)
- **Dāma, Vyāla, and Kaṭa** — Three demons conjured by the asura-magician **Śambara** are invincible precisely while they are **ego-less** (no "I" to fear for); once battle-experience breeds self-identity and the fear of death, the gods defeat them — egohood itself is the vulnerability. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Sthiti prakaraṇa)
- **Gādhi's māyā-plunge** — The brahmin **Gādhi**, granted a vision of Viṣṇu's māyā, enters water and instantly lives an entire other life: he dies, is reborn as a **caṇḍāla (Katañja)**, becomes by chance **king of the Kīra country**, is exposed and disgraced, immolates himself — then surfaces from the water where only moments have passed, and later finds "evidence" of that life in the world. Māyā authenticates itself. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Upaśama prakaraṇa)
- **Cūḍālā and Śikhidhvaja** — Queen **Cūḍālā**, wife of King **Śikhidhvaja** of Mālava, attains Self-knowledge (and yogic powers, including flight) while ruling; her husband, still seeking, abdicates to practice austerities in the forest. Rather than shame him, Cūḍālā comes to him **disguised as the young brahmin sage Kumbha** ("son of Nārada") and, as his teacher, dismantles his renunciate's attachment to renunciation itself — he burns his hut, staff, and rosary, and finally rests in the Self. She then **tests** him: as Kumbha she "becomes" by curse a woman (**Madanikā**) each night whom he serenely accepts as wife; she conjures an illusory **Indra** tempting him to heaven, and a scene of Madanikā's infidelity — he remains desireless and even-minded. Cūḍālā reveals herself; the two return as **jīvanmukta king and queen** and rule for ten thousand years — the text's fullest portrait of liberation-in-action, and one of Sanskrit literature's most striking woman-guru narratives. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Nirvāṇa prakaraṇa, Pūrvārdha)
- **Bhuśuṇḍa, the immortal crow** — Vasiṣṭha recounts visiting **Bhuśuṇḍa**, an ancient crow dwelling in a nest on the **kalpa-taru on a peak of Mount Meru**: the longest-lived of embodied beings, he has watched **ages and dissolutions (pralayas) repeat** — he remembers the churning of the ocean and other great events recurring in kalpa after kalpa, and world-configurations differing from cycle to cycle — surviving even cosmic fire and flood by resting in **prāṇa-contemplation** (the *prāṇa–apāna* movement), abiding in the space of consciousness while creation burns. His discourse to Vasiṣṭha is the corpus's chief teaching on **prāṇa-vidyā** and on longevity through witness-stillness. (*Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Nirvāṇa prakaraṇa, Pūrvārdha)
  - *Parallel (Rāmcaritmānas):* Tulsīdās's crow-narrator **Kākabhuśuṇḍi**, who retells the Rāma-kathā to Garuḍa and likewise remembers many repetitions of Rāma's deeds across kalpas, is this figure's devotional (bhakti) counterpart — see [Rāmcaritmānas](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md) and [the Chiranjīvis](../04-deep-dives/the-chiranjivis.md). [late text]

## The Mokṣopāya thesis and the dating debate [scholarly]

- **The Mokṣopāya recension** — Text-critical work (Walter Slaje, Jürgen Hanneder, and the Mokṣopāya project) holds that the oldest recoverable form of the work is the Kashmiri **Mokṣopāya**, composed by an anonymous author (tradition of the recension: on **Pradyumna hill** in Śrīnagar) around **950 CE** — a text that presents itself as rational analysis available to anyone, *not* yet claiming Vālmīki's authorship or Vedāntic scriptural authority. [scholarly]
- **Vedānticization into "Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha"** — In later centuries the Mokṣopāya was reworked — verses altered and added, the Vālmīki attribution and Rāmāyaṇa prestige foregrounded, the doctrine aligned with Advaita Vedānta — producing the vulgate *Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* printed in the Nirṇaya Sāgara edition with the **Tātparya-prakāśa** commentary of **Ānandabodhendra Sarasvatī**; the separate **Mokṣopāya-ṭīkā** of the Kashmiri **Bhāskarakaṇṭha** (c. 17th–18th c.) comments on the unrevised text. [scholarly]
- *Variant (older scholarship):* earlier studies dated the work far more broadly — B. L. Atreya and others argued for a kernel **before or near Gauḍapāda** (6th–7th c. CE) with growth to the 13th–14th c.; S. N. Dasgupta treated it as an idealist Vedānta work of roughly the 7th–8th c. The 10th-century Mokṣopāya dating is now the prevailing text-critical view, but the question remains genuinely debated. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **Terminus by quotation** — The abridgement by **Abhinanda of Kashmir** (see below) and citations in later Kashmiri and pan-Indian literature anchor the text's existence by the 10th–11th centuries; the vulgate's final shape is generally put by the **14th century**. [scholarly]

## Abridgements, translations, and reach

- **Laghu-Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha** — The influential abridgement (c. **9th–10th c.**) by **Abhinanda**, paṇḍit of Kashmir — roughly **6,000 verses** (counts vary by edition [disputed]) — which became the form in which the work traveled most widely, and itself a key datum in the dating debate. [scholarly]
- **Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha-Sāra** — An anonymous devotional-contemplative distillation of about **223–230 verses** in 10 chapters; prized in the milieu of **Ramaṇa Mahaṛṣi**, whose ashram circulated English renderings (Ramaṇa also cited Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha stories — notably Cūḍālā — in his talks). [scholarly]
- **Persian translations (Jūg Bāsisht)** — The work crossed into the Mughal-Persian world repeatedly: a translation of the Laghu by **Niẓām al-Dīn Pānīpatī** was prepared for **Prince Salīm** (the future Jahāngīr) in **1597**; the Ṣafavid philosopher **Mīr Findiriskī** studied, excerpted, and glossed the Persian text in Iran; and a fresh translation was made in the circle of **Dārā Shukoh** (mid-17th c.) — making it one of the most-translated Sanskrit works of the Indo-Persian encounter. [scholarly]
- **English** — **Vihari Lala Mitra's** four-volume complete translation (Calcutta, **1891–1899**) remains the only full English version; **Swami Venkatesananda's** *The Supreme Yoga* (1976) and *Vasiṣṭha's Yoga* are the standard modern condensations; **B. L. Atreya's** *The Philosophy of the Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha* (1936) is the classic study. [scholarly]
- **Companions in temperament** — Within this corpus, its nearest kin in uncompromising non-dualism are the [Avadhūta-Gītā](../04-deep-dives/avadhuta-gita.md), the [Ribhu-Gītā](../04-deep-dives/ribhu-gita.md), and the Aṣṭāvakra-Gītā (see the [lesser-Gītās catalog](../04-deep-dives/lesser-gitas-catalog.md)); among the Rāmāyaṇas it stands with the [Adhyātma Rāmāyaṇa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/adhyatma-and-variant-ramayanas.md) as "Vedānta in story-clothing," and it is cataloged in the [Itihāsa overview](./smriti-itihasa-epics.md) and the [Rāmāyaṇa hub](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/README.md).
