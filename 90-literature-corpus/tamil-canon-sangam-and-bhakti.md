# The Tamil Canon — Sangam, Tirukkuṟaḷ & Bhakti Corpus

> Literature-corpus node. The **Tamil** stream of the canon — a parallel, partly independent scriptural tradition running alongside the Sanskrit [śruti/smṛti](./README.md) corpus. It begins with secular **Sangam** poetry (heroic-and-love verse, oldest stratum of Tamil), passes through the ethical **Tirukkuṟaḷ** and the **twin epics**, and culminates in the great **bhakti** anthologies — Śaiva **Tirumurai** (the Nāyaṉārs) and Vaiṣṇava **Nālāyira Divya Prabandham** (the Āḻvārs), the devotional fire that reshaped South Indian Hinduism in [Kali Yuga](../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md). By traditional reckoning all crystallise within the present Kali Yuga (post-3102 BCE); academic dating places the strata c. 300 BCE – 1135 CE. See sibling nodes [shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md](./shruti-vedas-and-upanishads.md), [agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md](./agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md) and the master map [README.md](./README.md).

## Orientation: where the Tamil canon sits

- **A second classical language, a second canon.** Tamil is the only modern Indian language with a continuous literary record reaching back to the pre-Common-Era centuries independent of Sanskrit. Its earliest layer — **Sangam (caṅkam) literature** — is largely **secular**, not scriptural: heroic war-poetry and love-poetry, not cosmology. The religious Tamil canon (Tirumurai, Prabandham) is **later bhakti**, smṛti-class by the Sanskrit scheme. (Sangam literature — Wikipedia; Britannica "Sangam literature")
- **The "three Sangams" legend.** Tradition (recorded in the Iṟaiyaṉār Akapporuḷ commentary, c. 8th c. CE) holds that three successive literary academies (*caṅkam*) met at Madurai under Pāṇḍya patronage over ~9,990 years, the first two lost to floods (Kumari Kandam / the submerged southern land). Only the **third Sangam's** output survives. The 9,990-year span and the first two academies are **[folk]/[disputed]** — no text from them survives; the legend retrojects deep antiquity onto the corpus. (Sangam literature — Wikipedia)
- **Tamil bhakti as the engine of the movement.** The devotional movement that later swept north India is traditionally traced to **6th–9th c. Tamil Nadu**, sung by the 63 Śaiva **Nāyaṉārs** and 12 Vaiṣṇava **Āḻvārs**. Their hymns are treated as scripture: the Tirumurai is the "Tamil Veda" of Śaiva Siddhānta; the Prabandham is the "Drāviḍa Veda" / *Tamil Veda* of Śrī Vaiṣṇavism, recited in temple liturgy beside the Sanskrit Vedas. (Tirumurai — Wikipedia; Nālāyira Divya Prabandham — Wikipedia)
- **Scripture vs. academic dating.** Tamil tradition dates much of this to a hoary "Sangam age"; linguistic and archaeological work (Tamil-Brāhmī inscriptions, internal references to weekdays/planets) gives tighter and generally later ranges. Both are flagged below.

## I. Sangam literature — the oldest stratum (c. 300 BCE – 300 CE)

The foundational secular corpus. Modern scholars apply "Sangam literature" to the verse anthologies plus the grammar **Tolkāppiyam**; composition is dated broadly **c. 300 BCE – 300 CE**, with the surviving anthologies largely compiled c. 100–250 CE. (Sangam literature — Wikipedia; ClearIAS)

- **Tolkāppiyam** - the oldest surviving Tamil work; a treatise on grammar **and** poetics, attributed to the sage **Tolkāppiyaṉ**. Three books: *Eḻuttatikāram* (orthography/phonology), *Collatikāram* (morphology/syntax), *Poruḷatikāram* (subject-matter — the *akam*/*puṟam* poetic conventions, prosody, rhetoric). It codifies the entire grammar of classical Tamil love- and war-poetry. (Tolkappiyam — Wikipedia)
  - *Variant (dating, [disputed]):* estimates range from **c. 300 BCE** (traditional/early) to **5th c. CE**; the three books may be of different ages, *Poruḷatikāram* possibly latest. Many scholars give a stratified date c. 100 BCE – 250 CE for the core.
  - *Variant (authorship, [folk]):* tradition makes Tolkāppiyaṉ a disciple of the sage **Agastya** (Akattiyaṉ) and a student of the lost *Akattiyam* grammar — a legendary lineage, not historical.

- **The poetic division: *akam* and *puṟam*.** Sangam verse splits into **akam** ("interior" — love, the private/erotic, set in five landscapes/*tiṇai*) and **puṟam** ("exterior" — war, kings, ethics, the public world). This akam/puṟam scheme governs the whole corpus. (Sangam literature — Wikipedia)

### Eṭṭuttokai — the Eight Anthologies

The **eight collections** of short-to-medium verse. (Sangam literature — Wikipedia; Unacademy notes)

- **Naṟṟiṇai** - 400 akam (love) poems.
- **Kuṟuntokai** - 400 short akam poems (the title = "anthology of short poems").
- **Aiṅkuṟunūṟu** - "the five hundred short poems," akam, arranged by the five landscapes.
- **Patiṟṟuppattu** - "the ten tens," puṟam poems praising the **Chera** kings (a key historical source for the Chera dynasty).
- **Paripāṭal** - longer poems, partly devotional (to **Tirumāl/Viṣṇu**, **Murugaṉ/Cevvēḷ**) and on rivers — a **bridge toward bhakti** within the secular corpus.
- **Kalittokai** - akam poems in the *kali* metre; often dated later than the rest.
- **Akanāṉūṟu** - "the four hundred akam poems," longer love poems.
- **Puṟanāṉūṟu** - "the four hundred puṟam poems," the great anthology of heroism, kingship, death and ethics; prime historical source for Tamil dynasties.
  - *Variant (count/ordering):* the eight are conventionally listed but enumeration of poems and ascription of individual verses to named poets vary across manuscript traditions and editions.
  - *Mnemonic note:* a traditional Tamil verse lists the eight: *"Naṟṟiṇai, Kuṟuntokai, Aiṅkuṟunūṟu, Patiṟṟuppattu, Paripāṭal, Kalittokai, Akanāṉūṟu, Puṟanāṉūṟu."*

### Pattuppāṭṭu — the Ten Idylls

Ten **longer** poems (≈100–800 lines each), the "Ten Songs/Lays." (Sangam literature — Wikipedia)

- **Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai** - a "guide" poem directing devotees to **Murugaṉ**; arguably the **earliest sustained Tamil devotional poem**, later absorbed into the Śaiva canon (it appears as part of the **Eleventh Tirumurai**). [bridge to bhakti]
- **Porunarāṟṟuppaṭai**, **Ciṟupāṇāṟṟuppaṭai**, **Perumpāṇāṟṟuppaṭai**, **Mullaippāṭṭu**, **Maturaikkāñci**, **Neṭunalvāṭai**, **Kuṟiñcippāṭṭu**, **Paṭṭiṉappālai**, **Malaipaṭukaṭām** - bardic "guide-poems" (*āṟṟuppaṭai*), city-eulogies (e.g. *Maturaikkāñci* on Madurai, *Paṭṭiṉappālai* on the Chola port Kāveripaṭṭiṉam), and landscape idylls.

- **Patiṉeṇkīḻkaṇakku — the Eighteen Minor Works.** A slightly **later** didactic/ethical cluster (post-Sangam, c. 100–500 CE), the matrix in which **Tirukkuṟaḷ** sits (see §II). Includes *Nālaṭiyār*, *Nāṉmaṇikkaṭikai*, the *Aintiṇai* love anthologies, etc. (Sangam literature — Wikipedia)

## II. Tirukkuṟaḷ — the universal ethical classic

- **Tirukkuṟaḷ** (also *Kuṟaḷ*) - a work of **1,330 couplets** (*kuṟaḷ*, the shortest Tamil metre — two lines, 7 words) in **133 chapters** of 10 couplets each, attributed to the poet-sage **Tiruvaḷḷuvar**. The supreme classic of Tamil ethical literature, secular and non-sectarian in tone. (Kural — Wikipedia)
- **Three books (*pāl*):**
  - **Aṟam** (virtue/dharma) — chapters 1–38 (*Aṟattuppāl*).
  - **Poruḷ** (wealth/polity/the conduct of worldly affairs) — chapters 39–108 (*Poruṭpāl*), the largest section.
  - **Iṉpam** (love/pleasure, *kāma*) — chapters 109–133 (*Iṉpattuppāl*).
  - *Note:* this triad maps onto three of the Sanskrit *puruṣārthas* (dharma–artha–kāma); **mokṣa/vīṭu is deliberately omitted**, a much-discussed feature. [scholarly]
- *Variant (dating, [disputed]):* traditional accounts call it the last work of the **third Sangam** (hence ~1st c. BCE / "2,200 years ago"); linguistic analysis places it **after** the Sangam corpus, c. **450–500 CE**. Overall scholarly range: 300 BCE – 5th c. CE.
- *Variant (religious affiliation):* claimed by **Hindus**, **Jains** and others. Some scholars read it as a **Jain** work (vocabulary, ahiṃsā emphasis, terms for the deity like *Ātaṉ*, *Malarmicai Ēkiṉāṉ*); the mainstream view treats it as **trans-sectarian wisdom literature**. [disputed]
- *Variant (authorship, [folk]):* legends make Tiruvaḷḷuvar a weaver of Mylapore (Chennai) and tie him to the (possibly mythical) poetess **Avvaiyār**; biography is essentially legendary.
- *Reception:* honored as *Tamiḻ Maṟai* ("the Tamil Veda") / *Poyyāmoḻi* ("the unfailing word"); the **Manimekalai** quotes Tirukkuṟaḷ couplets, fixing it **before** that epic. (Manimekalai — Wikipedia)

## III. The Twin Epics (*iraṭṭaik kāppiyaṅkaḷ*)

The two great post-Sangam narrative epics, conventionally paired. (Cilappatikaram — Wikipedia; Manimekalai — Wikipedia)

- **Cilappatikāram** ("the Tale of the Anklet," *Silappadikaram*) - attributed to **Iḷaṅkō Aṭikaḷ** ("the venerable ascetic prince," said to be a Chera prince turned **Jain** monk). Three books (*kāṇṭam*) totalling ~30 cantos:
  - **Pukārkkāṇṭam** (Chola capital Pukār) — marriage of **Kaṇṇaki** and the merchant **Kōvalaṉ**; his affair with the courtesan-dancer **Mādhavi**; ruin and reconciliation.
  - **Maturaikkāṇṭam** (Pāṇḍya capital Madurai) — Kōvalaṉ sells Kaṇṇaki's jeweled **anklet**, is falsely accused of stealing the queen's anklet, and is executed; Kaṇṇaki proves his innocence (her anklet holds rubies, the queen's pearls), curses **Madurai**, which burns.
  - **Vañcikkāṇṭam** (Chera capital Vañci) — Kaṇṇaki's deification as the goddess of chastity **Paṭṭiṉi / Kaṇṇaki Amman**; King **Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ** builds her temple.
  - *Significance:* the only Tamil epic to span all three crowned Tamil dynasties (Chera, Chola, Pāṇḍya); **syncretic**, blending Jain, Hindu and Buddhist elements; foundational to the **Paṭṭiṉi/Kaṇṇaki cult** (alive today in Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Sri Lanka).
  - *Variant (dating, [disputed]):* traditional ~**100–250 CE** (linked to Ceṅkuṭṭuvaṉ); Kamil **Zvelebil** argues "cannot be before the 5th–6th c."; Iravatham **Mahadevan** narrows to **c. 450–550 CE** (from references to weekdays and Pāṇḍya chronology). Modern consensus leans **5th c. CE**.

- **Maṇimēkalai** ("the Jeweled Girdle") - attributed to **Cīttalai Cāttaṉār** (Cāttaṉār), a grain-merchant poet of Madurai; an explicitly **Buddhist** epic in ~30 cantos. Sequel to the Cilappatikāram: its heroine **Maṇimēkalai** is the daughter of **Kōvalaṉ and Mādhavi**, a dancer who renounces the world, takes the **Buddhist** path, gains the magic alms-bowl **Amuta-curapi** (the never-empty bowl), and ends with an exposition of Buddhist logic (*pramāṇa*) refuting rival schools.
  - *Significance:* the most important **Buddhist** work in Tamil; a unique window onto South Indian Buddhism. It **quotes the Tirukkuṟaḷ**, dating it after that text.
  - *Variant (dating, [disputed]):* commonly **c. 6th c. CE**; range ~2nd–6th c. CE in older scholarship, later dating now favored.
- *The "Five Great Epics" (*Aimperuṅkāppiyaṅkaḷ*):* later tradition groups these two with **Cīvaka Cintāmaṇi** (Jain), **Vaḷaiyāpati** and **Kuṇṭalakēci** (the last two largely lost) as the five great Tamil epics. [late text]

## IV. The Śaiva Tirumurai — the "Tamil Veda" of Śiva (Nāyaṉārs)

The **twelve-book** Śaiva canon, hymns to **Śiva** in Tamil, composed 6th–12th c. CE and canonized as scripture in **Śaiva Siddhānta**. The early hymns were lost and (by legend) **rediscovered** by the Chola king **Rājarāja I** (985–1014 CE) in a sealed chamber at Chidambaram, then **arranged into the canon by Nampi Āṇṭār Nampi** (c. late 10th–11th c.). The 12-book whole was finalized after Cēkkiḻār's *Periya Purāṇam* (1135 CE). (Tirumurai — Wikipedia; Tevaram — Wikipedia)

- **Tēvāram** = books **1–7**, the core hymns of the "three" (*mūvar*): **Campantar (Sambandar)**, **Appar** and **Cuntarar (Sundarar)**. (Tevaram — Wikipedia)
  - **Books 1–3 — Tirukñāṉacampantar (Sambandar)** - child-prodigy saint, 7th c.; hymns called *Tirukkaṭaikkāppu*. Associated with the defeat of Jainism at the Pāṇḍya court.
  - **Books 4–6 — Tirunāvukkaracar / Appar** - "father," a former Jain who reconverted to Śaivism, 7th c.; hymns called *Tēvāram* / *Tēvārap patikaṅkaḷ*.
  - **Book 7 — Cuntaramūrtti / Sundarar** - 8th c.; hymns called *Tiruppāṭṭu*. His list of devotees, the *Tiruttoṇṭattokai*, is the **seed-list of the 63 Nāyaṉārs** later expanded by Cēkkiḻār.
- **Book 8 — Māṇikkavācakar** - 9th c.; **not** counted among the three Tēvāram saints but among the great four (*nālvar*). Two works:
  - **Tiruvācakam** ("the sacred utterance") - 51 ecstatic devotional hymns, the most beloved Tamil bhakti poetry ("*he whose heart is not melted by the Tiruvācakam cannot be melted by any vācakam*").
  - **Tirukkōvaiyār** - 400 verses in akam (love-poetry) convention, Śiva as the divine lover.
- **Book 9 — Tiruvicaippā & Tiruppallāṇṭu** - hymns by **nine** poets (Tirumāḷikaittēvar, Cēntaṉār, Karuvūrttēvar, the Chola king **Gaṇḍarāditya**, Vēṇāṭṭaṭikaḷ, et al.).
- **Book 10 — Tirumantiram of Tirumūlar** - ~3,000 verses; the foundational **Śaiva Siddhānta + Tamil yoga/Tantra** text (8th c. or earlier by tradition). A scripture in its own right.
- **Book 11** - a miscellany of **12 poets**, including **Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār** (the earliest woman saint, 6th c.), the Chera king-saint **Cēramāṉ Perumāḷ Nāyaṉār**, **Nakkīrar**, and **Nampi Āṇṭār Nampi** himself; the *Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai* (from Pattuppāṭṭu) is included here.
- **Book 12 — Periya Purāṇam (Tiruttoṇṭar Purāṇam) of Cēkkiḻār** - **1135 CE**; the hagiography of all **63 Nāyaṉārs**, composed under Chola **Kulottuṅga II**. It "expands" Sundarar's *Tiruttoṇṭattokai* and Nampi's *Tiruttoṇṭar Tiruvantāti* into full lives. The definitive source for the Nāyaṉār legends (Kaṇṇappar, Ciṟuttoṇṭar, etc.). (Tirumurai — Wikipedia)
  - *Variant ([folk]):* the *Periya Purāṇam* is itself sometimes treated as a **devotional purāṇa** parallel to the Sanskrit Purāṇas — hence the "Periya" (great) Purāṇam.
- *Variant (compilation):* tradition credits **Nampi Āṇṭār Nampi** with arranging books 1–11 (under Rājarāja I); the 12-book canon's final shape with the *Periya Purāṇam* appended is **12th c.**. Counts of "63 Nāyaṉārs" derive from Sundarar's list **plus** later additions, so the named 63 are a **post-facto canon**.

## V. The Vaiṣṇava Nālāyira Divya Prabandham — the "Drāviḍa Veda" (Āḻvārs)

The **4,000-verse** (*nālāyira* = 4,000) Tamil hymn-corpus to **Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa**, composed by the **12 Āḻvārs** ("those immersed [in God]," 5th/6th–9th c. CE), canonized as the **Tamil Veda** of Śrī Vaiṣṇavism and recited in temple liturgy. Compiled into its present form by **Nāthamuni** (c. 9th–10th c.), the first of the Śrī Vaiṣṇava ācāryas, who (by legend) recovered the lost verses through yogic meditation on Nammāḻvār at Āḻvārtirunagari. (Nālāyira Divya Prabandham — Wikipedia)

- **The 12 Āḻvārs** (canonical order varies): **Poykai**, **Bhūtam (Pūtattāḻvār)**, **Pēy** (the "first three," *mutal āḻvārs*), **Tirumaḻicai**, **Nammāḻvār** (the greatest), **Madhurakavi** (disciple of Nammāḻvār), **Kulacēkara** (a Chera king), **Periyāḻvār**, **Āṇṭāḷ** (Kōtai — the **only woman** Āḻvār, foster-daughter of Periyāḻvār), **Toṇṭaraṭippoṭi**, **Tiruppāṇ**, and **Tirumaṅkai** (the most prolific). (Nālāyira Divya Prabandham — Wikipedia)
- **Four-thousand division (four *āyiram*/thousands):**
  - **Mutalāyiram** ("first thousand") - includes **Periyāḻvār Tirumoḻi**, Āṇṭāḷ's **Tiruppāvai** (30 verses, the most recited) and **Nācciyār Tirumoḻi**, Kulacēkara's **Perumāḷ Tirumoḻi**, etc.
  - **Periya Tirumoḻi** - largely **Tirumaṅkai Āḻvār's** great body of song.
  - **Iyaṟpā** - the *antāti* and *veṇpā*-metre works.
  - **Tiruvāymoḻi** ("the holy word of mouth") - **1,102 verses** by **Nammāḻvār**, the supreme jewel of the corpus, treated as the Tamil **Sāma Veda**; the focus of vast commentary.
- **Nammāḻvār's four works** are mapped onto the four Sanskrit Vedas in tradition: *Tiruviruttam* (Ṛg), *Tiruvāciriyam* (Yajur), *Periya Tiruvantāti* (Atharva), *Tiruvāymoḻi* (Sāma). [scholarly/traditional]
- **The 108 Divya Deśams** - the 108 Viṣṇu temples sung in the Prabandham (106 on earth + 2 celestial), the sacred geography of Śrī Vaiṣṇavism. (Nālāyira Divya Prabandham — Wikipedia)
- *Variant (dating, [disputed]):* Āḻvār life-dates span **5th–9th c. CE** in modern scholarship; **traditional** Śrī Vaiṣṇava chronology pushes the earliest Āḻvārs into the **Dvāpara/early Kali Yuga** (e.g. Āṇṭāḷ and others placed thousands of years earlier) — a **scriptural** dating sharply at odds with academic dating.
- *Variant (verse count):* "4,000" is canonical and idealized; surviving recensions vary slightly around the figure, and the exact apportionment among the four thousands differs by tradition/sampradāya (Vaṭakalai vs. Teṉkalai recitation usages).

## VI. Dating summary (scripture vs. academia)

| Text / corpus | Traditional dating | Academic dating |
|---|---|---|
| Tolkāppiyam | c. 300 BCE (or earlier, Agastya lineage) | c. 100 BCE – 250 CE (stratified; *Poruḷ* possibly to 5th c.) |
| Sangam anthologies (Eṭṭuttokai, Pattuppāṭṭu) | "third Sangam," deep antiquity | c. 300 BCE – 300 CE (compiled ~100–250 CE) |
| Tirukkuṟaḷ | last work of third Sangam (~1st c. BCE) | c. 450–500 CE (post-Sangam) |
| Cilappatikāram | c. 100–250 CE | c. 450–550 CE (5th c.) |
| Maṇimēkalai | post-Cilappatikāram | c. 6th c. CE |
| Tēvāram (Nāyaṉārs) | 6th–8th c. (saints' lives) | 7th–8th c. CE |
| Tiruvācakam (Māṇikkavācakar) | 9th c. | 9th c. CE |
| Divya Prabandham (Āḻvārs) | Dvāpara/early Kali (very early) | 5th–9th c. CE; compiled 9th–10th c. (Nāthamuni) |
| Periya Purāṇam (Cēkkiḻār) | 1135 CE | 1135 CE (12th c.) |
