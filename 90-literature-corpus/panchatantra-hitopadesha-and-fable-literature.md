# Panchatantra, Hitopadeśa & the Nīti-Kathā Fable Corpus

> Literature-corpus node. The **kathā (story) genre** — Smṛti-adjacent worldly literature, below even the Purāṇas in scriptural rank yet arguably India's most-travelled export: the **nīti-kathā** ("conduct-tale") fable books that teach *nīti* — the wise conduct of life, statecraft, friendship and survival — through talking animals inside nested frame-stories. All of it is **Kali-Yuga documented history** on the academic clock (core c. 200 BCE–300 CE onward **[scholarly]**); the tradition itself claims only a wise teacher, not revelation. Sits beside the [Itihāsa](smriti-itihasa-epics.md) and the [regional/oral epics](regional-and-oral-epics.md) in the master map [README.md](README.md); its ethics plug into [artha & the puruṣārthas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md), and its animal cast into [`../08-beings-and-bestiary/`](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md).

---

## Orientation: what nīti-kathā is

- **Nīti, the subject matter** — not mokṣa-teaching but *worldly* wisdom: how to win allies, detect flattery, survive kings, wage and avoid war. The Panchatantra calls itself a **nīti-śāstra** in story form; Arthur Ryder famously rendered *nīti* as "the wise conduct of life." It is the narrative sibling of the prose statecraft manuals (Arthaśāstra, Kāmandaki's *Nītisāra*) cataloged with the [Dharmaśāstras](agamas-tantras-dharmashastras.md), and of the ethics material in [`../05-concepts-and-dharma/`](../05-concepts-and-dharma/ahimsa-and-hindu-ethics.md). (Panchatantra, Kathāmukha; Ryder, introduction)
- **The frame-story engine** — the genre's signature device: an outer story opens, a character quotes a maxim, is asked "how was that?", and a new story opens inside it — *emboxed* tales three and four layers deep, each sealed with a verse. This architecture runs through the whole kathā corpus: Panchatantra, Hitopadeśa, Śukasaptati, Bṛhatkathā/Kathāsaritsāgara, Vetāla tales. (Panchatantra I–V, structure throughout)
- **Rank on the canon ladder** — the fable books are **neither Śruti nor formally listed Smṛti**; they are secular/didactic kāvya-adjacent literature. Yet tradition dignifies them: the Panchatantra's own preamble claims it distills the essence of **artha-śāstra and dharma-śāstra** for the education of princes. **[late text]** relative to the Vedic-epic core.
- **Two clocks** — *Traditional frame:* a timeless teacher (Viṣṇuśarman) composes the five books for a king's sons; no yuga-address is claimed beyond ordinary Kali-Yuga human history. *Academic frame:* the Panchatantra core is dated **c. 200 BCE – 300 CE** (Patrick Olivelle: "around 300 CE, give or take a century or two"), with the oldest surviving recension (Tantrākhyāyikā, Kashmir) and the fullest (Pūrṇabhadra, **1199 CE**) much later **[scholarly]**. See the documented-history spine at [recorded-history.md](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/recorded-history.md).

## The Viṣṇuśarman frame-story (Kathāmukha)

- **King Amaraśakti's problem** — in the southern city of **Mahilāropya**, King Amaraśakti despairs of his three dull sons (named **Bahuśakti, Ugraśakti, Anantaśakti** in Pūrṇabhadra's recension), "hostile to education." His ministers advise that a full grammar-first education takes twelve years — too long. (Panchatantra, Kathāmukha / prologue)
- **Viṣṇuśarman's wager** — the aged brahmin **Viṣṇuśarman** (eighty years old, past all desire for reward) promises to make the princes masters of nīti **within six months**, or forfeit his name. He composes **five books (tantras)** and teaches the princes through them; the wager succeeds, and the five books circulate as the *Pañcatantra* ("five treatises"). (Panchatantra, Kathāmukha)
  - *Variant (authorship):* southern recensions and the Southeast-Asian (Thai/Lao *Nandaka-prakaraṇa*, Javanese *Tantri Kāmandaka*) lines attribute the work to **Vasubhāga** rather than Viṣṇuśarman; scholarship treats both names as legendary rather than historical authors. **[scholarly]/[disputed]**
  - *Variant (recensions):* no single "original" survives. Main lines: **Tantrākhyāyikā** (Kashmir, oldest extant), the **southern Pañcatantra**, the Nepali line, the Bṛhatkathā-embedded versions (below), and **Pūrṇabhadra's Jain recension of 1199 CE** — the longest and today's de-facto vulgate (basis of Ryder's and most modern translations). Franklin **Edgerton (1924)** critically reconstructed an ur-text from the branches. **[scholarly]**

## The five tantras — frame-tales and signature stories

*(Book frames and placements below follow the Pūrṇabhadra recension of 1199 CE unless flagged; other recensions shuffle, add, or drop embedded tales.)*

### Book I — Mitra-bheda ("The Separation of Friends")

- **Piṅgalaka, Sañjīvaka, and the two jackals** — the frame: merchant Vardhamānaka abandons his exhausted bull **Sañjīvaka** in the forest; the bull recovers and his bellow terrifies the lion-king **Piṅgalaka**. Two jackal ministers' sons, **Karaṭaka and Damanaka**, broker a friendship between lion and bull — then **Damanaka**, feeling sidelined, poisons each against the other until Piṅgalaka kills Sañjīvaka; Karaṭaka's remonstrances frame much of the book's moralizing. The longest book, on how friendships are broken by calumny at court. (Panchatantra I)
- **The monkey and the wedge** — the tale traditionally told first: a meddling monkey pulls a half-driven wedge from a beam and is crushed — "meddle not in what is not your business." (Panchatantra I, embedded)
- **The lion and the hare** — the clever hare, last in line to be eaten by the lion **Bhāsuraka**, arrives late, blames a "rival lion," and lures Bhāsuraka to a well to attack his own reflection — wit defeats strength. (Panchatantra I, embedded)
- **The blue jackal** — the jackal **Caṇḍarava** falls into a dyer's vat of indigo, is taken by the forest animals for a heaven-sent king, rules — and betrays himself when he howls with the other jackals; the courtiers he had scorned destroy him. On forgetting one's own kind. (Panchatantra, Pūrṇabhadra recension, Book I embedded)
  - *Variant (placement):* the tale also circulates in the **Hitopadeśa** and in later folk retellings with differing frames and morals. **[folk]**

### Book II — Mitra-samprāpti ("The Winning of Friends")

- **Crow, mouse, tortoise, deer** — the frame: the dove-king **Citragrīva** and his flock, netted by a fowler, fly off *together* carrying the net and are freed when the mouse **Hiraṇyaka** gnaws it. The crow **Laghupatanaka**, awed, befriends the mouse; they join the tortoise **Mantharaka** at his lake, and the deer **Citrāṅga** completes the quartet. When the deer is trapped and the tortoise captured while helping, the four cooperate in a staged rescue — the book-length argument that **alliance among the weak defeats the strong**. (Panchatantra II)

### Book III — Kākolūkīya ("Of Crows and Owls")

- **The war of crows and owls** — the frame: the owl-king **Arimardana** massacres the crows by night; the crow-king **Meghavarṇa's** aged counselor **Sthirajīvin** has himself pecked, disgraced, and "defects" to the owls, gains their trust against wiser counsel, then guides the crows to **burn the owls' cave-fortress at noon** while they sleep. The book of war, espionage, and the peril of sheltering a former enemy. (Panchatantra III)
- **The hares and the elephants** — a hare-envoy speaks for "the Moon" and shows the elephant-king the trembling lunar reflection in the lake, frightening the herd from trampling the hare-warren — the weak prevail by borrowed authority. (Panchatantra III, embedded)
- **The brahmin and the three rogues** — three swindlers, stationed along the road, each in turn calls the brahmin's sacrificial goat a dog until he abandons it in disgust and they eat it — repetition manufactures belief. (Panchatantra III, embedded)

### Book IV — Labdha-praṇāśa ("The Loss of Gains")

- **The monkey and the crocodile** — the frame: the monkey **Raktamukha** feeds sweet rose-apples to a crocodile, whose jealous wife demands the monkey's heart. The crocodile lures his friend across the river; mid-stream he confesses, and the monkey answers that he **left his heart hanging on the fig tree** — ferried back, he leaps to safety and denounces the betrayer. On escaping by presence of mind what was lost by credulity. (Panchatantra IV, frame)

### Book V — Aparīkṣita-kāraka ("Rash Deeds / Action Without Examination")

- **The brahmin and the mongoose** — the tale of the loyal mongoose: a brahmin's household mongoose, left watching the infant, kills a black snake creeping toward the cradle; greeting the returning parent with bloodied mouth, it is **killed on the spot** — and the child is found safe beside the torn serpent. The genre's starkest verse-lesson against acting before examining. (Panchatantra V, embedded in Pūrṇabhadra; frame-story of Book V in the Tantrākhyāyikā/Edgerton reconstruction)
  - *Variant (who kills):* recensions split over whether the **brahmin Devaśarman or his wife** strikes the fatal blow. The motif travelled west as the Welsh legend of the dog **Gelert**. **[scholarly]** (Distinguish the *other* famous mongoose, the golden mongoose of the Mahābhārata's dāna-critique — see [dana-upakhyanas-shveta-and-the-mongoose.md](../04-deep-dives/dana-upakhyanas-shveta-and-the-mongoose.md).)
- **The barber who killed the monks** — Pūrṇabhadra's Book-V frame: the merchant **Maṇibhadra** dreams that a Jain monk struck with a stick turns to gold; it works — and a watching barber batters innocent monks expecting the same, ending on the gallows. Imitation without understanding. (Panchatantra V, Pūrṇabhadra frame)

## Transmission: the most-travelled book of India

- **Pahlavi crossing (c. 550 CE)** — the physician **Burzōy (Borzūya)** translates the Panchatantra into **Middle Persian (Pahlavi)** at the court of the Sasanian king **Khosrow I Anūshirvān**; the Pahlavi is lost, but a **Syriac** version (*Kalilag and Damnag*, c. 570 CE) survives from it. **[scholarly]**
- **Kalīla wa-Dimna (c. 750 CE)** — **ʿAbdallāh Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ** renders the Pahlavi into Arabic as **Kalīla wa-Dimna** — the jackals Karaṭaka and Damanaka living on as *Kalīla* and *Dimna*. It becomes a foundational classic of Arabic prose and the parent of the western branch. **[scholarly]**
- **Into Europe: the Fables of Bidpai** — from the Arabic: Greek (Symeon Seth, c. 1080), Hebrew (Rabbi Joel, 12th c.), and **John of Capua's Latin *Directorium Humanae Vitae*** (13th c., printed 1480), whence German, Spanish, Italian and **Sir Thomas North's English (1570)** — circulating as the **Fables of Bidpai/Pilpay** ("Bidpai" a corruption of *vidyāpati*, "master of learning"). **Jean de La Fontaine** (Fables, second collection, 1678) credits "Pilpay, sage indien" by name. Edgerton counted some **200 versions in over 50 languages** — a passing UNESCO-cited claim ranks its diffusion next only to scripture (cf. the note in [ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/recensions/ramcharitmanas-tulsidas.md)). **[scholarly]**
- **Embedded in the Bṛhatkathā stream** — the Kashmiri retellings of Guṇāḍhya's lost **Bṛhatkathā** carry the Panchatantra whole: Kṣemendra's *Bṛhatkathāmañjarī* (c. 1037 CE) and **Somadeva's Kathāsaritsāgara (c. 1070 CE), Lambhaka X**, which narrates the five books inside the Naravāhanadatta saga — see the Kathāsaritsāgara's cast under [Vidyādharas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md). **[scholarly]**

## Nārāyaṇa's Hitopadeśa ("Beneficial Instruction")

- **The text** — a Sanskrit reworking of the Panchatantra by the poet **Nārāyaṇa** under a patron named **Dhavala Candra**, composed (most hold) in **Bengal**; the author names the Panchatantra and "another work" — identified as **Kāmandaki's Nītisāra** — as his sources. Dated between c. **800 and 1373 CE**, the latter being its earliest surviving manuscript (Nepal). **[scholarly]/[late text]** (Hitopadeśa, preamble)
- **Four books, not five** — **Mitralābha** (winning friends), **Suhṛdbheda** (splitting friends — the Piṅgalaka/Sañjīvaka matter, reversed in order from the Panchatantra), **Vigraha** (war) and **Sandhi** (peace), the last two spinning the crow–owl war into a treatise on diplomacy between the goose-king Hiraṇyagarbha and the peacock-king. (Hitopadeśa I–IV)
- **Its own frame** — King **Sudarśana of Pāṭaliputra**, despairing of his sons, engages a sage — again called **Viṣṇuśarman** — who teaches them through these tales; the frame openly recycles the Panchatantra's. (Hitopadeśa, preamble)
- **Afterlife** — long the standard first reader of Sanskrit students; among the earliest Sanskrit works printed (Serampore 1803–04, ed. Carey), and a staple of colonial-era translation (Sir Edwin Arnold, 1861). **[scholarly]**

## Śukasaptati and the wider kathā shelf

- **Śukasaptati ("Seventy Tales of the Parrot")** — frame: with the merchant's son away, his wife **Prabhāvatī** resolves nightly to visit a lover; the household **parrot** tells a tale each night, pausing at a dilemma only resolvable at dawn, until — seventy tales later — the husband returns and the marriage is saved. Wit-tales, tricksters and clever wives; the parrot as nīti-teacher. Sanskrit text of uncertain date (extant recensions c. 12th c. CE; two lines, *simplicior* and *ornatior*) **[scholarly]/[late text]**; famously adapted into Persian as **Nakhshabī's Ṭūṭī-nāma (1330 CE)**.
- **Bṛhatkathā of Guṇāḍhya** — the lost mother-ocean of Indian story, composed in the **Paiśācī** dialect; legend makes it Śiva's own tale overheard and carried down by the gaṇa Puṣpadanta reborn, finally told to the poet Guṇāḍhya, who burned six of its seven parts when King Sātavāhana spurned it. Survives only through retellings: **Kathāsaritsāgara** ("Ocean of the Streams of Story," Somadeva, Kashmir, c. 1070 CE), *Bṛhatkathāmañjarī* (Kṣemendra), *Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha* (Budhasvāmin). **[scholarly]** (Kathāsaritsāgara, Kathāpīṭha)
- **Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati ("Twenty-five Tales of the Vetāla")** — King **Vikramāditya (Trivikramasena)** carries a corpse possessed by a riddling **vetāla**; each tale ends in a dilemma the king must answer, whereupon the corpse flies back to its tree. Embedded in the Kathāsaritsāgara (Book XII); vernacularized as the Hindi **Baitāl Pacīsī**. Cross-link the vetāla as a being-class in [vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md). **[late text]**
- **Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā ("Thirty-two Tales of the Throne")** — the 32 statuettes of Vikramāditya's recovered throne each tell King **Bhoja** a tale of Vikramāditya's magnanimity before he may ascend it; medieval Sanskrit, widely vernacularized (Siṅhāsan Battīsī). **[late text]**
- **Downstream and sideways** — the nīti-fable style feeds the vernacular story-hoards (cf. the [Tamil canon's](tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md) didactic corpus and the [oral epic](regional-and-oral-epics.md) layer), and its animal-fable logic keeps company with the bestiary rosters of [`../08-beings-and-bestiary/`](../08-beings-and-bestiary/nagas-and-serpents.md) — though its jackals and crows are pointedly *ordinary* animals, mirrors of human courts rather than divine beings.
