# Nyāya Sūtras of Gautama — Per-Text Profile

> Part of the **[Literature Corpus](../README.md)** → the six **[Darśanas](../upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md)**. Dedicated profile of **Akṣapāda Gautama's Nyāya Sūtras**, the root text (*mūla*) of the **Nyāya** darśana — Hindu philosophy's school of **logic, debate, and epistemology** (*pramāṇa-śāstra*, *ānvīkṣikī* = the "science of inquiry"). Nyāya is the classical *twin* of [Vaiśeṣika](../upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md) (its sister atomistic ontology), and the realist-theist counterweight to ritualist [Mīmāṃsā](../upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md) and to the [Brahma Sūtras](./brahma-sutras.md)' Vedānta. Companion to the [Brahma Sūtras profile](./brahma-sutras.md).
>
> **Two clocks:** by tradition the seer **Gautama / Akṣapāda** (the *r̥ṣi* "whose eyes are on his feet") is a sage of remote antiquity, the system being eternal *ānvīkṣikī* taught since [Treta/Dvāpara](../../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/README.md); academically the surviving sūtra-stratum is **c. 2nd c. BCE – 2nd c. CE** (likely multi-layered), with the developed school running into the medieval *Navya-Nyāya*.

---

## 1. What the text is

- **Name & genre** — *Nyāya-sūtra* ("aphorisms of *nyāya*"), where **nyāya** = "right reasoning / logical method / that by which a thing is led (nī) [to a conclusion]." The school's older self-description is **ānvīkṣikī** — the "science of inquiry / critical investigation," the discipline of examining things *after* (anu) perception and scripture by **reasoning**. Also called **Tarka-śāstra** (science of reasoning/debate) and **Pramāṇa-śāstra** (science of valid knowledge). (Wikipedia "Nyāya Sūtras"; Britannica "Nyaya")
- **Author** — **Akṣapāda Gautama** (also *Gotama*, *Dīrghatapas*); "Akṣapāda" = "having eyes (akṣa) on the feet (pāda)" — a nickname glossed as a sage so absorbed he watched his own steps, or who attached an eye to his foot. **Distinct from** Gautama Buddha and from the Vedic Gotama r̥ṣi, though tradition conflates the names. (Wikipedia; IEP "Nyaya")
- **Function** — to systematize the **means of valid knowledge (pramāṇa)** and the **art/rules of debate**, so that **correct knowledge (tattva-jñāna)** can be reached, error destroyed, and through it **liberation (apavarga / niḥśreyasa)** attained. Nyāya is *soteriology through epistemology*: it argues that the whole apparatus of logic exists to remove **mithyā-jñāna** (false knowledge), which is the root of suffering. (IEP; Britannica)
- **Sūtra brevity** — like all [darśana sūtras](../upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md) (cf. [Brahma Sūtras](./brahma-sutras.md)), the aphorisms are maximally compressed and unreadable without a *bhāṣya*; **Vātsyāyana's Nyāya-bhāṣya** is the indispensable first commentary (see §8).

## 2. Architecture — 5 / 10 / ~528

- **The standard count** — **5 adhyāyas** (books) → each with **2 āhnikas** (daily lessons / "diurnal portions") = **10 āhnikas** → **~528 sūtras**. (Wikipedia "Nyāya Sūtras"; Britannica)
  - *Variant (sūtra count):* the commonly cited total is **528**, but because Naiyāyikas split/merge sūtras differently the figure varies in editions (c. **528**, also reported as ~**538**); treat **5/10 as fixed, the sūtra total as commentator-dependent.** [disputed count]
- **Book I — the *uddeśa* (enumeration / table of contents)** — introduces and *defines* the **16 padārthas** (categories, §3); Āhnika 1 names and characterizes them, Āhnika 2 details the debate-categories (vāda, jalpa, vitaṇḍā, the fallacies, etc.). The "dictionary" book that the rest of the text expands. (Wikipedia; Britannica)
- **Book II — *pramāṇa* (epistemology)** — examines and defends the **four pramāṇas** (§4); answers skeptical attacks (a Mādhyamika-style objection that *no* means of knowledge can be validated without infinite regress), and treats doubt (saṃśaya) and the parts of inference. (Wikipedia; IEP)
- **Book III — *prameya* (the knowable objects / metaphysics)** — examines the **12 prameyas** (§5): the self (ātman), body, senses, their objects, cognition (buddhi), mind (manas) — arguing for a **permanent self** against the Buddhist no-self doctrine. (Wikipedia)
- **Book IV — *pravṛtti, doṣa, duḥkha, apavarga*** — activity, faults/defects, rebirth, the fruits of karma, suffering, and **liberation (apavarga)**; includes the famous epistemological digression on the reality of the external world and the whole/part (avayava/avayavin) problem against Buddhist idealism. The soteriological core. (Wikipedia; IEP)
- **Book V — *jāti* and *nigraha-sthāna*** — an exhaustive **catalogue of futile rejoinders (jāti, 24 types)** and **points of defeat (nigraha-sthāna, 22 types)** in debate; the "how to win/lose an argument" manual. (Wikipedia; Britannica)

## 3. The 16 padārthas (categories of *nyāya*)

> The defining list of the school: **knowing all sixteen correctly is itself said to conduce to liberation** (NS 1.1.1). They are *not* an ontology of the world (that is Vaiśeṣika's 6/7 categories) but a **logic-and-debate** category-set.

- **1. Pramāṇa** — the **means of valid knowledge** (perception, inference, comparison, testimony). (§4)
- **2. Prameya** — the **objects of valid knowledge** — the twelve knowables culminating in the self and liberation. (§5)
- **3. Saṃśaya** — **doubt**; the unsettled cognition wavering between alternatives, the *starting point* of all inquiry.
- **4. Prayojana** — **purpose / motive**; the aim that prompts one to act or investigate.
- **5. Dr̥ṣṭānta** — **example / illustrative instance**; a case on which disputant and opponent *agree*, used as evidential anchor (e.g., "the kitchen" as a known fire-smoke locus).
- **6. Siddhānta** — **established tenet / accepted doctrine** (four kinds: universally accepted, school-specific, implied, hypothetically granted).
- **7. Avayava** — the **members (limbs) of a syllogism** — the five-membered inference (§6).
- **8. Tarka** — **hypothetical/suppositional reasoning** (*reductio*); reasoning toward truth by exposing the absurdity of the contrary; an *aid* to pramāṇa, not itself a pramāṇa (§7).
- **9. Nirṇaya** — **ascertainment / settled conclusion**; the removal of doubt by deliberation, the *terminus* of inquiry.
- **10. Vāda** — **honest debate** (truth-seeking discussion). (§7)
- **11. Jalpa** — **wrangling / victory-seeking debate**. (§7)
- **12. Vitaṇḍā** — **destructive/cavilling debate** (refute without asserting a counter-thesis). (§7)
- **13. Hetvābhāsa** — **fallacious reasons / pseudo-probans** (the five classic logical fallacies, §6).
- **14. Chala** — **quibbling / equivocation**; willfully misconstruing an opponent's words (three kinds: verbal, generalizing, metaphorical).
- **15. Jāti** — **futile/unfair rejoinders** (24 types catalogued in Book V); answering by mere false analogy.
- **16. Nigraha-sthāna** — **points/grounds of defeat** (22 types in Book V); the conditions under which a debater is judged to have lost (self-contradiction, silence, irrelevance, etc.).
  - *(Source for the 16-list: Wikipedia "Nyāya Sūtras"; wisdomlib "Nyayasutra"; Britannica.)*

## 4. The four pramāṇas (sources of valid knowledge)

> Nyāya is the great **four-pramāṇa** school. (Contrast: [Vaiśeṣika](../upavedas-vedangas-darshanas.md) accepts only 2; Sāṃkhya 3; Mīmāṃsā/Vedānta 6.) Each pramāṇa yields a distinct, irreducible kind of knowledge.

- **1. Pratyakṣa — Perception** — knowledge born of **sense-object contact (indriyārtha-sannikarṣa)**, "non-erroneous, non-verbal, determinate" (NS 1.1.4). Subdivided into **nirvikalpaka** (bare/indeterminate, pre-conceptual) and **savikalpaka** (determinate, conceptualized) perception; later Nyāya adds extraordinary (*alaukika*) perception. The *primary* pramāṇa from which the others derive. (Philosophy Institute; Wikipedia "Pramana")
- **2. Anumāna — Inference** — knowledge of one thing through another via **vyāpti** (invariable concomitance / universal pervasion), e.g., fire from smoke. Three kinds (NS 1.1.5): **pūrvavat** (cause→effect, clouds→rain), **śeṣavat** (effect→cause, flood→upstream rain), **sāmānyatodr̥ṣṭa** (by general correlation, the sun's motion from its changed position). Formalized as the **five-membered syllogism** (§6). (Wikipedia; Ghosh, "Nyaya Theory of Pramana")
- **3. Upamāna — Comparison / analogy** — knowledge of the **relation between a name and the thing it names**, via likeness: told "a *gavaya* (wild ox) is like a cow," one later sees the animal in the forest and *identifies* it. A distinct pramāṇa, irreducible to inference or testimony. (Philosophy Institute; Wikipedia "Pramana")
- **4. Śabda — Verbal testimony** — knowledge from the **statement of a reliable authority (āpta)**; Gautama defines it as **āptopadeśa**, "the instruction of a trustworthy person" (NS 1.1.7). Two kinds: about **perceptible** matters and about **imperceptible** matters (dharma, the afterlife — here the supreme āpta is **Īśvara/the Veda**, §9). The pramāṇa that makes scripture epistemically binding. (Legalosphere; Wikipedia)
- **What Nyāya rejects** — it does **not** accept as independent pramāṇas: *anupalabdhi* (non-apprehension — Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsā/Advaita do), *arthāpatti* (postulation — Mīmāṃsā does), *aitihya* (tradition), *sambhava* (inclusion) — folding these into the four above. (Wikipedia "Pramana")

## 5. The 12 prameyas (objects of knowledge — Nyāya's metaphysics)

- The **twelve knowables** (NS 1.1.9): **(1) ātman** (the self/soul), **(2) śarīra** (body), **(3) indriya** (the senses), **(4) artha** (sense-objects), **(5) buddhi** (cognition/intellect), **(6) manas** (the inner organ/mind), **(7) pravr̥tti** (activity), **(8) doṣa** (faults: rāga/dveṣa/moha — attachment, aversion, delusion), **(9) pretyabhāva** (rebirth/transmigration), **(10) phala** (fruit of action), **(11) duḥkha** (suffering), **(12) apavarga** (final release). (Wikipedia "Nyāya Sūtras"; wisdomlib)
- **The chain of bondage and release** — Nyāya threads these into a causal sequence: **false knowledge (mithyā-jñāna) → faults (doṣa) → activity (pravr̥tti) → rebirth (pretyabhāva) → suffering (duḥkha)**; reverse the chain by destroying false knowledge with **right knowledge (tattva-jñāna)**, and the sequence unwinds to **apavarga** (NS 1.1.2). Epistemology *is* the path to mokṣa. (IEP; EBSCO "Nyaya")
- **The permanent self** — Book III argues at length that **ātman is a real, permanent substance** (knower, enjoyer, agent), inferred from desire, aversion, effort, pleasure, pain, and cognition — directly **against the Buddhist anātman** (no-self) doctrine. Liberation (apavarga) is the soul's **absolute cessation of suffering** — notably a *negative*, painless-but-not-blissful state (unlike Vedānta's *ānanda*-Brahman). [contrast with Vedānta] (IEP; Wikipedia)

## 6. The five-membered syllogism (pañcāvayava-vākya)

> Nyāya's signature contribution to world logic: a **formal inference stated in five steps**, combining what Western logic separates into deduction and induction-by-example.

- **1. Pratijñā (proposition)** — *"The hill has fire."* (the thesis to be proved)
- **2. Hetu (reason / probans)** — *"because it has smoke."* (the mark, *liṅga*)
- **3. Udāharaṇa (exemplification + universal rule)** — *"Wherever there is smoke, there is fire — as in a kitchen."* (states the **vyāpti** + a corroborating example, *sapakṣa*)
- **4. Upanaya (application)** — *"This hill has smoke [such as is invariably accompanied by fire]."* (subsumes the case under the rule)
- **5. Nigamana (conclusion)** — *"Therefore the hill has fire."* (re-asserts the thesis as proved)
  - *(The canonical "hill / smoke / fire" example: Wikipedia "Nyāya Sūtras"; wisdomlib "Five parts of syllogism.")*
- **Why five, not three** — the udāharaṇa makes the **universal grounded in an observed example**, so Nyāya inference is simultaneously *empirical* and *deductive*; Western Aristotelian syllogism collapses to three terms, and later Indian logicians (and some Naiyāyikas) debated whether 3 members suffice. [scholarly]
- **The five fallacies (hetvābhāsa, "pseudo-reasons")** (NS 1.2.4 ff.) — a flawed *hetu*: **(1) savyabhicāra/anaikāntika** (erratic, too wide), **(2) viruddha** (contradictory), **(3) prakaraṇasama/satpratipakṣa** (counterbalanced), **(4) sādhyasama/asiddha** (unproved, question-begging), **(5) kālātīta/bādhita** (mistimed / sublated). Mastery of these is what separates valid inference from sophistry. (Wikipedia; IEP)

## 7. Tarka and the three debate-types

- **Tarka (hypothetical reasoning)** — not a pramāṇa but an **aid to inference**: *suppositional reasoning* that supports a conclusion by showing the **absurdity of its contrary** (a *reductio ad absurdum* / *prasaṅga*). Used to confirm vyāpti when direct evidence is incomplete. (Wikipedia "Nyāya Sūtras"; IEP)
- **Vāda — honest/good debate** — discussion between two parties **both seeking truth**, each using valid pramāṇa and tarka, fair syllogisms, and conceding when refuted. The ideal, *truth-directed* dialogue (teacher–student, or fellow inquirers). (Britannica; IEP)
- **Jalpa — wrangling** — debate aimed at **victory, not truth**; legitimate pramāṇas *plus* the permitted "tricks" — quibble (chala), futile rejoinders (jāti), and seizing on the opponent's points of defeat (nigraha-sthāna). The combative courtroom/disputation mode. (Britannica; wisdomlib)
- **Vitaṇḍā — cavilling/destructive debate** — pure **refutation without advancing any counter-thesis** of one's own; the debater only demolishes. Nyāya regards it as the lowest form (associated with skeptics who hold no position, esp. the Mādhyamika); winning by destruction alone. (Britannica; IEP)
  - *Note:* jalpa and vitaṇḍā were defended as **"protective" debate** — fences (like thorn-branches round a sapling) to guard the truth from bad-faith opponents, even if they themselves are not truth-seeking. [scholarly]

## 8. Commentaries — the Prācīna ("Old") Nyāya tradition

- **Vātsyāyana — *Nyāya-bhāṣya*** (a.k.a. *Pakṣilasvāmin*; **c. 5th c. CE**, dated variously 4th–6th c.) — the **earliest surviving and authoritative commentary**; effectively *constitutes* classical Nyāya, since the bare sūtras are read through it. Begins the school's running engagement with Buddhist logicians. (Motilal Banarsidass; Wikipedia "Naiyayika")
- **Uddyotakara — *Nyāya-vārttika*** (**c. 550–630 CE**) — sub-commentary on Vātsyāyana; a vigorous defense of Nyāya realism against **Diṅnāga's Buddhist logic** (apoha theory, two-membered inference). (Wikipedia "Naiyayika")
- **Vācaspati Miśra — *Nyāya-vārttika-tātparya-ṭīkā*** (**c. 9th–10th c.**) — the great polymath's gloss on Uddyotakara; consolidates the old school. (IEP)
- **Udayana — *Nyāya-kusumāñjali*** & *Ātma-tattva-viveka* (**c. 975–1000 CE**) — the apex theist-logician; his *Kusumāñjali* ("handful of flowers [of argument]") gives the most developed **proofs of Īśvara** (§9) and "refutes" Buddhist no-self/atheism; transitional figure toward Navya-Nyāya. (Philosophy Institute; SEP "Gangesa")

## 9. The theistic proof — Nyāya's Īśvara vs. Mīmāṃsā's authorless Veda

- **Nyāya's God (Īśvara)** — a **single, eternal, omniscient Lord** who is the **intelligent efficient cause (nimitta-kāraṇa)** of the world (the *material* cause being the eternal atoms — Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika atomism); He orders the atoms, dispenses karmic fruits, and is the **author/utterer of the Veda**. Established by **inference (anumāna)**, not mere scripture — Nyāya is the school that argues God *rationally*. (Philosophy Institute; Britannica)
- **The arguments (developed esp. by Udayana, *Nyāya-kusumāñjali*, ~9 proofs)** —
  - **Kāryānumāna (cosmological/from-effect):** the world is an *effect* (it has parts, is non-eternal); every effect has an intelligent maker (like a pot ⇒ potter); therefore the world has an intelligent maker = Īśvara. (prepp.in; Philosophy Institute)
  - **From the Veda's authority:** the Veda is **valid yet impersonal in transmission** — its authority must trace to an **omniscient author (āpta)**, Īśvara, whose word makes śabda-pramāṇa reliable for *adr̥ṣṭa* matters (dharma, afterlife).
  - Also: the regulation of atoms/karma (*adr̥ṣṭa* needs a supervising intellect), and arguments from the transmission of skills, language, etc.
- **The Mīmāṃsā contrast — *apauruṣeya*** — **Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā denies any divine author**: the Veda is **apauruṣeya** ("not authored by any person," human or divine), **eternal, beginningless, and self-validating (svataḥ-prāmāṇya)**. Mīmāṃsakas argue an omnipotent perfect being would have **no motive (desire) to create**, and that an *eternal* Veda needs *no* author at all — so Nyāya's Veda-authorship proof is superfluous. This is the central **theist (Nyāya) vs. ritualist-atheist (Mīmāṃsā)** fault-line over the Veda. (Wikipedia "Apauruṣeyā"; Mīmāṃsā sources; kavvanah.blog "Nyaya and Monotheism")
  - *Note:* both schools accept the Veda as supremely authoritative — they **disagree only on *why*** (divinely authored & rationally inferred vs. authorless & self-evident). Vedānta ([Brahma Sūtras](./brahma-sutras.md)) sides closer to Mīmāṃsā on apauruṣeyatva while rejecting Nyāya's atomism. [contrast]

## 10. Navya-Nyāya — the "New Logic"

- **Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya — *Tattva-cintāmaṇi*** ("Thought-jewel of Reality," **c. 1325 CE**, Mithilā) — founds **Navya-Nyāya** ("New Nyāya"); the watershed dividing the *Prācīna* (old) school from the *Navya* (new). Reorganizes the whole system **around the four pramāṇas** (its four books = pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, śabda), dropping the 16-padārtha frame as the organizing scheme. (Wikipedia "Tattvachintamani"; SEP "Gangesa")
- **What was "new"** — a **hyper-precise technical metalanguage** for analyzing cognition and relations (*viśiṣṭa* qualifier–qualificand structures, **avacchedaka** "delimitor," abstraction operators like *-tva/-tā*) — a near-symbolic logic developed *without* mathematical notation; it made Indian logic extraordinarily exact about the *form* of inference and the conditions of vyāpti. (SEP "Gangesa")
- **The Navadvīpa school** — flourished esp. in Bengal (Navadvīpa) with **Raghunātha Śiromaṇi** (*Dīdhiti*, c. 1500), **Gadādhara**, **Jagadīśa**; this technical idiom spread into Sanskrit grammar, poetics, and Dharmaśāstra, becoming the lingua franca of late Sanskrit scholasticism. [scholarly] (SEP; IEP)

## 11. Dating — the two clocks

- **Traditional / scripture clock** — Akṣapāda Gautama is an ancient r̥ṣi; *ānvīkṣikī* is reckoned a primordial Vedic auxiliary science, the sūtras a timeless revelation of method (no historical date in tradition). [scripture]
- **Academic clock** — the sūtra-text is **multi-layered**: a core perhaps **2nd c. BCE**, reaching roughly its **present form by c. 2nd c. CE**. Estimates span **6th c. BCE – 2nd c. CE**; **Nāgārjuna's (c. 2nd c. CE) references** give a *terminus ante quem* for the surviving recension. The school then develops continuously through Vātsyāyana (~5th c.) → Udayana (~10th c.) → Gaṅgeśa (~14th c.). [scholarly] (Wikipedia "Nyāya Sūtras"; Britannica)
  - *Variant (composite authorship):* many scholars hold "Akṣapāda Gautama" names a *tradition/redactor* rather than a single author, given internal anti-Buddhist strata of different periods. [disputed]
