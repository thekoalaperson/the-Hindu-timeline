# Śruti — Vedas & Upanishads

> Part of `90-literature-corpus/` (the texts that RECORD the cosmic chronology, mapped back to yuga/event). Śruti ("that which is heard") is the highest-authority tier of Hindu scripture — the Vedas and their four strata, culminating in the Upanishads. Traditionally **apauruṣeya** (authorless / not of human origin) and eternal; on the cosmic clock they are "re-heard" and re-compiled by the Vyāsa of each Dvāpara Yuga, the most recent being Kṛṣṇa-Dvaipāyana Vyāsa at the close of the current 28th Mahāyuga's Dvāpara (see `../01-brahma-current-life/the-14-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/03-dvapara-yuga.md`).

---

## 1. What "Śruti" Is — and How It Sits on the Cosmic Clock

- **Śruti vs Smṛti.** Śruti ("heard") = the Vedas, held to be eternal, revealed sound perceived by ṛṣis (seers) in meditation; supreme authority. Smṛti ("remembered") = human-authored tradition (Itihāsa, Purāṇas, Dharmaśāstra, etc.) ranked below Śruti (Mīmāṃsā / Vedānta doctrine).
- **Apauruṣeya doctrine.** The Veda is *not* composed; it is co-eternal with Brahman, "seen" (dṛṣṭa) by ṛṣis, who are transmitters not authors (Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā of Jaimini; Vedānta).
  - *Variant (Nyāya / Vaiśeṣika):* the Veda is authoritative because it issues from a reliable speaker — God (Īśvara) — i.e. authored-by-God, not strictly authorless.
  - *Variant (Mīmāṃsā, Jaimini):* the Veda has no author at all, divine or human; its authority is intrinsic (svataḥ-prāmāṇya); even Īśvara is not posited as its source.
- **Vyāsa as compiler.** At each Dvāpara–Kali junction the divine arranger Vyāsa ("the divider") splits the single Veda into four for the diminished memory of Kali-Yuga humans (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.3–4; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 1.4; Mahābhārata). There are traditionally **28 Vyāsas** across the 28 Mahāyugas of the current Vaivasvata Manvantara; the 28th is **Kṛṣṇa-Dvaipāyana** ("Veda-Vyāsa," father of Dhṛtarāṣṭra/Pāṇḍu, narrator of the Mahābhārata).
- **Academic counter-frame.** Indologists treat the Vedas as orally composed human texts, layered over roughly **c. 1500–500 BCE** (see §6), preserved by extraordinarily exact oral mnemonics (pāṭha recitation styles) — a flat contradiction of apauruṣeya that this file records as the scholarly variant, not as a correction of the tradition.

## 2. The Four Vedas

- **Ṛgveda** — the oldest; ~1,028 hymns (sūktas) in 10 maṇḍalas, ~10,600 verses (ṛc) to Agni, Indra, Soma, Varuṇa, Uṣas, the Maruts, etc. Recited by the **Hotṛ** priest. (Ṛgveda Saṃhitā)
  - Notable: **Nāsadīya Sūkta** (10.129, creation/skepticism hymn "then was neither being nor non-being"); **Puruṣa Sūkta** (10.90, cosmic person sacrificed, origin of the four varṇas); **Gāyatrī mantra** (3.62.10).
- **Yajurveda** — sacrificial prose formulas (yajus) for ritual action; recited by the **Adhvaryu** priest. Split into two great branches:
  - **Śukla (White) Yajurveda** — saṃhitā (Vājasaneyi) kept *separate* from its Brāhmaṇa; "clear/orderly." Houses the famous **Śatarudriya / Rudram** and the **Īśā Upaniṣad** (as its 40th chapter).
  - **Kṛṣṇa (Black) Yajurveda** — mantra and Brāhmaṇa-style commentary *intermixed*; "unarranged." Source of the **Taittirīya** texts.
  - *Variant (origin myth):* the Śukla/Kṛṣṇa split is mythologized — Yājñavalkya, cursed by his teacher Vaiśampāyana, "vomited" the older (Kṛṣṇa) Yajus, which other pupils took as partridges (tittiri → Taittirīya); the Sun-god (Sūrya, as a horse, Vājin) then taught him the fresh Śukla recension (Vājasaneyi). (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.5; later commentarial tradition) [legendary etiology]
- **Sāmaveda** — melodic chant; ~1,875 verses, nearly all *borrowed from the Ṛgveda* (esp. maṇḍalas 8–9) but set to sāman melodies; recited by the **Udgātṛ** priest. Root of Indian classical music. (Sāmaveda Saṃhitā)
- **Atharvaveda** — spells, healing charms, blessings, curses, domestic and royal rites, plus speculative cosmological hymns; ~730 hymns / ~6,000 mantras in 20 books. Associated with the **Brahman** priest (overseer). Later/peripheral acceptance into the canon ("trayī," the triple Veda, originally named only the first three).
  - *Variant (canon status):* some orthodox sources speak of the **trayī vidyā** (threefold knowledge = Ṛk, Yajus, Sāman) and treat the Atharva as a later or lesser fourth; other traditions fully include it, yielding the standard "four Vedas." (Discussed across Brāhmaṇa literature and Mīmāṃsā.)

## 3. The Four-Layer Structure (each Veda has all four strata)

A movement from outward ritual action to inward contemplative knowledge. Together a Veda's four layers run: **Saṃhitā → Brāhmaṇa → Āraṇyaka → Upaniṣad.**

- **Saṃhitā ("collection")** — the core mantras/hymns/formulas themselves. The liturgical bedrock.
- **Brāhmaṇa** — prose manuals explaining the sacrifice (yajña): procedure, rationale, myth, etymology. Karma-kāṇḍa (the "action portion"). E.g. Aitareya & Kauṣītaki (Ṛg); Śatapatha (Śukla Yajus); Taittirīya (Kṛṣṇa Yajus); Pañcaviṃśa/Tāṇḍya & Jaiminīya (Sāma); Gopatha (Atharva).
  - **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa** notable: contains the **Manu + flood / Matsya (fish-avatāra) story** — earliest extant Hindu deluge account (1.8.1), see `../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/` (Viṣṇu avatāras) and `../01-...mahayuga-28-current/01-satya-yuga.md`.
- **Āraṇyaka ("forest text")** — transitional; for forest-dwelling renunciates (vānaprastha); internalizes ritual into meditation/symbolism. E.g. Aitareya & Kauṣītaki Āraṇyaka; Taittirīya Āraṇyaka.
- **Upaniṣad** — the culminating philosophical stratum ("sitting near" a teacher); jñāna-kāṇḍa (the "knowledge portion") = **Vedānta** ("end/culmination of the Veda"). Inquiry into Brahman (ultimate reality), Ātman (self), karma, rebirth, mokṣa.

*Note:* the boundary between Āraṇyaka and Upaniṣad is fluid — several Upaniṣads are literally the final chapters of an Āraṇyaka or Brāhmaṇa (e.g. Bṛhadāraṇyaka = end of the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa; Chāndogya = part of a Sāmaveda Brāhmaṇa; Taittirīya Upaniṣad inside the Taittirīya Āraṇyaka).

## 4. The Śākhās (Recensions / "Branches")

Each Veda was transmitted in multiple śākhās ("branches") — regional schools each preserving its own saṃhitā + Brāhmaṇa + Āraṇyaka + Upaniṣad. The **Caraṇavyūha** (attributed to Śaunaka) catalogs the traditional totals; only a fraction survive.

- **Ṛgveda** — traditionally **5 śākhās** (Caraṇavyūha); **fully extant: Śākala** (the standard text) **and Bāṣkala**; fragments of Āśvalāyana / Śāṅkhāyana (latter rediscovered among Rajasthan reciters).
- **Yajurveda** — by far the most branched; figures cited as **86 (or 101) śākhās**, of which ~**5** survive:
  - *Śukla:* **Mādhyandina** and **Kāṇva** (Vājasaneyi recensions).
  - *Kṛṣṇa:* **Taittirīya** (dominant, South India), **Maitrāyaṇī**, **Caraka-Kaṭha** (Kāṭhaka), **Kapiṣṭhala-Kaṭha** (partial).
- **Sāmaveda** — traditionally **~1,000 śākhās** ("a thousand paths," sahasra-vartman); only **Kauthuma**, **Rāṇāyanīya**, and **Jaiminīya (Talavakāra)** survive.
- **Atharvaveda** — traditionally **9 śākhās**; surviving: **Śaunaka** (the printed vulgate) and **Paippalāda** (preserved in Odisha/Gujarat manuscripts and partial recitation).
- *Variant (counts):* the Caraṇavyūha numbers differ across recensions of the Caraṇavyūha itself (e.g. Yajus given as 86 in some, 101 in others; Sāma "1000" is conventional/hyperbolic). Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* famously states there were **21 Ṛgveda, 100 Yajus, 1000 Sāma, 9 Atharva** śākhās — yet another tally. [counts disputed across sources]

## 5. The Upaniṣads

### 5a. The Mukhya (Principal) Upaniṣads — the ancient core

Variously counted as **10, 11, 12, or 13** "principal" Upaniṣads (the ones Śaṅkara and other Vedāntins commented on / Vedānta is built upon). The standard expanded list of **13 mukhya**, by Veda:

- **Ṛgveda:** **Aitareya**, **Kauṣītaki** (Kauṣītaki-brāhmaṇa).
- **Sāmaveda:** **Chāndogya** (largest after Bṛhadāraṇyaka; "tat tvam asi"), **Kena** (Talavakāra).
- **Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda:** **Taittirīya** (the five kośas / sheaths; Ānanda-mīmāṃsā), **Kaṭha** (Naciketas & Yama dialogue on death; the chariot-of-the-self image), **Śvetāśvatara** (theistic, Rudra-Śiva, bridge to bhakti), **Maitrī / Maitrāyaṇīya** (latest of the 13).
- **Śukla Yajurveda:** **Bṛhadāraṇyaka** (oldest & longest; Yājñavalkya–Maitreyī & Yājñavalkya–Gārgī dialogues; "neti neti"), **Īśā** (shortest; Yajus ch. 40).
- **Atharvaveda:** **Muṇḍaka** ("two birds on one tree"; higher vs lower knowledge), **Praśna** (six questions), **Māṇḍūkya** (12 verses on AUM and the four states of consciousness — turīya; basis of Gauḍapāda's Kārikā).

- *Variant (the "canonical 10/11"):* the older shortlist commented by Śaṅkara is usually given as **Īśā, Kena, Kaṭha, Praśna, Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Taittirīya, Aitareya, Chāndogya, Bṛhadāraṇyaka** (10); often **Śvetāśvatara** is added (11); some add **Kauṣītaki** and **Maitrī** to reach 13. Counts differ by tradition/commentator. [count disputed]
- *Variant (relative age, academic):* Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya are reckoned oldest (pre-Buddhist, c. 800–600 BCE); Kaṭha, Īśā, Śvetāśvatara, Muṇḍaka, Praśna, Māṇḍūkya, Maitrī are later. (Tradition treats all as beginningless Śruti — see §6.)

### 5b. The Muktikā 108 — the closed list

The **Muktikā Upaniṣad** (itself an Atharvaveda minor Upaniṣad) records a dialogue in which **Rāma instructs Hanumān** that liberation (kaivalya / videha-mukti) comes from studying the **108 Upaniṣads**, then enumerates them. This is the conventional "complete" list. (Muktikā Upaniṣad)

- Distribution across the four Vedas (per Muktikā): **Ṛgveda 10, Śukla Yajurveda 19, Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda 32, Sāmaveda 16, Atharvaveda 31** (= 108).
- Thematic classification of the 108 (Muktikā tradition / Colebrooke–Deussen scholarship):
  - **Mukhya (principal):** 13 (some lists 10) — the core above.
  - **Sāmānya-Vedānta (general):** ~21 — general philosophy.
  - **Sannyāsa:** ~17–19 — renunciation, monastic life.
  - **Yoga:** ~20 — haṭha/laya/mantra/dhyāna yoga (e.g. Yogatattva, Nādabindu, Haṃsa).
  - **Vaiṣṇava:** ~14 — Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa/Kṛṣṇa-Rāma theology (e.g. Nṛsiṃhatāpinī, Rāmatāpinī, Gopālatāpinī).
  - **Śaiva:** ~14–15 — Śiva/Rudra (e.g. Kaivalya, Atharvaśiras, Rudrahṛdaya).
  - **Śākta:** ~8–9 — Devī/Goddess (e.g. Devī, Tripurā, Bahvṛca).
  - *Variant (exact tallies):* the sub-counts vary by source — e.g. mukhya given as 11 vs 13; Sannyāsa as 17 vs 19; Śākta as 8 vs 9 — depending on whether one follows the Muktikā numbering or modern editors (Deussen, Schrader). The *total* is fixed at 108 (a sacred number). [sub-counts disputed]
- *Variant (more than 108):* many regional/sectarian Upaniṣads exist beyond the Muktikā 108 (e.g. **Allopaniṣad** [late, syncretic, disputed], **Gaṇapatya**, **Saura**, Christian-era and even apocryphal texts). Estimates run to **200+** named Upaniṣads; the Muktikā 108 is the "closed canon" only by one tradition. [late / non-canonical material flagged]

## 6. Dating — Traditional vs Academic (catalog of frames)

- **Traditional (Śruti / apauruṣeya frame):** the Vedas are eternal, beginningless, without author; not "dated" at all. Their *current arrangement* into four was effected by **Vyāsa at the end of the present Dvāpara Yuga**, i.e. just before Kali began in **3102 BCE** (Viṣṇu Purāṇa; Bhāgavata Purāṇa; Mahābhārata). On this frame the texts as compiled are ~5,100+ years old; as revelation they are timeless.
  - *Variant (sectarian dating polemics):* some traditional/revisionist Indian scholars push Ṛgvedic composition to the 4th–7th millennium BCE or earlier (linking to Sarasvatī-river drying, astronomical references à la Tilak/Jacobi). [disputed; not mainstream academic]
- **Academic (philological) frame:**
  - **Ṛgveda Saṃhitā** — bulk (maṇḍalas 2–9) **c. 1500–1200 BCE** (NW subcontinent / Punjab); maṇḍala 10 and the other Saṃhitās **c. 1200–900 BCE** (eastward, toward the Ganges).
  - **Brāhmaṇas & redaction of Saṃhitās** — **c. 1000–500 BCE** (the "circum-Vedic" period).
  - **Āraṇyakas & early Upaniṣads** (Bṛhadāraṇyaka, Chāndogya) — **c. 800–500 BCE**, pre-Buddhist.
  - **Later mukhya Upaniṣads** (Kaṭha, Māṇḍūkya, Śvetāśvatara, Maitrī, etc.) — **c. 500 BCE – early CE**.
  - **Minor / sectarian Upaniṣads of the Muktikā 108** — many are **medieval (1st millennium CE and later)**, well after the Vedic period proper. [late texts]
- Both frames agree on one mechanism: **oral transmission** of staggering fidelity (saṃhitā-pāṭha plus permutation recitations — pada, krama, jaṭā, ghana — encoding the text redundantly), which is *why* the wording was preserved whether one dates origin to 1500 BCE or to a prior Dvāpara Yuga.
