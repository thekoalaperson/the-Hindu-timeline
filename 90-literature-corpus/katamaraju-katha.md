# Katamaraju Katha — the Telugu cattle-war epic of Nellore

> Literature-corpus node, the **Telugu chapter of the oral-epic layer** — sibling of the pan-Indian survey [regional-and-oral-epics.md](regional-and-oral-epics.md) (whose §IV catalogs only its twin, the [Palnāḍu cycle](regional-and-oral-epics.md)) and of the master map [README.md](README.md). This is the **Katamaraju Kathalu** (Telugu *Kaṭama Rāju Kathalu*): the sung saga of a migrant **Yādava (Golla) cattle-chief** whose grazing-rights pact with **Nallasiddhi**, the Telugu-Choḍa king of Nellore, collapses into a great cattle-war. By traditional reckoning its hero is a scion of [Kṛṣṇa's Yādava line](../04-deep-dives/krishna-lila.md) deep in [Kali Yuga](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/README.md); academically it is a **living oral ballad-cycle** built around a **[scholarly] c. 1280–1297 CE** frontier war, first collected in print only in the 20th century. Everything here is **[folk]/[oral tradition]/[regional]** unless tagged otherwise.

---

## Orientation — the "second" great Telugu oral epic

- **The longest Telugu ballad-cycle** — The Katamaraju cycle is reckoned, after the [Palnāḍu epic](regional-and-oral-epics.md), the **oldest, longest and finest historical ballad-cycle in Telugu — and, by length, the longest of the two**. Where Palnāḍu narrates a single fratricidal war, Katamaraju is a **multi-generational cycle** of some **thirty-two long ballads**, each running past **2,000 lines**, arranged in epic sequence. Unlike Palnāḍu it has received little comprehensive scholarship, and most of its ballads remain unknown to the literary world. (Subba Rao's reckoning, per V. Narayanappa, *Ballad Cycle of Katama Raju in Telugu*, 2021) **[scholarly]/[folk]**
- **A pastoralist epic, not a martial-caste epic** — Its defining subject is **cattle**: a shepherd-king who "loves his cattle as his kith and kin," a war triggered by grazing rights and a cattle-tribute, and cows and oxen that **fight in the battle-line like heroes**. It is the classic Deccan **herder's counter-epic**, the Telugu peer of the Kannada [Juñjappa](regional-and-oral-epics.md) cowherd cycle and the Marathi Dhangar shepherd-god songs of [Birobā and Mhaṅkobā](dhangar-oral-epics-biroba-mhaskoba-dhuloba.md). **[folk]**
- **The deification engine** — As across the [oral-epic belt](regional-and-oral-epics.md), the fallen heroes become **gods**: Katamaraju and his kin are worshipped at village shrines and honoured in ritual dramatization on the banks of the **Paleru (Pālēru) river** in Prakāśam district, where the great battle is remembered. **[folk]**

## The two clocks

- **Scriptural / cyclic frame** — In the ballads' own genealogy Katamaraju is a **Kṣatriya of the Lunar race (Candravaṃśa)**, reckoned the **74th descendant from Viṣṇu and the 23rd from [Śrī Kṛṣṇa](../04-deep-dives/krishna-lila.md)** — i.e., the epic plants its cattle-king squarely on the Yādava trunk that runs from Dvāpara-Yuga Kṛṣṇa down into Kali Yuga. Katamaraju himself is held an **incarnation of Viṣṇu**, as is the miraculous single-horned white cow **Bollāvu**. No yuga-arithmetic is attached; folk memory, not śāstric chronology, places him "long ago" in Kali Yuga (which began **3102 BCE**). **[folk]**
- **Academic / historical frame** — **[scholarly]** The core war is datable: **Nallasiddhi** and his general **Khaḍga Tikkana** are the two historical persons of the cycle. Nallasiddhi was a **Telugu-Choḍa (Choḍa) king of Nellore** whose reign fell in the **last quarter of the 13th century**; inscriptions and ballads place the war during the closing years of the **Kākatīya queen Rudramadevī**, while **Pratāparudra** was heir-apparent — i.e., **c. 1280–1297 CE**. The oral cycle survives on **palm-leaf manuscripts** in minstrel households and was systematically collected only in the 20th century. **[scholarly]**
- **A name-collision to keep straight** — Some historians identify Nallasiddhi with **Manumasiddhi (II)** of Nellore, the patron of the poet Tikkana; the ballad tradition and Narayanappa distinguish them, making **Nallasiddhi the son of Manumasiddhi**. The corpus records the disagreement rather than resolving it. **[disputed]/[scholarly]**
  - *Variant (Wikipedia/popular summaries):* the Nellore adversary is given as **"Nalama Siddi / Manumasiddhi II,"** collapsing the two into one and dating the feud to **c. 1260 CE** — a reading the specialist literature rejects. **[disputed]**

## The Yādava line — three generations toward Katamaraju

- **The cycle's span** — The thirty-two ballads trace the **adventures of three generations**, from the patriarch **Avula Vālu Rāju** down to his grandson Katamaraju, "undergone epic process with a continuous theme." (Narayanappa 2021, after Subba Rao) **[folk]**
- **Vālu Rāju and his brothers** — **Kavulavula Ganga Rāju**, ruling **Elamanchi** (in present Visakhapatnam district), had four sons — **Vālu Rāju, Haripāludu, Nanda Gopāludu, Govardhanudu** — who all fell fighting **Kālabhūta Gaṅgu Rāju** of Khandemondemula Kōṭa. The names alone (Nanda-Gopa, Govardhana) advertise the Kṛṣṇa-Yādava self-image of the herder line. **[folk]**
- **Peddi Rāju's five sons** — Avula Vālu Rāju by his wife **Vallamma** had five sons — **Siṃhādri Rāju, Peddi Rāju, Yeranuka Rāju, Balanuka Rāju, Polu Rāju** — and a daughter **Komaramma (Komarakka)**. On the astrologers' word **Peddi Rāju** took the throne, ruling Elamanchi ten years, then Pampāḍri seven. **[folk]**
- **Katamaraju's birth** — Childless Peddi Rāju and his wife **Peddamma** worshipped Lord **Someśvara** at Kalyāṇanagara and were granted a son, **Katamaraju**, and a daughter, **Papanuka**, born under a *duṣṭa-nakṣatra* ("evil star") — an omen that shadows the whole cycle. Peddi Rāju fell at **Arlapenta** against **Valikēthuva Rāju**; the seven-year-old Katamaraju then slew Valikēthuva Rāju and avenged his father, and Peddamma performed **satī**. **[folk]**
- **Pasuvulanna, lord of the herds** — Sheltered by his uncle **Bhaktiraṇṇa** and reared at **Ḍonakoṇḍa** (present Ganga Donakonda, Prakāśam dist.), Katamaraju was **crowned sovereign over all the cattle-herds of the Yādavas**, winning the name **Pasuvulanna** ("elder-brother of the cattle"). He wed **Marlavāari Ganga / Vīra Ganga** but never lived with her, keeping **perfect brahmacarya** his whole life — a celibate cattle-king. **[folk]**

## The migration, the drought, and Goddess Ganga at the Paleru

- **The drought and the southward drive** — Grazing his herds in the **forests of Śrīśailam**, Katamaraju was driven south by a **terrible drought**, moving his cattle toward the fertile south with his lieutenants **Peyyala Yerrayya** (Nalanuka's son) and the Velama chieftain **Padma Rāghava**. This drought-migration is the hinge that carries the herder-king into Nellore's country and into war. **[folk]**
- **The wordy duel with Ganga** — At the river **Paleru**, in the **Kanigiri** region, the goddess **Ganga** barred his way, demanding that Katamaraju and his followers **worship her**. A famous **verbal duel (vāgyuddha)** followed — the epic's stock motif that "the hero thinks it beneath his dignity to salute a woman, even a goddess." At last Katamaraju saluted and worshipped Ganga; she blessed him and let his herds cross the Paleru into the south. (Ballad of Paleru / the argument between Ganga and Katamaraju) **[folk]**
- **Ganga, the Yādava kuladevata** — This is no incidental deity: **Ganga is the family goddess (kuladevata) of the Yādavas** and one of the great **[grāmadevatā village goddesses](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/gramadevatas-village-deities.md) of Andhra and Telangana** (cf. [Gangamma](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/gangamma.md); the river-goddess [Ganga](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganga.md)). Her village festivals, the **Ganga Jātaras**, are the very occasions at which the Katamaraju ballads are traditionally sung — epic and cult are one institution. **[folk]/[regional]**

## The broken grazing-pact — the parrot, the calf-tribute, and the trigger of war

- **The covenant at Nellore** — Reaching the Nellore country, Katamaraju **entered an agreement with king Nallasiddhi** for pasture and grazed his herds in the **forest of Nallamala**. The cycle preserves **two versions of what the pact was and how it broke** — cataloged, not merged. **[folk]/[disputed]**
- **The parrot version** — **Kundamādevi**, queen of Nallasiddhi, kept a **tame parrot**; it flew to the grazing-forest and vexed the cows with its singing, and **Padma Rāghava shot it dead with an arrow**. It died before the queen; enraged, she sent **hunters to slaughter the Yādava cattle**. Katamaraju killed the hunters, **refused the grazing-tax**, withdrew to Kanigiri, mustered his armies and fortified at **Yerragaddapadu (Erragaddapāḍu)**. (Ballad of Katama Raju grazing his cattle; the parrot episode) **[folk]**
  - *Variant (queen vs. concubine):* one recension makes **Kundamādevi the queen** of Nallasiddhi, another his **concubine**. Singers own their versions. **[folk]/[disputed]**
- **The calf-tribute version** — In the other telling the pact's **tribute is specific: Katamaraju agreed to hand over every male calf born to his cows during his stay** in the Nellore realm. He **broke the terms** by grazing not only the pastures but the **cornfields around Nellore city**. The queen then destroyed his cattle through hunters — **without Nallasiddhi's knowledge** — but Katamaraju, believing the king complicit, **refused the tribute**, holding the tax due to Nallasiddhi exactly cancelled by the loss inflicted on his herds. This broken calf-tribute pact is the war's proximate cause. (Narayanappa 2021, "another version") **[folk]/[disputed]**
- **The moral logic** — Both versions turn on the same charter-value: **"violation of agreement results in war,"** and the herder-king's cattle are treated as kin whose slaughter must be answered. This is the ideological core the epic exists to sing. **[folk]**

## The war — the embassy, Khaḍga Tikkana, and the great battle

- **The failed embassy** — Nallasiddhi first sent an **embassy** to Yerragaddapadu (the *Bhaṭṭu*/battle-embassy ballad), where "the ambassadors argue cleverly on behalf of their kings" — in vain. **[folk]**
- **Khaḍga Tikkana's charge** — The king then ordered his commander-in-chief **Khaḍga Tikkana, a Brahmin warrior**, to collect the overdue grazing-tax by force. Tikkana attacked the Yādavas near **Somaśila**, chased them to the banks of the **Penna (Peṇṇā) river**, killed many heroes, and **fell in the field**. (Khaḍga Tikkana is remembered in tradition as a **kinsman of the poet Tikkana Somayāji**, co-author of the Telugu Mahābhārata — one of the cycle's two historical anchors.) **[folk]/[scholarly]**
- **The battle of Yerragaddapadu** — Nallasiddhi then invaded **Yerragaddapadu** with a huge army; the fight raged **a full week**. The cycle's longest ballad, the **Battle of Yerragaddapadu, runs in seven *kāṇḍas* (chapters)** — including the Battle of the white cow **Bollāvu**, the Battle of the cows and oxen, and the final Battle of Katama Raju. **Even the cattle fought**: the Yādava cows and oxen, led by the single-horned Bollāvu (an incarnation of Viṣṇu), joined the war-line. Yādava champions **Kariyāvula Rāju, Bhaṭṭāvula Rāju, Aithama Rāju, Mummayya, Nāchakulla Nāyudu and Pochayya** fought at Katamaraju's side. **[folk]**
  - *Variant (the outcome):* in the version Narayanappa follows, **Katamaraju fights Nallasiddhi in person and wins, avenging himself on the king**. In the received historical/popular tradition the war is a **mutually annihilating slaughter** in which Katamaraju and his brother-heroes **die**, prompting the pilgrim uncle **Bhaktiraṇṇa** to return in a "cloud-chariot," release the confined Papanuka, order **satī** upon all the Yādava women, and perform **piṇḍa-pradāna** for the dead on the Paleru. The cult of the *fallen* heroes assumes this martyr-ending. Traditions are cataloged, not harmonised. **[folk]/[disputed]**
- **The battle-site names** — The palm-leaf cycle names the climactic battle for **Yerragaddapadu**, Katamaraju's own stronghold in the Kanigiri country. Popular and journalistic accounts instead locate/name the great battle at **Panchalingala (Pañcaliṅgala) on the Paleru river** — the site memorialised by the Golla ritual gathering. Both names attach to the same war. **[folk]/[disputed]**

## The bards, the performance, and the cult

- **Who sings it** — The ballads are chiefly sung by a **Mādiga bard-caste, the Kommula-vāru ("horn-people")**, hereditary minstrels who live by alms from the Yādavas whose epic they keep. Yādava sub-clan singers (**Suddula Gollalu**) also perform it, and in Visakhapatnam–Srikakulam the **Poḍapoṭhulavāru**. The palm-leaf manuscripts sit in the houses of **Yādava and Ādi-Andhra** minstrels — the epic is co-owned across caste lines. (Narayanappa 2021; cf. the Kommu bards' patron-eulogy tradition) **[folk]/[oral tradition]**
- **The instruments** — Performance is inseparable from its sound: the **kommu (horn)**, the **titti (leather bagpipe/wind-bag)**, the **pamba-jōḍu (paired trumpets)** and the **tāḷam (small bronze cymbals)**. The full cycle is sung **continuously across roughly three months**. **[oral tradition]**
- **Sung at the Ganga Jātaras** — The customary occasion is the **Ganga Jātara**, the village festival of the Yādava family-goddess Ganga — so the cattle-king's saga is recited as **liturgy inside a goddess-cult**, and the fallen heroes are worshipped as **village deities** (cf. the [grāmadevatā hero-cults](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/gramadevatas-village-deities.md)). **[folk]/[regional]**
- **The recurring type-scenes** — Scholarship isolates the cycle's stock motifs: the hero who **dies protecting the weak**; wives and sisters who **foreknow his death by nightmare and omen**; the **satī** of the hero's widow; the **cow as supernatural helper and warrior**; astrology that **blights lives** (Papanuka's evil star); the shepherd-king who **loves his cattle as kin**; and the rule that **broken pacts end in war**. (Narayanappa 2021) **[scholarly]**

## Sources

**Primary (oral / performed tradition, as sung and transcribed):**
- **Kaṭama Rāju Kathalu** — the Telugu oral ballad-cycle, surviving on **palm-leaf manuscripts** in Yādava and Ādi-Andhra minstrel households; performed by **Kommula-vāru** (Mādiga horn-bards), **Suddula Gollalu** and **Poḍapoṭhulavāru** to *kommu, titti, pamba-jōḍu* and *tāḷam*, sung across ~3 months at the **Ganga Jātaras**. Named ballads include the Ballad of Avula Vālu Rāju, the Coronation of Katama Raju, the Ballad of Paleru (the argument with Ganga), the Grass-tax ballad, the Embassy of Bhaṭṭu, and the seven-*kāṇḍa* **Battle of Yerragaddapadu**. **[oral tradition]/[folk]**
