# The Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya frame tales — Devarāja, Cañculā and Binduga

> Parent: [Literature Corpus](README.md) · sub-node of the [Śiva Purāṇa](puranas/shiva-purana.md) profile. The three *phala-śruti* ("fruit-of-hearing") stories that open the **Śiva Purāṇa** — the seven-chapter **Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya** ("Glory of the Śiva Purāṇa"), a prefatory eulogy in which **Sūta** tells **Śaunaka** at Naimiṣāraṇya three cautionary tales proving that merely *hearing* the Purāṇa liberates even the vilest sinner: the drunkard-murderer **Devarāja**, the adulteress **Cañculā**, and her prostitute-mongering husband **Binduga**. Sits beside the Śaiva [Śiva myth-cycle](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) and the vrata-kathā logic of the [Svasthānī Vrata Kathā](swasthani-brata-katha.md).

---

## Orientation: what the Māhātmya is and where it sits

- **A māhātmya, not a myth-cycle** — the **Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya** is the seven-chapter *self-praise* frame that precedes the body of the [Śiva Purāṇa](puranas/shiva-purana.md); its function is *phala-śruti* — declaring the merit (*phala*) of hearing (*śruti*) the text — and it argues that case not by doctrine but by three exemplary rescue-narratives. The chapters are: **(1)** Greatness of the Śivapurāṇa; **(2)** the liberation of Devarāja; **(3)** Cañculā's disillusion and detachment; **(4)** Cañculā's salvation; **(5)** Binduga's salvation; **(6)** rules for listening; **(7)** the rite of listening — injunctions and prohibitions. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya, chs. 1–7)
- **Borrowed pedigree** — the Māhātmya's own colophon states that these chapters "on the glory of the Śivapurāṇa are taken from the **Skanda Purāṇa**." The frame is thus a portable eulogy of the type that migrates between Purāṇas; cross-link the parallel prefatory-eulogy genre in the [Skanda Purāṇa](puranas/skanda-purana.md) and the survey in [smṛti-purāṇas](smriti-puranas.md). [scholarly]
- **The narration frame** — as with the Purāṇa proper, the outer voice is **Sūta (Romaharṣaṇa / Lomaharṣaṇa)** reciting to the ṛṣis of **Naimiṣāraṇya**, here answering **Śaunaka**, "a Vedic scholar and devotee of Śiva," who asks the surest means of liberation in the Kali age. Sūta's answer is: hear the Śiva Purāṇa — and he proves it with the three tales. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 2–5)
- **The two clocks** — *Scriptural / cyclic:* the tales present themselves as timeless edifying cases, staged in the ordinary sacred geography of the subcontinent (Gokarṇa, Pratiṣṭhāna, the Vindhyas) and addressed squarely to **Kali-Yuga** sinners for whom hearing is the last available ladder. *Academic [scholarly]:* the Śiva Purāṇa's surviving strata are dated **c. 10th–11th century CE** with later accretions (Hazra, Klostermaier; Debroy places the extant shape c. 1000–1100 CE), and the Māhātmya, as a detachable Skanda-borrowed *phala-śruti* prologue, belongs to this **late** [late text] devotional layer. Never collapse the mythic "case-study" register into a datable event.
- **The doctrinal payload** — all three tales encode the same radical *śravaṇa*-soteriology: **hearing the Purāṇa is efficacious even without merit, intention, or comprehension** — Devarāja is barely conscious, Cañculā hears by accident, Binduga hears as a mindless ghost. This is bhakti's *sound-grace*, the Śaiva counterpart of the [Bhagavad-gītā Māhātmya](../04-deep-dives/bhagavad-gita/gita-mahatmya-tales.md) and [Kārtika Māhātmya](../04-deep-dives/kartika-mahatmya-tales.md) rescue-tales. Cross-link the mechanics of sin and expiation: [pāpa, puṇya & prāyaścitta](../05-concepts-and-dharma/papa-punya-and-prayaschitta.md).

## Chapter 2 — the liberation of Devarāja

- **Devarāja the fallen brahmin** — a brahmin of the land of the **Kirātas**, "extremely poor and deficient in brahmanical knowledge," who abandoned all religious duty and lived by selling **liquor / beverages** — the archetypal degraded twice-born of the Kali age. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 2)
- **The catalogue of sins** — the text piles his crimes deliberately, to make the rescue maximally scandalous: by murder and deceit he **robbed brahmins, kṣatriyas, vaiśyas and śūdras alike**; he **killed his own mother, father, and first wife** for their wealth; he became a drunkard eating forbidden food; and he squandered the family fortune living with a courtesan named **Śobhāvatī**. He is, by design, guilty of the *mahāpātakas* (great sins) — brahmahatyā, drink, theft, and the murder of guru-figures. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 2)
- **Death at Pratiṣṭhāna** — journeying to **Pratiṣṭhāna** (Paithan, on the Godāvarī; cross-link [Godāvarī / Dakṣiṇa-Gaṅgā](../06-sacred-geography/godavari-gautami-dakshina-ganga.md)), Devarāja is struck down by a **violent fever** and, dying, "**happened to hear** the discourse on Śiva conducted by a brahmin" — an unnamed reciter expounding the Śiva Purāṇa nearby. He does not seek it, understand it, or repent; the sacred words merely **enter his ear as he dies**. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 2)
- **The tug-of-war for the soul** — at death **Yama's messengers** (*yamadūtas*) seize and bind him for hell; but **four gaṇas of Śiva** — "dressed in white, smeared with ash, wearing **rudrākṣa** garlands and wielding **tridents**" — arrive, **drive off Yama's men**, and free him. The single act of involuntary hearing has cancelled a lifetime of great sins. Cross-link the underworld apparatus: [Yama](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/yama.md) and the [narakas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md). (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 2)
- **The instant liberation** — the gaṇas set him in a **wonderful aerial chariot** (*vimāna*) and bear him to **Kailāsa**, where **Śiva and Pārvatī** receive him and he "**became a liberated soul instantaneously**" — the Māhātmya's proof-text that *śravaṇa* alone, absent all merit, suffices. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 2)
  - *Note (name-collision):* this **Devarāja** — a Kirāta drunkard-brahmin of the Śaiva Māhātmya — must **not** be conflated with the many other bearers of the name in this corpus: *Devarāja* as an epithet of **Indra** ("king of the gods"), nor **Devarāja Perumāḷ**, the Viṣṇu of Naimiṣāraṇya and Kāñcī. Same word, wholly unrelated figures.

## Chapters 3–4 — Cañculā's disillusion and salvation

- **Binduga and Cañculā of Bāṣkala** — in a second, longer tale Sūta describes **Binduga**, "a base brahmin" of the **seaside village of Bāṣkala** "where sinful people bereft of Vedic virtue" dwelt, and his devoted wife **Cañculā**. Binduga, though his wife was beautiful and faithful, **forsook her for a prostitute**, dissipating himself in a courtesan's house. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 3)
- **Cañculā's fall into adultery** — long-neglected and "unable at last to bear cupid's onslaught," the once-chaste Cañculā **took a paramour** and "began to indulge in sexual intercourse with her sinful lover at night." (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 3)
- **The nefarious contract** — discovering the affair, Binduga does **not** punish her but strikes a squalid bargain: the couple **enter a "mutual contract," Binduga profiting financially from his wife's adultery** — pimping her — and she continues "**till she no longer retained her youthful charms.**" The tale thus indicts *both* spouses equally. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 3)
- **Disillusion at Gokarṇa** — grown old, Cañculā chances to be at the **Gokarṇa** temple (the great Śaiva *ātma-liṅga* kṣetra on the Karnataka coast; cross-link [Gokarṇa Mahābaleśvara](../06-sacred-geography/gokarna-mahabaleshwar-atma-linga.md)) and "**happens to hear**" a scholar's discourse on the Śiva Purāṇa. The passage she overhears **describes the tortures inflicted on adulterous women**, and it "**made the woman tremble with fear.**" As with Devarāja, the transformative hearing is **accidental, not sought**. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 3)
- **The plea for refuge** — shaken, Cañculā **approaches the reciting brahmin**, confesses her whole sinful life, and begs: "**I seek refuge in Thee. I am in a pitiable plight. Lift me; lift me.**" Her *pāpa* → fear → *śaraṇāgati* is the tale's moral spine. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 3)
- **Instruction and asceticism** — the brahmin narrates the full **Śivapurāṇa** to her on the spot; "on hearing its excellent story," she says, "**I became detached from worldly pleasures.**" She stays in his service at Gokarṇa, then takes up the Śaiva ascetic discipline — **bark garments, matted hair, ash-smearing (*bhasma*), rudrākṣa beads** — "regularly repeated Śiva's names," and "**constantly meditated on the sentient, blissful body of Śiva.**" (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 4)
- **Death and apotheosis** — at the term of her practice Cañculā, "in her practice of the three-fold devotion, **cast off her body without difficulty.**" A divine *vimāna* sent by Śiva bears her to **Śiva's city**; she **assumes the form of Gaurī**, adorned with divine ornaments, and is made a **companion / chaperon (attendant) of Pārvatī** in the eternal abode — a courtesan-adulteress raised to the Goddess's inner retinue. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 4)

## Chapter 5 — Binduga's salvation from piśāca-hood

- **Cañculā's question to Pārvatī** — established among the Goddess's attendants, Cañculā asks **Pārvatī** the fate of her husband. Pārvatī answers that "your wicked, sinful husband Binduga, the foolish wretch enamoured of prostitutes, **went to hell after his death**," suffered its tortures for many years, and, "**due to the residue of sins, has now become a Piśāca in the Vindhya mountains**." (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 5)
- **The wind-eating ghost** — Binduga endures as a **piśāca** (flesh-eating ghoul; cross-link the underworld/being classes at [devas, asuras & the lokas](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devas-asuras-and-the-lokas.md) and the ordeal register of [Vipaścit in hell](../04-deep-dives/vipashchit-in-hell.md)) bound to the **Vindhya range**, "has **only wind for his diet**, and suffers all sorts of miseries" — the classic starving-ghost torment left when hell's account is nearly, but not fully, paid. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 5)
- **The Gandharva-king's recitation** — moved by Cañculā's wish to save him, Pārvatī dispatches the **Gandharva-king Tumburu** (the celestial musician; cross-link [Gandharvas & Kinnaras](../08-beings-and-bestiary/gandharvas-and-kinnaras.md)) to accompany Cañculā to the Vindhyas. Before the bound piśāca, **Tumburu narrates the entire Śivapurāṇa** — "**starting with the first Saṃhitā and ending with the seventh**" (Vidyeśvara through Vāyavīya), so that the whole text passes through the ghost's ears. The recitation is Cañculā's redemptive gift to the man who had ruined and pimped her. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 5)
  - *Variant (the agent of recital):* the brief frames Binduga as "rescued by **her** recital," and the tale's *causa* is indeed Cañculā's intercession; the *reciter* named in the text is the Gandharva **Tumburu** dispatched with her. Attribution preserved, not merged: **Cañculā procures and attends the recitation; Tumburu voices it.** (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 5)
- **The shedding of the ghost-body** — on hearing the Purāṇa, Binduga "**cast off all his sins and discarded his ghostly body**," **assumed a divine form**, and "**ascended the aerial chariot with great pleasure**," departing toward **Śiva's abode** together with his wife and Tumburu — the sinner-ghost redeemed at the last by sound alone. (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 5)
- **The unified moral** — the three tales form a graded proof: Devarāja is saved **hearing at the point of death**; Cañculā **hearing while alive**, with time to practise; Binduga **hearing after death**, as a ghost. Together they assert that no state — dying, living, or damned — is beyond the reach of *śravaṇa* of the Śiva Purāṇa, the Māhātmya's whole apologetic purpose and its charter for the rite of listening laid out in chs. 6–7. Cross-link the soteriology: [puruṣārthas, karma & mokṣa](../05-concepts-and-dharma/purusharthas-and-karma-samsara-moksha.md). (Śivapurāṇa-Māhātmya 5–7)
