# Swasthani Brata Katha — the Goma Brahmani cycle (Nepal)

> Parent: [Literature Corpus](README.md). Nepal's single most-recited vernacular sacred narrative — the **Śrī Svasthānī Vrata Kathā**, read one chapter a night through the winter month of **Māgh** on the banks of the **Śālinadī** at Sankhu (Kathmandu Valley). It frames itself as an extract of the **Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra Khaṇḍa**, welding a Śaiva cosmological cycle (Dakṣa's sacrifice, Satī, Pārvatī) to the human-realm story of **Goma Brahmani** — a girl married by fate to a seventy-year-old brahmin, widowed and abandoned, whose son **Navarāj** becomes a king and whose whole line is saved by the vow of the goddess **Svasthānī**. Sits beside Nepal's other living cults ([Kumārī–Taleju–Machhindranāth](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/nepal-kumari-taleju-machhindranath.md), [Paśupatinātha](../06-sacred-geography/pashupatinath-kathmandu-nepal-kshetra.md)) and the pan-Śaiva [Śiva myth-cycle](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md). **[regional]** unless tagged otherwise.

---

## Orientation: the household scripture of Nepal

- **What it is** — The **Svasthānī Vrata Kathā** (Nepali *Svasthānī Bratakathā*, "the vow-story of Svasthānī") is a vow (*vrata*) manual and story-book dedicated to the goddess **Svasthānī** — glossed "she of her own place / self-abiding one," a form of the Great Goddess read as **Pārvatī / Mahādevī**. It is arguably the most widely owned and recited Hindu book in Nepal: a printed copy sits in ordinary Parbatiyā and Newar households, and the story is read aloud, one chapter per evening, across the lunar month. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā) **[regional]**
- **The Māgh recitation frame** — The vrata runs a **full lunar month of Māgh** (mid-January to mid-February), conventionally from **Pauṣa Pūrṇimā to Māgh Pūrṇimā**; the printed text is divided so that **one adhyāya (chapter) is read each night**, the reading completing with the month. The great public centre is the **Śālinadī river at Sankhu (Śaṅkharāpura)**, northeast of Kathmandu, where observants bathe in the freezing river at dawn, keep a restricted diet, and gather for the nightly recitation for the whole month. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā; Śālinadī māhātmya) **[regional]**
- **Who keeps it** — Primarily (though not only) **women**, who take the vow for *saubhāgya* — marital good fortune, a husband's long life, a good marriage for the unwed, sons, and family welfare — the same devotional economy as other Pārvatī-vows across the subcontinent, but here fixed to one Nepali text and one Nepali river. (vrata practice; cf. [festivals & vratas](../05-concepts-and-dharma/festivals-and-vratas.md)) **[regional]**
- **The two clocks** — *Scriptural / cyclic:* the book presents itself as timeless Purāṇic revelation (Skanda Purāṇa material), its Śiva–Satī–Pārvatī events belonging to the recurring Śaiva mythic frame and the Goma cycle to an unnumbered sacred antiquity. *Academic:* the text is a demonstrably **Nepali composition** whose **oldest dated manuscript is 1573 CE**, which grew from a short Sanskrit core into a long Nepali vernacular recension of **31 chapters** only in the eighteenth–twentieth centuries **[scholarly]** (Jessica Vantine Birkenholtz, *Reciting the Goddess*, 2018). Never collapse the two: the Purāṇic self-attribution is a claim of authority, not a datable provenance.

## The frame: a Purāṇa within a Purāṇa

- **The borrowed pedigree** — The text is framed as an extract of the **Skanda Purāṇa, Kedāra Khaṇḍa**, and so inherits the Śaiva-Purāṇic voice: a cosmic dialogue in which the origin and reward of the Svasthānī vow are expounded through illustrative narrative. The attribution places it in the ambit of the pan-Indian [Skanda Purāṇa](puranas/skanda-purana.md) ([khaṇḍa map](puranas/skanda-purana-khanda-map.md)), but no Sanskrit Skanda-Purāṇa recension outside Nepal carries this Svasthānī material — the pedigree is asserted, the composition is local. **[scholarly]/[regional]**
- **The vrata-kathā logic** — Like all vow-stories, the book proves its rite by narrated result: those who keep the Svasthānī vow are lifted from ruin, those who scorn it are shattered until they submit. The two great illustrative arcs are the **divine cycle** (Śiva, Satī, Pārvatī) and the **human cycle** (Goma Brahmani and her descendants), the second modelling for ordinary devotees what the first stages among the gods. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, frame chapters) **[regional]**
  - *Variant (the framing dialogue):* printed recensions open the exposition of the vow through the standard vrata device of a goddess/consort asking the means of women's welfare and a god narrating the answer; the exact interlocutors and invocation vary between editions and should not be over-specified. **[disputed]**
- **The goddess Svasthānī** — Depicted in the printed books as a **many-armed weapon-bearing form of the Devī** (a Mahādevī / Durgā-type icon), Svasthānī is the vow's boon-granting deity; her name is read locally as "self-established / abiding-in-her-own-place," and she is functionally continuous with [Pārvatī / the Navadurgā forms](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/devi-forms-and-navadurga.md). (Svasthānī iconography) **[regional]**
- **Textual history [scholarly]** — Birkenholtz's manuscript study traces the Svasthānī from a **short Sanskrit text (a handful of chapters, c. eight)** in the **oldest surviving manuscript of 1573 CE**, through progressive expansion and vernacularisation, to the **standardised 31-chapter Nepali print recension** of the late nineteenth–twentieth centuries; the Goma/Navarāj human cycle swells across this history, so that the book grows *toward* its domestic, women-centred core over roughly four centuries. This makes the Svasthānī a rare, well-documented case of a living scripture caught in the act of being composed. (Birkenholtz 2018) **[scholarly]**

## Book I — the Śaiva cosmological cycle

*(The earlier chapters, telling the god-story that the Goma cycle then mirrors.)*

- **Dakṣa's sacrifice and Satī's fire** — The daughter of Dakṣa Prajāpati, **Satī**, weds **Śiva** against her father's contempt; when Dakṣa holds a great sacrifice and pointedly excludes Śiva, Satī goes uninvited, is humiliated, and **casts herself into the fire (or yogic self-immolation)**. Śiva's hosts destroy the sacrifice. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, Śaiva chapters; cf. [Satī, Dakṣa & Pārvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md)) **[regional]**
- **The dismembered goddess and the pīṭhas** — The grief-maddened Śiva carries **Satī's corpse** across the worlds; her body is cut apart (by Viṣṇu's discus in the pan-Indian telling) and the pieces fall to earth as the **Śakti pīṭhas** — the charter for the goddess-seats, including those the Nepali tradition localises in the Valley (e.g. **Guhyeśvarī** near Paśupatinātha). (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā; cf. [51 Śakti Pīṭhas](../06-sacred-geography/51-shakti-peethas.md)) **[regional]**
  - *Variant (local pīṭha count):* which of Satī's parts fell where, and how many seats result, differs between the Svasthānī, the Devī-Bhāgavata/Tantric pīṭha lists, and Nepali local claim; catalogued, not reconciled. **[disputed]**
- **Śiva's mourning and withdrawal** — Bereft, Śiva sinks into ascetic isolation and wandering grief — the pause between the goddess's two lives that the vow-narrative dwells on. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, Śaiva chapters) **[regional]**
- **Rebirth as Pārvatī and the winning of Śiva** — The goddess is reborn as **Pārvatī, daughter of Himavān** (the Himālaya); she undertakes fierce **tapas** to win the mourning Śiva. Kāma, sent to rouse desire in the ascetic, is **burned to ash by Śiva's third eye (Madana-dahana)**; Śiva, tested and won, weds Pārvatī. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, Śaiva chapters; cf. [Śiva myth-cycle](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md)) **[regional]**
- **The god-marriage as template** — The Śiva–Pārvatī union — a goddess's fidelity across death and rebirth rewarded with the ideal husband — is the divine paradigm the vow promises to reproduce for its human keepers; it is why the Svasthānī is above all a **women's marital vow**. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā) **[regional]**
  - *Variant (Kumāra):* some recensions extend the cycle to the **birth of Kumāra/Skanda** and the slaying of **Tārakāsura**, tying the Valley's Śaiva story to the war-god ([Kārttikeya](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md)); the emphasis varies by edition. **[disputed]**

## Book II — the Goma Brahmani cycle (the human illustration)

*(The heart of the recitation, and the reason the book is a household possession.)*

- **Goma married to the old brahmin** — **Goma**, a beautiful young brahmin girl, is given in marriage — by the working of fate/curse and the will of Śiva — to **Śiva Śarma (Śiva Bhaṭṭa), a brahmin of seventy years**. The gross mismatch of a girl to an aged husband is the narrative's engine: it dramatises a woman's dharma of obedience and fidelity under the harshest possible marital fortune. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, Goma cycle) **[regional]**
- **The son Navarāj and the abandonment** — Goma bears a son, **Navarāj**. Soon after, poverty drives the aged **Śiva Śarma to leave home to beg / go on pilgrimage**, and he **does not return**, leaving Goma a **destitute single mother** with an infant — the pit from which the vow will raise her. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, Goma cycle) **[regional]**
  - *Variant (death vs. desertion):* recensions differ on whether the old husband **dies on the road** or simply **abandons** the family; both are told, and the destitution is the fixed point either way. **[disputed]**
- **Goma takes the Svasthānī vow** — In her misery Goma is taught / undertakes the **Svasthānī vrata** in the month of Māgh (the god Śiva is often the disguised instructor in vrata-kathā of this type). By the vow's power her fortunes turn and her son thrives. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, Goma cycle) **[regional]**
- **Navarāj made king** — Grown, **Navarāj wins a kingdom** by the vow's grace and the classic device of the **succession-ordeal — the late king's state elephant garlands the rightful heir**, and the wandering brahmin's son is crowned king of a realm left heirless. The beggar-widow's child becomes a monarch. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, Goma cycle) **[regional]**
- **The forgotten mother, and reunion** — Raised to kingship, **Navarāj neglects (or fails to recognise) his mother Goma**; when she comes to his court she is turned away and suffers before she is at last **recognised and honoured** — the vow demanding that prosperity not forget its source. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, Goma cycle) **[regional]**
- **Candrāvatī and the desecrated prasād** — The cycle's cautionary sub-story: royal women of the line (the **Candrāvatī** episode) **scorn the Svasthānī prasād — casting the vow's sacred flowers/offerings into the river**. Catastrophe follows — **husbands and sons lost, kingdom and wealth stripped away, the princesses reduced to beggary and widowhood** — until, chastened, they **re-perform the vrata in full** and everything is restored: spouses revived, fortune returned. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā, Goma cycle) **[regional]**
  - *Variant (who desecrates):* editions vary on the culprits and their number (a queen, co-wives, the king's daughters) and on exactly which are widowed vs. impoverished; the moral — contempt for the prasād ruins, renewed observance redeems — is invariant. **[disputed]**
- **The moral shape** — The Goma cycle rehearses, in a brahmin household, the very passage the Śiva–Pārvatī cycle stages among the gods: a faithful woman carried from destitution and loss to restoration and honour through devotion to the goddess — the domestic gospel that made the book Nepal's most recited. (Svasthānī Vrata Kathā) **[regional]**

## The vrata as rite

- **Month, river, discipline** — The vow is kept for the **whole of Māgh**, its exemplary public form the dawn bathing and month-long observance at the **Śālinadī at Sankhu**; keepers take a restricted single meal, wear the vrata's markers, and attend the nightly chapter-reading to its Māgh-Pūrṇimā completion. (Śālinadī vrata; Nepali calendar — cf. [pañcāṅga](../00-time-and-cosmology/hindu-calendar-and-panchanga.md)) **[regional]**
- **The prasād and the eightfold offering** — Central to the rite is the **Svasthānī prasād** — a set of offerings (the tradition dwells on **groups of eight**, with portions given to a brahmin and portions of flowers set into the river) whose respectful handling is, per the Candrāvatī story, the difference between blessing and ruin. (Svasthānī vrata practice) **[regional]**
  - *Variant (offering counts):* the exact ingredients and the numbers (eight items, 108, etc.) vary by manual and locality; the **prohibition on scorning the prasād** is the load-bearing rule, not any one count. **[disputed]**
- **A women's charter, not exclusively** — Though open to men, the vow is **overwhelmingly a women's rite for marital welfare**, its narrative logic (fidelity rewarded, the good husband secured or preserved) squarely addressed to wives, widows, and the unmarried — the Nepali analogue to the Pārvatī-vows of the wider Śaiva world. **[regional]**

## Placing it: Nepali Hinduism in the making

- **A scripture localising the Purāṇic** — Birkenholtz reads the Svasthānī as a case study in how **Purāṇic Hinduism was made Nepali**: a text that borrows the Skanda-Purāṇa's authority, plants the pan-Indian Śiva–Satī–Pārvatī cycle in the **Kathmandu Valley's own river and pīṭhas**, and grows a vernacular domestic story (Goma) that speaks to Nepali household life — Sanskritic prestige and local devotion fused in one recited book. **[scholarly]**
- **Newar and Parbatiyā together** — The manuscript and print history spans **Sanskrit, Newar, and Nepali** transmission; the vow is kept across the Valley's Hindu communities, one of the shared practices (with the [Kumārī–Taleju](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/nepal-kumari-taleju-machhindranath.md) complex and [Paśupatinātha](../06-sacred-geography/pashupatinath-kathmandu-nepal-kshetra.md)) that constitute a distinctively Nepali Śaiva-Śākta religious world. **[scholarly]/[regional]**
- **A living, still-growing text** — Because the Svasthānī went to print and stayed in daily use, its recension history is unusually visible: unlike the anonymous deep pasts of the [oral hero-epics](maithili-oral-epics-salhesh-dina-bhadri.md) or the fixed Sanskrit [Purāṇas](puranas/skanda-purana.md), here a scripture's four-century **expansion from short Sanskrit core to long Nepali household epic** can be tracked manuscript by manuscript. **[scholarly]**
