# Bṛhatkathā, Kathāsaritsāgara & the Vikramāditya Story Cycle

> Literature-corpus node — the **kathā ocean**: Guṇāḍhya's lost Paiśācī **Bṛhatkathā** ("The Great Tale") and its surviving Sanskrit retellings, above all Somadeva's **Kathāsaritsāgara** ("Ocean of the Streams of Story," c. 1070 CE **[scholarly]**), plus the two Vikramāditya tale-cycles it feeds (Vetāla and Siṃhāsana). *Two clocks:* the frame-myth is timeless — Śiva speaking on Kailāsa; the inner saga sits in ordinary Kali-Yuga royal history (Udayana of Vatsa, whom Purāṇic king-lists place in the post-Bhārata [Candravaṃśa](../03-lineages-vamsha/suryavamsha-and-chandravamsha.md)); every *extant* text is Kali-Yuga documented history, c. 8th–11th c. CE **[scholarly]**. This file is the **canonical home** of the Bṛhatkathā/Kathāsaritsāgara stream cited across the corpus (e.g. in [Vidyādharas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md) and [Ujjain](../06-sacred-geography/ujjain-avantika.md)). Up: [`90-literature-corpus/README.md`](README.md).

## Orientation: a lost ocean and its three rivers

- **The Bṛhatkathā of Guṇāḍhya** — the "Great Tale," composed in **Paiśācī** (the "goblin-tongue" of the piśācas — see the language note under [Vidyādharas, Siddhas & spirits](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md)), is **entirely lost**; tradition itself explains the loss by the burning-legend below. Its existence and fame are attested by Subandhu (*Vāsavadattā*), Bāṇa (*Harṣacarita*, 7th c., who praises it) and Daṇḍin (*Kāvyādarśa*, on literature in *bhūta-bhāṣā*); modern estimates of its composition range from the **1st c. BCE to the 6th c. CE**, with no consensus. **[scholarly]** (Kathāsaritsāgara I; Lacôte, *Essai sur Guṇāḍhya et la Bṛhatkathā*)
- **Somadeva's Kathāsaritsāgara (c. 1070 CE)** — the fullest surviving descendant: a Kashmiri brahmin's Sanskrit verse retelling in **18 lambhakas ("books") and 124 taraṅgas ("waves"/chapters), c. 21,000+ ślokas**, composed (between c. 1063 and 1081 **[scholarly]**) for **queen Sūryavatī (Sūryamatī)**, wife of King Ananta of Kashmir, to divert her mind amid the court's troubles; Somadeva says he faithfully condenses Guṇāḍhya's original. (Kathāsaritsāgara, colophon/epilogue statements) **[scholarly]**
- **Kṣemendra's Bṛhatkathāmañjarī (c. 1037 CE)** — the earlier and much terser Kashmiri verse abridgement (roughly a third of Somadeva's bulk) by the polymath Kṣemendra; same Kashmiri recension-family, same overall frame. **[scholarly]**
- **Budhasvāmin's Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha (c. 8th–9th c. CE, date disputed)** — a Nepalese-transmitted verse epitome, surviving **incomplete** (28 cantos, c. 4,500 verses); strikingly different from the Kashmiri pair — Naravāhanadatta narrates in the first person and the Śiva–Pārvatī goddess-frame is absent — leading **Félix Lacôte** to argue it stands closest to the lost original. **[scholarly] [disputed]**
- **Other descendants** — the Jain Prakrit **Vasudevahiṇḍi** of Saṅghadāsa is widely held to rework Bṛhatkathā matter around Vasudeva **[scholarly]**; the Tamil **Peruṅkatai** (c. 9th c. **[scholarly]**) carries the Udayana saga into the [Tamil corpus](tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md) **[regional]**; and the Kashmiri retellings embed whole classics — the **Panchatantra** rides inside Kathāsaritsāgara Book X (see [Panchatantra & fable literature](panchatantra-hitopadesha-and-fable-literature.md)).
- **Rank on the canon ladder** — none of this is Śruti or Smṛti: it is kathā/kāvya, worldly story-literature. Yet the frame claims the highest pedigree imaginable — the tale is **Śiva's own speech** — a self-aware bid to give secular story a divine source. **[late text]** For the documented-history spine, see [recorded-history.md](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/recorded-history.md).

## The Kathāpīṭha frame-legend: how the Great Tale fell to earth

*(Kathāsaritsāgara, Lambhaka I "Kathāpīṭha," Taraṅgas 1–8; parallels in Bṛhatkathāmañjarī I.)*

- **Śiva's tale to Pārvatī** — on [Kailāsa](../06-sacred-geography/meru-kailash-and-cosmic-mountains.md), Pārvatī asks [Śiva](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/shiva-myth-cycle.md) for a story **never before heard**. He first offers her own former birth as Satī and Dakṣa's sacrifice (see [Satī, Dakṣa & Pārvatī](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/sati-daksha-and-parvati.md)); she is piqued at being told a "stale" story, so, doors barred, he begins the **great tale of the Vidyādharas** — the adventures of the seven Vidyādhara emperors. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.1)
- **Puṣpadanta overhears** — Śiva's gaṇa **Puṣpadanta** slips in unseen by his power of invisibility, hears the whole tale, and repeats it to his wife **Jayā**, Pārvatī's doorkeeper — who recites it in the goddess's presence. Pārvatī, furious that her "unheard" tale is common property, learns from Śiva how it leaked. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.1)
- **The double curse** — Pārvatī curses **Puṣpadanta to be born a mortal**; the gaṇa **Mālyavān**, who dares intercede for his friend, is cursed likewise. Relenting, she fixes the terms of release: Puṣpadanta will be freed when, meeting the **piśāca Kāṇabhūti** in the Vindhya forest and remembering his origin, he **tells him the great tale**; Mālyavān will be freed when he **hears the tale from Kāṇabhūti and publishes it to the world**. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.1)
- **Kāṇabhūti's own curse** — Kāṇabhūti is really the **yakṣa Supratīka**, cursed by [Kubera](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/kubera.md) (for his friendship with a rākṣasa) to live as a piśāca in the Vindhya wilds until the tale passes through him — a yakṣa-turned-goblin hinge between heaven and earth (cf. [Yakṣas & Guhyakas](../08-beings-and-bestiary/yakshas-and-guhyakas.md)). (Kathāsaritsāgara I.1–2)
- **First birth: Vararuci/Kātyāyana** — Puṣpadanta is born as the grammarian **Vararuci (Kātyāyana)**, minister of King Nanda; Taraṅgas 2–5 carry his court-and-grammar saga (Upakośā his wife, the contest around Pāṇini's grammar, the Nanda intrigues) until he finds Kāṇabhūti in the Vindhyas, tells the seven-part tale, remembers himself, and is released. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.2–5)
- **Second birth: Guṇāḍhya at Pratiṣṭhāna** — Mālyavān is born as **Guṇāḍhya**, a brilliant poet at **Pratiṣṭhāna on the [Godāvarī](../06-sacred-geography/godavari-gautami-dakshina-ganga.md)**, serving King **Sātavāhana**. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.6)
- **The water-sport pun and the grammar wager** — sporting in the water, the king pelts his queen with sweetmeats (*modaka*) when she cries "*mā udakaiḥ*" ("not with water!" — heard as *modakaiḥ*, "with sweets!"); mocked for his Sanskrit, Sātavāhana despairs. Guṇāḍhya offers to teach him grammar in six years; the rival paṇḍit **Śarvavarman** vows to do it in **six months** — Guṇāḍhya swears that if Śarvavarman succeeds, he will **renounce Sanskrit, Prakrit and his own regional tongue**. Śarvavarman, by the grace of [Kārtikeya/Kumāra](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md), receives the **Kātantra (Kālāpaka) grammar** and wins; Guṇāḍhya, bound by his vow and left speechless in the three tongues, withdraws to the Vindhya forest and adopts **Paiśācī**. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.6–7)
- **The Bṛhatkathā written in blood** — meeting Kāṇabhūti, Guṇāḍhya hears the seven great tales in Paiśācī and, over **seven years**, sets them down in **700,000 ślokas — writing in his own blood, for there was no ink in the forest**; Kāṇabhūti is released from his curse. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.8)
- **Sātavāhana's rejection** — Guṇāḍhya sends the manuscript to court with his disciples **Guṇadeva and Nandideva**; the king spurns it — a work in the ghoulish Paiśācī, written in blood. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.8)
- **The burning of six-sevenths** — grieving, Guṇāḍhya climbs a hill, reads the tale aloud leaf by leaf, and **casts each leaf into the fire** as the beasts and birds of the forest gather weeping, forgetting to graze. He burns **six of the seven books — 600,000 ślokas** — sparing only the seventh, the tale of **Naravāhanadatta**, because his disciples beg for it. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.8)
- **The remnant saved** — Sātavāhana falls off his food; hunters report that the game has grown lean, entranced around a reciting poet. The king traces the marvel, recognizes the rejected genius, and begs for what remains; Guṇāḍhya, his curse fulfilled, gives up the body and returns to Śiva's host, while Sātavāhana receives the surviving **100,000-śloka seventh book** — and, learning its celestial pedigree from the disciples, prefixes the **Kathāpīṭha** ("pedestal of the tale") recounting its descent. (Kathāsaritsāgara I.8)

## The 18-lambhaka map: Naravāhanadatta's rise to Vidyādhara emperor

- **The frame-hero** — the surviving seventh of Śiva's tales is the saga of **Naravāhanadatta**, son of **King Udayana of Vatsa** (capital Kauśāmbī) and Vāsavadattā, destined from birth to become **cakravartin of the Vidyādharas** — the sky-going wizard race whose roster lives in [Vidyādharas, Siddhas & spirits](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md). (Kathāsaritsāgara IV onward)
- **The eighteen books** — in the Brockhaus/Tawney ordering: **I Kathāpīṭha** (the Guṇāḍhya legend above), **II Kathāmukha** (Udayana wins Vāsavadattā, daughter of Caṇḍamahāsena of Ujjayinī — the saga's [Ujjain](../06-sacred-geography/ujjain-avantika.md) anchor), **III Lāvāṇaka** (the Padmāvatī stratagem of minister Yaugandharāyaṇa), **IV Naravāhanadattajanana** (the hero's birth), **V Caturdārikā**, **VI Madanamañcukā** (his chief bride), **VII Ratnaprabhā**, **VIII Sūryaprabha**, **IX Alaṃkāravatī**, **X Śaktiyaśas** (carrying the **Panchatantra**), **XI Velā**, **XII Śaśāṅkavatī** (carrying the **Vetāla cycle**), **XIII Madirāvatī**, **XIV Pañca**, **XV Mahābhiṣeka** (the **coronation**), **XVI Suratamañjarī**, **XVII Padmāvatī**, **XVIII Viṣamaśīla**. Editions differ in the ordering of the later books. **[scholarly]** (Kathāsaritsāgara, book colophons)
- **The Mahābhiṣeka** — after wooing brides mortal and Vidyādhara, gathering the magical *vidyās*, and warring against the Vidyādhara usurper Mandaradeva's faction, Naravāhanadatta is **consecrated emperor over the Vidyādharas** on the divine mountain, fulfilling Śiva's decree — the goal-post of the whole ocean of stories. (Kathāsaritsāgara XV, Mahābhiṣeka)
- **Story-within-story engine** — around this spine hang hundreds of emboxed tales — Jīmūtavāhana's self-sacrifice to Garuḍa, the flying Brahmin boys, ḍākinī and piśāca lore, merchant-voyages and clever wives — the corpus that made "ocean of story" a byword; the being-classes it populates are cataloged in [`../08-beings-and-bestiary/`](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md). (Kathāsaritsāgara, passim)
  - *Variant (Budhasvāmin):* the Bṛhatkathāślokasaṃgraha tells the same Naravāhanadatta rise **without** the Kailāsa frame and with different adventures and emphases (e.g. an expanded merchant-world realism), first-person — evidence that the Kashmiri goddess-frame may be a regional overlay. **[scholarly] [disputed]**

## Vetāla-pañcaviṃśati — the riddling corpse (summary)

*(Embedded at Kathāsaritsāgara XII, Śaśāṅkavatī; independent recensions by Śivadāsa and Jambhaladatta; Hindi Baitāl Pacīsī. Summarized here — the tale-cycle's full treatment is kept as its own deep file; the vetāla as a being-class is under [Vidyādharas, Siddhas & spirits](../08-beings-and-bestiary/vidyadharas-siddhas-and-spirits.md).)*

- **The frame** — King **Trivikramasena** of Pratiṣṭhāna (later tradition: **Vikramāditya of Ujjain**) is daily gifted a fruit by the mendicant **Kṣāntiśīla**; each hides a gem. In payment the ascetic asks the king to fetch, by night, a **corpse hanging from a śiṃśapā tree** in the cremation ground. The corpse is possessed by a **vetāla**, which tells the silent king a tale ending in a **dilemma**; bound to answer if he knows, the king speaks — and the corpse flies back to the tree. **Twenty-four times** the cycle repeats. (Kathāsaritsāgara XII, Vetāla frame)
- **Representative riddle: the three suitors** — three young Brahmins love Mandāravatī, who dies; one sleeps upon her **ashes**, one carries her **bones** to the Gaṅgā, one wanders and wins a **spell of revival**. She restored, whose wife is she? The king judges: the spell-giver acted as a **father** (giving life), the bone-carrier as a **son** (performing rites), but the ash-sleeper — constant in devotion — is the **husband**. (Kathāsaritsāgara XII, Vetāla tale)
- **Representative riddle: the transposed heads** — a devotee and his brother-in-law behead themselves before the Goddess; granted their lives, the distraught wife **reattaches the heads to the wrong bodies**. Which man is her husband? The king answers: **the one bearing the husband's head**, for the head rules the body's identity. (Kathāsaritsāgara XII, Vetāla tale)
- **The unanswerable question and the pay-off** — the last riddle (a father and son wed a daughter and her mother: what kinship do their children bear?) leaves even the king rightly **silent**; the satisfied vetāla warns him that **Kṣāntiśīla intends to sacrifice him** to win vetāla-powers. When the mendicant bids the king prostrate before the corpse, Trivikramasena asks to be **shown how** — and beheads the sorcerer as he bows. Śiva and the celestials, pleased, promise the king future sovereignty (in Somadeva, ultimately over the Vidyādharas). (Kathāsaritsāgara XII, Vetāla conclusion) **[late text]**

## Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā — the thirty-two statuettes (summary)

*(Independent Sanskrit cycle, also called Vikramacarita; four main recensions edited by Edgerton; vernacular Siṅhāsan Battīsī. Kept as summary here — the cycle's full treatment is its own deep file.)*

- **Bhoja finds the throne** — **King Bhoja** of Dhārā (the historical Paramāra king, r. 11th c. **[scholarly]**) unearths the buried **siṃhāsana (lion-throne) of Vikramāditya** — in most recensions given to Vikramāditya by Indra — beneath a mound whose very soil made the farmer standing on it speak with royal generosity. (Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā, frame) **[late text]**
- **The thirty-two gatekeepers** — the throne's **32 statuettes** are celestial women (apsaras-like, petrified by a curse); each time Bhoja moves to ascend, one statue halts him and tells a tale of **Vikramāditya's magnanimity, daring, or self-sacrifice**, ending: *sit here only if you can match this*. After the thirty-second tale the statues, their curse spent, are released — and Bhoja, humbled, is deemed worthy by renunciation of the claim. Recensions vary in tale-lists and in the ending. (Siṃhāsana-dvātriṃśikā I–XXXII) **[late text]**

## The Vikramāditya legend complex [disputed]

- **The archetypal king of Ujjain** — Vikramāditya ("Sun of Valor") is the paradigm *cakravartin* of medieval story: throne at **Ujjayinī**, patron goddess Harasiddhi, brother the renouncer-poet **Bhartṛhari** — the whole civic legend is housed at [Ujjain / Avantikā](../06-sacred-geography/ujjain-avantika.md) **[folk]**. In the Vetāla and Siṃhāsana cycles he is the model of fearless generosity. **[late text]**
- **Vikrama Saṃvat (epoch 57 BCE)** — the era that dominates North Indian [calendars](../00-time-and-cosmology/hindu-calendar-and-panchanga.md) is traditionally counted from Vikramāditya's victory over the **Śakas** (hence his title *Śakāri*). Epigraphy, however, shows the era first called **Kṛta** and **Mālava**, with the name "Vikrama" attached only from about the **9th c. CE** — the retro-fitting of era to legend is itself part of the legend's growth. **[scholarly] [disputed]**
- **The navaratna (nine gems)** — late tradition seats nine luminaries at his court: **Dhanvantari, Kṣapaṇaka, Amarasiṃha, Śaṅku, Vetālabhaṭṭa, Ghaṭakarpara, Kālidāsa, Varāhamihira and Vararuci**. The list comes from a verse in the *Jyotirvidābharaṇa*, ascribed to Kālidāsa but reckoned a much later work (c. 12th c. CE or after) **[late text] [scholarly]**; it is chronologically impossible as history — Varāhamihira belongs to the 6th c. CE (see [Siddhāntic astronomy](../00-time-and-cosmology/siddhantic-astronomy-and-astronomers.md)) and Kālidāsa to c. the 4th–5th c. **[scholarly]**. Note the loop: **Vararuci**, the frame-legend's own first incarnation of Puṣpadanta, is drafted into the nine.
- **Who was he?** — many scholars read the legend as a halo condensed around historical kings who bore *Vikramāditya* as a **title** — above all **Chandragupta II** (r. c. 375–415 CE), Śaka-conqueror with Ujjain associations; others defend a lost 1st-c. BCE Mālava king; tradition (e.g. the *Bhaviṣya Purāṇa* framing and vernacular tale-books) treats him simply as a real universal monarch, son of Gandharvasena. No resolution: the corpus records the dispute. **[disputed] [scholarly]**
