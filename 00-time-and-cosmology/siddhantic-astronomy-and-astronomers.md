# Siddhāntic Astronomy & Its Astronomers

> Hierarchy location: `00-time-and-cosmology/` — the **academic "second clock."** Where the rest of this section narrates the *scriptural/cyclic* time of kalpas, manvantaras and yugas, this file tracks the **historical, dated, empirical jyotiṣa-gaṇita tradition** — the human astronomers who, century by century, computed the sky that the myths describe. It is the *gola/gaṇita* (mathematical-astronomical) trunk whose doctrines are catalogued thematically in [Jyotiṣa & the Zodiac](jyotisha-and-the-zodiac.md); its outputs feed the [units of time](units-of-time.md) and ride on the 27 [nakṣatras](nakshatras.md). See the section [README](README.md) for how this node connects to the rest of cosmology. [scholarly]

This file keeps the **two clocks** apart. The mythic texts date by the *cyclic* clock (Kali-yuga began 18 Feb 3102 BCE). This file uses the **academic/historical clock** — CE/BCE dates from the history of science. The two meet only at the **epoch points** the astronomers *chose*, where a real scholar adopts a mythic zero-point as a computational convenience. Almost every entry below sits in **kali-historical** time. [scholarly]

---

## 1. The proto-tradition: Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa

- **Lagadha & the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa** — the oldest surviving Indian astronomical text, ascribed to **Lagadha**, core c. 1400–1200 BCE (redacted later), in two recensions: the **Ārca/Ṛgveda** (36 verses) and **Yājuṣa/Yajurveda** (43 verses). Purely **calendrical-ritual**: a 5-year luni-solar *yuga*, tithi-reckoning, intercalary months, Sun and Moon against the 27 nakṣatras. It uses **no rāśis** (the 12-sign zodiac enters India only later, with Greek contact). The "winter solstice at the start of Dhaniṣṭhā" datum is the chief internal clue to its antiquity. (Source: histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly]; cf. [Jyotiṣa & the Zodiac](jyotisha-and-the-zodiac.md) §6.)

## 2. The Five Siddhāntas

- **The Pañca-Siddhānta corpus** — by the early centuries CE, five mature astronomical schools circulated, named the **Sūrya (Saura), Paitāmaha (Brahma), Vāsiṣṭha, Romaka, and Pauliśa** Siddhāntas. **Romaka** ("Roman/Byzantine") and **Pauliśa** show absorption of Greco-Babylonian and Greco-Roman astronomy — the historical moment the epicyclic geometry and the 12-sign zodiac entered India. (Source: Varāhamihira, *Pañcasiddhāntikā*; histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)
- **Sūrya Siddhānta** — the surviving cornerstone (extant recension mid-1st-millennium CE), framed as revealed by **Sūrya** to the asura **Maya**. Gives planetary mean motions, Earth's sphericity and circumference, eclipse/parallax computation, and the **4,320,000-year Mahāyuga** as the least common period synchronising all planetary revolutions — the formal hinge that welds the *astronomers' clock* to the *cosmological clock*. (Source: *Sūrya Siddhānta*; histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)

## 3. The classical Siddhāntic astronomers [kali-historical]

- **Āryabhaṭa (b. 476 CE) — Āryabhaṭīya, 499 CE** — composed at age 23 (his own statement), the founding dated text of the *gola* tradition. In 4 sections (**Gītikā, Gaṇita, Kālakriyā, Gola**, 121 verses) it gives: a place-value sine table via his *kha-dvādaśaka* alphabetic numerals; **π ≈ 3.1416** (62832/20000, called *āsanna*, "approximate" — a rare admission of irrationality); the rule for the area of a triangle; and, radically, the doctrine that the **Earth rotates on its axis** (the heavens only *appear* to turn, "as a man in a boat sees the bank move"). He places his epoch at Kali-yuga's start with a **midnight (ārdharātrika)** system, later revising to a sunrise (audayika) system. (Source: *Āryabhaṭīya*; histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)
  - *Variant (two schools):* the **ārdharātrika** (midnight) vs **audayika** (sunrise) reckonings give two parameter-sets both traced to Āryabhaṭa; later authors line up behind one or the other. [scholarly]
  - *Note:* Āryabhaṭa's heliocentric-leaning rotation idea was **rejected by most successors** (Brahmagupta explicitly), so it did not become orthodox. [disputed]
- **Varāhamihira (505–587 CE)** — the great synthesiser. His **Pañcasiddhāntikā** (c. 550 CE) summarises and compares the five Siddhāntas — our chief window onto the lost Romaka/Pauliśa texts. His **Bṛhat Saṃhitā** is the encyclopedia of the *saṃhitā* (mundane) branch (omens, comets, architecture, meteorology), and his **Bṛhat Jātaka** a root *horā* (natal) text. Of the *navaratna* legend of Vikramāditya's court. (Source: histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly]; cf. [Jyotiṣa & the Zodiac](jyotisha-and-the-zodiac.md).)
- **Bhāskara I (c. 600–680 CE)** — Āryabhaṭa's most faithful expositor; his **Mahābhāskarīya**, **Laghubhāskarīya** and a prose **Āryabhaṭīya-bhāṣya (629 CE)** are the earliest known Sanskrit commentary on Āryabhaṭa. He gives a remarkable **rational approximation of the sine** function and is the first to write numbers in the now-universal **place-value system with a circle for zero** in a fully positional form. (Source: histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)
- **Brahmagupta (598–668 CE) — Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta, 628 CE** — composed at Bhillamāla (Bhinmal, Rajasthan). The first text to treat **zero as a number** with arithmetic rules and to handle **negative numbers** ("debts" vs "fortunes"); gives the general solution of the quadratic, **Brahmagupta's formula** for a cyclic quadrilateral's area, and the *cakravāla*-precursor to Pell's equation. Astronomically a thoroughgoing *gola* treatise — and the vehicle by which Indian astronomy reached Baghdad: translated as the **Zīj al-Sindhind** (c. 770 CE), it carried the decimal system westward. He polemically **rejected Āryabhaṭa's rotating Earth.** (Source: *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*; histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)
  - *Note:* Brahmagupta's later **Khaṇḍakhādyaka** (665 CE) is a practical *karaṇa* (handbook) following the ārdharātrika Āryabhaṭa parameters. [scholarly]
- **Lalla (c. 8th c. CE)** — his **Śiṣyadhīvṛddhida-tantra** ("treatise increasing the intelligence of students") is a major post-Brahmagupta *siddhānta*, important for planetary theory, eclipse correction, and refutation of false doctrines (*mithyā-jñāna-nirākaraṇa*). (Source: histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)
- **Bhāskara II / Bhāskarācārya (1114–1185 CE) — Siddhānta Śiromaṇi, 1150 CE** — the summit of the classical tradition, written at Ujjain. Four books: **Līlāvatī** (arithmetic), **Bījagaṇita** (algebra), **Grahagaṇita** and **Golādhyāya** (planetary mathematics and spherics). Anticipates **differential calculus** ideas — an instantaneous *tātkālika-gati* (instantaneous velocity of a planet) and a statement approaching **Rolle's theorem / the derivative of sine** — centuries before Newton/Leibniz. Treats division by zero (calling the quotient *khahara*, "infinite") and gives a complete solution of Pell's equation via the **cakravāla** (cyclic) method. (Source: *Siddhānta Śiromaṇi*; histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)

## 4. The Kerala School of Astronomy & Mathematics [kali-historical]

- **Mādhava of Saṅgamagrāma (c. 1340–1425 CE)** — founder of the **Kerala school**; credited with **infinite-series expansions** for π, sine and cosine — i.e. the **Mādhava–Leibniz series** (π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − …) and the **Mādhava–Newton power series for sin/cos** — anticipating European calculus by ~250 years. His own works mostly survive only as quotations in his successors. (Source: histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)
- **Nīlakaṇṭha Somayājī (1444–1544 CE) — Tantrasaṅgraha (1501)** — the school's master systematiser; in his **Tantrasaṅgraha** and the great commentary **Āryabhaṭīya-bhāṣya** he proposed a **partially heliocentric model** in which **Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn orbit the Sun, which in turn moves round the Earth** — a geo-heliocentric scheme structurally akin to (and predating) Tycho Brahe's. (Source: histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)
  - *Variant (school transmission):* the series results are preserved in **Jyeṣṭhadeva's Yuktibhāṣā** (c. 1530, in Malayalam prose — arguably the first "calculus textbook," giving *proofs*) and in **Śaṅkara Vāriyar's** commentaries. [scholarly]
  - *Disputed [scholarly]:* whether Kerala-school results transmitted to Europe (via Jesuit missionaries in Cochin) or whether the calculus was independently re-discovered — an open question in the history of science. [disputed]

## 5. The observatory age: Jai Singh II [kali-historical]

- **Sawai Jai Singh II (1688–1743 CE) — the Jantar Mantar observatories** — Rajput ruler of Amber/Jaipur who, c. 1724–1738, built **five masonry observatories** (Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Mathura, Vārāṇasī) of giant fixed instruments — the **Samrāṭ Yantra** (a ~27 m gnomon-sundial reading time to ~2 seconds), **Jaya Prakāśa**, **Rāma Yantra**, and zodiacal **Rāśivalaya** instruments. He compiled the star-table **Zīj-i Muhammad Shāhī** (1730s), drawing on Sanskrit *siddhānta*, Islamic *zīj* (Ulugh Beg), and contemporary European astronomy (La Hire's tables) — the last great flowering of pre-telescopic positional astronomy in India, fusing all three traditions. (Source: histories of Indian astronomy [scholarly].)
  - *Note:* the **Jaipur Jantar Mantar** is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2010); the masonry instruments were built precisely because Jai Singh judged small brass instruments too imprecise. [scholarly]

## 6. Two clocks, one sky [scholarly]

- **The synchronisation point.** The *siddhānta* astronomers adopted the **Kali-yuga epoch (18 Feb 3102 BCE)** and the **Mahāyuga of 4,320,000 years** as the master cycle in which all planets return to mean conjunction at 0° Meṣa — so the *historical* science (this file) and the *cosmological* myth (the rest of this section) are, **by construction, the same clock**, differing only in which end one reads from. (Source: *Sūrya Siddhānta*; *Āryabhaṭīya* [scholarly].)
- **Trajectory.** Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa (ritual calendar, no zodiac) → Greco-Babylonian contact (rāśis, epicycles, the Five Siddhāntas) → Āryabhaṭa's dated mathematical astronomy (499 CE) → the Brahmagupta–Bhāskara classical summit → the Kerala calculus → Jai Singh's observational synthesis. Throughout, the **frame stays geocentric** (with manda/śīghra epicyclic corrections) except for the dissenting voices of Āryabhaṭa (rotation) and Nīlakaṇṭha (geo-heliocentric). [scholarly]
