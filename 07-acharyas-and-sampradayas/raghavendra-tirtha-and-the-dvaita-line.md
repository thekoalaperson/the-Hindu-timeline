# Rāghavendra Tīrtha & the Post-Madhva Dvaita Line

> Detail node under [`07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/`](README.md) — the succession of Dvaita ācāryas *after* [Madhva](ramanuja-and-madhva.md): **Jayatīrtha** the commentator, **Vyāsatīrtha** the dialectician-organizer, and **Rāghavendra Tīrtha** of Mantrālayam, the most widely worshipped post-Madhva saint. Up-link: section index [`07-…/README.md`](README.md).
> Sits in the **first ~1.2% of Kali Yuga** (post-3102 BCE traditional; **14th–17th c. CE** academic [scholarly]) — though the hagiography of Rāghavendra reaches back cyclically to **Satya (Kṛta) Yuga**, identifying him as a rebirth of [Prahlāda](../04-deep-dives/prahlada-narasimha.md). Both clocks are kept distinct below; see the [two-clocks method](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/recorded-history.md).

## The paramparā after Madhva — how the line runs

- **Madhva's direct successors** — tradition counts four principal sannyāsī disciples continuing the Uḍupi-centred order: **Padmanābha Tīrtha** (first successor and first commentator on Madhva), **Naraharitīrtha** (the administrator-saint who by tradition seeded Haridāsa song — see [Haridāsa movement](haridasa-and-carnatic-bhakti.md)), **Mādhava Tīrtha**, and **Akṣobhya Tīrtha**. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School of Vedānta*; Wikipedia, *Padmanabha Tirtha*) [scholarly]
- **Akṣobhya Tīrtha and the *tat tvam asi* debate** — Akṣobhya, last of the four, is remembered for a public debate at **Mulbāgal** with the Advaitin **Vidyāraṇya** over the mahāvākya *tat tvam asi*, with the Śrī Vaiṣṇava ācārya **Vedānta Deśika** as arbiter; the traditional verdict-verse — *"asinā tattvamasinā … akṣobhya-munir acchinat"* ("with the sword *tat tvam asi* the sage Akṣobhya cut down the great forest of difference-denial") — declares Akṣobhya the winner. (Source: Dvaita matha tradition; Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*) [traditional]
  - *Variant (scholarly caution):* the encounter rests on tradition and a verse of contested provenance; the chronology of the three figures makes the meeting possible but not independently documented. [scholarly] [disputed]
- **The maṭha network** — from this line descend the great Dvaita monasteries beyond Uḍupi's [Aṣṭa Maṭha](../06-sacred-geography/udupi-krishna-matha.md): the **Uttarādi Maṭha** (continuing through Jayatīrtha's succession), the **Vyāsarāja Maṭha**, and the **Rāghavendra (Kumbakonam/Mantrālayam) Maṭha** — reverentially grouped as the **"Mathatraya,"** the three chief seats of post-Madhva Vedānta scholarship. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*; Wikipedia, *Uttaradi Math*) [scholarly]

## Jayatīrtha (Ṭīkācārya) — the great commentator

- **Jayatīrtha** — sixth pontiff in Madhva's line (c. **1345–1388 CE** [scholarly]; matha lists vary slightly), the ācārya whose commentaries (*ṭīkās*) fixed the canonical interpretation of Madhva's terse Sanskrit — hence his titles **Ṭīkācārya / Ṭīkākṛtpāda** ("the commentator"). Dvaita tradition ranks him with Madhva and Vyāsatīrtha as one of the school's three greatest teachers. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*; Wikipedia, *Jayatirtha*) [scholarly]
- **Origins** — born **Dhoṇḍo Pant Raghunāth** in a Deśastha Brahmin family at **Maṅgaḷaveḍhe** near Paṇḍharpur (Maharashtra), a cavalry noble's son. (Source: Wikipedia, *Jayatirtha*; matha hagiography) [traditional]
- **The horse-and-river conversion legend** — hagiography: the young warrior, drinking from the Bhīmā river without dismounting his horse, is asked by the ascetic **Akṣobhya Tīrtha**, *"kiṃ paśuḥ pūrva-dehe?"* ("were you a beast in a former body?") — the question awakens his memory of a prior birth as the **bullock that carried Madhva's books**, listening perpetually to the master's teaching; he renounces the world and takes sannyāsa from Akṣobhya. (Source: *Aṇu-Jayatīrtha-vijaya* / matha hagiography; retold in Sharma) [traditional] [folk]
- **Nyāya-sudhā** — his magnum opus, "the Nectar of Logic," an exhaustive commentary on Madhva's **Anuvyākhyāna** (itself the verse-supplement to the Brahma-Sūtra-Bhāṣya); universally treated as the single most authoritative work of Dvaita Vedānta after Madhva's own. The traditional tag *"sudhā vā paṭhanīyā, vasudhā vā pālanīyā"* — "either master the Sudhā or rule the earth" — measures its prestige; completing its study (*Sudhā-maṅgala*) remains a scholarly rite of passage in Mādhva maṭhas. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*; Wikipedia, *Nyaya Sudha*) [scholarly]
- **Other works** — tradition credits him with ~18–22 works, nearly all commentaries on Madhva: **Tattva-prakāśikā** (on the Brahma-Sūtra-Bhāṣya), **Prameya-dīpikā** (on the Gītā-Bhāṣya), **Nyāya-dīpikā** (on the Gītā-Tātparya), ṭīkās on Madhva's ten *prakaraṇas*, and the independent polemical tract **Vādāvalī** (against Advaita's world-illusion). (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*) [scholarly]
- **Bṛndāvana at Malkhēḍ** — Jayatīrtha's mortal remains rest in a **bṛndāvana** (the Mādhva saint's tomb-shrine) at **Malkhēḍ (ancient Mānyakheṭa)** on the Kāginī river in northern Karnataka, still a pilgrimage site of the Uttarādi Maṭha. (Source: Wikipedia, *Jayatirtha*; Uttarādi Maṭha tradition) [regional]

## Vyāsatīrtha (Vyāsarāja) — the dialectician at Vijayanagara *(brief; canonical home: [Haridāsa & Carnatic bhakti](haridasa-and-carnatic-bhakti.md))*

- **Vyāsatīrtha** — c. **1460–1539 CE** [scholarly], the third of the Dvaita "greats"; **rāja-guru of the Vijayanagara empire** under Kṛṣṇadevarāya, bridge between the Sanskrit **Vyāsa-kūṭa** scholars and the Kannada **Dāsa-kūṭa** singer-saints (he mentored both **Purandara Dāsa** and **Kanaka Dāsa**). Full treatment, works, and Haridāsa context in [haridasa-and-carnatic-bhakti.md](haridasa-and-carnatic-bhakti.md). (Source: Wikipedia, *Vyasatirtha*; Sharma) [scholarly]
- **The Nyāyāmṛta debate-chain** — his **Nyāyāmṛta** (with *Tarka-tāṇḍava* and *Tātparya-candrikā*, the "Vyāsa-traya") provoked the most sustained inter-Vedānta polemic in Sanskrit history: **Madhusūdana Sarasvatī's** Advaita rejoinder *Advaitasiddhi*, answered by **Rāmācārya's** *Nyāyāmṛta-taraṅgiṇī*, answered again by **Brahmānanda Sarasvatī** — a dialectical ladder running into the 18th century. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*; Wikipedia, *Nyayamruta*) [scholarly]
- **Hanumān consecrations** — tradition credits him with installing **732 images of Hanumān/Mukhyaprāṇa** across South India, embedding [Vāyu](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/vayu.md)-[Hanumān](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/hanuman.md) worship in the Dvaita landscape. (Source: matha tradition; Wikipedia, *Vyasatirtha*) [traditional]
- **Nava-Bṛndāvana** — his bṛndāvana stands on the island of **Nava Bṛndāvana** in the Tuṅgabhadrā near Anegundi/Hampi, alongside eight other Mādhva pontiffs — see [Kishkindha–Hampi–Anegundi](../06-sacred-geography/kishkindha-hampi-anegundi.md). (Source: Wikipedia, *Nava Brindavana*) [regional]
- **Rebirth chain (tradition)** — Mādhva hagiology identifies Vyāsatīrtha as an incarnation of **[Prahlāda](../04-deep-dives/prahlada-narasimha.md)**, the daitya-prince devotee of Narasiṃha — the same soul held to return later as **Rāghavendra Tīrtha**, linking the school's most beloved saint back to Satya-Yuga bhakti. (Source: matha tradition; *Rāghavendra Vijaya* tradition) [traditional]

## From Vyāsatīrtha to Rāghavendra — the Kumbakonam line

- **Vijayīndra Tīrtha** — c. **1514–1595 CE** [scholarly], disciple in Vyāsatīrtha's circle and pontiff of the southern (Kumbakonam) maṭha; a famously prolific polemicist — tradition credits him with **104 works** — who conducted long-running debates with the Śaiva-Advaita polymath **Appayya Dīkṣita** in the Tanjore country. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*; Wikipedia, *Vijayindra Tirtha*) [scholarly]
- **Sudhīndra Tīrtha** — Vijayīndra's successor (d. **1621** [scholarly]), scholar and patron of poetics at Kumbakonam; guru and consecrator of Rāghavendra. (Source: Wikipedia, *Sudhindra Tirtha*) [scholarly]

## Rāghavendra Tīrtha (Rāyaru) — scholar, saint of Mantrālayam

*Primary hagiographic source: the near-contemporary Sanskrit* **Rāghavendra Vijaya** *of Nārāyaṇācārya; historical framing per Sharma and modern accounts.*

### Life

- **Birth and early life** — born **Veṅkaṭanātha (Veṅkanna Bhaṭṭa)** in **1595 CE** [scholarly] at **Bhuvanagiri** (Tamil country) to Timmaṇṇa Bhaṭṭa, in a family of Vīṇā-playing scholars once patronized by the Vijayanagara court; he was himself an accomplished **vīṇā player** and Sanskrit prodigy who studied under his brother-in-law Lakṣmīnarasiṃhācārya at Madurai. He married **Sarasvatī** and had a son, Lakṣmīnārāyaṇa, living in scholarly poverty. (Source: *Rāghavendra Vijaya*; Wikipedia, *Raghavendra Tirtha*) [scholarly]
- **Sannyāsa (1621 CE)** — his guru **Sudhīndra Tīrtha** chose him as successor; hagiography adds that Veṅkaṭanātha hesitated for his young family until the **goddess of learning appeared to him in a dream** commanding acceptance; he was ordained at Tanjore in **1621** with the monastic name **Rāghavendra Tīrtha**. (Source: *Rāghavendra Vijaya*; Wikipedia, *Raghavendra Tirtha*) [traditional]
- **Pontificate** — for five decades he headed the Kumbakonam maṭha (thereafter called the **Rāghavendra Maṭha**), touring South India, teaching the Nyāya-sudhā, restoring worship, and receiving honours from the Tanjore Nāyaks, the Adil Shahi court, and other rulers of the fractured post-Vijayanagara south. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*; Wikipedia) [scholarly]

### Works — "Parimaḷācārya"

- **Nyāya-sudhā-parimaḷa** — his most celebrated work: a lucid gloss ("the Fragrance") on Jayatīrtha's **Nyāya-sudhā**, earning him the title **Parimaḷācārya**. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*) [scholarly]
- **Sūtra and Gītā works** — **Tantra-dīpikā** (on the Brahma Sūtras via Jayatīrtha's Tattva-prakāśikā) and glosses in the Gītā-prasthāna (e.g. *Gītārtha-saṅgraha* / Gītā-vivṛti works of the maṭha canon). (Source: Sharma; Wikipedia, *Raghavendra Tirtha*) [scholarly]
- **Khaṇḍārthas** — plain word-by-word Sanskrit expositions (*khaṇḍārtha*) of the **ten principal Upaniṣads** in Madhva's reading — deliberately accessible teaching texts. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*) [scholarly]
- **Ṛg-veda commentary** — a Dvaita commentary on the opening portion of the **Ṛg-veda** (the first three adhyāyas/forty sūktas), extending Madhva's own Ṛg-bhāṣya project. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*) [scholarly]
- **Total corpus** — tradition and modern surveys credit him with roughly **forty works** spanning Vedānta, Mīmāṃsā, grammar, and stotra literature. (Source: Sharma; Wikipedia) [scholarly]

### Miracle traditions (hagiography)

- **The Nawab's covered plate** — the **Nawab of Adoni, Siddi Masud Khan**, testing the saint, offered him a platter of meat under a cloth; Rāghavendra sprinkled water from his kamaṇḍalu and the platter was found to hold **fresh fruits and flowers**; the astonished Nawab became his patron. (Source: Mantrālayam maṭha tradition; retellings of *Rāghavendra Vijaya*) [traditional] [folk]
- **Venkaṇṇa the cowherd** — an illiterate cowherd boy, blessed by the saint, later found himself commanded by the Nawab to read a letter — and could; the boy rose to become **Dīvān Venkaṇṇa of Adoni**, and it was through him that the saint's request for the village of Manchāle was conveyed and granted. (Source: Mantrālayam maṭha tradition) [folk]
- **Reviving the dead** — hagiography credits him with restoring life to a child (in some tellings a young prince, in others a Brahmin boy) sprinkling sanctified water — one of a stock of grace-miracles (curing madness, feeding multitudes from little) that fill the *Rāghavendra Vijaya* and later Kannada/Telugu folk retellings. (Source: *Rāghavendra Vijaya*; folk tradition) [traditional] [folk]
  - *Variant handling:* the miracle-lists differ across the Sanskrit hagiography, matha oral tradition, and 20th-century devotional films; the corpus records them as devotional biography, not history. [scholarly]

### Mantrālayam and the bṛndāvana (1671)

- **Choice of Manchāle/Mantrālayam** — Rāghavendra asked the Nawab not for wealth but for the village of **Manchāle (Mantrālayam)** on the **Tuṅgabhadrā** (today in Kurnool district, Andhra Pradesh). Tradition explains the choice cyclically: this was the ground where **[Prahlāda](../04-deep-dives/prahlada-narasimha.md) performed a yajña in Kṛta Yuga** — the saint, as Prahlāda's rebirth, was returning to his own ancient sacrifice-ground. (Source: Mantrālayam maṭha tradition) [traditional] — *scriptural/cyclic clock; the documented grant and settlement are 17th c. CE* [scholarly].
- **Jīva-samādhi — entering the bṛndāvana alive (1671 CE)** — on a day he had announced in advance (the second day of the dark fortnight of Śrāvaṇa, Virodhikṛt saṃvatsara = **1671 CE** [scholarly]), Rāghavendra sat in meditation reciting the praṇava while disciples built the **bṛndāvana** over him with slabs — a voluntary **sashareera (bodily) samādhi**, not a burial after death. He is held to remain **alive within the bṛndāvana for 700 years**, blessing devotees — the theological basis of the living cult at Mantrālayam. (Source: *Rāghavendra Vijaya* tradition; Mantrālayam maṭha tradition; Wikipedia, *Raghavendra Tirtha*) [traditional]
  - *Variant (the stones):* tradition holds the samādhi-slabs were cut from a rock **sanctified by Rāma's touch** in Tretā Yuga — a second deliberate cyclic anchoring. (Source: Mantrālayam maṭha tradition) [folk] [regional]
- **The cult of Rāyaru** — Mantrālayam became one of South India's most-visited shrines; devotees across sectarian lines call him **"Rāyaru"/"Rāghavendra Svāmī,"** take **mṛttikā** (clay from near the bṛndāvana) as prasāda, and observe his **ārādhanā** each Śrāvaṇa; hundreds of satellite Rāghavendra maṭhas replicate the bṛndāvana across India. (Source: Mantrālayam maṭha tradition; Wikipedia) [regional]
- **The Thomas Munro story (1800 CE)** — celebrated colonial-era legend: **Sir Thomas Munro**, then Collector of Bellary, came to Mantrālayam to settle a dispute over the maṭha's revenue endowment; tradition says the saint **emerged from the bṛndāvana and conversed with Munro alone** (visible to no one else), and Munro ordered the endowment continued. Devotional literature asserts the episode was noticed in the **Madras Government Gazette (1800)**; the documentary claim is repeated everywhere but rarely produced. (Source: Mantrālayam maṭha tradition; popular accounts) [folk] [disputed]

## Why this line matters

- **Doctrine stabilized, then popularized** — Madhva wrote tersely; **Jayatīrtha** made the system teachable (Nyāya-sudhā); **Vyāsatīrtha** made it dialectically formidable and socially broad (Nyāyāmṛta + the Haridāsas); **Rāghavendra** made it *devotionally universal* — a Dvaita saint worshipped far beyond the Mādhva fold. The three are honoured together in the Mādhva school as its greatest post-founder teachers. (Source: Sharma, *History of the Dvaita School*) [scholarly]
- **Continuity with the wider map** — the sampradāya frame is in [Four Vaiṣṇava Sampradāyas](four-vaishnava-sampradayas.md); the founder's biography and doctrine in [Rāmānuja & Madhva](ramanuja-and-madhva.md); the opposing Advaita pole in [Ādi Śaṅkara](adi-shankara.md); the musical wing in [Haridāsa & Carnatic bhakti](haridasa-and-carnatic-bhakti.md); the mother-shrine in [Uḍupi Kṛṣṇa Maṭha](../06-sacred-geography/udupi-krishna-matha.md).
