# Mīrābāī — the Rajput Princess-Saint of Krishna

## Who she was — the princess who would not be a queen

- **Mīrābāī (Mīrā / Meera / Sant Mīrābāī)** — 16th-c. **Rajput princess and Kṛṣṇa-bhakta poet-saint**, the most celebrated *woman* voice of the north-Indian Bhakti movement. Born into the **Rāṭhoṛ** royal house of **Merta** (Marwar, Rajasthan), married into the rival royal house of **Mewar (Sisodiyā)**, she abandoned palace, purdah and widow-propriety to sing and dance for Kṛṣṇa among sādhus — the archetype of the noble-born woman who chooses the divine Beloved over worldly station. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; Britannica, *Mira Bai*)
- **Her one Beloved — Giridhar Nāgar** — Mīrā addresses Kṛṣṇa by his lifting-of-Govardhana epithet **Giridhar / Giridhara Gopāla / Giridhar Nāgar** ("the Mountain-Lifter," cf. the Govardhana episode in [`krishna-lila.md`](../04-deep-dives/krishna-lila.md)). This is the **signature-name (*chāp / bhaṇitā*)** woven into the closing line of her *padas* — "Mīrā ke prabhu Giridhar Nāgar" ("Mīrā's lord, the noble Mountain-Lifter"). (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; chittorgarh.com)
- **The mood — madhura-bhāva** — her devotion is **mādhurya / madhura-bhāva**, the lover's mood: she regards herself as Kṛṣṇa's **bride / beloved**, betrothed to him alone. Several legends say she declared in childhood, before an image of Kṛṣṇa, "*He* is my husband" — making her earthly marriage, in her own telling, a formality over a prior spiritual vow. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; Britannica)
- **Language** — composed chiefly in **Rajasthani (Mārwāṛī / Mewāṛī)** mixed with **Braj-bhāṣā** (the Kṛṣṇa-devotional literary tongue of Vraja), with a strong parallel **Gujarati** transmission from Saurashtra/Dwārkā. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; chittorgarh.com)

## Dating and family — the two clocks [disputed]

No contemporary documentary anchor for Mīrā survives; lineage details come from later Rajput chronicle and hagiography.

- **Traditional / commonly-cited span [traditional]:** **c. 1498–1546/1547 CE** — birth ~1498, "disappearance" ~1547. The death-end is fixed by the merging-into-the-idol legend at Dwārkā (below). (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; Britannica)
  - *Variant [traditional]:* some popular reckonings give **c. 1504–1550s**; the chittorgarh.com tradition and others float the years by a decade — none rests on dated record.
- **Birthplace** — **Kuṛkī (Kudki)**, in present-day Pali/Beawar district, Rajasthan; childhood at **Merta**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*)
- **Lineage** — daughter of **Ratan Singh Rāṭhoṛ**; granddaughter of **Rāo Dūdā** (Dudaji) of Merta; great-granddaughter of **Rāo Jodhā**, founder of Jodhpur — so a kinswoman of the Marwar founding line and cousin of the warrior **Jaimal Rāṭhoṛ**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; chittorgarh.com)
- **Marriage (1516)** — wed **Bhojrāj (Bhoj Rāj)**, crown prince of **Mewar**, son of the great Rāṇā **Saṅgrām Singh (Rāṇā Sāṅgā)** — i.e. Mīrā married into the Sisodiyā dynasty of Chittor at the height of its struggle against the Delhi Sultanate / Mughals. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; vajiramandravi.com)
- **Widowhood** — Bhojrāj was wounded in battle (c. 1518) and **died c. 1521**, before succeeding to the throne; Mīrā was thus widowed young and never reigned as queen. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; encyclopedia.com)
- **The persecuting in-laws [disputed]** — hagiography blames Bhojrāj's successors — variously his brother **Ratan Singh** and especially **Rāṇā Vikramāditya** (and a sister-in-law **Ūdābāī**) — for trying to force the widow into seclusion and to murder her for the shame of her public devotion. *Scholarly caveat [scholarly]:* it is debated whether persecution came from in-laws at all, or whether her flight reflects the **political collapse of Mewar** under Mughal pressure; the named villains are hagiographic fixtures, not documented. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*)

## The defiance — caste, purdah, and the open courtyard

- **Refusal of widow-propriety** — as a Rajput widow she was expected to live in withdrawn seclusion (or, in the harshest readings, to have died by **satī**); instead she **sang and danced publicly** before Kṛṣṇa's image, mixed with wandering devotees, and treated the temple courtyard, not the zenana, as her home. This is the core scandal of her story and the engine of the persecution legends. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; Britannica)
- **Caste-transgression** — she is remembered consorting with low-caste sādhus and (in the most famous strand) accepting the **leather-worker (Camār) saint [Rāvidās](north-bhakti-sants.md#the-nirguṇa-sants--formless-devotion-caste-rejection)** as guru — a deliberate inversion of Rajput caste-pride. Her *padas* repeatedly reject rank: she will trade the palace for the company of saints. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*)
- **Refusal of the Mother-Goddess / family deity** — a recurring motif: ordered to worship the Mewar **kuldevī (Durgā/Kālī)** as a Sisodiyā bride, Mīrā refuses, insisting she bows to **no one but Giridhar**. (Source: chittorgarh.com; popular hagiography) [folk]

## The poison and the snake — the survival legends [folk]

The signature miracle-cycle, in which the in-laws' murder attempts are foiled by Kṛṣṇa:

- **The cup of poison [folk]** — the Rāṇā (Vikramāditya in most tellings) sends Mīrā a cup of poison disguised as **caraṇāmṛta / nectar (prasād)**. She drinks it as Kṛṣṇa's gift and is **unharmed** — the poison turns to nectar / amṛta. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; chittorgarh.com)
- **The basket with the serpent [folk]** — she is sent a covered basket said to hold flowers (or a flower-garland) but containing a **venomous snake**; on opening it she finds instead **a garland of flowers**, or, in other tellings, **a Kṛṣṇa image / śālagrāma**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*)
  - *Variant [folk]:* the snake itself is transformed into the idol of Kṛṣṇa.
- **The bed of nails / iron spikes [folk]** — a further attempt sends a bed of iron spikes (or has her shut in to be drowned); she lies down and it becomes **a bed of flowers**. (Source: chittorgarh.com; popular hagiography)
- **Theological reading** — these are standard *bhakta*-protection miracles (the devotee whom the world tries to kill is shielded by God), shaped to dramatize Mīrā's specific conflict — **Rajput honor-violence vs. surrendered love**. [scholarly]

## The end — merging into the image [folk]

- **Disappearance at Dwārkā (~1547) [folk]** — in old age Mīrā is said to have travelled to **Vṛndāvana** and finally to **Dwārkā** (Gujarat), Kṛṣṇa's city. When Mewar emissaries came to summon her home, she entered the temple of **Raṇchoṛ-rāy / Dwārkādhīś**, embraced the image, and **merged bodily into the Kṛṣṇa-mūrti**, leaving only her garment (or her hair) — a *liberation-by-absorption* ending, the lover dissolving into the Beloved. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; chittorgarh.com)
  - *Variant [folk]:* the merging occurs at Vṛndāvana rather than Dwārkā.
- **The Vṛndāvana / Jīva Gosvāmī legend [folk]** — a famous teaching-anecdote (Gauḍīya transmission): told that the Vṛndāvana ascetic **Jīva Gosvāmī** would not meet a woman, Mīrā retorted that she thought **Kṛṣṇa was the only true male (puruṣa)** in Vṛndāvana and everyone else was a *gopī* — whereupon the abashed scholar came out to meet her. Illustrates her devotional one-pointedness and her place in the Vraja Vaiṣṇava memory. (Source: Back to Godhead, *Mirabai and the Gaudiya Vaishnava Tradition*) [folk]

## The Rāvidās-discipleship memory [disputed]

- **The tradition** — Mīrā is widely remembered as a **disciple of [Rāvidās / Raidās](north-bhakti-sants.md#the-nirguṇa-sants--formless-devotion-caste-rejection)**, the Camār (leather-worker) nirguṇa sant of Vārāṇasī; some *padas* in her name name him as her *guru* and praise him. This makes Mīrā a bridge between **saguṇa** Kṛṣṇa-devotion and the **nirguṇa Sant-mat** stream, and dramatizes her caste-defiance (a princess taking an "untouchable" master). (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; north-bhakti-sants.md)
- **The scholarly doubt [scholarly]** — there is **no corroborating historical evidence** for the discipleship; chronology is strained and the *guru*-naming verses are likely later accretions to her open corpus. Like the Rāmānanda–Kabīr link (see [`kabir.md`](kabir.md#the-rāmānanda-discipleship-question-disputed)), the Rāvidās tie is best read as **hagiographic construction** binding the great bhaktas into a single discipular network. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*) [disputed]

## The poetry — ~1,300 attributed *padas* vs. the small core [scholarly]

This is the central text-critical fact about Mīrā: her name is an **open umbrella** over centuries of accreted song.

- **The huge attributed corpus** — anywhere from several hundred up to **~1,300 *padas* (devotional lyrics)**, and in devotional reckoning "millions of hymns," are sung under Mīrā's name across north and west India. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; Grokipedia, *Mirabai*)
- **The tiny attested core [scholarly]** — **no manuscript survives from her own century.** The **earliest written records of poems credited to Mīrā date only to the early 18th century** — more than 150 years after her ~1547 disappearance — and the oldest such records carry only **a couple of poems** in her name. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; johnstrattonhawley.org)
- **Hawley's "umbrella" thesis [scholarly]** — **John Stratton Hawley** argues there is "always an element of enigma" in speaking of *the* poetry of Mīrā: her signature **"may well have served as an umbrella for a number of other female poets,"** and "the force of her life story doubtless drew poets of both genders to her banner." A conservative estimate traces perhaps **two to three hundred** *padas* to a core Mīrā tradition; the rest accreted around her name over four centuries. (Source: Hawley, *Three Bhakti Voices* / johnstrattonhawley.org; Grokipedia)
- **Parita Mukta's social reading [scholarly]** — Mukta (*Upholding the Common Life*) shows the living Mīrā-tradition as a **community-owned, low-caste and peasant song-corpus** in Rajasthan/Gujarat/Saurashtra — Mīrā as a voice claimed *by* the oppressed, not merely a fixed literary author; this reframes "authenticity" away from a single autograph text. (Source: scholarly literature on Mirabai; cf. Hawley)
- **Open-ended roster flag** — because the corpus is an open tradition, *any* list of "Mīrā's poems" is provisional; counts cited here (400–1,300 attributed; ~200–300 plausible-core) are **scholarly estimates, not a closed canon.** [scholarly]

## The recensional spread — three regional streams [scholarly]

The *padas* survive in three principal manuscript/oral traditions, each shaped by its host community:

- **Rajasthani recension** — associated with **Mewar / Marwar**; preserves the heroic Rajput-defiance coloring and Mārwāṛī–Mewāṛī dialect; transmitted partly through **Dādūpanthī** manuscript collections. (Source: Grokipedia; Wikipedia, *Mirabai*)
- **Braj recension** — preserved in **Vṛndāvana** manuscript collections of the **Puṣṭimārga (Vallabha)** and **Gauḍīya** Kṛṣṇa-sampradāyas (cf. [`nimbarka-vallabha-chaitanya.md`](nimbarka-vallabha-chaitanya.md)); Braj-bhāṣā coloring, integrating Mīrā into mainstream Vraja Kṛṣṇa-bhakti. (Source: Grokipedia)
- **Gujarati recension** — preserved in **Saurashtra / Dwārkā**, tied to the death-at-Dwārkā tradition; Gujarati-language transmission with its own large body of *bhajans*. (Source: Grokipedia; Wikipedia, *Mirabai*)
- **Effect** — the same Mīrā is a **Rajput honor-rebel**, a **Vraja gopī-bride**, and a **Gujarati peasant-saint** depending on the stream — the recensions encode their communities' uses of her, not a single recoverable original. [scholarly]

## The hagiographic record — how the life was built [scholarly]

- **Nābhādās, *Bhaktamāl* (c. 1600)** — the earliest datable mention: a **brief notice** placing Mīrā among the Kṛṣṇa-bhaktas, attesting she was already famous ~50 years after her death — but **no detailed biography**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; Wikipedia, *Bhaktamal*)
- **Priyādās, commentary on the *Bhaktamāl* (1712)** — the first **full narrative** Mīrā: the poison, the snake-basket, the persecuting Rāṇā, the miracles. The familiar dramatic life is largely an **18th-century literary elaboration** of Nābhādās's bare notice. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; johnstrattonhawley.org)
- **Rajput bardic / Dādūpanthī chronicles (17th–18th c.)** — wove Rajput **honor-and-defiance** themes into the saint's story, constructing the heroic princess-rebel image. (Source: Grokipedia)
- **The Akbar–Tānsen legend [folk] [disputed]** — a popular story has Emperor **Akbar** and the musician **Tānsen** visit Mīrā in disguise to hear her sing. *Scholarly verdict:* **historically implausible** — Tānsen joined Akbar's court roughly **15 years after Mīrā's death**; the episode is later legend, prized for placing Mīrā at the apex of Mughal-era musical fame. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*) [disputed]

## Legacy

- **The woman-saint archetype** — Mīrā is the paradigmatic *female* bhakta of north India: spiritual autonomy against patriarchal and caste constraint, the bride who chooses God over dynasty. (Source: Britannica; biographyonline.net)
- **A living song-tradition** — her *bhajans* ("Pāyo jī maine Rām ratan dhan pāyo," "Mere to Giridhar Gopāl") remain among the most-sung devotional songs across Rajasthan, Gujarat and the wider Hindi belt, carried in folk, classical and film performance. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mirabai*; vedantu.com / NCERT *Meera ke Pad*) [folk]
- **Place in the bhakti network** — counted with [Kabīr](kabir.md), [Rāvidās](north-bhakti-sants.md#the-nirguṇa-sants--formless-devotion-caste-rejection), Sūrdās and Tulsīdās among the great north-Indian sants; uniquely she bridges the **saguṇa Kṛṣṇa** devotion of [`krishna-lila.md`](../04-deep-dives/krishna-lila.md) and the caste-transcending **Sant-mat** ethos. (Source: north-bhakti-sants.md)
- **Note — Guru Granth Sāhib [disputed]** — unlike Kabīr and Rāvidās, Mīrā's verse is **not included** in the Sikh **Gurū Granth Sāhib**; the question of any Mīrā material at the Sikh court is a specialist scholarly topic (cf. Hawley, *Mirabai at the Court of Guru Gobind Singh*). (Source: Hawley, giss.org PDF) [scholarly]
