# Kabīr — Nirguṇa Sant and the Kabīr Panth

> Detail node under [`07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/`](README.md) — a standalone deep-dive on **Kabīr Dās**, the Julāhā weaver-poet of Vārāṇasī, the towering voice of **nirguṇa** (formless-divine) bhakti, his three recensional traditions, his paradox-poetics, his double critique of Hindu and Islamic orthodoxy, and the **Kabīr Panth** sect. ## Who he was — the weaver of Kāśī

- **Kabīr (Kabīr Dās / Bhagat Kabīr)** — 15th-c. **nirguṇa sant** and poet of **Vārāṇasī (Kāśī/Banāras)**; by tradition a low-caste **Julāhā** (Muslim weaver caste, recent converts from Hinduism). He never renounced his loom for monastic withdrawal — he **wove cloth his whole life**, and the imagery of **thread, warp, weft, loom and cloth** saturates his verse. His blunt, jolting couplets are the single most-quoted body of vernacular devotional poetry in north India. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*; PolSci Institute)
- **The name** — *Kabīr* is the Arabic *al-Kabīr* ("the Great"), one of the 99 names of Allah — a Muslim name on a poet who became Hinduism's most famous iconoclast, encapsulating his trans-sectarian position. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)
- **Theology in one line** — the divine is **nirguṇa Brahman**: formless, nameless, imageless, beyond temple and mosque, found **within** every person (the Upaniṣadic Brahman–Ātman identity, echoing [Advaita](adi-shankara.md)). He called this absolute **"Rām"** — but emphatically *not* the Daśaratha-son [Rāma of the Rāmāyaṇa](../04-deep-dives/ramayana/README.md); a name-beyond-name for the attributeless One. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*; Anantam IAS)
- **Language** — composed in **Sadhukkaṛī / Pancmel Khichṛī** ("the mendicants' polyglot stew"): a mixed register blending **Khaṛī-bolī, Braj, Bhojpurī, Awadhī and Marwāṛī**, deliberately unbound to any one regional literary tongue. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)

## Dating — the disputed life [disputed]

The two clocks diverge sharply for Kabīr; no contemporary documentary anchor survives.

- **Traditional span [traditional]:** **1398–1518 CE** — the famous ~120-year lifespan (birth Saṃvat 1455, full-moon of Jyeṣṭha; death at Maghar 1518). This is the date used by the Kabīr Panth and most popular reckonings. (Source: learnreligions.com; Wikipedia, *Kabir*)
- *Variant — academic majority [scholarly]:* **c. 1440–1518** — keeps the well-attested **Maghar death c. 1518** but compresses the birth, rejecting the 120-year span as hagiographic.
- *Variant [scholarly]:* **c. 1398–1448** — an alternative compression keeping the early birth.
- *Anchor point:* the **death at Maghar (near Gorakhpur) c. 1518** is the most stable datum; the birth-end is what floats. The 120-year figure is read by scholars as a **symbolic / round number**, not a record. [scholarly]

## Birth legends — the floated infant [folk]

- **The Brāhmaṇa-widow origin [folk]** — the most widespread Hindu-side legend: born to a **Brāhmaṇa widow** (in some tellings blessed/cursed by Rāmānanda), who abandoned the shameful infant on **Lahartārā Lake (Lahartara pond)** in Vārāṇasī, where the childless Muslim weaver couple **Nīrū and Nīmā** found and raised him. This neatly makes Kabīr **Hindu by blood, Muslim by upbringing** — claimable by both. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*; north-bhakti-sants.md)
- *Variant — Panthī miraculous birth [folk]:* the Kabīr Panth's own account: Kabīr descended from **Satlok (the True Realm)** as a **body of light**, incarnating on a **lotus** in Lahartārā Lake, witnessed by the ṛṣi Aṣṭānand — no human parents at all, marking him as a divine avatāra (*Satguru*), not a born human. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)
- **Kabīr Chaurā & Nīrūṭīlā** — his family quarter in Vārāṇasī is **Kabīr Chaurā**, today site of the **Kabīr maṭha**; a nearby house, **Nīrūṭīlā**, preserves the graves of the adoptive parents Nīrū and Nīmā. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)

## The Rāmānanda-discipleship question [disputed]

- **The tradition** — Kabīr is universally remembered as a disciple of **[Svāmī Rāmānanda](north-bhakti-sants.md#rāmānanda--the-root-of-the-north-indian-movement)**, the Vaiṣṇava bhakti-organizer of Vārāṇasī whose breadth (accepting *both* saguṇa and nirguṇa Brahman) is the standard explanation for Kabīr's nirguṇa "Rām." (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*, *Ramananda*)
- **The trick-initiation legend [folk]** — the celebrated story: a Muslim weaver could not openly become a Brāhmaṇa-saint's pupil, so Kabīr lay across the steps of the **Pañcgaṅgā ghāṭ** before dawn; Rāmānanda, descending in the dark, tripped over him and cried out **"Rām! Rām!"** — Kabīr seized that involuntary cry as his **initiating mantra** and proclaimed himself a disciple. (Source: PolSci Institute; popular hagiography) [folk]
- *The scholarly doubt [scholarly]:* postmodern text-criticism notes the **near-total absence of Rāmānanda from Kabīr's own attested verse**, the chronological strain of fitting the two lifespans together, and the convenient sectarian utility of the link (it Hinduizes a Muslim-born poet and legitimizes the Rāmānandī line). Many treat the discipleship as **later hagiographic construction**; others defend a historical core. The same skepticism attaches to Rāmānanda's "twelve disciples" roster generally. (Source: Wikipedia, *Ramananda*, *Kabir*)

## The three recensions — three Kabīrs [scholarly]

Kabīr **wrote nothing down himself**; his verse was **orally composed in the 15th c. and transmitted *viva voce* into the 17th c.** before being collected — by then in three geographically and sectarianly distinct streams that **do not fully agree**. This is the heart of the **historical-core problem**: there is no single authentic text, only divergent traditions.

- **1. Eastern recension — the *Bījak* (Kabīr Panth)** — the **holy scripture of the Kabīr Panth**, compiled probably in the **17th century** by the eastern (Banāras) branch at **Kabīr Chaurā**. *Bījak* means "seed / map of hidden treasure." It is the most distinctively **nirguṇa and iconoclastic** of the three, richest in **ulaṭbāṃsī** (see below). Three main genres:
  - ***Ramainī*** — longer doctrinal/cosmological poems (in *caupāī–dohā* form).
  - ***Śabad (Śabda)*** — sung lyrics, the ecstatic/devotional songs.
  - ***Sākhī*** ("witness," from Skt. *sākṣī*) — the terse, aphoristic **couplets (dohā)** that are Kabīr's signature form, "witnessing" the truth. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*; *The Bijak of Kabir*, tr. Linda Hess & Shukdev Singh)
- **2. Western / Punjab recension — the Ādi Granth (Sikh)** — Kabīr's verses entered the **[Ādi Granth / Guru Granth Sāhib](../90-literature-corpus/README.md)** compiled by **Gurū Arjan Dev in 1604** at Kartārpur; he is the **largest non-Sikh (bhagat) contributor** to the Sikh scripture. This recension is the **earliest dated written corpus** of Kabīr and so a key witness — but it is theologically smoothed toward the Sikh frame. (Source: learnreligions.com; Wikipedia, *Kabir*)
  - *Note [scholarly]:* **two ulaṭbāṃsī poems entered in the Kartārpur Pothī (1604) were later marked "useless" and dropped** from the final Ādi Granth — an early act of editorial selection against Kabīr's most shocking, paradoxical verse.
- **3. Rājasthānī recension — the *Kabīr Granthāvalī* (Dādū-panthī)** — preserved in **Rajasthan**, largely through the **Dādū Panth** (followers of the sant **Dādū Dayāl**, d. 1603); the modern scholarly edition is the *Kabīr Granthāvalī*. Sits textually between the Bījak and the Ādi Granth. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*; *The Bijak of Kabir* intro)
- *The convergence problem [scholarly]:* a poem may appear in two recensions with **different wording, different message, or attributed nirguṇa here and saguṇa there**; estimates of authentic verse range wildly. **V. C. Mishra judged only ~6 of the 100 poems in Tagore's famous 1915 *Songs of Kabir* to be authentic** — Tagore (working from Kṣitimohan Sen) is accused of reading early-20th-c. universalist theology back onto Kabīr. **Westcott counted 82 attributed works.** There is no agreed "real Kabīr" text. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)

## Ulaṭbāṃsī — the upside-down language

- **Ulaṭbāṃsī ("upside-down speech")** — Kabīr's hallmark **paradox-poetics**: deliberately absurd, inverted, riddling images — *the river burns, the frog swallows the snake, the cow milks the calf, a son is born to a barren woman* — that read as **impenetrable, crazy, even shocking** to the uninitiated. (Source: Wikipedia/scholarship via Linda Hess; RSSB, *The Upside-Down Language of Kabir*)
- **Function** — not mere word-play but a **teaching device**: it (1) screens esoteric **yogic/tantric** experience from the unprepared, (2) shatters ordinary conceptual logic to force a non-dual seeing, and (3) is meant to **make the listener feel like a fool** — that disorientation is part of the method. (Source: RSSB; Vinod Verma, *Pilgrimage Upside-Down: Kabir Ulatbansi Pilgrim*, IJRTP)
- **Inheritance [scholarly]** — Kabīr did not invent the form; he took ulaṭbāṃsī from the **Nāth-yogīs and the Sahajiyā tantric tradition** (sandhyā-bhāṣā / "twilight language") and bent it to nirguṇa-Sant ends. Cross-ref the Nāth/tantra background in [`shaiva-schools.md`](shaiva-schools.md) and [`shakta-and-tantra-lineages.md`](shakta-and-tantra-lineages.md). (Source: scribd, *Language and Themes in Kabir's Poetry*)

## The double critique — neither temple nor mosque

- **Against Hindu ritualism** — he derided **idol-worship, image-temples, pilgrimage to Kāśī/Gaṅgā bathing, Brāhmaṇa priestcraft, the sacred thread, and above all caste/untouchability**. *"If by worshipping stones one can find God, I shall worship a mountain."* (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*; Anantam IAS)
- **Against Islamic orthodoxy** — with equal force he attacked the **muezzin's call, namāz, the ḥajj to Mecca, circumcision, and ritual cow-slaughter (qurbānī)** — mocking the mullā and qāzī as he mocked the paṇḍit. *"If God wanted circumcision, why weren't you born that way?"* (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*; PolSci Institute)
- **The Linda Hess summary [scholarly]** — Kabīr "declared his independence from **both** the major religions," vigorously assailing the follies of each while **borrowing freely** from both as he saw fit — drawing on Vaiṣṇava bhakti, Advaita non-dualism, Nāth-yoga interiority and (debatably) Sufi love-mysticism. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)
- *Persecution [folk/traditional]:* legend has him **harassed by both communities** in his lifetime — and, in one strand, hauled before Sikandar Lodī. His independence cost him on both sides. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)

## Death at Maghar — the body of flowers [folk]

- **The Maghar choice** — by tradition Kabīr deliberately went to die at **Maghar** (a town reputed inauspicious, where death supposedly denied liberation) **rather than Kāśī** (where death supposedly guaranteed mokṣa) — a final blow against ritual geography: *the divine is within, not in a place.* (Source: poetryfoundation.org, *His death in Benares*; Wikipedia, *Kabir*)
- **The flowers legend [folk]** — at his death his **Hindu and Muslim disciples quarrelled** over the corpse — cremation vs burial. When the shroud was lifted, the body had **vanished, leaving only a heap of flowers**, which were divided: the Hindus **cremated** their share (memorialized at Kāśī / a samādhi), the Muslims **buried** theirs (the tomb at Maghar) — the sectarian tug-of-war dissolved by the saint who belonged to neither. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*; north-bhakti-sants.md)
- **Two memorials** — Maghar today has **both a Hindu samādhi-temple and a Muslim tomb (mazār) side by side**; in Banāras likewise a Hindu-managed and a Muslim-managed shrine, both reciting his songs daily. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)

## The Kabīr Panth — the sect

- **Kabīr Panth ("the Path of Kabīr")** — the **Sant-mat sect** that reveres Kabīr as founder and **Satguru / divine incarnation**, not merely as a poet. It crystallized **after his death (17th–18th c.)**, well past his lifetime — an institution built on a memory. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)
- **Scripture** — the **Bījak** (above) is its core canon, supplemented by later Panthī compositions (e.g. the *Anurāg Sāgar*, *Sukh Nidhān*) that elaborate a **Satlok cosmology** in which Kabīr is the supreme God descending to liberate souls — a marked theological development beyond the historical poet's plain nirguṇa. [late text]
- **Branches** — two principal seats give two main sub-lineages: the **Kabīr Chaurā maṭha (Banāras)** and the **Dharmadāsī / Chattīsgaṛh branch** (tracing to Kabīr's disciple **Dharmadās**), plus numerous regional offshoots. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)
- **Size & spread** — roughly **9.6 million** adherents, concentrated in north and central India (notably Chhattisgarh, MP, UP, Bihar) with a diaspora; census growth from **843,171 (1901)**. The Panth straddles the Hindu and Sikh worlds and is officially counted within Hinduism while keeping a distinct nirguṇa-Sant identity. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)

## Reach and afterlife

- **Into Sikhism** — the largest non-Sikh presence in the [Guru Granth Sāhib](../90-literature-corpus/README.md); scholars debate how far Kabīr **shaped Gurū Nānak** and the early Sant-Sikh idiom — some see direct influence, others stress real theological differences. (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*) [disputed]
- **Sant-mat lineage** — claimed as a fountainhead by later Sant and **Rādhāsoāmī / RSSB** traditions, which prize his upside-down language. (Source: RSSB)
- **Modern reception** — **Rabindranath Tagore's *Songs of Kabir* (1915)** made him a global figure of universalist mysticism — at the cost (per critics) of an authenticity gap; he remains a folk-musical staple (the living **Kabīr bhajan/Kabīr-vāṇī** tradition, Malwa, Rajasthan, Banāras). (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*; Hinduism Today)
- *Contested gender reading [disputed]:* some of his attributed verse on women is read by scholars (e.g. Nikky-Guninder Kaur Singh) as **derogatory**; others read the "woman" figures **allegorically** (māyā, the body, the seeker-soul). (Source: Wikipedia, *Kabir*)

## The historical-core problem — a caution

- No autograph, no contemporary dated record, a **century-wide birth dispute**, an **oral corpus written down only generations later in three disagreeing recensions**, a hagiography that both Hinduizes and divinizes him, and a sect-theology layered on top. The honest position [scholarly]: **a real 15th-c. weaver-poet of Banāras certainly existed**, but the recoverable "authentic Kabīr" is a small, blurry core inside a vast accreted tradition — every quotation should be tagged by its **recension**.
