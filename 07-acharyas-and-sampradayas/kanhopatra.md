# Kānhopātrā — the Courtesan-Saint of Pandharpur

> Detail node under [`07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/`](./README.md) (master index: [`../README.md`](../README.md)). The 15th-c. **Vārkarī woman sant** — daughter of a courtesan of **Mangalvedha**, celebrated for her beauty and dance, who renounced royal patronage to die at the feet of **Viṭṭhal** in the Pandharpur temple, the one person traditionally interred *within* the shrine.
> Frame against the lineage roster in [`north-bhakti-sants.md`](north-bhakti-sants.md) and the garland of women sants — [Janābāī](janabai.md), [Muktābāī](nivritti-sopan-muktabai.md), Soyarābāī (with [Chokhāmeḷā](chokhamela.md)) and [Bahiṇābāī](bahinabai.md). Two clocks: this is **academic / historical** Kali-Yuga dating (15th c. CE), not Purāṇic yuga-time — and the historical figure rests almost wholly on Vārkarī hagiography and her own *abhaṅgas*.

## Who She Was

- **Kānhopātrā (Kanhopatra)** — a 15th-c. Marathi **woman poet-saint** of the **Vārkarī** movement, remembered as a courtesan's daughter of unmatched beauty who turned wholly to the worship of **Viṭṭhal** (Viṭhobā) at **Pandharpur**, and whose *abhaṅgas* survive in the Vārkarī song-corpus. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography; Tharu & Lalita, *Women Writing in India*)
- **Birth & parentage [traditional]** — daughter of **Śyāmā**, a wealthy **courtesan (*gaṇikā* / *naṭī*)** of **Mangalveḍhā (Mangalvedha)**, a town near Pandharpur in present-day Solapur district; her father is unnamed in the tradition, and she was raised to her mother's hereditary profession of song and dance. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography) [folk]
- **Trained in beauty and dance** — by tradition she was schooled from childhood in **singing and *nṛtya* (dance)** and grew into a woman of extraordinary beauty, so that her mother expected her to become a celebrated courtesan; instead her gifts were turned to **devotional song**. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography) [folk]
- **The "two-clock" caveat [scholarly]** — like the other early Vārkarī sants, her biography is preserved as **hagiography woven around her surviving verse**, not independent record; the dramatic arc (courtesan-mother, royal summons, death at the temple) is the constant, while names and details vary by telling.

## Dating — the Vārkarī Generation [disputed]

- **15th c. CE [scholarly]** — she is conventionally placed in the **15th century**, after the founding generation of [Jñāneśvar](jnaneshwar.md) and [Nāmdev](namdev.md) and before [Eknāth](eknath.md) and [Tukārām](tukaram.md); the Bedar/Bidar Sultanate setting of her central legend fits a late-medieval Deccan horizon. (Source: Tharu & Lalita, *Women Writing in India*; Vārkarī hagiography)
- **Mangalveḍhā / Bedar context** — her town **Mangalveḍhā** lay within the orbit of the **Bahmani** and successor **Bidar (Bedar)** sultanate of the Deccan; the "sultan's summons" of her legend reflects this Muslim-ruled late-medieval Maharashtra. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography) [regional]

## The Legend — Beauty, the King, and the God [folk]

- **The turn to Viṭṭhal** — though raised for the courtesan's life, Kānhopātrā is said to have **refused to give herself to any man**, declaring she would wed none but a man equal to herself in beauty and worth — and hearing of **Viṭhobā of Pandharpur**, she fixed her whole heart on the god as her only lord and husband. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography) [folk]
- **Flight to Pandharpur** — learning of the Vārkarī devotion and the deity of the Candrabhāgā, she **left Mangalveḍhā for Pandharpur**, joined the company of devotees, and gave herself to *kīrtana*, dance and song before **Viṭṭhal-Rukmiṇī** in the temple. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography) [folk]
- **The sultan's summons** — word of her beauty reached the **Sultan of Bidar/Bedar**, who sent soldiers ordering her brought to his **harem (*zanāna*)**; she refused the royal patronage and the king's command outright. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography) [folk]
  - *Variant (the negotiated delay):* in some tellings she pleads to first take *darśana* of Viṭṭhal, or asks the soldiers' leave to worship one last time, using the reprieve to throw herself wholly on the god. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography) [regional]
- **Surrender at the temple** — pressed by the king's men and unwilling to be defiled, she **came before the image of Viṭṭhal** in the inner shrine and cried out that the god alone was her refuge and protector — the burden of her *abhaṅgas*. (Source: Kānhopātrā's *abhaṅgas*; Vārkarī hagiography)
- **Death before the god** — at the feet of Viṭṭhal, **choosing the god over the king, she gave up her life** in the temple — by tradition expiring in the act of surrender rather than be carried to the sultan's harem; the god received His devotee at the last. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography) [folk]
  - *Variant (the king's wrath / the miracle):* some retellings add that the frustrated sultan or his men reach the shrine only to find her already dead, the god having claimed her — God once more defending the lowly woman against the powerful, the recurring shape of the women-saints' legends (cf. [Janābāī](janabai.md)'s deliverance). [regional]

## The Samādhi-Tree in the Temple

- **Interred within the temple precinct** — uniquely, Kānhopātrā is said to have been **buried inside the precinct of the Śrī Viṭṭhal-Rukmiṇī temple** at Pandharpur, at the southern side of the shrine — and she is **traditionally held to be the only person interred within the temple** itself. (Source: Sri Vitthal-Rukmini temple tradition; Vārkarī hagiography) [regional]
- **The *tarati* tree** — from her *samādhi* there grew a **tarati tree (a *Capparis* / caper-thorn)**, which still stands within the temple complex and is venerated as marking her resting-place — a living memorial to the courtesan-saint at the very heart of the Viṭṭhal cult. (Source: Sri Vitthal-Rukmini temple tradition) [regional]
- **Significance [scholarly]** — that a courtesan's daughter should be the **one body honored inside the god's own house** is read as the most radical of the Vārkarī movement's egalitarian claims: that *bhakti*, not birth, caste, gender or trade, opens the divine — the same charter that admits the maidservant [Janābāī](janabai.md), the *mahār* [Chokhāmeḷā](chokhamela.md) and the potter, tailor and gardener saints.

## Her Works — the *Abhaṅgas*

- **The Vārkarī corpus** — a body of Marathi **_abhaṅgas_** survives under her name within the Vārkarī song-tradition, sung in *kīrtana* alongside those of [Jñāneśvar](jnaneshwar.md), [Nāmdev](namdev.md) and [Janābāī](janabai.md); roughly two to three dozen are commonly attributed to her. (Source: Kānhopātrā's *abhaṅgas*; Tharu & Lalita, *Women Writing in India*)
- **The cry to Viṭṭhal as sole refuge** — her signature note is the **desperate appeal to Viṭṭhal as her only protector and refuge** — the helpless devotee throwing herself on the god against the world's threat; the famous line **"नको देवराया अंत आता पाहू" ("Do not, O Lord, test me to the end now")**, a plea to the god not to abandon her, is among her best-known verses. (Source: Kānhopātrā's *abhaṅgas*)
- **Self-deprecation and surrender** — she signs herself with the language of her **lowly, "fallen" station** — a courtesan's daughter with none to claim her — making her unworthiness the very ground of her appeal to a god who saves the abject; the tone is closer to the cornered cry than to Janābāī's domestic intimacy. (Source: Kānhopātrā's *abhaṅgas*) [scholarly]
- **Open roster caveat [scholarly]** — as with all the early sants, the corpus is an **open roster** transmitted orally and in the *Sakal Sant Gāthā* anthologies, mixing an authentic core with later attribution; precise authorship is unrecoverable.

## Place in the Tradition

- **The garland of Vārkarī women saints** — Kānhopātrā stands in the standard roster of **Marathi women bhakti poets** with the maidservant [Janābāī](janabai.md), the child-yoginī [Muktābāī](nivritti-sopan-muktabai.md), the *mahār* poet **Soyarābāī** (wife of [Chokhāmeḷā](chokhamela.md)) and the autobiographer [Bahiṇābāī](bahinabai.md) — the women's voices of the Viṭṭhal devotion. (Source: Tharu & Lalita, *Women Writing in India*) [scholarly]
- **Pan-Indian parallels** — as a beautiful woman who spurned earthly suitors and a king for a divine husband, she is set beside **Mīrābāī** of Rajasthan, the Tamil Āṇḍāḷ and the Kashmiri Lal Ded in pan-Indian rosters of women saints who chose the god over the world. (Source: Tharu & Lalita, *Women Writing in India*) [scholarly]
- **Living veneration** — she is honored as **Sant Kānhopātrā** in the Vārkarī tradition; pilgrims at Pandharpur revere her *samādhi* and the **tarati tree** within the temple, and her *abhaṅgas* remain part of the living Marathi devotional repertoire. (Source: Sri Vitthal-Rukmini temple tradition) [regional]
