# Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār (Puṉitavati) — The Pēy-Devotee Nāyaṉār

*Tamil Nadu (Kāraikkāl), c. 6th c. CE — the earliest of the 63 Nāyaṉārs and the foremost woman saint of Tamil Śaiva bhakti.*
*Sits under [07-acharyas-and-sampradayas](README.md) (master index [../README.md](../README.md)). Roster: [The Ālvārs & Nāyaṉārs](alvars-and-nayanars.md). The four foremost: [The Nālvar](the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md). Doctrinal frame: [Śaiva Schools & Orders](shaiva-schools.md). Literary corpus: [../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md](../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md).*

**Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār** ("the Mother of Kāraikkāl") is the earliest datable of the 63 [Nāyaṉārs](alvars-and-nayanars.md#the-sixty-three-nāyaṉārs-shaiva) and the **foremost of the three women** among them — a 6th-century Tamil Śaiva poet-saint born **Puṉitavati** ("the pure one") at the port town of **Kāraikkāl** (Karikal, Karaikal) on the Coromandel coast. She is reckoned the **first major woman poet of Tamil bhakti** [scholarly], and her four surviving works open the genre of the *antāti* (chain-verse) in Tamil devotional poetry. She is named in **Cuntarar's (Sundarar's) *Tiruttoṇḍa Tohai*** — the seed-list of the saints — and so belongs to the oldest stratum of the canon. Her iconic skeletal, cymbal-clashing form, dancing at the feet of the dancing Śiva, is one of the most recognizable images of South Indian bronze and temple art. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))

**Dating note (two clocks):** academic consensus places her in the **6th c. CE** [scholarly] — likely the earliest Nāyaṉār, predating the [Nālvar](the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md); the hagiography that frames her life is later (Cēkkiḻār's 12th-c. *Periya Purāṇam*), so the **historical** poet must be distinguished from the **legendary** Puṉitavati of the miracle-narratives. [disputed]

## Hagiography — Puṉitavati of Kāraikkāl (Periya Purāṇam)

- **Birth & marriage** - born **Puṉitavati**, daughter of **Taṉatattaṉ**, a wealthy merchant of Kāraikkāl; a devout Śiva-worshipper from childhood, she was married to **Paramatattaṉ**, son of another merchant, and kept house as an exemplary, pious wife. (Source: [Periya Purāṇam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periya_Puranam); [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))
- **The two mangoes** - her husband sent home **two mangoes**; before his meal Puṉitavati gave one to a hungry **Śiva-devotee (a wandering mendicant)** who came begging, then served the remaining mango to Paramatattaṉ. Finding it extraordinarily sweet, he asked for the second; with no fruit left, she prayed to Śiva and **a second mango miraculously appeared in her hand**, which she served. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar)) [folk]
- **The husband's awe & withdrawal** - this second mango was even sweeter; when the astonished husband demanded another, the divine fruit **vanished from her hand** the moment he questioned its source. Realizing his wife commanded such grace, Paramatattaṉ was filled not with love but with **awe and fear of the divine in her**, and quietly resolved to withdraw from her. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar)) [folk]
- **Paramatattaṉ's flight & second marriage** - on a trading voyage the husband settled in a distant town (in some tellings **Madurai**), prospered, **remarried**, and named his new daughter **Puṉitavati** after his first wife — in reverence, treating her as a goddess rather than a spouse. Puṉitavati's kin brought her to him; he and his new family **fell at her feet and worshipped her**, confirming he no longer regarded her as wife but as a holy being. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar)) [folk]
- **Prayer to shed beauty** - released from worldly marriage, Puṉitavati prayed to Śiva to **strip away her physical beauty** — the flesh she no longer needed for a husband — and to grant her instead the **emaciated, skeletal form of a *pēy*** (a ghoul / demoness of the cremation ground), so she might serve purely as one of His ghostly attendants. Śiva granted it; her body wasted to **bones, sunken eyes, and veins**, the *pēy* form. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar)) [folk]
- **Pilgrimage on her head** - she journeyed north to **Mount Kailāsa**, refusing to set her feet on the sacred mountain and so completing the climb **walking on her hands / head downwards** in humility. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar)) [folk]
- **"Ammai" — Mother** - on Kailāsa **Śiva Himself addressed her as "Ammai"** ("Mother") — the title by which she is forever known, **Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār**, the Mother of Kāraikkāl. She begged two boons: **undying love for Him, freedom from rebirth**, and — if reborn she must be — **never to forget Him**; and to **witness His cosmic dance eternally** at His feet. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar)) [folk]
- **The dance at Tiruvālaṅkāṭu** - Śiva directed her to **Tiruvālaṅkāṭu** ("the forest of the banyan," near Chennai), where He performs His cosmic *tāṇḍava* in the cremation-ground. There the skeletal Ammaiyār **sits/dances at His feet**, beating time, eternally watching the dance — the scene fixed in her iconography and in the Tiruvālaṅkāṭu temple's identity as a primal dance-hall (*tāṇḍava sthala*). (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))

## Iconography — the skeletal cymbal-player

- **The *pēy* form in bronze** - she is depicted as an **emaciated skeleton** — ribs, hollowed cheeks, pendulous breasts, bulging eyes, wild upright hair — **seated cross-legged and clashing hand-cymbals (*tāḷam*)** in time to Śiva's dance. The Chola-period bronzes of Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār are celebrated masterpieces of South Indian metal sculpture [scholarly]. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))
- **Placement at Naṭarāja's feet** - in temple iconographic programs she is shown **at the base of the dancing Śiva (Naṭarāja)**, often alongside the sage **Vyāghrapāda** and other witnesses of the dance — visually canonizing her boon to behold the *tāṇḍava* forever. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))
- **A theology of renounced beauty** - her deliberate exchange of feminine beauty for a ghoulish, sexless, skeletal body is read as a radical bhakti statement — devotion that **dissolves the body and gender as obstacles to grace** [scholarly]; she is the rare saint who is at once female, *pēy*, mother, and poet.

## The four works — Tirumuṟai Book 11 (~143 verses)

Her surviving poetry — about **143 verses** in total — is gathered in **Tirumuṟai Book 11**, the miscellany volume of later and minor Śaiva poets. She is the **only one of the four [Nālvar](the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md)-era saints' generation whose work is placed in Book 11 rather than the Tēvāram (Books 1–7)**, reflecting both her early date and the separate transmission of her *antāti* corpus. (Source: [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai); [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))

- **Aṟputat Tiruvantāti** ("The Sacred Antāti of Wonders") - her principal and most celebrated poem, **100 verses** in *veṇpā* metre arranged as an *antāti* — a chain in which the last word/syllable of each verse opens the next, looping back at the close. Among the **earliest examples of the *antāti* form** in Tamil, it sings the wonders (*aṟputam*) of Śiva and her own longing. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))
- **Tiru Iraṭṭai Maṇimālai** ("The Sacred Double Garland of Gems") - a **20-verse** poem in the *iraṭṭai maṇimālai* form, which **alternates two metres** (*veṇpā* and *kaṭṭaḷaik kalittuṟai*) like gems strung alternately in a garland. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))
- **Mūtta Tiruppatikam I & II** ("The Elder/Ancient Sacred Decads") - **two *patikams*** (hymn-decads), of **11 verses each** (~22–23 verses total), composed in praise of **Śiva at Tiruvālaṅkāṭu** and His cremation-ground dance. Called *mūtta* ("elder / ancient") because she is the senior-most of the Nāyaṉār hymnists — these are reckoned among the **oldest hymns in the Tēvāram-Tirumuṟai tradition**, predating the *patikams* of Sambandar and Appar. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar); [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai))
  - *Note on totals [scholarly]:* 100 (*Aṟputat Tiruvantāti*) + 20 (*Iraṭṭai Maṇimālai*) + ~22–23 (two *Mūtta Tiruppatikams*) ≈ **~143 verses**; minor variation in counts arises from how the two decads are numbered.
- **Macabre cremation-ground imagery** - her hymns are noted for vivid descriptions of the **cremation ground (*kāṭu*)** — ghouls, jackals, burning corpses, scattered bones — over which Śiva dances; this *pēy*-poetry shaped the later Tamil literary vision of the dancing Śiva of the burning ground. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar)) [scholarly]

## Place in the canon & the Periya Purāṇam

- **Named by Cuntarar** - she is included in **Cuntarar's *Tiruttoṇḍa Tohai*** (the 11-verse roll-call that fixes the foremost devotees), which runs only up to roughly her own time — confirming her as one of the **earliest** named Nāyaṉārs. (Source: [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars); cf. [the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md](the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md))
- **Cēkkiḻār's hagiography** - the full miracle-narrative (mangoes, the husband's awe, the prayer for the *pēy* form, Kailāsa, Tiruvālaṅkāṭu) is versified by **Cēkkiḻār** in the **Periya Purāṇam** (c. 1135 CE, *Tirumuṟai* Book 12), the master-hagiography of all 63 Nāyaṉārs; her chapter is the **Tiruttoṇṭa-tokai** account of *Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār Nāyaṉār*. (Source: [Periya Puranam — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periya_Puranam))
- **Earlier than the Nālvar** - because she is sung by Cuntarar and her *Mūtta Tiruppatikams* are styled "elder," tradition and scholarship alike rank her **before Sambandar, Appar and Cuntarar** in time — the senior poet of the whole Tamil Śaiva hymn-tradition. See the four foremost in [the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md](the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md). [scholarly]
- **Among the women saints** - one of **three women** in the canonical 63 (with **Maṅgaiyarkkaracī** and **Icaiñāṉiyār**), and by far the most celebrated as a **poet** in her own right — paralleling **Āṇḍāḷ** as sole female Ālvār on the Vaiṣṇava side ([alvars-and-nayanars.md](alvars-and-nayanars.md#the-twelve-Ālvārs-vaishnava)). (Source: [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars))

## Variants & disputes

- *Variant (place of the husband's flight) [folk]:* most tellings send Paramatattaṉ to a distant trading town and name his new daughter Puṉitavati; some specify **Madurai** or the Pandya country. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))
- *Variant (one mango vs the begging devotee) [folk]:* the trigger is usually the **second mango** miraculously supplied, but tellings differ on whether the first was given to a *Śiva-bhakta* mendicant or directly to a hungry stranger before her husband's meal. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))
- *Variant (verse count) [scholarly]:* the corpus is given as **~143 verses** (100 + 20 + two decads); some sources round to "about 140" depending on how the two *Mūtta Tiruppatikams* are counted. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))
- *Variant (dating) [disputed]:* the **6th c. CE** academic placement makes her possibly the **earliest** Nāyaṉār; precise dates are unrecoverable since the only "biography" is Cēkkiḻār's 12th-c. hagiography — the two-clocks gap between the historical poet and the legendary Puṉitavati. [scholarly]
- *Variant (Tiruvālaṅkāṭu festival) [regional]:* at the **Tiruvālaṅkāṭu** temple her connection is annually celebrated, and a separate temple/shrine at **Kāraikkāl** honours her birthplace; the **Maṅgani Tiruvizhā** ("mango festival") commemorates the mango miracle. (Source: [Karaikkal Ammaiyar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaikkal_Ammaiyar))
