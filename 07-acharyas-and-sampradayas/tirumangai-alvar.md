# Tirumaṅgai Āḻvār — the robber-saint Parakāla

> Ali Nāḍu / Tirukkuṟaiyalūr, Chōḷa country, Tamil Nadu — c. 8th–9th c. CE (academic) / Kali 397 ≈ 2705 BCE (traditional).
> Parent: [07-acharyas-and-sampradayas](./README.md). See the survey of the twelve saints in [alvars-and-nayanars.md](./alvars-and-nayanars.md), the hagiographic frame in [alvar-hagiographies-guruparampara.md](./alvar-hagiographies-guruparampara.md), and the shrine he rebuilt in [../06-sacred-geography/srirangam-ranganatha.md](../06-sacred-geography/srirangam-ranganatha.md). Master index: [../README.md](../README.md).

**Tirumaṅgai Āḻvār** — born **Kaliyan** (Tamil *Kalikaṉṟi*, "he who quelled Kali"), titled **Parakāla** ("beyond Time / death to the adversary") — is reckoned the **last chronologically and the second-most prolific of the twelve Ālvārs**, after Nammāḻvār. A minor warrior-chieftain of the Chōḷa marches, he ran through his treasury and then took to the highway as an armed robber — to *feed Vaiṣṇavas*, not to enrich himself — until Viṣṇu himself, disguised as a bridegroom, waylaid the waylayer, could not be robbed of a single toe-ring, and initiated him into the eight-syllable *Ashtākṣara* mantra. Redeemed, Kaliyan became the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition's model of the sinner-turned-saint, poured out **six works of ~1,253–1,361 verses** (chief among them the vast *Periya Tirumoḻi*), sang the largest number of the **divya deśams** of any single Ālvār, and — by the same brigand's cunning now turned to God's service — funded the great third enclosure-wall of **Srirangam** with a golden Buddha carried off from Nāgapaṭṭinam. **Two-clock dating note:** academic consensus places him in the **8th–9th c. CE** [scholarly], often as a contemporary of the Pallava Nandivarman II Pallavamalla; Vaiṣṇava tradition assigns his birth to **Kali year 397 ≈ 2705 BCE** [traditional] [disputed], the yuga having begun **3102 BCE**.

## Names, station & identity

- **Kaliyan / Kalikaṉṟi** - his birth-name and best-known Tamil epithet, "the bull-calf (or the quencher) of Kali," i.e. the age of vice he is said to subdue by his hymns (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Parakāla** - his Sanskrit honorific, glossed "he who is beyond Time (Kāla)" or "Death (Kāla) to the enemy" — from his martial past; the Parakāla Maṭha of Mysore takes its name from him (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam, Parakāla chapters — Divya Prabandham hagiography]).
- **Nīlaṉ** - a further birth-name given in the hagiographies; some listings distinguish *Nīlaṉ* the man from *Kaliyaṉ* the title (Source: [Divya Sūri Charitam, Kaliyan chapters]). [disputed]
- **The garland of titles** - tradition heaps on him **Naṟkavipperumāḷ** ("lord of good poets"), **Aruḷmāri** ("rain of grace"), **Āḍalmādhavaṉ**, **Kṣatriya-siṃhamaṇi** ("crest-jewel of warriors") and **Maṅgai-vēḷ-kōṉ** — signalling both his poetic pre-eminence and his warrior caste (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **"Tirumaṅgai"** - the cult-name derives from his fief **Tirumaṅgai / Ali Nāḍu** in the Chōḷa country (chief town **Tirukkuṟaiyalūr**), whose chieftain he was; he is thus "the Āḻvār of Tirumaṅgai," styled *Tirumaṅgai-maṉṉaṉ*, "king of Tirumaṅgai" (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Amśa (partial incarnation)** - Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition regards him as an *aṃśa* of Viṣṇu's bow **Śārṅga** (or of the Lord's *Sudarśana*/attendant in some tellings), fitting his archer-warrior identity (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam, Parakāla chapters]). [traditional]
  - *Variant (which divine emblem):* the amśa is most often the **bow Śārṅga**; some hagiographic lists instead pair each Ālvār with an *āyudha/āyudhapuruṣa* of Viṣṇu differently, so the attribution varies. (Source: [Divya Sūri Charitam]) [folk]

## From chieftain to highway robber — the Kumudavallī vow

- **The warrior in royal service** - Kaliyaṉ held a military command / small chiefdom under the Chōḷa king, drawing revenue from Ali Nāḍu; the hagiographies present him as a valiant but worldly soldier before his conversion (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam, Parakāla chapters]).
- **Kumudavallī** - he fell in love with **Kumudavallī** (Tamil *Kumudavalli*), a maiden of surpassing beauty raised by a **Vaiṣṇava physician (vaidya)** of Tirunāṟaiyūr — variously said to have been discovered on a lotus, and revered as a form of Śrī / a celestial soul in mortal birth (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)). This is the **canonical home** for the Kumudavallī legend in this corpus.
  - *Variant (her nature):* some tellings make her a lotus-born foundling and *aṃśa* of Lakṣmī; others simply the physician's Vaiṣṇava daughter. (Source: [Divya Sūri Charitam]) [folk]
- **The three conditions** - Kumudavallī would wed him only if he **(1)** received the Vaiṣṇava sacraments (*pañca-saṃskāra*), becoming a Śrīvaiṣṇava; **(2)** **fed 1,008 Śrīvaiṣṇavas daily for a full year**; and, in fuller tellings, **(3)** served them with his own hands and reverence. Kaliyaṉ accepted and undertook the marriage (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam, Parakāla chapters]). [traditional]
  - *Variant (the number / term):* the daily count is most often **1,008** Vaiṣṇavas for **one year**; some retellings say he fed *all* Vaiṣṇavas who came, without a fixed figure. (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)) [disputed]
- **Ruin, arrest, and the road** - the ceaseless feeding drained his wealth; he defaulted on the tribute owed to the Chōḷa king, was **imprisoned**, and (in the fuller tellings) escaped or was released; unable to abandon the sacred kitchen, he turned **highway robber**, waylaying travellers **to fund the feeding of devotees** — a theft sanctified by its end, in the tradition's telling (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam, Parakāla chapters]). [traditional]
- **The reading of the deed** - the hagiographies are careful that his brigandage is *bhāgavata-kaiṅkarya* gone to a criminal extreme, not greed — the very excess of his service is what draws the Lord down to reclaim him (Source: [Divya Sūri Charitam]). [scholarly]

## Tirumaṇaṅkollai — the Lord robbed, and the Ashtākṣara given

- **The wedding party in the wood** - to redeem him, **Viṣṇu and Lakṣmī descended in disguise as a bridegroom and bride** with a marriage procession, passing deliberately through Kaliyaṉ's forest at **Tirumaṇaṅkollai** ("the grove of the wedding-plunder") near **Tirunāṟaiyūr** (Nāchiyār Kōvil), in the Chōḷa country (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam, Parakāla chapters]).
- **The one ornament he could not take** - Kaliyaṉ ambushed the party and stripped its jewels, but when he stooped to prise the **toe-ring (*metti*) from the bridegroom's foot**, he **could not lift or loosen it** — his hand froze, the ring would not yield, and the "groom" was revealed as the Lord himself (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)). [traditional]
  - *Variant (the immovable item):* the ornament that defeats him is usually the **toe-ring**; some tellings make it that the whole hoard becomes unliftably heavy, or that he is rooted to the spot. (Source: [Divya Sūri Charitam]) [folk]
- **The mantra from the bridegroom's lips** - the Lord, far from punishing him, bent to Kaliyaṉ's ear and **whispered the eight-syllable *Ashtākṣara* — *Oṃ Namo Nārāyaṇāya*** — the supreme Nārāyaṇa mantra, initiating the robber on the spot. Overwhelmed, Kaliyaṉ fell at his feet and was transformed into **Tirumaṅgai Āḻvār** (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam, Parakāla chapters]). [traditional]
- **The shrine of the plunder** - the deity of the spot is remembered as the Lord **who let himself be robbed** to catch a devotee; Tirumaṇaṅkollai/Tirunāṟaiyūr is enshrined in his hymns as the place of his own initiation, and the episode is the hinge of every hagiography of him (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Echo in the first decad** - the opening decad of the *Periya Tirumoḻi* (**1.1**, *"Vāḍiṉēṉ vāḍi…"*, "I withered, worn out…") voices the world-weariness and the discovery of the Nārāyaṇa name as the sole refuge — read by the tradition as the fruit of this initiation (Source: *Periya Tirumoḻi* 1.1).

## The six works — Periya Tirumoḻi and the "one-and-a-quarter thousand"

His hymns make up roughly **a third of the 4,000-verse [Nālāyira Divya Prabandham](./alvars-and-nayanars.md)** and place him second only to [Nammāḻvār](./nammalvar.md) in output (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).

- **Periya Tirumoḻi** ("the great sacred saying") - his magnum opus, **1,084 verses (pāsurams)**, an enormous garland of decads praising Viṣṇu across scores of shrines; it is the single largest work of any Ālvār save the Tiruvāymoḻi (Source: [Periya Tirumoḻi — Divya Prabandham]).
- **Tirukkuṟuntāṇḍakam** - a short *tāṇḍakam* (long-metre) poem, **20 verses** (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Tiruneḍuntāṇḍakam** - the "long *tāṇḍakam*," **30 verses**, prized for its condensed theology and used liturgically (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Tiruveḻukūṟṟirukkai** - a virtuoso **single verse** in the *ratha-bandha* / "chariot" pattern, its lines swelling and contracting on the numbers 1–7 (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Siṟiya Tirumaḍal** - the "lesser *maḍal*," a love-plaint in the Tamil *maḍal* convention (the lover threatening the public shaming-ride) turned to God (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Periya Tirumaḍal** - the "greater *maḍal*," its longer companion (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Total** - commonly summed at **~1,253 verses** (Wikipedia/roster count) though some Śrīvaiṣṇava enumerations give **1,361** or other totals depending on how the *maḍal*s and single-verse poems are counted (Source: [alvars-and-nayanars.md](./alvars-and-nayanars.md)).
  - *Variant (verse total):* the figure ranges **~1,253 to ~1,361** across lists; the discrepancy is in tallying the two *maḍal*s and the *ratha-bandha* verse, not in the Periya Tirumoḻi's 1,084. (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)) [disputed]
- **Most divya deśams sung** - by dedicating decads to shrine after shrine, Tirumaṅgai praises the **largest number of the 108 divya deśams** of any Ālvār (his hymns are the "praise" fixed to many temples), making him the chief poetic surveyor of the Viṣṇu temple network (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).

## Srirangam's third wall & the golden Buddha of Nāgapaṭṭinam

- **Servant of Raṅganātha** - after his conversion Tirumaṅgai attached himself to **Raṅganātha of Srirangam**, and turned his old soldier's resourcefulness to *building for the Lord* — undertaking major works on the temple, chiefly its **third prākāra (enclosure-wall)** and associated maṇḍapas (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam, Parakāla chapters]; see [../06-sacred-geography/srirangam-ranganatha.md](../06-sacred-geography/srirangam-ranganatha.md)).
- **The Nāgapaṭṭinam gold** - short of funds for the wall, he went by night to **Nāgapaṭṭinam**, entered a **Buddhist vihāra**, and **carried off its great golden image of the Buddha**, melting or selling the gold to pay for the Srirangam construction — the brigand's craft, now consecrated to temple-building (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam, Parakāla chapters]). [traditional] [disputed]
  - *Variant (means of taking it):* tellings differ on whether he **stole** the Buddha outright, **won/seized** it as spoils, or ferried it across a river with divine aid; all agree the **gold of a Nāgapaṭṭinam Buddha funded Srirangam's wall**. (Source: [Divya Sūri Charitam]) [folk]
  - *Historical note:* Nāgapaṭṭinam was a real and prominent **Buddhist port-centre** in the Pallava–Chōḷa era (later home to the Cūḍāmaṇi Vihāra), which lends the legend a plausible setting; the episode is nonetheless a hagiographic memory, not a documented event [scholarly].
- **The sampradāya's reading** - the tale is told approvingly: sectarian rivalry with Buddhism aside, its point is that the *same daring* that made Kaliyaṉ a robber, once surrendered, raised the Lord's own house (Source: [Divya Sūri Charitam]). [scholarly]

## Dating, iconography & cult

- **Academic frame** - historical-critical scholarship dates Tirumaṅgai to the **8th–9th c. CE** [scholarly], the latest of the Ālvārs; internal references and the Pallava/Chōḷa milieu (some place him under **Nandivarman II Pallavamalla**, builder of the Vaikuṇṭha Perumāḷ temple at Kāñchī) anchor the estimate (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Traditional frame** - Vaiṣṇava chronology assigns his birth to **Kali year 397 ≈ 2705 BCE** [traditional], in the Tamil month **Kārttikai**, **Kṛttikā** star — Kali Yuga having begun **3102 BCE**; the two frames are kept distinct and not reconciled (Source: [Guruparampara Prabhāvam]). [disputed]
- **Iconography** - uniquely among the Ālvārs he is depicted as a **warrior**: seated or standing with a **sword/spear (*val/vēl*)** in hand, sometimes with a **stylus/manuscript**, and often flanked by his consort **Kumudavallī** — the only Ālvār shown armed (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).
- **Liturgical place** - his *Periya Tirumoḻi* and the two *tāṇḍakam*s are recited in the great **Adhyayana Utsava** at Srirangam and other Śrīvaiṣṇava temples, and his decads remain the praise-texts of the many shrines he sang (Source: [Thirumangai Alvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirumangai_Alvar)).

## Significance

- **The sinner-saint paradigm** - Tirumaṅgai is Śrīvaiṣṇavism's supreme example that **no past disqualifies grace**: a robber the Lord could not be robbed of becomes the second-greatest of the Ālvārs — a standing argument that surrender (*prapatti*) outweighs deeds, paralleling the outcaste-saints of the Śaiva canon (cf. [alvars-and-nayanars.md](./alvars-and-nayanars.md)).
- **The temple-builder among poets** - alone among the Ālvārs he is remembered as a *builder*, tying the Tamil bhakti movement physically to the fabric of **Srirangam**, the foremost divya deśam and later seat of [Rāmānuja](./ramanuja-and-madhva.md).
- **The widest gazetteer of shrines** - by singing more divya deśams than any other saint, he is the chief poetic witness to the geography of Tamil Vaiṣṇavism.
- **Open roster flag:** verse totals (~1,253 vs ~1,361), the exact terms of Kumudavallī's vow, the manner of the Nāgapaṭṭinam taking, and his dating (2705 BCE vs 8th–9th c. CE) all vary across traditional and academic sources [disputed].
