# The Nālvar — Sambandar, Appar, Sundarar & Māṇikkavācakar

*Tamil Nadu, c. 6th–9th c. CE — the four foremost Nāyaṉārs whose hymns became the "Tamil Veda" of Śaivism.*
*Sits under [07-acharyas-and-sampradayas](README.md) (master index [../README.md](../README.md)). Parent roster: [The Ālvārs & Nāyaṉārs](alvars-and-nayanars.md). Doctrinal frame: [Śaiva Schools & Orders](shaiva-schools.md). Literary corpus: [../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md](../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md).*

The **Nālvar** ("the Four") are the four most celebrated of the 63 Nāyaṉārs (Tamil Śaiva poet-saints): **Tirugñāna Sambandar**, **Appar (Tirunāvukkaracar)**, **Cuntarar (Sundarar)**, and **Māṇikkavācakar**. The first three are the *muvar* ("the Three") whose hymns make up the **Tēvāram** (Tirumuṟai Books 1–7); Māṇikkavācakar's *Tiruvācakam* and *Tirukkōvaiyār* form Book 8. Together they sang the temple-by-temple pilgrimage hymning (*paṇ*-set songs of the *sthalas*) that grounded the devotional substrate later systematized by the [Śaiva Siddhānta](shaiva-schools.md#śaiva-siddhānta--the-dualist-mantramārga--the-tamil-ācāryas) ācāryas. **Dating note (two clocks):** academic consensus places them c. 6th–9th c. CE [scholarly]; traditional Tamil chronology assigns far earlier dates to Māṇikkavācakar [traditional] [disputed].

## Tirugñāna Sambandar — the child saint (Tirumuṟai 1–3)

- **Birth & milk legend** - born at **Sirkazhi (Sīrkāḻi / Kāḻi)** in the Cauvery delta to a Brahmin couple, **Śivapāda Hṛdayar** and **Bhagavatiyār**; 7th c. CE (Pallava era), conventionally placed c. 644–660 CE. As a toddler (age three) left at the temple tank, he wept and was fed **divine milk by Pārvatī** from a golden cup; his father saw milk on his lips and, asked who fed him, the child pointed skyward and sang his first hymn *"Toḍuḍaiya ceviyaṉ"* — earning the name **Tiru-Gñāna-Sambandar**, "the one related to (born of) sacred wisdom/milk." (Source: [Sambandar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambandar))
- **Tēvāram corpus (Books 1–3)** - his hymns are the **Tirukkaḍaikkāppu**, forming the first three Tirumuṟai. Tradition credits ~16,000 hymns; **383 (or 384) hymns / ~4,181 stanzas survive** — the largest surviving Tēvāram share. Set to the Tamil *paṇ* (melodic modes) and sung as *patikam* (decads, normally 10–11 verses each). (Source: [Sambandar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambandar); [Tevaram — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevaram))
- **Pandya reconversion** - the central historical-hagiographic episode: the **Pandya king of Madurai (Kūṉ Pāṇḍiyaṉ / Neḍumāṟaṉ)** had embraced Jainism under his Jain courtiers; his Śaiva queen **Maṅgaiyarkkaracī** (herself a Nāyaṉār) and minister **Kulacciṟai** (also a Nāyaṉār) summoned Sambandar, who cured the king's burning fever/illness, defeated the Jains in contests of **fire and water** (his palm-leaf hymn survived the flame and floated upstream), and **reconverted the king and realm to Śaivism**. His hymns carry sharp **anti-Jain (and anti-Buddhist) polemic**. (Source: [Sambandar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambandar))
  - *Variant (impalement of the 8,000 Jains) [folk] [disputed]:* later hagiography (Cēkkiḻār's *Periya Purāṇam*) holds that, on losing the contests, **8,000 Jains were impaled on stakes** at Madurai — depicted in Tamil temple murals (notably the Madurai Mīnākṣī temple). Modern scholars treat the mass-impalement as sectarian legend rather than recorded event. (Source: [Sambandar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambandar))
- **Companions & miracles** - travelled with **Tiru-Nīlakaṇṭa Yāḻppāṇar**, a *yāḻ* (lute) player who set his hymns to music; raised a merchant's daughter (**Pūmpāvai**) from death by his hymn; given a **pearl palanquin, parasol and cymbals by Śiva**. (Source: [Sambandar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambandar))
- **Death at the wedding** - at his own marriage (age ~16) at **Nallūr-Perumaṇam**, he prayed and **merged into the divine effulgence (jyoti)** of the sanctum, taking the entire wedding party with him into Śiva. (Source: [Sambandar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambandar))

## Appar (Tirunāvukkaracar) — sacred ash & the Jain ordeals (Books 4–6)

- **Names & origins** - born **Maruḷ-nīkkiyār** ("remover of darkness/delusion") at **Tiruvāmūr** (Tirumunaippāḍi country), c. 570 CE (lived into the 7th c.), of a **Veḷḷāḷar** (agriculturist) family; later titled **Tirunāvukkaracar**, "king of the (sacred) tongue/speech," and affectionately **Appar** ("father") — the epithet given him by Sambandar. (Source: [Appar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appar))
- **Jain phase & reconversion** - orphaned and raised by his devout elder sister **Tilakavatiyār** (whose betrothed died in war, so she remained a Śaiva ascetic). Appar joined a **Jain monastery, was renamed Dharmasena**, and rose to head the *paḷḷi* at **Tiruppātirippuliyūr**. Struck by an agonizing **colic / stomach disease (cūlai)** the Jains could not cure, he returned to his sister, who applied **sacred ash (Tirunīṟu)** and taught him the five-syllable mantra; at the **Atikai Vīraṭṭāṉam** temple he spontaneously sang his first *patikam* (*"Kūṟṟāyiṉa vāṟu"*) and was **healed**, embracing Śaivism for life. (Source: [Appar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appar))
- **The ordeals under Mahendravarman I** - the Jains, threatened by his apostasy, incited the Pallava king **Mahendravarman I** (then a Jain) to persecute him. Hagiography records a series of **survived ordeals**: confinement in a **lime kiln** for seven days (he emerged cool, singing); poisoning by **poisoned milk/food**; trampling by a **maddened elephant** (which turned on its handlers); and being **thrown into the sea bound to a stone**, on which he floated to shore singing the *Cuṇṇam* hymn. The king, convinced, reconverted to Śaivism. (Source: [Appar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appar)) [folk]
- **Sacred-ash devotion & humble service** - Appar made **Tirunīṟu (vibhūti, holy ash)** the emblem of his bhakti; he is iconographically shown bearing the **uḻavārappaḍai**, a farmer's hoe/weeding-tool he used to **clean and clear overgrown, dilapidated Śiva temples** along his pilgrimages — a model of devotional manual service (*toṇḍu*). (Source: [Appar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appar))
- **Tēvāram corpus (Books 4–6)** - **313 hymns / ~3,130 stanzas survive**, compiled as Tirumuṟai Books 4, 5 and 6; his Book 6 (*Tirutāṇḍakam*) is especially revered for metrical and emotional depth. Travelled and sang with Sambandar at several shrines. (Source: [Appar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appar); [Tevaram — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevaram))

## Cuntarar (Sundarar) — Śiva's comrade & the roll-call of the 63 (Book 7)

- **Names & origins** - born **Nampi Ārūraṉ** at **Tirunāvalūr** (late 7th c., an 8th-c. saint; Zvelebil places his death c. 730 CE), of a Śaiva Brahmin (Āṭiśaiva) family; called **Cuntaramūrti** ("the beautiful one"), **Tampirāṉ Tōḻaṉ** ("comrade of the Lord"), and **Vaṉ-toṇḍaṉ** ("the harsh/obstinate devotee"). (Source: [Sundarar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundarar))
- **The slave-deed legend** - at his wedding, an **old Brahmin (Śiva in disguise)** interrupted and produced a **palm-leaf bond/deed (ōlai)** proving that Sundarar's grandfather had pledged the family as **hereditary bond-slaves** to him; the village elders authenticated it, and the old man vanished into the **Tiruveṇṇainallūr** Śiva shrine. Sundarar accepted the claim as Śiva's call, abandoned the wedding, and began his hymns by **chiding Śiva** — hence his "harsh devotee" intimacy. His first hymn opened *"Pittā piṟai cūḍī"* ("O madman who wears the crescent…"). (Source: [Sundarar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundarar))
- **Two wives** - married the temple dancer **Paravai Nācciyār** at Tiruvārūr, and later the Veḷḷāḷa girl **Caṅkili (Sangili) Nācciyār** at Tiruvoṟṟiyūr; Śiva himself acted as **go-between/messenger (tūtu)** to reconcile him with Paravai. Breaking his oath (sworn before the Tiruvoṟṟiyūr *makiḻam* tree never to leave Caṅkili), he was struck **blind**, and gradually regained sight through penitent hymns en route to Tiruvārūr. (Source: [Sundarar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundarar))
- **Tēvāram corpus (Book 7)** - his hymns form Tirumuṟai Book 7, the **Tiruppāṭṭu**: **100 hymns / ~1,026 stanzas**. (Source: [Sundarar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundarar); [Tevaram — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevaram))
- **The Tiruttoṇḍa Tohai — naming the 63** - Sundarar's signature contribution to the tradition: the **Tiruttoṇḍa Tohai (Tiruttoṇḍattokai)**, a hymn of **11 verses** that **names the foremost Śiva-devotees ("the holy servants")** group by group — the seed-list from which the canonical **63 Nāyaṉārs** were fixed. Each verse ends *"I am the servant of…"* (*aḍiyēṉ*). This list was expanded by **Nambiyāṇḍār Nambi** into the *Tiruttoṇḍar Tiruvandhādhi* and finally versified by **Cēkkiḻār** as the *Periya Purāṇam*. (Source: [Sundarar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundarar); [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars))
  - *Note on the roster's growth [scholarly]:* Sundarar's Tohai stops at his own time and includes **Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār**; Sundarar himself and his parents were added later, completing the count of 63. See the full roster in [alvars-and-nayanars.md](alvars-and-nayanars.md#the-sixty-three-nāyaṉārs-shaiva).
- **Cēramāṉ Perumāḷ & the ascent** - close friend of the **Cēra king-saint Cēramāṉ Perumāḷ Nāyaṉār**, with whom he made pilgrimages. Tradition holds **Śiva sent a white elephant** to carry Sundarar bodily to **Kailāsa**; Cēramāṉ followed on horseback. (Source: [Sundarar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundarar); [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai))

## Māṇikkavācakar — the Tiruvācakam (Book 8)

- **Names & origins** - born at **Vādavūr (Tiruvātavūr)**, about seven miles from **Madurai**, into a Brahmin family; titled **Māṇikkavācakar**, "he whose utterances are rubies/gems." Conventionally a **9th-c.** saint [scholarly], but the dating is sharply **disputed**. (Source: [Manikkavacakar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manikkavacakar))
  - *Variant (very early dating) [traditional] [disputed]:* some accounts make him minister to the Pandya **Neḍuñceḻiyaṉ (3rd c. CE)** or **Arikēsari Māṟavarman (c. 7th c.)**, pushing him centuries earlier than the academic 9th-c. placement. He is **not** named in Sundarar's Tiruttoṇḍa Tohai, which complicates fitting him among the 63. (Source: [Manikkavacakar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manikkavacakar))
- **Minister & the horse legend** - served as **prime minister (with the title *Teṉṉavaṉ Brahmarāyaṉ*)** to a Pandya king. Sent to **Tirupperuntuṟai (Āvuḍaiyārkōyil)** with treasury gold to **buy cavalry horses**, he met an **ascetic guru (Śiva in disguise)**, was **initiated and renounced worldly office**, and spent the money **building a Śiva temple** instead. When the king demanded the horses, **Śiva sent jackals/foxes transformed into horses**, which at night reverted to jackals and scattered; the king imprisoned and tortured Māṇikkavācakar until Śiva flooded the **Vaigai river** (the famous "carrying earth for the embankment" episode, with Śiva taking a labourer's wage of a *piṭṭu* rice-cake) to vindicate him. (Source: [Manikkavacakar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manikkavacakar))
- **Tiruvācakam & Tirukkōvaiyār (Book 8)** - his **Tiruvācakam** ("sacred utterance") and **Tirukkōvaiyār** together form Tirumuṟai Book 8; the Tiruvācakam comprises **51 compositions / ~600+ verses** of intense longing and surrender. The maxim runs: *"He whose heart is not melted by the Tiruvācakam can be melted by no other utterance (āgama)."* The *Tirukkōvaiyār* is a 400-verse *akam* (love) poem reread allegorically as the soul's love for Śiva. (Source: [Manikkavacakar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manikkavacakar); [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai))
- **Chidambaram & merging** - debated and defeated a delegation of **Buddhists** (some accounts: Sri Lankan/Ceylonese) at **Chidambaram**, reportedly restoring the speech of a mute princess; finally, at the **Naṭarāja temple, Chidambaram**, he sang the **Accō Patikam** and **merged into the form of Naṭarāja**, attaining liberation. (Source: [Manikkavacakar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manikkavacakar))

## Temple-sthala hymning, the paṇ tradition & Shaiva Siddhānta grounding

- **Sthala pilgrimage hymning** - the Tēvāram is organized around **sacred sites (*tēvāra-stalams* / *pāḍal peṟṟa stalams*)**: the saints physically travelled temple to temple and composed a *patikam* praising the Śiva of each place — **~276 temples** are "sung shrines" (*pāḍal peṟṟa stalam*), a devotional gazetteer of the Tamil Śaiva landscape. This made the hymns inseparable from pilgrimage and from each shrine's local Śiva. (Source: [Tevaram — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevaram))
- **The paṇ musical tradition** - each Tēvāram hymn is set to a **paṇ**, an ancient Tamil melodic mode (precursor/cousin of the Carnatic *rāga*); the corpus preserves a system of **~23–24 paṇs**. Hymns were performed by hereditary singers — the **Ōtuvārs** (temple chanters) — to *yāḻ* (lute) and cymbals, a practice continued daily in Tamil Śiva temples after the *pūjā*. (Source: [Tevaram — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevaram))
- **Canonization into the Tirumuṟai** - the scattered Tēvāram palm-leaves were rediscovered (legend: walled up at **Chidambaram**) and organized into **Books 1–7** by **Nambiyāṇḍār Nambi** under the Chola **Rājarāja I** (c. late 10th–early 11th c.); Māṇikkavācakar's work became **Book 8**; **Cēkkiḻār's *Periya Purāṇam* (c. 1135 CE)** became **Book 12**, completing the 12-volume Tirumuṟai. (Source: [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai))
- **Grounding of Shaiva Siddhānta** - the Nālvar's emotional, grace-centred bhakti is the **devotional foundation** on which the later **[Śaiva Siddhānta](shaiva-schools.md#śaiva-siddhānta--the-dualist-mantramārga--the-tamil-ācāryas)** ācāryas (the 13th–14th c. **Santāna Kuravar** — Meykaṇḍar, Aruḷnandi, Umāpati) built their systematic dualist-realist theology (*pati–paśu–pāśa*); the Tirumuṟai and the Meykaṇḍa Śāstras are jointly canonical for Tamil Śaivas. (Source: [Shaiva Siddhanta — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaiva_Siddhanta))
- **Parallel to the Vaiṣṇava stream** - the Nālvar/Tēvāram stand to Tamil Śaivism as the **Ālvārs / Nālāyira Divya Prabandham** stand to Tamil Vaiṣṇavism — two parallel 6th–9th c. bhakti waves; see [alvars-and-nayanars.md](alvars-and-nayanars.md) for the twelve Ālvārs and the full 63-Nāyaṉār roster. [scholarly]

## Variants & disputes

- *Variant (the "Three" vs "the Four") [scholarly]:* the **muvar** ("Three": Sambandar, Appar, Sundarar) are the **Tēvāram** authors (Books 1–7); the **Nālvar** ("Four") adds **Māṇikkavācakar** (Book 8). Some listings of the Tēvāram trio therefore exclude Māṇikkavācakar, who belongs to the broader Tirumuṟai but not the Tēvāram proper. (Source: [Tevaram — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevaram))
- *Variant (Sambandar's dates) [disputed]:* commonly placed c. 644–660 CE, but his exact century shifts with the identification of the Pandya/Pallava kings he interacted with; the **6th–7th c. CE** range is given by different scholars. (Source: [Sambandar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambandar))
- *Variant (Appar's birth caste/place) [scholarly]:* usually **Veḷḷāḷar** of **Tiruvāmūr**; the "Vagisar/Vāgīśa" title and the *Dharmasena* Jain name appear in hagiography (*Periya Purāṇam*). (Source: [Appar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appar))
- *Variant (Māṇikkavācakar's century) [disputed]:* see above — academic 9th c. vs traditional 3rd–7th c. The dispute hinges on which Pandya patron he served and his absence from Sundarar's roster. (Source: [Manikkavacakar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manikkavacakar))
- *Open-ended roster note:* survival counts (Sambandar 383/384 hymns; Appar 313; Sundarar 100) are of **surviving** hymns; tradition claims far larger original outputs (e.g. ~16,000 for Sambandar), now lost — figures should be read as canonical-survival, not original totals. (Source: [Tevaram — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevaram))
