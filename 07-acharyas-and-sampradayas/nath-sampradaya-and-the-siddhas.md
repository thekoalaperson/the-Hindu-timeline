# The Nāth Sampradāya, the Navnāth & the 84 Mahāsiddhas

> Detail node under [`07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/`](README.md) — the **Siddha/Nāth esoteric world**: the Śaiva yogī order of "Lords" (*nātha*), its **Navnāth** roster of nine masters, and the trans-sectarian **84 Mahāsiddhas** (*Caurāsī Siddha*) shared with Vajrayāna Buddhism. Expands the stub Nāth block in [`shaiva-schools.md`](shaiva-schools.md).
> Sits in the historical Kali-Yuga; dating is **[traditional]** (guru-succession / hagiography) vs **[scholarly]** (epigraphy / text-criticism) per the [two-clocks method](../01-manvantaras/manvantara-07-vaivasvata/mahayuga-28-current/04-kali-yuga/recorded-history.md). Cross-link the tantric matrix in [`shakta-and-tantra-lineages.md`](shakta-and-tantra-lineages.md), the southern parallel in [`tamil-siddhars.md`](tamil-siddhars.md), and the Nāth-lineage Vārkarī founder [`jnaneshwar.md`](jnaneshwar.md).

## The order — Nāth Sampradāya, descent and structure

- **Nāth (Nātha) Sampradāya** — a **Śaiva Siddha-yogī** order of India and Nepal, fusing Śaivism, Tantra and Yoga with Buddhist Siddha influence; its members are *yogīs* of the "Lord" (*nātha*) lineage, with **Śiva (Ādinātha)** as the first guru. (Source: Wikipedia, *Natha Sampradaya*; Britannica, *Gorakhnath*)
  - *Dating [disputed]:* one survey dates the tradition's emergence to the **12th–13th c. CE** (Jones & Ryan 2006, cited in Wikipedia). Mallinson separates the gurus from the order:
    - The first historical gurus, Matsyendra and Gorakṣa, probably lived in the **9th–10th** and **11th–12th c.** respectively, both in the Deccan.
    - The earliest references to the Nāth ascetic order as an *organized entity* date only to the **beginning of the 17th c.**
    - The name "Nāth" for the order appears only around the 18th c. (*Siddha-siddhānta-paddhati* 5.43). Before that, its forerunners were simply called *yogī* / *jogī*.
    - (Source: Mallinson, "Nāth Saṃpradāya", *Brill's Encyclopedia of Hinduism* vol. 3, 2011, pp. 1–5) [scholarly]
- **Antecedents [disputed]** — scholars *relate* the Nāths to older heterodox Śaiva streams, especially the **Kāpālika** and **Kaula** (Muller-Ortega), and also the **Pāśupata** and the Śāktas (O'Brien-Kop) (see [`shaiva-schools.md`](shaiva-schools.md)). A direct line of descent has not been shown: McLeod writes that the Nāths "may be linked" to the Kāpālikas or Kālāmukhas, since they share their unorthodox lifestyle, but that neither the doctrines nor the evidence linking them has been uncovered. (Source: Wikipedia, *Natha Sampradaya*, citing Muller-Ortega 2010, O'Brien-Kop 2022 and McLeod 1987)
  - *The Kaula link (text):* the firmest early anchor is Abhinavagupta's *Tantrāloka* (c. 1000 CE). Its opening benedictions salute **Macchanda-vibhu** (*stān me sa macchandavibhuḥ prasannaḥ*, TĀ 1.7). In its list of Kaula siddha couples, TĀ 29.32 closes a group of six authorized couples (*ṣaḍyugmaṃ sādhikārakam*) with *macchandaḥ kuṅkuṇāmbā ca*, Macchanda and Kuṅkuṇāmbā. Mallinson identifies this Macchanda with **Matsyendra** and Kuṅkuṇāmbā as his consort; he reads the -āī names of TĀ 29.29–42 as the wives of Matsyendra's six non-celibate sons. (Source: *Tantrāloka* 1.7, 29.29–32, GRETIL; Mallinson 2011, pp. 5, 10) [scholarly identification]
- **Kānphaṭa ("split-ear") yogīs** — Briggs records that the followers of Gorakhnāth are known as *Yogī* (from their haṭha-yoga), *Gorakhnāthī* (from their reputed founder), *Darśanī* (from their huge earrings) and "most distinctively" *Kānphaṭa*, because the ear-cartilage is split to insert the earrings (*kuṇḍala* / *mudrā*). In the Panjab, the Himalaya and Bombay they are often simply called *Nātha*, and in western India *Dharamnāthī*. (Source: Briggs 1938, ch. 1)
  - *The name and the practice [scholarly]:* Mallinson notes that the Nāths themselves avoid the outsiders' name *Kānphaṭā* and prefer *Darśanī yogī*. The earliest evidence for earrings worn through the *cartilage*, rather than the lobe, is no older than the **late 18th c.**, when such earrings first appear in miniatures. The Jodhpur paintings of the early 19th c. still show lobe-rings, then Kānphaṭās within two decades. The custom is associated with Gorakhnāth, and in some legends he introduced it to distinguish his followers from those of Kānhapā and Jālandharnāth. (Source: Mallinson 2011, pp. 10–11)
  - *Founder [traditional]:* Gorakṣa is the order's *reputed* founder. Briggs notes that Matsyendra too "is sometimes looked upon as the founder" of the Kānphaṭas. (Source: Briggs 1938, ch. 1 and p. 233)
  - *Gorakhpur [historical]:* the Gorakhnāth Maṭh at **Gorakhpur** is today the order's most influential seat.
    - Mallinson dates the temple's establishment to the **17th c.**, and notes that until Partition **Jogi Tilla** in the Punjab was generally recognized as the order's headquarters. Since then Gorakhpur and Haridwar have vied for primacy. (Source: Mallinson 2011, p. 13)
    - *Variant (Wikipedia):* an older shrine on the site was converted into a mosque under ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī; Nāth followers later built a smaller shrine, and devotees and yogīs added to it in the 18th, 19th and 20th c. (Source: Wikipedia, *Gorakhnath*)
- **Twelve panths [traditional]** — tradition divides the Kānphaṭas into **twelve sub-sects** (*bārah panth*), "each organized by a disciple of Gorakhnath". Briggs adds that some panths were formed soon after Gorakhnāth and others were "later brought into line" with the scheme, and that the names in the various lists "far exceed twelve". A council of the twelve panths, the *Bhek Bārah Panth*, was elected at the twelve-yearly Kumbh Melā at Hardwar, and its head was chosen from each panth in turn. (Source: Briggs 1938, ch. 2 and ch. 4)
  - *Scholarly reading [scholarly]:* David Gordon White sees the panths as an amalgamation of groups descended from Matsyendra, Gorakṣa or their pupils, not as subdivisions of one monolithic order (Wikipedia, *Natha Sampradaya*). Mallinson calls the twelve panths the Nāths' oldest organizing principle.
    - Its first references are in Sikh writings of the very early 17th c. (*Gurū Granth Sāhib*; *Vārāṅ Bhāī Gurdās* 8.13). The earliest list of the twelve names is in the *Dabistān-i Mazāhib* (first half of the 17th c.).
    - Later lists vary a great deal, and a parallel division into 18 panths also exists.
    - The list now authoritative, propagated by the Yogi Mahasabha, is Satyanāthī, Dharmanāthī, Rāmnāthī, Bairāg, Kapilānī, Āīpanthī, Naṭeśvarī, Gaṅganāthī, Rāval, Pāvpanthī, Mannāthī and Pāgalpanthī. The Āīpanth is perhaps the oldest.
    - The followers of **Kānhapā** are excluded from the scheme.
    - (Source: Mallinson 2011, pp. 9–10)
- **Two grades of initiation [traditional]** — initiation has two stages. The novice (*Aughaṛ*) serves his guru and at feasts receives only half a Yogī's share. The full Yogī has had his ears split and the earrings inserted; this is the *Darśanī* or Kānphaṭa proper. Mallinson likewise uses *Aughaṛ* for Nāth ascetics who do not wear earrings. (Source: Briggs 1938, ch. 2; Mallinson 2011, p. 10)
- **Doctrinal/practical core [scholarly]** — the body is perfected as the vehicle of liberation, through *haṭha-yoga*, *kuṇḍalinī*, *nāda* (inner sound) and the pursuit of *kāya-siddhi* / immortality (*jīvanmukti*). This is the great historical bridge from tantric Śaivism to pan-Indian yoga. (Source: Wikipedia, *Natha Sampradaya*)
  - *Texts:* the texts are dated as follows.
    - The earliest haṭha works attributed to Gorakṣa are the *Vivekamārtaṇḍa* and the *Gorakṣaśataka*, both 13th c. The *Amaraughaprabodha* is slightly later (probably 14th c.).
    - Svātmārāma drew on all of these to compile the *Haṭhayoga-pradīpikā* in the 15th c.
    - Works that presuppose an organized Nāth Sampradāya, including the *Siddha-siddhānta-paddhati* and the *Gorakṣa-siddhānta-saṅgraha*, date from the **18th c. onward** [late text]. (Source: Mallinson 2011, pp. 5, 16)
    - One recension of the *Gorakṣaśataka* opens with the invocation *oṃ parama-gurave gorakṣanāthāya namaḥ*, before verse 1 (Kuvalayananda–Shukla ed.). The recension Briggs printed praises **Mīnanātha**, "in whom Ādinātha himself" became the ocean of knowledge (GorS 2, Briggs ed.). (Source: *Gorakṣaśataka*, both GRETIL recensions)
- **Tamil parallel [regional]** — the Nāth Siddhas overlap with the southern **Tamil Siddhars** (*Cittar*). They share figures (Gorakṣa = **Korakkar**, Matsyendra = **Macchamuni**) and a common programme of alchemy and yoga; cross-linked in [`tamil-siddhars.md`](tamil-siddhars.md). (Source: Wikipedia, *Siddhar*, *Natha Sampradaya*)

## The Navnāth — the nine Nāthas

- **Navnāth ("nine Nāthas")** — the canonical line of nine perfected gurus from whom the order's *panths* descend. Rosters **vary by region** and are never to be merged. (Source: Wikipedia, *Navanatha Sampradaya*, *Natha Sampradaya*)
  - *How old is the list of nine [scholarly]:* early tantric taxonomies of Nāths do not agree on the number. Kubjikā texts speak of 25, other Paścimāmnāya, Trika and Kālī works of four, the *Ṣaṭsāhasra-saṃhitā* of 18, and the 13th-c. *Matsyendra-saṃhitā* (9.9) of seven. None of them includes Gorakṣa.
    - The first list to name Gorakṣa is an inscription of **1279** near the Kalleśvara temple at Kukkunoor (Karnataka). It includes Ādinātha but not Matsyendra.
    - The earliest list of nine matching later Nāth usage is the Telugu ***Navanāthacaritra*** (late 15th c.). It includes Śiva, Mīna (Matsyendra), Gorakṣa, Sāraṅgadhara (Cauraṅgī) and Nāgārjuna.
    - No Sanskrit text lists the nine before the c. 18th-c. *Gorakṣa-siddhānta-saṅgraha*.
    - Many lists from the 18th c. on *omit* Gorakṣa, because he has become the godhead (Śrīnāth) of whom the nine are avatāras.
    - (Source: Mallinson 2011, pp. 3–4)
  - *North-Indian Ādinātha-headed rosters [traditional]:* one runs **Ādinātha, Udaynāth, Satyanāth, Santoṣnāth, Acchambhenāth, Kanthaḍnāth, Caurangī, Matsyendra, Gorakṣa**. Another runs **Omkārnāth, Udaynāth, Santoṣnāth, Acalnāth, Gajbalināth, Jñānnāth, Caurangīnāth, Matsyendra, Gorakṣa**. Other lists pair each Nāth with a deity. (Source: gorakhnath.org lists, reproduced in Wikipedia, *Navanatha Sampradaya*)
  - *Maharashtrian roster [regional]:* the *Navnāth Bhaktisār* names nine: **Macchindra (Matsyendra), Gorakṣa, Gahinī, Jālindar (Jālandhar), Kānifa, Bhartarī (Bhartṛhari), Revaṇa, Nāga(nātha) and Carpaṭi**. This is also the list Nisargadatta Mahārāj's circle called "the most widely accepted". **Aḍbhaṅg** is *not* one of the nine there. He is a disciple whom Gorakṣa trains (adhyāya 34), and he is listed among the Nāths assembled at Badarikāśrama (adhyāya 40). (Source: *Navnāth Bhaktisār*, via the Marathi Wikisource *Navnāth Kathāsār*, adhyāyas 34, 40; Wikipedia, *Navanatha Sampradaya*)
    - *The nine Nārāyaṇas [text vs retelling]:* the *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* names nine sons of Ṛṣabha, wandering *śramaṇas* skilled in self-knowledge: *kavir havir antarīkṣaḥ prabuddhaḥ pippalāyanaḥ / āvirhotro 'tha drumilaś camasaḥ karabhājanaḥ* (BhP 11.2.20–21; same line at 5.4.11). The Bhāgavata says nothing about Nāths. The *Navnāth Bhaktisār* (adhyāya 1) makes each Nāth an avatāra of one of these nine "Nārāyaṇas", sent down by Kṛṣṇa at the start of the Kali age. Its mapping is Kavi → Matsyendra; **Hari** (BhP *Havi*) → Gorakṣa; Antarikṣa → Jālandhar; Prabuddha → Kānifa; Pippalāyana → Carpaṭi; Āvirhotra → Nāganātha; Drumila (also called **Dhruvamīna**) → Bhartarī; Camasa → Revaṇa; Karabhājana → Gahinī. (Source: BhP 11.2.20–21, GRETIL; *Navnāth Kathāsār* table and adhyāya 1) [regional]
    - *Dattātreya as guru [regional][late text]:* in the Bhaktisār, Dattātreya is the guru of Matsyendra, Jālandhar, Bhartarī, Revaṇa, Nāganātha and Carpaṭi. Mallinson notes that Dattātreya has been linked with the Nāths only in the Deccan, and that the link was formalized around the 18th c. He argues that by making the nine Nāths the nine Nārāyaṇas, texts such as the Bhaktisār symbolically united the Nāths with the **Mahānubhāv** sect. (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār* table; Mallinson 2011, p. 4)
  - *Jñāneśvar's line [text]:* Jñāneśvar's own colophon gives the line Śiva → (the teaching heard in the fish) → Matsyendra → Gorakṣa → **Gahinī** → **Nivṛtti** → Jñānadeva. Matsyendra gave the *mudrā* to "Gorakṣa-rāya", and Gorakṣa in turn passed on the bliss of non-duality to "Śrī-Gahinīnātha" (*saṃpādileṃ saprabhava śrīgahinīnāthā*). Gahinī saw beings suffering in the Kali age and gave the charge to Nivṛtti. The whole line is called a *śiṣya-paramparā* from "the ādi-guru Śaṅkara". The work is dated Śaka 1212 (1290 CE). See the [`jnaneshwar.md`](jnaneshwar.md) Vārkarī genealogy. (Source: *Jñāneśvarī* 18.1752–1759 and closing colophon, Marathi Wikisource numbering)
- **Ādinātha** — Śiva himself as the primordial guru and source of the *nātha* teaching. The *Haṭhayoga-pradīpikā* opens *śrī-ādināthāya namo 'stu tasmai yenopadiṣṭā haṭha-yoga-vidyā* ("homage to Śrī Ādinātha, who taught the science of haṭha-yoga") and says that Matsyendra, Gorakṣa and others knew it (HYP 1.1, 1.4). (Source: *Haṭhayoga-pradīpikā* 1.1–4, GRETIL)
- **Matsyendranāth (Macchindranāth / Mīnanāth)** — the first **human** guru of the Nāths, and the master to whom the **Kaula** *Kaulajñāna-nirṇaya* is attributed (ed. P. C. Bagchi, 1934). In Nepal he is identified with the bodhisattva **Avalokiteśvara / Karuṇāmaya**, worshipped as the rain-god **Bunga Dyaḥ** (Red Matsyendranāth of Patan, whose birthplace is held to be Bungamati) in a great chariot festival. Hindus there regard him as a form of Śiva. (Source: Wikipedia, *Matsyendranath*; Briggs 1938, p. 233)
  - *Dating [scholarly]:* Mallinson places him in southern India, probably the Deccan, in the **9th–10th c.** His evidence is the *Tantrāloka*'s Macchanda with his consort Kuṅkuṇāmbā, "the Goddess from the Konkan" (TĀ 29.32), together with the provenance of the texts of his cults. Tradition credits him with reforming the cremation-ground *Kula* rites into the tamer *Kaula*. Works ascribed to him include the *Kaulajñāna-nirṇaya*, *Akulavīra-tantra* and *Matsyendra-saṃhitā*; many were probably attributed to him after his death. (Source: Mallinson 2011, pp. 4–5, 16; *Tantrāloka* 29.32; Wikipedia, *Matsyendranath*)
  - *Variant — the fish (Jñāneśvarī, 1290 CE):* Śiva, the "Tripurāri", spoke the teaching into Śakti's ear on the shore of the milk-ocean. It came into the hands of the one who lay hidden in the belly of a *makara* amid the waves, Matsyendra. (Source: *Jñāneśvarī* 18.1752–1754)
  - *Variant — the fish (Navnāth Bhaktisār, 1819):* a fish in the **Yamunā** swallows a drop of Brahmā's seed, and Kavi Nārāyaṇa takes birth as the embryo in its womb. Śiva, teaching Pārvatī on the riverbank, hears the unborn Matsyendra answer from the fish's belly that "all is Brahman". He tells him to receive the same teaching from **Dattātreya** at Badarikāśrama. (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*, adhyāya 1)
  - *Variant — the fish (popular):* the fish swims to the ocean floor, where Śiva is teaching Pārvatī the secrets of yoga, and Matsyendra overhears it and practises inside the fish. (Source: Wikipedia, *Matsyendranath*) [folk]
  - *Variant — birth (Briggs):* a fish swallowed the unburnt navel of a pious Satya-yuga king, Udhodhar, and later gave birth to Matsyendra. (Source: Briggs 1938, p. 233) [folk]
  - *Variant — the Buddhist Mīnapa:* Tibetan tradition tells of a fisherman-siddha, **Mīna**, who is swallowed by a fish while working in the Bay of Bengal. Wikipedia treats him as probably the same figure. (Source: Wikipedia, *Matsyendranath*; Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti no. 8 Mīnapa) [scholarly identification]
  - *Names kept apart [disputed]:* the *Jñāneśvarī* calls Matsyendra "Mīna" (18.1755), and Mallinson lists Mīna, Macchandar and Macchaghna among his piscine names. The *Haṭhayoga-pradīpikā* siddha list, however, names *matsyendra* and *mīna* as two separate siddhas (HYP 1.5). Briggs notes that Bengal tradition likewise makes Mīna and Matsyendra two different yogīs. The further identification with the Buddhist **Lūipa** ("Fish-Gut Eater") is reported by Wikipedia as Tibetan tradition, citing Schomer & McLeod 1987. (Source: *Jñāneśvarī* 18.1755; HYP 1.5; Mallinson 2011, p. 4; Briggs 1938, p. 230 n.; Wikipedia, *Natha Sampradaya*, *Matsyendranath*)
  - *Variant — the kingdom of women (Navnāth Bhaktisār):* Hanumān sends Matsyendra to **Strīrājya**, where he lives with Queen **Mainākinī** (also called Tilottamā and Padminī) at her capital Śṛṅga-Muruḍ. Gorakṣa gets in disguised as a woman, as the drummer in the troupe of the courtesan **Kaliṅgā**. As he plays the *mṛdaṅga*, the drum keeps sounding *"calo Macchindar, Gorakh āyā!"* ("Come on, Macchindar, Gorakh has come!"). (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*, adhyāyas 3, 19–22) [regional]
  - *Variant — Kadalī (Maithili/Bengali):* the *Gorakṣa-vijaya* tells of Matsyendra's rescue from the land of women by Gorakṣa. Mallinson dates the Maithili *Gorakṣa-vijaya* (of Vidyāpati) to the early 15th c., with Bengali reworkings from perhaps the 17th c. onward; Banglapedia credits the first Bengali verse *Gorakṣa-vijaya* to the 16th-c. poet Śekh Phayajullā. In Banglapedia's account, Matsyendra, cursed by Śiva, comes among the women of the lady-land **Kadalī** and abandons his religious practice; the rescue by Gorakṣa is the subject of the *Gorakṣa-vijaya* as Mallinson summarizes it. The Kadri maṭh above Mangalore, perhaps the oldest Nāth monastery still in use, is sometimes identified with this *Kadalī-deśa*. (Source: Mallinson 2011, pp. 5, 7, 16; Banglapedia, *Goraksanatha*) [regional]
  - *Variant — Ceylon / Nepal:* in one legend Matsyendra falls in love with the queen of **Ceylon**. Gorakṣa calls him to account, and Matsyendra leaves with their two sons, who become Pārasnāth and Nimnāth, "founders of the Jain religion" in Briggs's phrase. In a Nepalese version he leaves his body in Gorakṣa's care and enters a dead king's body, until Gorakṣa recalls him. (Source: Briggs 1938, p. 233) [folk]
- **Gorakṣanāth (Gorakhnāth)** — the chief **organizer** of the order and its reputed founder, the disciple of Matsyendra, and the reputed author or inspiration of the **Gorakṣa-śataka**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Gorakhnath*; Britannica; Briggs 1938)
  - *Variant on dating [disputed]:* historians place him somewhere in the first half of the 2nd millennium CE. Briggs gives the 11th–12th c., Abbott the 13th (from Bābā Farīd material and *Jñāneśvarī* manuscripts), and Grierson the 14th (from Gujarati evidence). (Source: Wikipedia, *Gorakhnath*)
    - Mallinson finds the first datable references in the **early 13th c.**: Vibhūticandra (born in north Bengal in the late 12th c.) and Harihara's Kannada *Ragaḷe*s. In the *Revaṇasiddheśvara Ragaḷe*, Revaṇa beats Gorakha in a magic contest.
    - Further references follow in the 1279 Kalleśvara inscription, a 1287 Somnāth inscription, the *Matsyendra-saṃhitā* and Abhayadatta's *Lives*. From these Mallinson places Gorakṣa in the Deccan in the **11th–12th c.**
    - He notes that, contrary to the hagiographies, the early accounts do not make him a celibate ascetic: the *Revaṇasiddheśvara Ragaḷe* has him living in Kolhapur with two wives.
    - (Source: Mallinson 2011, pp. 5–6)
    - The *Jñāneśvarī* (1290 CE) places him three teacher-generations before Jñāneśvar (Gorakṣa → Gahinī → Nivṛtti → Jñāneśvar). (Source: *Jñāneśvarī* 18.1755–1758) [traditional lineage]
  - *Variant on the haṭha link [disputed]:* Guy Beck holds that the connections between Gorakhnāth, the Kānphaṭas and haṭha-yoga are "beyond question". Wikipedia reports Mallinson as calling false the association of haṭha-yoga's *origins* with the Nāths, and as linking those origins instead with the Daśanāmī renouncers, the figure of Dattātreya and the Rāmānandīs. His encyclopedia entry still treats the *Vivekamārtaṇḍa* and *Gorakṣaśataka* as among the earliest haṭha works, attributed to Gorakṣa at or soon after their composition (13th c.). (Source: Wikipedia, *Gorakhnath*, "Hatha yoga"; Mallinson 2011, pp. 5, 16) [scholarly]
  - *Variant — Buddhist Gorakṣa:* in Abhayadatta's *Lives*, **Gorakṣa** is no. 9, the "Immortal Cowherd", linked to Mīnapa (no. 8) and Caurangī (no. 10). (Source: Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti, numbering as in the Himalayan Art Resources and Tsadra RyWiki Abhayadatta lists; epithet from Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*)
  - *Variant — birth (Navnāth Bhaktisār):* Matsyendra gives a childless brahmin woman, Sarasvatī of Candragiri in Bengal, a pinch of consecrated ash. She throws it onto a dung-heap, and twelve years later he calls Gorakṣa out of it. (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*, adhyāyas 1–2, 9) [regional]
- **Jālandharanāth (Jvālendra / Hāḍipā / Jālindar / Bālnāth)** — usually said to come from Jalandhar in the Punjab and to have travelled to Bengal. He is mentioned in texts from at least the 13th c., is often called Matsyendra's disciple, and is the guru of both **Kānhapā** and **Gopīcand**. He was the pre-eminent Nāth at Jogi Tilla and Jodhpur until about two hundred years ago. He appears in both the Nāth and the Buddhist Mahāsiddha lists. (Source: Mallinson 2011, p. 5; Wikipedia, *Jalandharnath*, *Mahasiddha*)
  - *Bengali cycle [regional]:* Banglapedia's **Haripa** (Hāḍipā, one of Jālandhara's names in Mallinson's list) is the guru of King **Gopīcandra**, son of Mainamati (Mayanāmatī); Banglapedia makes Haripa and Mainamati disciples of Gorakṣa. (Source: Banglapedia, *Goraksanatha*; Mallinson 2011, pp. 5–6)
  - *Navnāth Bhaktisār [regional]:* Antarikṣa Nārāyaṇa is born from the sacrificial fire of the Kuru king Bṛhadravā (adhyāya 11). He meets Mainavatī and teaches her (13). Her son King Gopīcand has him buried in a pit of horse-dung (14), and Kānifa frees him and humbles Gopīcand (16–17). (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*, adhyāyas 11–17)
- **Kāṇipā / Kṛṣṇapāda (Kāṇifnāth / Kāṇhapā)** — the disciple of Jālandhara and a major Maharashtrian Nāth, the Prabuddha-avatāra of the Bhaktisār. Scholars identify him with the Buddhist Mahāsiddha **Kāṇhapa (Kṛṣṇācārya)**, the "Dark Siddha" of Abhayadatta's list. (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār* table; Wikipedia, *Natha Sampradaya*, *Mahasiddha*) [scholarly identification]
  - *History [scholarly]:* Mallinson lists his names as Kānhu, Kaṇerī, Kṛṣṇapāda, Karṇaripā, Kāniph and Kānanīpā. Old Bengali songs by this siddha, in which he calls himself a **Kāpālika**, date to about the 10th c. In the early 15th-c. *Gorakṣa-vijaya* he accompanies Gorakṣa, but later he is made Jālandhar's disciple. His followers' panth, said to be Kāpālika in practice, stands outside the twelvefold scheme. (Source: Mallinson 2011, pp. 6, 10)
- **Caurangīnāth ("four-limbed")** — the prince whose hands and feet are cut off on a stepmother's false accusation, and who is then restored and perfected. The tellings differ and are kept apart:
  - *Jñāneśvarī (1290 CE):* Matsyendra met the limb-broken (*bhagnāvayava*) Caurangī at **Saptaśṛṅgī**, and he "became whole in every limb". (Source: *Jñāneśvarī* 18.1754)
  - *Abhayadatta (Buddhist, no. 10):* the son of a king. His young stepmother, rejected by him, accuses him falsely, and his arms and legs are cut off in the forest. On Mīnapa's direction the cowherd Gorakṣa finds him and tends him for twelve years, after which his limbs have grown back. (Source: Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti, Tibetan Tengyur version, as summarized by Tsering; number as in the Himalayan Art Resources and Tsadra RyWiki Abhayadatta lists) [Buddhist]
  - *Bengal (Mallinson's summary):* also called **Sāraṅgadhara**, he is the son of King **Devapāla of Bengal**. Here it is his *father* who has his arms and legs cut off and abandons him in a forest. **Matsyendra** rescues him and teaches him the yoga by which his limbs grow back. His principal shrine is at Sialkot. (Source: Mallinson 2011, p. 6) [scholarly summary of tradition]
  - *Navnāth Bhaktisār:* he is **Kṛṣṇāgar**, son of King Śaśāṅgar, wronged by his stepmother's lust. Matsyendra and Gorakṣa obtain him from the king, and he performs tapas. (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*, adhyāyas 30–31) [regional]
  - *Punjabi Pūraṇ Bhagat [folk]:* Pūraṇ of **Sialkot**, betrayed by his father's young queen Lūṇāṃ, loses his hands and feet and is cast into a dry well. Gorakhnāth rescues and heals him after twelve years, and Pūraṇ becomes a Yogī; through his boon Lūṇāṃ bears **Rasālū**. Briggs notes that Pūraṇ Bhagat "is called also… Chauranginath". (Source: Briggs 1938, ch. 9 "Legend", citing Temple, *Legends of the Panjab*, and Rose)
- **Gahinīnāth** — the Maharashtrian Nāth who was guru of **Nivṛttināth**, and so the spiritual grandfather of [`jnaneshwar.md`](jnaneshwar.md) and the channel through which the Nāth current feeds the **Vārkarī** movement. In the Bhaktisār he is the Karabhājana-avatāra, born from a mud figure that the young Gorakṣa shaped for village children while reciting the *sañjīvanī* mantra, and raised by the brahmin Madhunābha. (Source: *Jñāneśvarī* 18.1757–1758; *Navnāth Kathāsār* table and adhyāya 10)
- **Revaṇnāth, Carpaṭināth, Nāgnāth, Bhartṛnāth** — the remaining Maharashtrian masters who complete the nine:
  - **Revaṇa (Revaṇ-siddha)** — the Camasa-avatāra, born from Brahmā's seed fallen on the bank of the Revā. He revives the dead sons of a brahmin (adhyāyas 34–36). (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*) [regional]
    - *Kannada variant:* in Harihara's early 13th-c. *Revaṇasiddheśvara Ragaḷe*, Revaṇa defeats Gorakha in a magic contest. (Source: Mallinson 2011, p. 5)
  - **Nāganātha (Vaṭa-siddha)** — the Āvirhotra-avatāra, born of the nāginī Padminī, who had swallowed Brahmā's seed and, on the sage Āstika's advice, hid in the hollow of a banyan during Janamejaya's snake sacrifice (adhyāyas 36–37). (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*) [regional]
  - **Carpaṭi** — the Pippalāyana-avatāra, who lives with Nārada in Satyaloka (adhyāyas 38–39). He is also named in the *Haṭhayoga-pradīpikā* siddha list (HYP 1.6) and appears among the Buddhist 84 as Carbaripa. In the north he is remembered as an alchemist of the Chamba Himalaya, whose Hindi verses take the *avadhūta*'s line and shun the Nāth yogī's outward marks. (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*; HYP 1.6; Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*; Mallinson 2011, p. 6)
  - **Bhartarī / Bhartṛhari** — the Drumila / Dhruvamīna-avatāra; see the Bhartṛhari cycle below.

## The living devotional cycle — Navnāth Bhaktisār & the ballads

- **Navnāth Bhaktisār** — the popular **Marathi verse hagiography** of the nine Nāths by **Dhuṇḍisut Mālū Narahari**. Its closing ovīs date its completion to **Śaka 1741 (Pramāthī year), Jyeṣṭha śuddha pūrṇimā, i.e. 1819 CE**. It runs to **40 adhyāyas and c. 7,600 ovīs**, narrating the Nāths' births, initiations, miracles and wanderings. Mallinson groups it with the texts that presuppose an organized Nāth Sampradāya, which he dates from the 18th c. onward. The **Navnāth Kathāsār** is a separate, later Marathi prose summary of it. The Bhaktisār is still read devotionally as a *pothī* in Maharashtrian households. (Source: Marathi Wikipedia, *Navnāth Bhaktisār (grantha)*; Marathi Wikisource, *Navnāth Kathāsār*) [regional]
- **Bhartṛhari (Bhartṛnāth) cycle [folk]** — the **king of Ujjain** who renounces his throne to become a Nāth yogī. Different tellings make him the disciple of Gorakṣa or of Jālandhara, and he is often identified with the poet and grammarian of the same name [disputed]. He is the subject of a vast north-Indian **mendicant ballad** tradition sung by *Jogī*-bards, alongside the Rāṇī Piṅgalā songs. (Source: Mallinson 2011, p. 6; Bhartṛhari–Gopīcand ballads)
  - *North-Indian ballad version [folk]:* the trigger is the fruit of immortality and the unfaithful queen **Piṅgalā**, and the king renounces under Gorakṣa / Jālandhara. (Source: Bhartṛhari–Gopīcand ballad cycles)
  - *Navnāth Bhaktisār version [regional]:* Bhartarī is born of the Sun's seed, kept in the sage Kaulika's begging-bowl, and is taken in by King Vikrama as his brother. His wife Piṅgalā is *devoted* to him: a message sent to test her love falsely reports his death, and she dies of it. Bhartarī wastes away in grief for twelve years, until Dattātreya sends **Gorakṣa**, whose meeting ends his delusion and gives him the initiation of renunciation (*vairāgya-dīkṣā*). (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*, adhyāyas 24–29)
- **Gopīcand cycle [folk]** — the **Bengal/Rajasthan king** whose mother **Maināvatī** (Mayanāmatī) persuades him to renounce, and who is initiated by **Jālandhara/Hāḍipā**. The **Bhartṛhari–Gopīcand** ballads are the most widely sung Nāth epics, setting renunciation against royal and marital attachment. (Source: Nāth hagiography; Bhartṛhari–Gopīcand ballads; Banglapedia, *Goraksanatha*)
  - *Navnāth Bhaktisār version [regional]:* Gopīcand, son of King Trilocana, buries Jālandhar and is then humbled by Kānifa. He visits his sister's kingdom, where she dies and is revived by the guru's grace. (Source: *Navnāth Kathāsār*, adhyāyas 13–18)

## The 84 Mahāsiddhas — Caurāsī Siddha

- **Caurāsī Siddha ("the 84 perfected ones")** — the conventional roster of **eighty-four** tantric adepts. Later tradition credits them as fountainheads of **haṭha-yoga, rasāyana alchemy** and **Sahaja Tantra**. Both Hindu and Tibetan Buddhist traditions have a list of eighty-four, and the two lists overlap in part. (Source: Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti; Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*)
- **Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti** — Abhayadatta Śrī's **"Lives of the Eighty-Four Siddhas"**, the standard Buddhist enumeration of the 84, dated to the 11th or 12th c. by Wikipedia; Mallinson calls it a 13th-c. Tibetan account [disputed]. Each life has a biographical legend and a doctrinal song. Only the Tibetan translation, by sMon grub Shes rab, seems to survive, as the *Grub thob brgyad cu rtsa bzhi'i lo rgyus*. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*; Mallinson 2011, p. 3; Robinson, *Buddha's Lions*; Dowman, *Masters of Mahāmudrā*)
- **The Haṭhayoga-pradīpikā's mahāsiddhas [text]** — the Hindu counterpart roster is not a list of 84. Svātmārāma names about thirty *mahāsiddhas* who, by the power of haṭha-yoga, "break the staff of Time" and roam the universe. They are Ādinātha, Matsyendra, Śābara, Ānandabhairava, Cauraṅgī, Mīna, Gorakṣa, Virūpākṣa, Bileśaya, Manthāna, Bhairava, Siddhi, Buddha, Kanthaḍi, Koraṇṭaka, Surānanda, Siddhapāda, Carpaṭi, Kānerī, Pūjyapāda, Nityanātha, Nirañjana, Kapālī, Bindunātha, Kākacaṇḍīśvara, Allāma, Prabhudeva, Ghoḍā-colī, Ṭiṇṭiṇi, Bhānukī, Nāradeva, Khaṇḍa and Kāpālika. (Source: *Haṭhayoga-pradīpikā* 1.5–9, GRETIL)
- **Shared Hindu–Buddhist members [scholarly]** — figures who appear in both traditions include **Mīna/Mīnapa**, **Gorakṣa**, **Caurangī**, **Jālandhara (Jālandharipā)**, **Carpaṭi (Carbaripa)** and, by scholarly identification, **Kāṇha/Kṛṣṇācārya (= Kāṇipa)**. Mallinson notes that early siddha lists (Abhayadatta, the Maithili *Varṇaratnākara*, HYP 1.5–8) include Ādinātha, Matsyendra, Gorakṣa, Cauraṅgī, Jālandhara, Carpaṭa, Kānhapa and Bhartṛhari, all of whom recur in later lists of the nine Nāths; Jālandhara himself is not in the HYP list. He takes the siddhas' popularity to have transcended sectarian boundaries, and finds nothing in Nāth doctrine to support a Buddhist origin for the Nāths. (Source: Mallinson 2011, p. 3; HYP 1.5–8; Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti; Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*, *Natha Sampradaya*)
  - *Correction note:* **Saraha**, sometimes added to this shared list, is a Buddhist siddha and does not appear in the HYP list or in the Nāth Navnāth rosters above.
- **Lūipa** — conventionally the **first** of the 84 in Abhayadatta's order, the "Fish-Gut Eater". He is a royal renunciate who attains realization eating the fish-entrails thrown away by fishermen on the Ganges, and a foundational guru of the Cakrasaṃvara transmission. (Source: Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti; epithet from Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*)
- **Saraha** — the "Great Brahmin" Siddha, who takes up the life and craft of an arrow-maker. He is the author of the *Dohākoṣa* ("Treasury of Songs") and the fountainhead of **Mahāmudrā / Sahaja** poetry. His Apabhraṃśa *dohā* form is held to be echoed later by [Kabīr](kabir.md) [scholarly]. (Source: Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti; Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*)
- **Nāgārjuna (the Siddha)** — the "Philosopher and Alchemist" Nāgārjuna of the Siddha lists. He is a **tantric alchemist** and master of **rasāyana** and *amṛta*-making, distinguished in scholarship from the Madhyamaka philosopher. His disciple is **Nāgabodhi**. (Source: Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti; Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*)
- **Virūpa** — no. 3 in Abhayadatta's order, the Siddha who **stops the sun** so that he can keep drinking at a tavern. He is foundational to the Sakya **Lamdré**, and an emblem of antinomian power. (Source: Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti, numbering as in the Himalayan Art Resources and Tsadra RyWiki Abhayadatta lists; Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*)
- **Tilopa & Nāropa** — the Siddha Tilopa, traditionally from **Bengal**, and his disciple **Nāropa** (the Six Dharmas or Six Yogas of Nāropa). They are the human source of the **Kagyü** Mahāmudrā transmission to Marpa and Milarepa. (Source: Caturaśīti-siddha-pravṛtti; Vajrayāna Mahāsiddha tradition)
- *Variant on the number [scholarly]:* the "84" is a **typological** rather than an exact count. Different lists name overlapping but non-identical sets. Even among Tibetan lists, Lopez found only 34 names common to Abhayadatta's and Ratnākaragupta's lists, and von Schroeder about 36. Dowman treats 84 as a "whole" or "perfect" number. (Source: Wikipedia, *Mahasiddha*)
  - *Textual parallel [text]:* the Nāth yoga texts use 84 the same way. The *Gorakṣaśataka* says there are as many postures as there are species of living beings, one for each lakh of the 84 lakh, so that Śiva made "a hundred less sixteen", 84, seats (GorS 5–6; the GRETIL text reads *ṣoḍeśānaṃ*, usually read *ṣoḍaśonaṃ*). The *Haṭhayoga-pradīpikā* likewise speaks of "eighty-four āsanas taught by Śiva" (HYP 1.35). (Source: *Gorakṣaśataka* 5–6, Kuvalayananda–Shukla ed.; HYP 1.35, GRETIL)
