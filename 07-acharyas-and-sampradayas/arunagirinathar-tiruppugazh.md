# Arunagirinathar — the gopuram leap and the Tiruppugazh

> Parent: [Ācāryas & Sampradāyas](README.md) · Up to: [Master Index](../README.md) · Deity: [Gaṇeśa & Kārttikeya (Murugan)](../02-deities-and-cosmic-hierarchy/ganesha-and-kartikeya.md) · Setting: [Aruṇācala / Tiruvaṇṇāmalai](../06-sacred-geography/arunachala-tiruvannamalai-fire-hill.md) · Shrine-network: [Aṟupaḍai Vīḍu — the six war-camps of Murugan](../06-sacred-geography/arupadai-veedu-murugan.md)

**Aruṇagirinātar** (Tamil அருணகிரிநாதர்) is the great medieval poet-saint of **Murugan / Kārttikeya / Skanda**, remembered as the composer of the **Tiruppugazh** ("Holy Praise"), the most rhythmically intricate hymn-corpus in Tamil. His life is not a canonical Purāṇic episode but a **hagiographic tradition** that crystallised around the Tiruppugazh songs and their companion works (*Kandar Anubhūti*, *Kandar Alaṅkāram*), transmitted through Tiruvaṇṇāmalai temple lore and the later printed *vāḻkkai varalāṟu* (life-accounts). Its defining scene is a **suicide-leap from the temple gopuram** that Murugan interrupts mid-fall — turning a dissolute wastrel into the god's own singer. He belongs to the Śaiva-Kaumāra devotional world of Tamil Nadu, adjacent to but distinct from the [63 Nāyaṉārs](alvars-and-nayanars.md#the-sixty-three-nāyaṉārs-shaiva) and the [Nālvar](the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md).

**Dating note (two clocks).** In the scriptural frame this sits deep in **Kali Yuga** (begun 3102 BCE), Murugan being the eternal war-god of the [Sūrapadma war](../04-deep-dives/surapadma-samharam-kanda-puranam.md). In the academic frame, Aruṇagirinātar is placed **[scholarly] c. 15th c. CE**, active at **Tiruvaṇṇāmalai** under a Vijayanagara-era king named in tradition as **Prabhuḍa Deva Rāya (Prauḍha Devarāya)**; the precise reign and century are **[disputed]**, since the life is reconstructed from hagiography, not dated records.

## The dissolute youth and the gopuram leap

- **Birth and ruin** — born at **Tiruvaṇṇāmalai** (the town at the foot of the fire-hill [Aruṇācala](../06-sacred-geography/arunachala-tiruvannamalai-fire-hill.md)); his father died early and he was raised by his mother and elder sister **Adhi (Aḍi)**. Squandering the family's means, he fell into a life of dissipation with courtesans (*tēvaraḍiyār*), exhausted his sister's wealth, and contracted a wasting venereal disease that left him diseased and destitute. (Source: Aruṇagirinātar hagiographic tradition, 15th c.) [folk] [hagiography]
  - *Variant (the sister's devotion) [folk]:* in the best-known telling his sister sold or pledged everything to fund his vices and never reproached him; the turning point comes when, penniless, he returns to her one last time and she — refusing him nothing — offers her own body, shattering him into shame. Other tellings soften this to her simply having nothing left to give. (Source: hagiographic tradition)
- **The leap from the tower** — consumed by self-disgust, he climbed the north **gopuram** (soaring gateway-tower) of the **Aruṇācaleśvarar / Aṇṇāmalaiyār temple** and **flung himself off** to end his life. (Source: hagiographic tradition; Tiruppugazh corpus) [folk]
  - *Variant (which tower) [regional]:* tradition variously names the **Vallāla Mahārāja gopuram** on the north or the great east **Rājagopuram**; the shrine that marks the spot inside the temple fixes it by cult memory, not record.
- **Murugan catches him** — before he struck the ground **Murugan intervened**, holding him up / arresting the fall, and appeared to him in grace. The god not only spared his life but **cured his disease** and claimed him as his singer. (Source: hagiographic tradition) [folk] [hagiography]

## "Muttai taru" — the Vel on the tongue

- **The Vel writes on his tongue** — Murugan **inscribed the seed of song on Aruṇagiri's tongue with the tip of his *Vēl*** (the divine spear), granting him instant, inexhaustible poetic power (*kavitā-śakti*). The tradition holds that the god gave him the **opening words "Muttait taru" (முத்தைத்தரு)** — "grant me the pearl…" — and commanded him to **sing**, whereupon the entire first Tiruppugazh, *"Muttait taru patti tiru nakai…"*, poured out. (Source: Tiruppugazh, opening hymn; hagiographic tradition) [folk]
  - *Note on the incipit [scholarly]:* the received corpus is conventionally headed by the Tiruvaṇṇāmalai song beginning *"Muttait taru"*, addressed to Murugan of the fire-hill — which is why the tradition attaches the god's first-written words to that line specifically.
- **"Summā iru" — the command to be still** — a second, mystical strand of the same encounter holds that when Aruṇagiri, overwhelmed, tried to speak, Murugan silenced him with **"Summā iru"** ("Be still / remain in silence, beyond words"). This wordless initiation is memorialised in the twelfth verse of the **Kandar Anubhūti** — *"Summā iru, col aṟa"* ("'Be still, beyond speech' — when You said that, oh!, I understood nothing at all") — reading the leap as a *jñāna*-initiation into silence, not merely a gift of eloquence. (Source: *Kandar Anubhūti*, v. 12) [scholarly]

## The Tiruppugazh and the companion works

- **Tiruppugazh (திருப்புகழ், "Holy Praise")** — thousands of hymns to Murugan sung across his shrines, above all the [six war-camps (Aṟupaḍai Vīḍu)](../06-sacred-geography/arupadai-veedu-murugan.md) and Tiruvaṇṇāmalai. Its signature is **chandam** — dense, drum-like **rhythmic meter** (each song set to an intricate *tāḷa* pattern), making the Tiruppugazh unique among Tamil devotional poetry for its musical-metrical complexity rather than melodic *paṇ* alone. (Source: *Tiruppugazh* [Aruṇagirinātar]) [scholarly]
  - *Note on numbers [scholarly]:* tradition credits **~16,000 songs**; roughly **1,300–1,365 survive** (counts vary by edition). As with the [Tēvāram survival figures](the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md), the large total is a claim of original output, the smaller a count of what came down.
- **Kandar Anubhūti (கந்தர் அனுபூதி, "Experience of Skanda")** — a tight **51-verse** mystical poem of direct God-experience, widely recited as a protective / obstacle-removing hymn; regarded as the distilled core of his realisation. (Source: *Kandar Anubhūti* [Aruṇagirinātar]) [scholarly]
- **Kandar Alaṅkāram (கந்தர் அலங்காரம், "Ornament of Skanda")** — an ornamented praise-poem (traditionally ~**54 verses**); certain verses are used in **Kaumāra prognostic / mantric practice**, read for their oracular force. (Source: *Kandar Alaṅkāram* [Aruṇagirinātar]) [folk]
- **Further works** — *Kandar Antāti*, *Vēl Viruttam*, *Mayil Viruttam*, *Sēval Viruttam* (hymns to the **Vel** spear, the **peacock** mount, and the **rooster** banner of Murugan), *Tiru Eḻukūṟṟirukkai*, and the *Tiruvaguppu* — together forming the pan-Kaumāra bhakti layer laid over the older Tamil Murugan cult. (Source: Aruṇagirinātar corpus) [scholarly]

## The contest with Sambandāṇḍāṉ and the parrot errand

The best-known episode of the hagiography unfolds at the **court of King Prabhuḍa Deva Rāya** at Tiruvaṇṇāmalai, and explains why Aruṇagirinātar's temple icon is a **parrot**.

- **The rival Sambandāṇḍāṉ** — a court poet and **Śākta / goddess-worshipper** (a devotee of Kālī / the Goddess), Sambandāṇḍāṉ (also spelt *Sambandhāṇḍār / Sambandandan*) grew jealous of Aruṇagiri's standing and challenged him to a **contest of deities** — each to prove his god supreme by summoning divine manifestation. (Source: hagiographic tradition) [folk]
  - *Disambiguation [scholarly]:* this Śākta rival is not the child-saint **Tirugñāna Sambandar** of the [Nālvar](the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md), nor the poet **Villiputtūrār** with whom later folklore sometimes conflates Aruṇagiri's contests. The names are distinct despite the surface echo of "Sambanda-."
- **The deity-summoning** — Sambandāṇḍāṉ invoked his Goddess but she did not fully manifest to the court; **Aruṇagirinātar sang the Tiruppugazh** and **Murugan appeared** — in some tellings blazing forth so that only the god's radiance could be borne. Humiliated, the rival contrived a deadlier test. (Source: hagiographic tradition) [folk]
- **The errand for the Pārijāta** — Aruṇagiri was challenged to fetch the **Pārijāta / Kalpaka flower** from the celestial garden (the wish-tree of [Indra's heaven, of Samudra-manthana fame](../04-deep-dives/parijata-harana.md)) as living proof of his god's grace. To reach Devaloka he **left his own body** by yogic power and **transmigrated his soul into the body of a parrot (kili)**, flying up to bring the flower. (Source: hagiographic tradition) [folk] [hagiography]
  - *Variant (the blind king) [folk] [disputed]:* one strand makes the errand a cure rather than a mere proof — the flower (or Murugan's grace obtained through it) was sought to **restore sight to the blind king / a blinded royal**, the impossible task set precisely so that Aruṇagiri would fail. Other tellings keep it purely a contest of supremacy with no cure attached; the corpus preserves both.
- **The treachery and the trapped soul** — while his human body lay lifeless and untended, **Sambandāṇḍāṉ had it cremated / destroyed** (in some tellings the king was deceived into ordering it). Returning with the Pārijāta, Aruṇagiri found **no body to return to** and so **remained in the parrot's form** — his soul locked in the bird. (Source: hagiographic tradition) [folk]
- **Why his icon is a parrot** — for this reason the saint is enshrined and depicted at Tiruvaṇṇāmalai **as a green parrot** (and elsewhere as a man with a parrot), the bird-body being the form his devotion left him in. His shrine sits within the **Aruṇācaleśvarar temple**, by the pillared hall associated with his singing. (Source: hagiographic tradition; temple cult) [regional] [folk]
  - *Note [scholarly]:* the parrot icon is the **material trace** of the legend — the hagiography exists to explain a cult image already venerated in the temple, a common pattern in Tamil *sthala* tradition where narrative follows icon.

## Place in the tradition

- **A Kaumāra bhakti apex** — where the [Nālvar](the-nalvar-tamil-shaiva-saints.md) and [Ālvārs / Nāyaṉārs](alvars-and-nayanars.md) hymn Śiva and Viṣṇu across the [sung shrines](../06-sacred-geography/275-paadal-petra-sthalams.md), Aruṇagirinātar is the supreme singer of **Murugan**, extending the Tamil war-god's cult — rooted in the [Aṟupaḍai Vīḍu](../06-sacred-geography/arupadai-veedu-murugan.md) and the [Sūrapadma-Kanda-Purāṇam war-cycle](../04-deep-dives/surapadma-samharam-kanda-puranam.md) — into a full rhythmic-devotional literature. (Source: *Tiruppugazh*) [scholarly]
- **Living recitation** — the Tiruppugazh is sung daily in Murugan temples across Tamil Nadu and the diaspora, its *chandam* meters chanted to *tāḷa*; the *Kandar Anubhūti* is recited as a protective hymn. Aruṇagiri stands to Tamil **Kaumāra** worship much as the Tēvāram poets stand to Śaivism. (Source: contemporary Murugan cult) [regional]
- **Two clocks, held apart** — the human poet is a **[scholarly] 15th-c.** figure of documented South India; the god who catches him, writes with the Vel, and manifests in court belongs to the timeless Kaumāra sacred order. The hagiography deliberately joins them at the gopuram's edge; the corpus records both frames without collapsing either.
