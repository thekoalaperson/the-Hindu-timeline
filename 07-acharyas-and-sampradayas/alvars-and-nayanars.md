# The Ālvārs & Nāyaṉārs — Tamil Bhakti Saints

*Tamil Nadu, c. 6th–9th c. CE — the southern origin of the Bhakti movement.*
*Sits under [07-acharyas-and-sampradayas](./README.md). Literary corpus cross-reference: [../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md](../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md).*

The Ālvārs (Vaishnava, "those immersed in God") and Nāyaṉārs (Shaiva, "hounds/leaders of Śiva") were two parallel waves of Tamil saint-poets who turned ritual Vedic religion into emotional, vernacular, temple-centred devotion (*bhakti*) directed at Viṣṇu and Śiva respectively. Their hymns — the **Nālāyira Divya Prabandham** (Vaishnava) and the **Tirumuṟai** including the **Tēvāram** (Shaiva) — became the "Tamil Veda" and seeded the pan-Indian Bhakti movement of later centuries [scholarly]. **Dating note:** academic consensus places them c. 6th–9th c. CE; traditional Vaishnava chronology pushes the Ālvārs into the Dvāpara/Kali yugas (millennia BCE) [traditional] [disputed].

---

## The Twelve Ālvārs (Vaishnava)

Canonical set of twelve Viṣṇu-devotees whose 4,000 verses form the Nālāyira Divya Prabandham; they sang the 108 *divya deśams* (sacred Viṣṇu shrines). Compiled and canonized by **Nāthamuni** (c. 824–924 CE), who recovered the hymns and set them to music, calling them *Drāviḍa Veda* (Source: [Naalayira Divya Prabandham — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naalayira_Divya_Prabandham)).

- **Poigai Āḻvār** - first of the *Mudhal* ("first three") Ālvārs; tradition makes the trio contemporaries born on successive days. Composed *Mudhal Tiruvandhādhi* (100 verses). Traditionally placed in the Dvāpara Yuga [traditional]; academically c. 6th–7th c. CE [scholarly]. (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars))
- **Bhūtath Āḻvār** (Pūtattāḻvār) - second *Mudhal* Ālvār; *Iraṇḍām Tiruvandhādhi* (100 verses). (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars))
- **Pey Āḻvār** - third *Mudhal* Ālvār; *Mūnṟām Tiruvandhādhi* (100 verses), said to have first "seen" Lakṣmī beside Viṣṇu. (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars))
- **Tirumaḻiśai Āḻvār** - Kanchipuram saint of disputed (reputedly part-sage) birth; *Nānmukan Tiruvandhādhi* and *Tiruchanda Viruttam*. (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars))
- **Nammāḻvār** (Caṭakōpaṉ / Māṟaṉ) - foremost and most prolific Ālvār; born Tirukkuṟuhūr (Āḻvārtirunagari). Author of *Tiruvāymoḻi*, *Tiruviruttam*, *Tiruvāsiriyam*, *Periya Tiruvandhādhi* — together **1,352 of the 4,000 verses**, the theological core later expounded by Śrīvaiṣṇava ācāryas. Revered as *Prapanna-jana-kūṭastha* (head of the surrendered). (Source: [Nammalvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nammalvar))
- **Madhurakavi Āḻvār** - disciple of Nammāḻvār rather than a direct Viṣṇu-devotee; his *Kaṇṇinuṇ Siṟuttāmbu* (11 verses) praises his *guru* — an early *ācārya-bhakti* model. (Source: [Naalayira Divya Prabandham — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naalayira_Divya_Prabandham))
- **Kulaśekhara Āḻvār** - Chera king-saint; *Perumāḷ Tirumoḻi*, marked by intense *dāsya/vātsalya* (servant/parental) devotion. Traditionally identified with a Chera ruler [traditional] [disputed]. (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars))
- **Periyāḻvār** (Viṣṇucittar) - Brahmin of Srivilliputhur, c. 8th–9th c. CE; famed for *vātsalya bhakti* (loving Viṣṇu as the child Kṛṣṇa) and his *Tiruppallāṇḍu* (benediction) opening the Prabandham. Foster-father of Āṇḍāḷ. (Source: [Periyalvar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periyalvar))
- **Āṇḍāḷ** (Kōdai / Goda) - the **only female Ālvār**, foster-daughter of Periyāḻvār; c. 9th c. CE. Author of *Tiruppāvai* (30 verses, sung in Mārgaḻi) and *Nācciyār Tirumoḻi*; her *bridal mysticism* (*madhura bhakti*) made her the model bride of Viṣṇu, worshipped as a form of Bhū-devī. (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars))
- **Toṇḍaraḍippoḍi Āḻvār** (Vipra Nārāyaṇa) - "dust of the feet of the Lord's servants"; *Tirumālai* and *Tiruppaḷḷiyeḻucci* (Srirangam wake-up hymn). (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars))
- **Tiruppāṇ Āḻvār** - saint of low (Pāṇar bard) birth carried into Srirangam sanctum; sole work *Amalanādipirāṉ* (10 verses), a head-to-foot vision of Raṅganātha. Emblem of bhakti transcending caste [scholarly]. (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars))
- **Tirumaṅgai Āḻvār** (Parakāla / Kaliyan) - chieftain-turned-saint, latest and second-most prolific (~1,253 verses); *Periya Tirumoḻi*, *Tirukkuṟuntāṇḍakam* and others; credited with funding Srirangam temple works. (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars))

### Ālvār variants
- *Variant (academic vs traditional dating):* Wikipedia/Britannica place the Ālvārs c. 5th–10th c. CE (Britannica: 7th–10th c.); Vaishnava tradition assigns legendary dates ranging from **4203 BCE to 758 BCE**, locating the first Ālvārs in the Dvāpara Yuga (Source: [Alvars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvars)). [disputed]
- *Variant (count of "ten"):* some older listings count **ten Ālvārs**, treating Āṇḍāḷ and Madhurakavi as appended/honorary (Madhurakavi being a *guru*-devotee, Āṇḍāḷ a goddess) rather than "core" hymnists. [folk]
- *Variant (compiler):* the Prabandham's recovery and 9th–10th c. compilation is traditionally credited to **Nāthamuni**; the present canonical arrangement and temple recitation were later fixed by Rāmānuja-era ācāryas (Source: [Naalayira Divya Prabandham — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naalayira_Divya_Prabandham)).
- *Variant (Nāthamuni's source):* legend holds Nāthamuni recovered the lost 4,000 by reciting Madhurakavi's 11 verses 12,000 times until Nammāḻvār revealed the whole corpus — a traditional, non-historical account [traditional] [folk]. (Source: [Naalayira Divya Prabandham — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naalayira_Divya_Prabandham))

## The Sixty-Three Nāyaṉārs (Shaiva)

Canonical body of 63 Śiva-devotees from all castes and walks of life (kings, washermen, hunters, weavers, a child, women), whose hymns and hagiographies form the heart of Shaiva Siddhānta. Their stories were versified by **Cēkkiḻār** in the *Periya Purāṇam*. The roster was fixed in stages: **Cuntarar (Sundarar)** named the saints in his *Tiruttoṇḍa Tohai* (11 verses, up to Karaikkāl Ammaiyār); **Nambiyāṇḍār Nambi** expanded this into the *Tiruttoṇḍar Tiruvandhādhi* (89 verses) c. 10th c.; with Cuntarar and his parents added, the count became the canonical 63 (Source: [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars)).

### The Nālvar — the four foremost
- **Tirugñāna Sambandar** (Campantar) - child-prodigy saint of Sirkazhi, said to have been fed divine milk by Pārvatī; 7th c. CE (Pallava era). His hymns fill **Tirumuṟai Books 1–3** (~383 hymns) and led the Pandya king back to Śaivism from Jainism. (Source: [Tevaram — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevaram))
- **Appar** (Tirunāvukkarasar / "king of speech") - 7th c. CE; born Vaḷḷalār, converted to Jainism then back to Śaivism, surviving ordeals (poison, an elephant, the sea). Hymns fill **Books 4–6** (~312 hymns); coined the *Tirunīṟu* (sacred-ash) devotion and the *paṇ* musical tradition. (Source: [Appar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appar))
- **Sundarar** (Cuntaramūrti / Nampi Ārūraṉ) - 8th c. CE; "comrade" of Śiva who acted as His messenger. Hymns form **Book 7** (~100 hymns) and the foundational *Tiruttoṇḍa Tohai* listing the saints. (Source: [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars))
- **Māṇikkavācakar** - minister-turned-saint of Madurai, c. 9th c. CE; author of *Tiruvācakam* ("sacred utterance") and *Tirukkōvaiyār*, forming **Book 8** of the Tirumuṟai (Tiruvācakam = 51 compositions). Famed for raw emotional intensity: "He whose heart does not melt at the Tiruvāsagam cannot be melted by any *āgama*." (Source: [Manikkavacakar — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manikkavacakar))

### Other notable Nāyaṉārs
- **Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār** - one of three women among the 63; earliest Nāyaṉār, c. 6th c. CE. Renounced beauty to become a skeletal *pēy* (ghoul) attendant at Śiva's dance; ~143 verses (incl. *Aṟputa Tiruvandhādhi*) compiled in **Tirumuṟai Book 11**. (Source: [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai))
- **Tirumūlar** - yogi-saint, author of the **Tirumandiram** (~3,000 verses), the core text of Tamil Shaiva Siddhānta philosophy and yoga, forming **Tirumuṟai Book 10**. Dating disputed (traditionally very early; academically c. 7th–8th c. or later). (Source: [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai)) [disputed]
- **Nandanar** (Tirunāḷaippōvār) - Pulaiyar (untouchable) labourer-saint who entered the Chidambaram sanctum; emblem of bhakti dissolving caste barriers. (Source: [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars)) [scholarly]
- **Kaṇṇappar** - tribal hunter who offered his own eyes to Śiva; archetype of devotion over ritual purity. (Source: [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars))
- **Cēramāṉ Perumāḷ Nāyaṉār** - Chera king-saint and friend of Sundarar; some of his hymns appear in **Book 11**. (Source: [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai))

### Nāyaṉār / Tirumuṟai variants
- *Variant (canon assembly):* the scattered Tēvāram hymns were rediscovered (legend: walled-up palm leaves at Chidambaram) and organized into **Tirumuṟai Books 1–7** by **Nambiyāṇḍār Nambi** under Chola king Rājarāja I; **Cēkkiḻār's Periya Purāṇam (c. 1135 CE)** was added as **Book 12**, completing the 12-volume canon (Source: [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai)).
- *Variant (Books 8–11 contents):* Book 8 = Māṇikkavācakar; Book 9 = *Tiruvisaippā/Tiruppallāṇḍu* (nine later poets); Book 10 = Tirumūlar's *Tirumandiram*; Book 11 = miscellany incl. Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār and Cēramāṉ Perumāḷ (Source: [Tirumurai — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirumurai)).
- *Variant (64th saint):* **Tiruvaḷḷuvar** (author of the *Tirukkuṟaḷ*) is added as a **64th** figure in the annual Mylapore procession of the Nāyaṉārs since c. 1905 — a modern regional addition, not part of the universal 63 (Source: [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars)). [late text] [folk]
- *Variant (the "9 + 63"):* some traditions distinguish the 63 individual saints from collective groups (e.g. the *toṇḍar* / nine devotee-bands) also honoured in Sundarar's list; counts of named saints vs honoured groups therefore differ. (Source: [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars)) [folk]

## Significance & legacy

- **Tamil origin of Bhakti** - the Ālvārs and Nāyaṉārs (6th–9th c.) pioneered vernacular, emotional, temple-based devotion that later spread north, fueling the medieval Bhakti movement across India [scholarly]. (Source: [Nayanars — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayanars))
- **Caste transcendence** - both corpora canonize low-caste and female saints (Tiruppāṇ, Nandanar, Kaṇṇappar; Āṇḍāḷ, Kāraikkāl Ammaiyār), asserting that devotion outranks birth.
- **Scripture status** - the Prabandham ("Tamil Veda") and Tirumuṟai are recited in temple liturgy to this day; they ground the **Śrīvaiṣṇava** (via Nāthamuni → Yāmuna → Rāmānuja) and **Shaiva Siddhānta** theological traditions respectively. See [../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md](../90-literature-corpus/tamil-canon-sangam-and-bhakti.md).
