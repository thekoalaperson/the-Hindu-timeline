# Samarth Rāmdās — Founder of the Rāmdāsī Sampradāya

> Detail node under [`07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/`](./README.md) (master index: [`../README.md`](../README.md)). The 17th-c. Maharashtra **saint-philosopher** (1608–1681), the major **non-Vārkarī** Marathi *sant* — the activist, *dharma*-and-statecraft counterpart to the devotional Viṭhobā stream of [Tukārām](tukaram.md).
> Set him against the Vārkarī roster in [`north-bhakti-sants.md`](north-bhakti-sants.md); his pilgrimage geography and his seat at Sajjangad fold into [`../06-sacred-geography/README.md`](../06-sacred-geography/README.md). Two clocks: this is **academic / historical** (Kali-historical) dating, not Purāṇic yuga-time.

## Who He Was

- **Samarth Rāmdās (born Nārāyaṇ Sūryājipant Ṭhosar; *Rāmdās Svāmī*, *Samarth*)** — Marathi **Rāma-and-Hanumān devotee, poet, and organizer-philosopher**, founder of the **Rāmdāsī Sampradāya**, who fused intense personal *bhakti* with an unusual programme of physical fitness, civic organization, and *kṣātra* (martial/political) energy — what later writers crystallized as **"Mahārāṣṭra dharma."** (Source: Wikipedia, *Samarth Ramdas*)
  - *Distinctive stance [scholarly]:* unlike the quietist, surrender-centred Vārkarī *sants*, Rāmdās preached an **activist devotion** — strength, alertness, discipline, and worldly competence as religious duty, the *iṣṭa-devatā* being not the passive Viṭhobā but the warrior **Rāma** and the strongman **Hanumān (Mārutī)**. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
  - *Name [tradition]:* the boyhood name **Nārāyaṇ** was replaced by **Rāmdās** ("servant of Rāma") after his Rāma-*mantra* initiation; "**Samarth**" ("the capable / the competent") is an honorific reflecting his self-reliance teaching. (Source: Wikipedia)

## Dating — the Two Clocks [scholarly]

- **Lifespan c. 1608–1681 CE** — birth conventionally on **Rāma Navamī, Śaka 1530 (1608 CE)**, *mahāsamādhi* on **Māgha Vadya Navamī, Śaka 1603 (1681 CE)** at Sajjangad — a near-exact contemporary of [Tukārām](tukaram.md) (c. 1608–1650) and of Chhatrapati **Śivājī** (1630–1680). (Source: Wikipedia, *Samarth Ramdas*; Hindupedia)
  - *Note:* the death year **1681** is well anchored; both the Vārkarī apex and the Rāmdāsī activist stream thus crest in the **same 17th-c. Maharashtra moment**, under the same rising Maratha polity — but as two contrasting religious idioms.

## Life Arc

- **Birth at Jāmb** — born **Nārāyaṇ** in the village of **Jāmb** (Jāmba) in present-day **Jālnā district**, Marathwada, on the **Godāvarī** belt, into a **Deśastha Brahmin** family of Sūryopāsaka (sun-worshipping) tradition; father **Sūryājipant Ṭhosar**, mother **Rāṇūbāī**; an elder brother **Gaṅgādhar (Śreṣṭha)**. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
- **The runaway bridegroom** — the founding legend: at his **wedding (c. age 12)**, on hearing the priest's "*sāvadhān!*" ("be alert / beware!"), young Nārāyaṇ **bolted from the marriage mandap** and renounced household life on the spot — the word *sāvadhān* becoming a cipher for his lifelong watchful, world-renouncing alertness. [folk] (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
- **Twelve years of austerity at Ṭākaḷī** — he settled at **Ṭākaḷī** on the **Godāvarī** near **Nāśik / Pañcavaṭī** (the Rāmāyaṇa's Daṇḍaka country) and practised **twelve years (c. 1620–1632) of severe *tapas***: standing in the river reciting the **Gāyatrī** and the **thirteen-syllable Rāma *mantra* ("Śrī Rām Jaya Rām Jaya Jaya Rām")** said to **130 million (13 crore) times**, with minimal food, plus study of the Rāmāyaṇa and Vedānta. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
  - *Vision [folk]:* tradition holds that **Rāma (with Hanumān) granted him *darśana*** at the close of this *sādhanā*, confirming his mission and his Hanumān-devotion. (Source: Hindupedia)
- **All-India pilgrimage (c. 1632–1644)** — for roughly **twelve years** he wandered the whole subcontinent on foot — Himālaya to Rāmeśvaram, including **Kāśī, the Gangetic plain, and the Rāma/Hanumān *tīrthas*** — surveying a society he judged demoralized under Islamic-Sultanate political pressure, which sharpened his conviction that *dharma* needed **physical strength and organization**, not devotion alone. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
- **The Maruti temple campaign** — returning to the Kṛṣṇā valley, he began **installing Hanumān (Mārutī) images and founding *maṭhs* (mission-centres)**, traditionally counted as **eleven principal Mārutī temples (Akrā Mārutī)** plus a wider network across Maharashtra — gymnasium-shrines meant to build bodily vigour and martial readiness alongside worship. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
- **Seat at Sajjangad** — his final headquarters was **Sajjangad** ("fort of the good"), a hill-fort near **Sātārā** granted for his use; he made it the **mother-*maṭh* of the Rāmdāsī order**, established the **Rāma temple** there, and died there in **1681**; Sajjangad remains the order's principal pilgrimage seat (folds into [`../06-sacred-geography/README.md`](../06-sacred-geography/README.md)). (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
- **Disciples** — chief among them **Kalyāṇ Svāmī** (his foremost disciple and scribe of much of the *Dāsbodh*), and the woman ascetic **Veṇābāī** (Veṇāstrī), an authority in his line — the order admitting both *brahmacārī* and householder followers. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)

## Founding the Rāmdāsī Sampradāya

- **The Rāmdāsī (Rāmadāsī) Sampradāya** — a **non-Vārkarī Marathi order** centred on **Rāma as *iṣṭa-devatā* and Hanumān as ideal devotee-strongman**, propagating a synthesis of **practical Advaita Vedānta, daily discipline, and civic activism**; its *maṭh* network doubled as nodes of social and (in tradition) political mobilization. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
  - *Contrast with the Vārkarī stream:* where the Pandharpur Vārkarī cult (see [`north-bhakti-sants.md`](north-bhakti-sants.md)) is egalitarian, *kīrtan*-centred, surrender-focused and politically quietist, the Rāmdāsī line is **Rāma-centred, strength-and-statecraft focused, and explicitly engaged with worldly power** — the two great Maharashtra devotional idioms of the century. (Source: Wikipedia; scholarly consensus)
- **"Mahārāṣṭra dharma" and *swarājya*** — Rāmdās is the *sant* most associated with articulating a **collective, this-worldly *dharma* of the Marathi people** — civilizational self-respect, defence of *dharma*, and self-rule (***swarājya***) — a current that later nationalist writers (notably in the colonial era) read as the spiritual charter of the Maratha resurgence. [disputed — see below] (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)

## The Śivājī Association [disputed]

- **Rāmdās as Śivājī's guru** — **traditional Rāmdāsī and later popular accounts** make Samarth Rāmdās the **spiritual preceptor (*rājguru*) of Chhatrapati Śivājī**, inspiring his *Hindavī swarājya*; the legend has Śivājī offering his whole kingdom to the guru, who returns it to be ruled in trust, and granting Sajjangad to him. (Source: Hindupedia; Rāmdāsī hagiography)
  - *Variant (skeptical / academic) [scholarly]:* most modern historians regard the **guru–disciple relationship as late, weakly evidenced, and probably exaggerated** — contact, if any, came **only near the end of Śivājī's reign (c. 1670s)**; Śivājī's better-documented spiritual ties run elsewhere, and the strong Rāmdās–Śivājī narrative owes much to **19th–20th c. nationalist reconstruction**. Catalog both; do not collapse. (Source: scholarly consensus; Wikipedia)
  - *Note:* whichever way the historicity falls, the **association itself** — true or constructed — is what made Rāmdās the emblematic *sant* of *kṣātra*-dharma and Maratha self-rule in modern memory.

## The Works

- **Dāsbodh (Dāsabodha)** — his **magnum opus**: a long Marathi treatise in *ovī* metre cast as a **guru–disciple dialogue**, dictated (tradition says) to the disciple **Kalyāṇ** in the **Śivthar Ghaḷ** cave; structured in **20 *daśakas* (decades) × 10 *samāsas* = 200 chapters**, expounding **practical Advaita Vedānta together with worldly competence** — leadership, organization, vigilance, eloquence, statecraft, and the duties of an effective person ("*uttam puruṣa*"). It is the doctrinal core of the order. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dasbodh*; Hindupedia)
  - *Distinctive [scholarly]:* the *Dāsbodh* is almost unique among *sant* texts in **devoting whole chapters to practical management and statecraft** — a manual of *karma-yoga*-as-administration, not only metaphysics.
- **Manāche Ślok / Manobodh ("Verses to the Mind")** — a set of **205 short Marathi verses in *bhujaṅgaprayāta* metre** addressed **to one's own mind (*man*)**, counselling moral discipline, truthfulness, Rāma-remembrance, and renunciation of base impulses; among the most-memorized and daily-recited Marathi devotional texts. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
- **Bhīm-rūpī Mārutī Stotra** — his celebrated Marathi **Hanumān hymn** ("*Bhīmarūpī mahārudrā...*"), a vivid praise of Hanumān as a fierce, gigantic, protective strongman — recited across Maharashtra and emblematic of the order's Hanumān-as-strength theology; one of **several Mārutī stotras** he composed for the temple network. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)
- **Other works** — assorted *abhaṅgas*, *karuṇāṣṭakas* (verses of supplication), *ārtīs*, and a **Marathi rendering of the Rāmāyaṇa material** are attributed to him; the authentic boundary of the corpus is, as with most *sant* canons, an open philological question. (Source: Wikipedia; Hindupedia)

## Hagiography & Afterlife

- **Mahīpati's *Bhaktalīlāmṛta*** — the 18th-c. Marathi hagiographer **Mahīpati** (1715–1790), author of the *Bhaktavijaya* and *Bhaktalīlāmṛta*, records the **life and miracles of Rāmdās** within his great compendia of *sant* biography — a principal (and devotional, not critical) source for the traditional life. [late text] (Source: Wikipedia, *Mahipati*; Rāmdāsī hagiography)
- **Modern reception** — Rāmdās became a **touchstone of Maharashtrian and Hindu-nationalist thought** (B. G. Tilak and later writers invoking "Mahārāṣṭra dharma"), even as academic historians pared back the Śivājī-guru claim; the *Dāsbodh* and *Manāche Ślok* remain living texts in Marathi households, and **Sajjangad** an active pilgrimage centre. (Source: Wikipedia; scholarly literature)
