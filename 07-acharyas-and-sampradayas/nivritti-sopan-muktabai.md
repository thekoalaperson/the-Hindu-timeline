# Nivṛttināth, Sopāndev & Muktābāī — the Jñāneśvar Sibling-Saints

> Detail node under [`07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/`](./README.md) (master index: [`../README.md`](../README.md)). The **three other prodigy siblings** of the founding **Vārkarī** household of Āḷandī — the eldest brother and *guru* **Nivṛttināth**, the third brother **Sopāndev**, and the youngest, the woman-saint **Muktābāī** — each a venerated sant and *abhaṅga*-poet in their own right, not merely the supporting cast of [`jnaneshwar.md`](jnaneshwar.md).
> Frame against the founder-philosopher in [`jnaneshwar.md`](jnaneshwar.md), the yogic order in [`nath-sampradaya-and-the-siddhas.md`](nath-sampradaya-and-the-siddhas.md), the pilgrimage-companion in [`namdev.md`](namdev.md), and the later woman-sant in [`bahinabai.md`](bahinabai.md). Two clocks: this is **academic / historical** dating (c. 1273–1297 CE), not Purāṇic yuga-time.

## The household and the four children

- **The four siblings of Viṭṭhalapant** — the excommunicated Brāhmaṇa **Viṭṭhalapant Kulkarṇī** of Āpegāv and his wife **Rukmiṇībāī** had four children, **all venerated as saints**: **Nivṛttināth** (eldest), **Jñāneśvar**, **Sopāndev**, and **Muktābāī** (youngest). The family is itself the lineage — *guru*, philosopher, poet and woman-mystic in one sibling-cluster. (Source: Wikipedia, *Nivruttinath*, *Muktabai*) [traditional]
- **Birth-order and rough dates [scholarly/traditional]** — tradition places the births across roughly **1273–1279 CE**: Nivṛttināth eldest (c. 1273), Jñāneśvar (c. 1275), Sopāndev (c. 1277), Muktābāī youngest (c. 1279). The dates hang off Jñāneśvar's relatively secure 1275–1296 anchor (see [`jnaneshwar.md`](jnaneshwar.md)). (Source: Wikipedia, *Nivruttinath*, *Sopan*, *Muktabai*)
- **Outcast childhood** — branded *saṃnyāsī-putra* ("children of a renunciant") because their father returned from *sannyāsa* to householder life, the four were **denied the sacred thread and excommunicated**; they grew up as social outcasts, which shaped the egalitarian, vernacular *bhakti* all four would carry. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*) [traditional]

## Nivṛttināth — the eldest brother, the Nāth-to-Vārkarī bridge

- **Nivṛttināth (Nivṛttinātha, c. 1273 – c. 1297)** — the **eldest sibling** and the **spiritual hinge** of the household: a Nāth-initiated *yogī* who became **Jñāneśvar's own *guru***, transmitting the Śaiva Siddha interiority into what became the Viṭṭhal-*bhakti* Vārkarī movement. (Source: Wikipedia, *Nivruttinath*) [traditional]
- **Initiation by Gahinīnāth** — separated from the family during a forest encounter (variously at **Tryambakeśvar / Brahmagiri**), the boy Nivṛttināth received Nāth *dīkṣā* directly from **Gahinīnāth (Gahininātha)**, placing him in the line **Ādinātha → Matsyendranātha → Gorakṣanātha → Gahinīnāth → Nivṛttināth** (see [`nath-sampradaya-and-the-siddhas.md`](nath-sampradaya-and-the-siddhas.md)). (Source: Wikipedia, *Nivruttinath*, *Natha Sampradaya*) [traditional]
  - *Variant (the tiger-cave):* the separation is often told as the family fleeing a tiger near Brahmagiri, Nivṛttināth sheltering in a cave where Gahinīnāth found and initiated him. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography; Wikipedia, *Nivruttinath*) [folk]
- **Guru to his own brother** — Nivṛttināth in turn **initiated Jñāneśvar**, making the *guru-śiṣya* bond a sibling bond. The Vārkarī tradition holds that **the entire movement descends through him** as the human node between the Nāth order and Marathi *bhakti*. (Source: Wikipedia, *Nivruttinath*) [traditional]
- **He commanded the Jñāneśvarī** — it was at **Nivṛttināth's instruction** that Jñāneśvar composed the **Jñāneśvarī** (the Marathi Gītā-commentary, 1290); the *guru*'s order is the work's stated occasion. He is thus the silent cause of the founding text of Marathi spirituality. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*, *Dnyaneshwari*) [traditional]
- **His own abhaṅgas** — Nivṛttināth is also remembered as a **composer of *abhaṅgas***, a smaller corpus than his brother's but counted within Vārkarī liturgy. (Source: Wikipedia, *Nivruttinath*) [traditional]
- **Samādhi at Tryambakeśvar** — after Jñāneśvar's *samādhi* he is said to have gone to **Tryambakeśvar (Trimbak)** near Nashik, where he took *samādhi* c. **1297**; his shrine there is a Vārkarī pilgrimage site. (Source: Wikipedia, *Nivruttinath*) [traditional]

## Sopāndev — the third sibling, "Sopān-kākā"

- **Sopāndev (Sopāna, c. 1277 – 1296)** — the **third of the four**, an *abhaṅga* poet venerated as a sant in his own right; affectionately called **"Sopān-kākā" ("Uncle Sopān")** in Vārkarī devotion. (Source: Wikipedia, *Sopan*) [traditional]
- **His abhaṅgas** — a corpus of devotional **abhaṅgas** is attributed to him, in the same Viṭṭhal-*bhakti* idiom as his siblings; he is named in the standard household-of-saints garland though his output is the least extensively preserved of the four. (Source: Wikipedia, *Sopan*; Vārkarī hagiography) [traditional]
- **Independent samādhi-shrine at Saswad** — Sopāndev took *samādhi* at **Saswad (Sāsvaḍ)**, southeast of Pune on the Karhā river, **shortly after Jñāneśvar's** in 1296. His **samādhi-temple at Saswad** is a distinct Vārkarī pilgrimage centre, and his **pālkhī (palanquin)** still processes from Saswad to Pandharpur in the annual *vārī* alongside those of Jñāneśvar and Tukārām. (Source: Wikipedia, *Sopan*; *Pandharpur Wari*) [traditional]

## Muktābāī — the youngest, woman-saint and prodigy

- **Muktābāī (Muktāī, c. 1279 – 1297)** — the **youngest sibling** and a celebrated **woman-saint of Marathi *bhakti***, a fixed name in any garland of women bhakti-poets alongside [Janābāī](janabai.md) and the later [Bahiṇābāī](bahinabai.md). A prodigy in her own right, not merely Jñāneśvar's little sister. (Source: Wikipedia, *Muktabai*; Tharu & Lalita, *Women Writing in India*) [scholarly]
- **The Tāṭīce Abhaṅga ("Verses of the Door")** — her most famous work: when the hurt Jñāneśvar withdrew and **shut the door (*tāṭī*)** against the world, the young Muktābāī coaxed him out with a celebrated cycle of **abhaṅgas**, each pleading *"open the door, saint"* and teaching that a true sant must **bear insult with an open heart**. The poems are read both as a sisterly vignette and as a compact statement of the *bhakti* ethic of forbearance. (Source: *Tāṭīce Abhaṅga*; Wikipedia, *Muktabai*; Tharu & Lalita) [traditional]
- **Guru to the aged yogī Cāṅgadeva** — the proud occult-master **Cāṅgadeva (Changdev)**, said to be **1,400 years old** through yogic power, became disciple of the household; tradition makes the **child Muktābāī his *guru***, her terse rebukes finally breaking his ego where his miracles failed. He is the addressee of Jñāneśvar's **Cāṅgadeva Pāsaṣṭī**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Muktabai*, *Changdev*) [folk]
  - *Variant (the wall and the blank letter):* in the wall-riding contest Cāṅgadeva arrived on a tiger with a serpent-whip; the siblings rode a moving mud wall. His inability to address the child-sages produced the **blank 65-line letter** answered by the *Cāṅgadeva Pāsaṣṭī*. (Source: Wikipedia, *Changdev*) [folk]
- **Mukāī and the food** — a folk vignette tells of Muktābāī baking *mande* (a flat sweet bread) on **Jñāneśvar's own back**, heated by his yogic fire, when no oven was at hand — emblem of the siblings' shared *siddhi*. (Source: Vārkarī hagiography; Wikipedia, *Muktabai*) [folk]
- **Her abhaṅgas** — beyond the *Tāṭīce Abhaṅga* a body of independent **abhaṅgas** is attributed to her, making her one of the **earliest named women poets in Marathi**. (Source: Tharu & Lalita, *Women Writing in India*; Wikipedia, *Muktabai*) [scholarly]
- **Disappearance in a storm** — unlike her brothers' entombed *samādhis*, Muktābāī is said to have **vanished into light during a thunderstorm** on the bank of the **Tāpī (Tapti) river near Mehūn (Edalabad / Muktainagar)** c. **1297** — a *samādhi* by disappearance rather than burial. Her shrine stands at **Muktainagar**, named for her. (Source: Wikipedia, *Muktabai*) [folk]

## The prodigy generation's near-simultaneous end

- **Four samādhis within a year (1296–1297)** — the whole generation closes in close sequence: **Jñāneśvar's *sañjīvana samādhi* at Āḷandī (1296)**, then **Sopāndev at Saswad (1296)**, **Muktābāī's disappearance on the Tāpī (1297)**, **Nivṛttināth at Tryambakeśvar (1297)**, and **Cāṅgadeva's** departure with them. The household that founded the Vārkarī movement exits almost together. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*, *Muktabai*, *Nivruttinath*, *Sopan*) [traditional]
- **Living institution** — three of the four (Jñāneśvar, Sopāndev, Nivṛttināth) hold **samādhi-shrines that anchor *pālkhī* processions** into the Pandharpur *vārī*; Muktābāī's Muktainagar shrine and the *Tāṭīce Abhaṅga* keep the youngest sibling a living presence in Marathi devotion. The pilgrimage-companion of the household, the tailor-sant **[Nāmdev](namdev.md)**, carries their memory into the next generation of sants. (Source: Wikipedia, *Pandharpur Wari*; and [`namdev.md`](namdev.md))
