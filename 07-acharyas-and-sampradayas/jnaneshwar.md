# Jñāneśvar (Dnyāneśvar / Jñānadeva) — Founder of the Vārkarī Movement

> Detail node under [`07-acharyas-and-sampradayas/`](./README.md) (master index: [`../README.md`](../README.md)). The 13th-c. **Nāth**-lineage child-prodigy of **Āḷandī**, founder-philosopher of the Marathi **Vārkarī** Viṭṭhal-*bhakti* movement and of Marathi devotional literature, author of the **Jñāneśvarī** and the **Amṛtānubhava**, who took **living *samādhi*** at 21.
> Frame against the lineage roster in [`north-bhakti-sants.md`](north-bhakti-sants.md), the *abhaṅga* apex in [`tukaram.md`](tukaram.md), and the Nāth order in [`shaiva-schools.md`](shaiva-schools.md). Two clocks: this is **academic / historical** dating (c. 1275–1296 CE), not Purāṇic yuga-time.

## Who He Was

- **Jñāneśvar (Marathi Dnyāneśvar; also Jñānadeva, Jñānobā, Māulī "Mother")** — the **founding luminary of the Vārkarī sampradāya** and the first great vernacularizer of Vedānta into Marathi; a Nāth-initiated *yogī* turned Viṭṭhal-*bhakta* of **Āḷandī** (Ālandī) on the Indrāyaṇī near Pune. His honorific **Māulī** ("Mother") expresses the affection the tradition holds for him as its nurturing source. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*; Prepp)
- **Dating [scholarly]:** **c. 1275–1296 CE**, with *samādhi* firmly placed at **1296** (Śaka 1218). His major work is internally dated to **1290** (Śaka 1212). The relatively secure dates make him an anchor-point for fixing the rest of the early Vārkarī chronology. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*; *Dnyaneshwari*)

## Nāth Lineage — the Yogic Descent

- **The Nāth transmission** — Jñāneśvar's spiritual authority runs not through Brahminical *guru-paramparā* but through the **Nāth Siddha-yogī order** (see [`shaiva-schools.md`](shaiva-schools.md)): the line **Ādinātha (Śiva) → Matsyendranātha → Gorakṣanātha → Gahininātha → Nivṛttinātha → Jñāneśvar**. He is thus a Nāth *yogī* who carried haṭha/kuṇḍalinī interiority into a Kṛṣṇa-Viṭṭhal *bhakti* idiom. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*; *Natha Sampradaya*)
- **Viṭṭhalapant, the father** — Jñāneśvar's father **Viṭṭhalapant Kulkarṇī** of Āpegāv, a learned Brāhmaṇa, took *sannyāsa* (renunciation) and then, on his guru's order, **returned to householder life** and resumed marriage with his wife **Rukmiṇībāī** — a reversal Brahminical orthodoxy held to be impossible. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*; Prepp) [folk/traditional]
- **Nivṛttinātha, the elder brother and guru** — the eldest of the four children, **Nivṛttināth**, received Nāth initiation directly from **Gahininātha** and in turn became **Jñāneśvar's own guru**, commanding him to compose the Marathi Gītā-commentary. The guru-disciple bond is here also a sibling bond — the family is the lineage. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*) [traditional]

## The Four Prodigy Siblings and Their Ostracism

- **The four children** — Viṭṭhalapant and Rukmiṇībāī had four extraordinary children, each a saint in the tradition: **Nivṛttināth** (eldest, the guru), **Jñāneśvar**, **Sopāndev**, and the sister **Muktābāī** (youngest). All four are venerated together in Vārkarī memory. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*, *Muktabai*)
- **Branded *saṃnyāsī-putra*** — because their father had **returned from renunciation to fatherhood**, orthodox Brāhmaṇas of the region treated the children as illegitimate "children of a renunciant," **denied them the sacred thread (*upanayana*) and excommunicated the family** from the Brahmin community. The siblings grew up as social outcasts. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*; Prepp) [traditional]
- **The parents' penance** — to lift the curse on their children, **Viṭṭhalapant and Rukmiṇībāī are said to have ended their own lives** (by drowning at the saṅgama at Prayāg / or at Āḷandī, variants differ), after which the boys sought a *śuddhi-patra* (certificate of purification) from the pundits of Paithan. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*) [folk]
  - *Variant (Paithan ordeal):* the Brāhmaṇas of **Paithan** demanded miracles before relenting; this is the setting traditionally given for the buffalo and wall legends below. [folk]

## The Legends [folk]

- **The buffalo that recited the Veda** — challenged at Paithan to prove that scripture was not the monopoly of the twice-born, the boy Jñāneśvar is said to have made a **water-buffalo (named Cakhdyā) recite the Ṛgveda**, demonstrating that the divine self is one in Brahmin and beast alike — a charter-myth against caste gatekeeping of the Veda. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*; Vārkarī hagiography)
- **Riding the wall (Cāṅgadeva episode)** — the aged, proud yogī **Cāṅgadeva (Changadev)**, master of occult powers, rode to meet the children **on a tiger, holding a live serpent as a whip**; the siblings, seated on a **mud wall, made the wall move forward to meet him**, showing that command over a dead wall outmatched command over a living tiger. Humbled, Cāṅgadeva became a disciple. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*, *Changdev*; Vārkarī hagiography)
  - *Variant (the blank letter):* Cāṅgadeva, unable to decide how to address the child-sage (neither as superior nor inferior), sent a **blank letter of 65 lines' worth of paper**; Jñāneśvar's reply, **expounding each implied line, became the *Cāṅgadeva Pāsaṣṭī* ("the 65 [verses] for Cāṅgadeva")**. Muktābāī's terse rebukes are said to have finally broken the old yogī's ego. (Source: Wikipedia, *Changdev*; *Amrutanubhav*)
- **Muktābāī and the *tāṭī* abhaṅgas** — when Jñāneśvar once withdrew in hurt and shut the door (*tāṭī*), his young sister **Muktābāī** coaxed him out with a celebrated set of *abhaṅgas* (the *Tāṭīce Abhaṅga*) — a famous vignette of the household's saintly intimacy. (Source: Wikipedia, *Muktabai*) [folk]

## His Works — the Foundation of Marathi Spirituality

- **Jñāneśvarī (Bhāvārtha-dīpikā, "Lamp on the Inner Meaning")** — his masterwork: a **Marathi commentary-cum-paraphrase of the [Bhagavad Gītā](../90-literature-corpus/README.md)** in roughly **9,000 *ovī*** verses, composed (dictated to the scribe **Saccidānanda Bābā**) in **1290 CE** at Newāsā. It does not merely translate but **expands and re-poeticizes** the Gītā, opening Vedāntic teaching to non-Sanskrit speakers — the **foundational text of Marathi literature and of Vārkarī thought**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwari*; Prepp) [scholarly]
  - *The *ovī* form:* the supple Marathi quatrain-metre Jñāneśvar perfected for narrative-philosophical verse, distinct from the shorter *abhaṅga* of the later sants. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwari*)
  - *Textual note [scholarly]:* the standard recension owes much to **Eknāth's c. 1584 restoration** of a corrupted text (see [`north-bhakti-sants.md`](north-bhakti-sants.md)); no autograph survives.
- **Amṛtānubhava (Amṛtānubhav, "Nectar of Mystical Experience")** — a shorter, **independent philosophical work** (~800 *ovī* across 10 chapters) setting out his **non-dual *cidvilāsa* / *sphūrti-vāda*** Vedānta: the play of pure Consciousness, the unity of Śiva and Śakti, and a critique of mere *māyā*-doctrine. Composed after the Jñāneśvarī, it is his own voice rather than a commentary. (Source: Wikipedia, *Amrutanubhav*) [scholarly]
- **Cāṅgadeva Pāsaṣṭī** — the **65-verse epistle to Cāṅgadeva** (see legend above), a compact statement of his non-dual metaphysics framed as instruction to the proud old yogī. (Source: Wikipedia, *Changdev*; *Amrutanubhav*)
- **Haripāṭha and *abhaṅgas*** — a body of devotional **abhaṅgas**, including the **Haripāṭha** (a garland of verses on the saving power of the divine Name), which became core Vārkarī liturgy sung on the *vārī*. These mark his pivot from Nāth yoga to public *nāma-bhakti*. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*) [scholarly]

## Sañjīvana Samādhi — the Living Entombment

- **Sañjīvana samādhi at Āḷandī (1296)** — at the age of about **21**, having completed his works and a pilgrimage (traditionally with **Nāmdev**), Jñāneśvar is said to have entered a chamber at **Āḷandī** and taken **sañjīvana ("living") samādhi** — voluntarily sealing himself in conscious meditation rather than dying an ordinary death. The Vārkarī tradition holds him to be **still present in samādhi** beneath the shrine. (Source: Wikipedia, *Dnyaneshwar*; Vārkarī hagiography) [folk/traditional]
- **The siblings' end** — within roughly a year **Sopāndev, Muktābāī, Nivṛttināth** and **Cāṅgadeva** are each said to have followed in samādhi or departure, closing the prodigy generation. (Source: Wikipedia, *Muktabai*, *Changdev*) [folk]

## Place in the Succession — the Founder-Philosopher

- **Jñāneśvar → Nāmdev → Eknāth → Tukārām** — the canonical Vārkarī four-saint sequence. Jñāneśvar stands at its **head as founder-philosopher**: where [Tukārām](tukaram.md) is the emotional *kalaśa* (finial), Jñāneśvar is the **intellectual foundation-stone**, the one who first gave the movement a scripture in Marathi. (Source: Wikipedia, *Warkari*; and [`north-bhakti-sants.md`](north-bhakti-sants.md))
  - **[Nāmdev](north-bhakti-sants.md)** — tailor-sant, his contemporary and pilgrimage-companion, co-founder of Vārkarī *kīrtan*.
  - **[Eknāth](north-bhakti-sants.md)** — Brāhmaṇa consolidator of Paithan, restorer of the Jñāneśvarī text.
  - **[Tukārām](tukaram.md)** — Śūdra grocer-sant of Dehu, the *abhaṅga* apex.
  - *Note:* the chain runs from a **Nāth-yogī excommunicate-Brahmin** (Jñāneśvar) to a **Śūdra grocer** (Tukārām) — its social breadth is itself the lineage's claim.
- **The Āḷandī pālkhī** — Jñāneśvar's **pādukā (sandals)** are carried each year in a **pālkhī (palanquin) from Āḷandī to Pandharpur**, converging with Tukārām's pālkhī from Dehu in the great **Āṣāḍhī vārī** — the living institution his life founded. The procession in its present organized form was reshaped in the **1820s** by **Haibatravbābā Ārphaḷkar**. (Source: Wikipedia, *Pandharpur Wari*; and [`tukaram.md`](tukaram.md))
